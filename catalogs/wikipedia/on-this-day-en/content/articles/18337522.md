A **Christian** ([/ˈkrɪstʃən, -tiən/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/f/fd/En-us-Christian.ogg/En-us-Christian.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/f/fd/En-us-Christian.ogg/En-us-Christian.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>) is a person who follows or adheres to [Christianity](5211.md), a [monotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism) [Abrahamic religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religion) based on the life and teachings of [Jesus Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_in_Christianity). Christians form the largest religious community in the world.[^cite_note-7] The words *[Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_\(title\))* and *Christian* derive from the [Koine Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koine_Greek) title *Christós* (Χριστός), a translation of the [Biblical Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_Hebrew) term *[mashiach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mashiach)* (מָשִׁיחַ) (usually rendered as *messiah* in English).[^cite_note-8] While there are diverse interpretations of Christianity which sometimes conflict,[^cite_note-Woodhead_2004_n.p-9][^cite_note-10] they are united in believing that Jesus has a unique significance.[^cite_note-Woodhead_2004_n.p-9] The term *Christian* used as an adjective is descriptive of anything associated with Christianity or [Christian churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_churches), or in a proverbial sense "all that is noble, and good, and Christ-like."[^cite_note-CCEL-11]

According to a 2011 [Pew Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pew_Research_Center) survey, there were 2.3 billion Christians around the world, up from about 600 million in 1910.[^cite_note-Global_Christianity-3] Today, about 37% of all Christians live in the [Americas](29833.md), about 26% live in [Europe](9239.md), 24% live in [sub-Saharan Africa](27067.md), about 13% live in [Asia](689.md) and the [Pacific](23070.md), and 1% live in the [Middle East](19323.md) and [North Africa](21714.md).[^cite_note-Global_Christianity-3] Christians make up the majority of the population in 158 countries and territories.[^cite_note-Global_Christianity-3] 280 million Christians live as a [minority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minority_group). About half of all Christians worldwide are [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic), while more than a third are [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant) (37%).[^cite_note-Global_Christianity-3] [Eastern Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Christians), including the [Eastern Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox), [Oriental Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Orthodox), and [Church of the East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_East), comprise 12% of the world's Christians.[^cite_note-Global_Christianity-3] Other Christian groups make up the remainder. By 2050, the Christian population is expected to exceed 3 billion due to overall [total fertility rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_fertility_rate) according to [Pew Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pew_Research_Center).[^cite_note-Global_Christianity-3] According to a 2012 Pew Research Center survey, Christianity will remain the [world's largest religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_religious_groups#Largest_religious_groups) in 2050, if current trends continue. In recent history, Christians have [experienced persecution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_Christians) of varying severity, especially in the [Middle-East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_the_Middle_East), North Africa, [East Asia](19605700.md), and [South Asia](21566765.md).[^cite_note-BBC_genocide_2019_BBC-12][^cite_note-Kay_2019_NP-13][^cite_note-Wintour_2019_Guardian-14]

## Etymology

The Greek word Χριστιανός (*Christianos*), meaning 'follower of Christ', comes from Χριστός (*Christos*), meaning '[anointed one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anointing)',[^cite_note-15] with an adjectival ending borrowed from Latin to denote adhering to, or even belonging to, as in slave ownership.[^cite_note-16] In the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) [Septuagint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septuagint), *christos* was used to translate the [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language) מָשִׁיחַ (*Mašíaḥ*, '[messiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messiah)'), meaning "\[one who is] anointed".[^cite_note-17] In other European languages, equivalent words to Christian are likewise derived from the Greek, such as *chrétien* in French and *cristiano* in Spanish.

The abbreviations *Xian* and *Xtian* (and similarly formed other parts of speech) have been used since at least the 17th century: *[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary)* shows a 1634 use of *Xtianity* and *Xian* is seen in a 1634–38 diary.[^cite_note-oed-x-18][^cite_note-19] The word *[Xmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xmas)* uses a similar contraction.

## Early usage



The [Church of Saint Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Saint_Peter) near [Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antioch) (modern-day [Antakya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antakya)), the city where the [disciples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disciple_\(Christianity\)) were called "Christians"

[^cite_note-20]

The first recorded use of the term (or its [cognates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognate) in other languages) is in the [New Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament), in [Acts 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_11) after Barnabas brought Saul (Paul) to [Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_centers_of_Christianity#Antioch) where they taught the [disciples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disciple_\(Christianity\)) for about a year. The text says that "the disciples were called Christians first in Antioch" ([Acts 11\:26](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_11\:26)). The second mention of the term follows in [Acts 26](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_26), where [Herod Agrippa II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_Agrippa_II) replied to [Paul the Apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle), "Then Agrippa said unto Paul, Almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian." ([Acts 26\:28](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_26\:28)). The third and final New Testament reference to the term is in [1 Peter 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_Peter_4), which exhorts believers: "Yet if *\[any man suffer]* as a Christian, let him not be ashamed; but let him glorify God on this behalf." ([1 Peter 4\:16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_Peter_4\:16)).[^cite_note-21]

Kenneth Samuel Wuest holds that all three original New Testament verses' usages reflect a derisive element in the term *Christian* to refer to followers of Christ who did not acknowledge the emperor of Rome.[^cite_note-22] The city of Antioch, where someone gave them the name *Christians*, had a reputation for coming up with such nicknames.[^cite_note-23] However Peter's apparent endorsement of the term led to its being preferred over "Nazarenes" and the term *Christianoi* from [1 Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_Peter) becomes the standard term in the [Early Church Fathers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Church_Fathers) from [Ignatius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignatius_of_Antioch) and [Polycarp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polycarp) onwards.[^cite_note-24]

The earliest occurrences of the term in non-Christian literature include [Josephus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephus_on_Jesus), referring to "the tribe of Christians, so named from him;"[^cite_note-25] [Pliny the Younger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Younger) in [correspondence with Trajan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistulae_\(Pliny\)); and [Tacitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacitus_on_Christ), writing at the beginning of the 2nd century. In the *[Annals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annals_\(Tacitus\))* he relates that "by vulgar appellation \[they were] commonly called Christians"[^cite_note-26] and identifies Christians as [Nero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nero)'s scapegoats for the [Great Fire of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Fire_of_Rome).[^cite_note-27]

### Nazarenes

Another [term for Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Christian_synonyms) which appears in the New Testament is [*Nazarenes*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazarene_\(title\)). [Jesus](1095706.md) is named as a Nazarene in [Matthew 2\:23](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_2\:23), while Paul is said to be Nazarene in [Acts 24\:5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_24\:5). The latter verse makes it clear that Nazarene also referred to the name of a sect or heresy, as well as the town called [Nazareth](21702.md).

The term Nazarene was also used by [Tertullus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertullus) (*Against Marcion* 4\:8), the lawyer representing the Jews, who records the phrase "the Jews call us Nazarenes". In c. 331 AD, [Eusebius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eusebius) records that Christ was called a Nazoraean from the name [Nazareth](21702.md), and that in earlier centuries "Christians" were once called "Nazarenes".[^cite_note-28] The Hebrew equivalent of *Nazarenes*, *Notzrim*, occurs in the [Babylonian Talmud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_Talmud) in reference to an earlier, non-Christian group, versus the current modern-Hebrew usage for Christians.

### Definition

&#x20;A wide range of beliefs and practices are found across the world among those who call themselves Christian. [Denominations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_denomination) and sects disagree on a common definition of "Christianity". For example, [Timothy Beal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_Beal) notes the disparity of beliefs among those who identify as Christians in the United States as follows:&#x20;

> Although all of them have their historical roots in Christian theology and tradition, and although most would identify themselves as Christian, many would not identify others within the larger category as Christian. Most Baptists and fundamentalists ([Christian Fundamentalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Fundamentalism)), for example, would not acknowledge Mormonism or Christian Science as Christian. In fact, the nearly 77 percent of Americans who self-identify as Christian are a diverse pluribus of Christianities that are far from any collective unity.[^cite_note-29]

[Linda Woodhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linda_Woodhead) attempts to provide a common belief thread for Christians by noting that "Whatever else they might disagree about, Christians are at least united in believing that Jesus has a unique significance."[^cite_note-Woodhead_2004_n.p-9] [Michael Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Martin_\(philosopher\)) evaluated three historical Christian creeds (the [Apostles' Creed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostles'_Creed), the [Nicene Creed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicene_Creed) and the [Athanasian Creed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athanasian_Creed)) to establish a set of basic Christian assumptions which include belief in [theism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theism), the [historicity of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historicity_of_Jesus), the [Incarnation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incarnation_\(Christianity\)), [salvation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_soteriology) through faith in Jesus, and [Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_views_of_Jesus) as an ethical role model.[^cite_note-30]

### Hebrew and Jewish terms



[Nazareth](21702.md) is described as the childhood home of [Jesus](1095706.md). Various languages employ the word [*Nazarene*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazarene_\(title\)) as a general designation for members of the Christian religion.

[^cite_note-31]

The [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew) word meaning Christian is *Notzri* (נוֹצְרִי) 'Nazarene', derived from the mention in Christian scripture that Jesus came from the Jewish village of Nazareth (נָצְרַת) 'Natzeret/Natzrat' in the [Galilee](12639.md), today in [northern Israel](324281.md).[^cite_note-32] As Jesus is not a part of Judaism, modern Hebrew-speaking Israelis and English-speaking Jews refer to Christianity’s [Messianic Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messianic_Judaism) sect as *Yehudim Meshihi'im* (יְהוּדִים מְשִׁיחִיִּים) 'Messianic Jews', and generally, as Christians.

### Arabic terms

In [Arabic-speaking cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world), two words are commonly used for Christians: *Naṣrānī* (نصراني), plural *Naṣārā* (نصارى) is generally understood to be derived from [Nazarenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazarene_\(sect\)), believers of Jesus of Nazareth through [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language) (Aramaic); *Masīḥī* (مسيحي) means followers of the Messiah.[^cite_note-SOFIR-33] Where there is a distinction, *Naṣrānī* refers to people from a Christian culture and *Masīḥī* is used by Christians themselves for those with a religious faith in Jesus.[^cite_note-Tayler-34] In some countries *Naṣrānī* tends to be used generically for non-Muslim Western foreigners.[^cite_note-35]

Another Arabic word sometimes used for Christians, particularly in a political context, is *Ṣalībī* (صليبي 'Crusader') from *ṣalīb* (صليب 'cross'), which refers to [Crusaders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades) and may have negative connotations.[^cite_note-SOFIR-33][^cite_note-36] However, *Ṣalībī* is a modern term; historically, Muslim writers described European Christian Crusaders as *al-Faranj* or *Alfranj* (الفرنج) and *Firinjīyah* (الفرنجيّة) in Arabic.[^cite_note-37] This word comes from the name of the [Franks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franks) and can be seen in the Arab history text [Al-Kamil fi al-Tarikh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kamil_fi_al-Tarikh) by [Ali ibn al-Athir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_ibn_al-Athir).[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39]

In the [Maltese language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltese_language), a [Semitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitic_languages) European language related to Arabic written in the Latin alphabet, Christians are referred to as *Nsara*, singular masculine *Nisrani*.[^cite_note-40] The [Romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_languages)-borrowed *Kristjan* may also be used.[^cite_note-41]

The most common [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language) word is *Masīhī* (مسیحی), from [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language). Other words are *Nasrānī* (نصرانی), from [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Syriac) for 'Nazarene', and *Tarsā* (ترسا), from the [Middle Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Persian) word *Tarsāg*, also meaning 'Christian', derived from *tars*, meaning 'fear, respect'.[^cite_note-42]

An old Kurdish word for Christian frequently in usage was *felle* (فەڵە), coming from the root word meaning 'to be saved, attain salvation'.[^cite_note-43]

The Syriac term *Nasrani* ('Nazarene') has also been attached to the [Saint Thomas Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Thomas_Christians) of [Kerala](4349459.md), India. In [northern India](1552939.md) and [Pakistan](23235.md), Christians are referred to *ʿĪsāʾī* ([Hindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindi_language): ईसाई, [Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu_language): عیسائی).[^cite_note-John2020-44][^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46] *Masīhī* ([Hindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindi_language): मसीही, [Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu_language): مسیحی) is a term Christians use to refer to themselves as well.[^cite_note-Bharati2004-47]

In the past, the [Malays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malays_\(ethnic_group\)) used to call Christians in [Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malay_language) by the Portuguese loanword *Serani* (from Arabic *Naṣrānī*), but the term now refers to the modern [Kristang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristang_people) creoles of [Malaysia](3607937.md). In the [Indonesian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_language), the term *Nasrani* is also used alongside *Kristen*.

The Chinese word is [基督](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%9F%BA%E7%9D%A3)[徒](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%BE%92) (*jīdū tú*), literally 'Christ follower'. The name *Christ* was originally phonetically written in Chinese as 基利斯督, which was later abbreviated as 基督.[^cite_note-48] The term is *Kî-tuk* in the southern [Hakka dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakka_dialect); the two characters are pronounced *Jīdū* in Mandarin Chinese. In Vietnam, the same two characters read *[Cơ đốc](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/C%C6%A1%20%C4%91%E1%BB%91c)*, and a "follower of Christianity" is a *tín đồ Cơ đốc giáo*.



Japanese Christians (*Kurisuchan*) in Portuguese costume, 16–17th century

In Japan, the term *[kirishitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirishitan)* (written in Edo period documents 吉利支丹, 切支丹, and in modern Japanese histories as キリシタン), from Portuguese *cristão*, referred to Roman Catholics in the 16th and 17th centuries before the religion was banned by the [Tokugawa shogunate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_shogunate). Today, Christians are referred to in [Standard Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Japanese) as キリスト教徒 (*Kirisuto-kyōto*) or the English-derived term クリスチャン (*kurisuchan*).

Korean still uses 기독교도 ([RR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revised_Romanization): *Gidokkyodo*) for 'Christian', though the Portuguese loanword 그리스도 (RR: *Geuriseudo*) now replaced the old [Sino-Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Korean_vocabulary) 기독 (RR: *Gidok*), which refers to Christ himself.

In Thailand, the most common terms are คนคริสต์ ([RTGS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Thai_General_System_of_Transcription): *khon khrit*) or ชาวคริสต์ (RTGS: *chao khrit*) which literally means 'Christ person/people' or 'Jesus person/people'. The Thai word คริสต์ (RTGS: *khrit*) is derived from *Christ*.

In the [Philippines](23440.md), the most common terms are *Kristiyano* (for 'Christian') and *Kristiyanismo* (for 'Christianity') in most [Philippine languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine_languages); both derive from Spanish *cristiano* and *cristianismo* (also used in [Chavacano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chavacano)) due to the country's rich history of early Christianity during the [Spanish colonial era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_\(1565%E2%80%931898\)). Some Protestants in the Philippines use the term *Kristiyano* (before the term *[born again](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_again)* became popular) to differentiate themselves from [Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_the_Philippines) (*Katoliko*).

The region of modern Eastern Europe and Central Eurasia has a long history of Christianity and Christian communities on its lands. In ancient times, in the first centuries after the birth of Christ, when this region was called Scythia, the geographical area of [Scythians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythians) – Christians already lived there.[^cite_note-49] Later the region saw the first states to adopt Christianity officially – initially [Armenia](10918072.md) (301 AD) and [Georgia](48768.md) (337 AD), later [Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianization_of_Bulgaria) (c. 864) and [Kyivan Rus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kievan_Rus') (c. 988 AD).

In some areas, people came to denote themselves as Christians ([Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): христиане, крестьяне; [Ukrainian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_language): християни, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Ukrainian): *khrystyiany*) and as Russians ([Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): русские), [Ruthenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruthenians) ([Old East Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_East_Slavic_language): русини, руснаки, romanized: *rusyny, rusnaky*), or Ukrainians ([Ukrainian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_language): українці, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Ukrainian): *ukraintsi*).

In time the Russian term крестьяне (*khrest'yane*) acquired the meaning 'peasants of Christian faith' and later 'peasants' (the main part of the population of the region), while the term [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): христиане (*khristiane*) retained its religious meaning and the term [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): русские (*russkie*) began to mean representatives of the heterogeneous Russian nation formed on the basis of common Christian faith and language, which strongly influenced the history and development of the region. In the region, the term *Orthodox faith* ([Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): православная вера, *pravoslavnaia vera*) or *Russian faith* ([Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): русская вера, *russkaia vera*) from the earliest times became almost as common as the original *Christian faith* ([Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): христианская, крестьянская вера *khristianskaia, krestianskaia*).

Also in some contexts the term *[cossack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cossack)* ([Old East Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_East_Slavic_language): козак, казак, romanized: *kozak, kazak*) was used to denote "free" Christians of steppe origin and East Slavic language.

### Other non-religious usages

Nominally "Christian" societies made "Christian" a default label for citizenship or for "people like us".[^cite_note-50] In this context, religious or ethnic minorities can use "Christians" or "you Christians" loosely as a shorthand term for mainstream members of society who do not belong to their group – even in a thoroughly secular (though formerly Christian) society.[^cite_note-51]

## Demographics

For a detailed breakdown of Christian demographics, see [Christianity by country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_by_country).

As of 2020, [Christianity](5211.md) had approximately 2.4 billion adherents.[^cite_note-globalreligiousfutures.org-52][^cite_note-All_Religions_global_totals-53][^cite_note-World-54][^cite_note-ForeignPolicy-55][^cite_note-Major_Religions_Ranked_by_Size-56] The faith represents about a third of the world's population and is the largest religion in the world. Christians have composed about 33 percent of the world's population for around 100 years. The largest Christian denomination is the [Roman Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church), with 1.3 billion adherents, representing half of all Christians.[^cite_note-57]

Christianity remains the dominant religion in the [Western World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_World), where 70% are Christians.[^cite_note-Global_Christianity-3] According to a 2012 [Pew Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pew_Research_Center) survey, if current trends continue, Christianity will remain the [world's largest religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_religious_groups#Largest_religious_groups) by 2050. By 2050, the Christian population is expected to exceed 3 billion. While Muslims have an average of 3.1 children per woman—the highest rate of all religious groups—Christians are second, with 2.7 children per woman. High birth rates and conversion were cited as the reason for [Christian population growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_population_growth). A 2015 study found that approximately 10.2 million [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim) [converted to Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Converted_to_Christianity).[^cite_note-Believers_in_Christ_from_a_Muslim_Background-58] Christianity is growing in [Africa](5334607.md),[^cite_note-59] [Asia](689.md),[^cite_note-Time_2001-60][^cite_note-61][^cite_note-62][^cite_note-Lausanne-63] [Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe),[^cite_note-64] [Latin America](18524.md),[^cite_note-Time_2001-60] the [Muslim world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world),[^cite_note-Believers_in_Christ_from_a_Muslim_Background-58][^cite_note-65] and [Oceania](22621.md).[^cite_note-66]

### Socioeconomics

A [Pew Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pew_Center) study about [religion and education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religiosity_and_education) around the world in 2016, found that Christians ranked as the second most educated religious group around in the [world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World) after Jews with an average of 9.3 years of schooling,[^cite_note-Pew2016-70] and the highest numbers of years of schooling among Christians were found in [Germany](11867.md) (13.6),[^cite_note-Pew2016-70] [New Zealand](4913064.md) (13.5)[^cite_note-Pew2016-70] and [Estonia](28222445.md) (13.1).[^cite_note-Pew2016-70] Christians were also found to have the second highest number of [graduate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_degree) and [post-graduate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-graduate) degrees per capita while in absolute numbers ranked in the first place (220 million).[^cite_note-Pew2016-70] Between the various [Christian communities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Christianity), Singapore outranks other nations in terms of Christians who obtain a university degree in institutions of [higher education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_education) (67%),[^cite_note-Pew2016-70] followed by the [Christians of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Israel) (63%),[^cite_note-71] and the [Christians of Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Georgia_\(country\)) (57%).[^cite_note-Pew2016-70]

According to the study, Christians in [North America](21139.md), [Europe](9239.md), [Middle East](19323.md), [North Africa](21714.md) and [Asia Pacific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Pacific) regions are highly educated since many of the world's [universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universities) were built by the historic [Christian denominations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_denominations),[^cite_note-Pew2016-70] in addition to the historical evidence that "Christian monks built libraries and, in the days before printing presses, preserved important earlier writings produced in Latin, Greek and Arabic".[^cite_note-Pew2016-70] According to the same study, Christians have a significant amount of [gender equality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_equality) in educational attainment,[^cite_note-Pew2016-70] and the study suggests that one of the reasons is the encouragement of the [Protestant Reformers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Reformers) in promoting the [education of women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_of_women), which led to the eradication of illiteracy among females in Protestant communities.[^cite_note-Pew2016-70]

## Culture

Main article: [Christian culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_culture)



Set of pictures showcasing [Christian culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_culture) and famous Christian leaders

[Christian culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_culture) describes the cultural practices common to Christian peoples. There are variations in the application of Christian beliefs in different cultures and traditions.[^cite_note-72] Christian culture has influenced and [assimilated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_assimilation) much from the [Greco-Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Roman_world), [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire#Culture), [Western culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture),[^cite_note-Caltron_J.H_Hayas-73] [Middle Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_the_Middle_East),[^cite_note-74][^cite_note-75] [Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Slavic_cultures),[^cite_note-Ware1993-76] [Caucasian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasus),[^cite_note-Ware1993-76] and [Indian cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_India).

Since the spread of Christianity from the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant) to Europe and North Africa and [Horn of Africa](62142.md) during the early [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire), Christendom has been divided in the pre-existing [Greek East and Latin West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_East_and_Latin_West). Consequently, different versions of the Christian cultures arose with their own [rites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rite_\(Christianity\)) and practices, centered around the cities such as [Rome](25458.md) ([Western Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Christianity)) and [Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archdiocese_of_Carthage), whose communities was called Western or Latin [Christendom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christendom),[^cite_note-77] and [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople) ([Eastern Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Christianity)), [Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antioch) ([Syriac Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Christianity)), [Kerala](4349459.md) ([Indian Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_India)) and [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria), among others, whose communities were called Eastern or Oriental Christendom.[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-79][^cite_note-Cameron_2006_42–49-80] The [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) was one of the peaks in [Christian history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_history) and [Christian civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_civilization).[^cite_note-Cameron_2006_42–49-80] From the 11th to 13th centuries, [Latin Christendom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Christendom) rose to the central role of the [Western world](21208200.md) and [Western culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture).[^cite_note-Crisis_in_Western_Education-81]

Western culture, throughout most of its history, has been nearly equivalent to Christian culture, and a large portion of the population of the Western Hemisphere can be described as practicing or nominal Christians. The notion of "Europe" and the "Western World" has been intimately connected with the concept of "Christianity and Christendom".[^cite_note-Crisis_in_Western_Education-81] Outside the Western world, Christians has had an influence and contributed on various cultures, such as in Africa, the Near East, Middle East, East Asia, Southeast Asia, and the Indian subcontinent.[^cite_note-82][^cite_note-83]

Christians have made noted contributions to a range of fields, including philosophy,[^cite_note-A._Spinello-84][^cite_note-85] [science and technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Christians_in_science_and_technology),[^cite_note-Gilley-86][^cite_note-Steane-87][^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89] [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_and_health_care),[^cite_note-S._Kroger-90][^cite_note-91] [fine arts and architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Catholic_Church_artists),[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93] [politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_and_politics), [literatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Catholic_authors),[^cite_note-94] [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Music),[^cite_note-E._McGrath-95] and [business](39206.md).[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-97] According to *100 Years of Nobel Prizes* a review of the Nobel Prizes award between 1901 and 2000 reveals that (65.4%) of [Nobel Prizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prizes) Laureates, [have identified Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Christian_Nobel_laureates) in its various forms as their religious preference.[^cite_note-Nobel_Prize-98]

## Persecution

Main article: [Persecution of Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_Christians)

See also: [Anti-Christian sentiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Christian_sentiment)

In 2017, [Open Doors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Doors), a [human rights NGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Christian_human_rights_non-governmental_organisations), estimated approximately 260 million Christians are subjected annually to "high, very high, or extreme persecution",[^cite_note-Weber_2017_CT-99] with North Korea considered the most hazardous nation for Christians.[^cite_note-Enos_2017_NK-100][^cite_note-101]

In 2019, a report[^cite_note-Mounstephen_2019_FCO_interim_rep-102][^cite_note-Mounstephen_2019_FCO_final_rep-103] commissioned by the United Kingdom's [Secretary of State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Foreign_and_Commonwealth_Affairs) of the [Foreign and Commonwealth Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_and_Commonwealth_Office) (FCO) to investigate global persecution of Christians found [religious persecution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_persecution) has increased, and is highest in the Middle East, North Africa, India, China, North Korea, and Latin America, among others,[^cite_note-Kay_2019_NP-13] and that it is global and not limited to Islamic states.[^cite_note-Mounstephen_2019_FCO_final_rep-103] This investigation found that approximately 80% of persecuted believers worldwide are Christians.[^cite_note-Wintour_2019_Guardian-14]
