The [far-left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far-left) in France encompasses political organizations, movements, and ideologies that are considered to be at the furthest left end of the political spectrum. It includes a variety of political traditions, such as [revolutionary socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutionary_socialism), [anarchism](12.md), [Maoism](46636.md), and [Trotskyism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trotskyism). Historically, the far-left has distinguished itself by opposing the reformist left represented by the [French Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Communist_Party) (PCF) and the [French Section of the Workers' International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Section_of_the_Workers'_International) (SFIO), advocating instead for the overthrow of capitalism and the establishment of a classless and egalitarian society.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECosseron200720-1][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPiot20089-2]

## History

### Origins

The roots of the far-left in France can be traced back to the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution) (1789–1799), particularly to the ideas of [Gracchus Babeuf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gracchus_Babeuf) and the "[Conspiracy of the Equals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conspiracy_of_the_Equals)" in 1796. Babeuf envisioned a [classless society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classless_society) and sought to overthrow the [French Directory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Directory) to achieve "perfect equality". His vision laid the groundwork for modern communist and socialist ideologies. The ideas of this movement were later reflected in the *Manifesto of the Equals,* co-authored by Babeuf and Sylvain Maréchal.[^cite_note-3]

### 19th century

During the 19th century, far-left ideas evolved through various revolutionary movements. Early socialists and communists emerged within republican circles opposed to the [Bourbon Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourbon_Restoration_in_France) and the rise of [Napoleon III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_III). Thinkers such as [Pierre-Joseph Proudhon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Joseph_Proudhon) and [Karl Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx) began to influence the ideological underpinnings of the far-left. The revolutionary fervor culminated in the [Paris Commune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Commune) of 1871, which became a defining moment for the far-left. The Paris Commune—a radical socialist and revolutionary government—served as a model for future far-left movements, emphasizing workers' control and direct democracy.[^cite_note-4]

### Early 20th century

The [Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution) of 1917 had a profound impact on the French far-left. The establishment of the [Third International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_International) inspired the creation of the PCF (PCF) in 1920; however, some revolutionary socialists rejected the authoritarianism of the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union), leading to the emergence of various Trotskyist, anarchist, and [left-communist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-communist) currents. The interwar years also saw the rise of [syndicalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syndicalism) and [anarcho-syndicalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarcho-syndicalism), with the [CGT-SR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conf%C3%A9d%C3%A9ration_G%C3%A9n%C3%A9rale_du_Travail-Syndicaliste_R%C3%A9volutionnaire) playing a key role. These groups emphasized direct action and workers' autonomy, contrasting with the centralized structure of the PCF.[^cite_note-5]

### Post-World War II

After 1945, the far-left gained renewed momentum. The PCF became a dominant force in French politics, but its alignment with Soviet policies led to criticism from other revolutionary groups. The [Fédération Anarchiste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%A9d%C3%A9ration_Anarchiste) (FA) and Trotskyist organizations like the [Parti Communiste Internationaliste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internationalist_Communist_Party_\(France\)) (PCI) expanded their activities. In the 1960s, the New Left emerged, driven by anti-colonial movements and opposition to the [Vietnam War](32611.md). Groups such as the [Ligue Communiste Révolutionnaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligue_Communiste_R%C3%A9volutionnaire) (LCR) and [Lutte Ouvrière](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutte_Ouvri%C3%A8re) (LO) gained prominence, advocating for workers' councils and anti-capitalist struggle.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPiot200852-6]

### 1968 and its aftermath

The [May 1968 protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_1968_events_in_France) marked a watershed moment for the far-left. Students and workers united in strikes and demonstrations, challenging both capitalism and traditional political structures. The protests inspired a wave of activism, leading to the growth of Maoist and [autonomist Marxist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomist_Marxist) groups. The [Gauche Prolétarienne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauche_Prol%C3%A9tarienne) (GP), a Maoist organization, and the [Union des Jeunesses Communistes Marxistes-Léninistes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_des_Jeunesses_Communistes_Marxistes-L%C3%A9ninistes) (UJCML) played pivotal roles; however, state repression and internal divisions eventually led to their decline.

### Late 20th century

The decline of [Soviet Communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Communism) in the 1980s forced far-left groups to adapt. The [Parti des Travailleurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parti_des_Travailleurs_\(France\)) (PT) emerged from the Trotskyist tradition, while anarchist groups like [Alternative Libertaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_Libertaire) gained influence within social movements. The rise of the alter-globalization movement in the 1990s, exemplified by protests against the [World Trade Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Trade_Organization) (WTO), provided new opportunities for the far-left to organize. Groups such as [ATTAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATTAC) and [Ras l'Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ras_l'Front) focused on anti-capitalist and anti-fascist activism.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECosseron2007-7]

### 21st century

The far-left has continued to play a significant role in French politics and social movements. The [Nouveau Parti Anticapitaliste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nouveau_Parti_Anticapitaliste) (NPA), founded in 2009, sought to unite anti-capitalist forces. Figures like [Olivier Besancenot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivier_Besancenot) and [Philippe Poutou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippe_Poutou) became prominent voices for the movement. The 2010s have seen the rise of autonomist activism, including the [ZAD de Notre-Dame-des-Landes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZAD_de_Notre-Dame-des-Landes) and Black Bloc tactics during protests. The far-left also played a role in the [Gilets Jaunes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilets_Jaunes) movement, emphasizing direct democracy and anti-austerity policies.[^cite_note-8]

## Current challenges and prospects

The far-left in France faces challenges such as internal fragmentation, declining membership, and competition from other left-wing movements like [La France Insoumise](52520313.md) (LFI); however, its influence remains significant in social movements, labor strikes, and grassroots activism. As global issues like climate change and inequality persist, the far-left continues to adapt its strategies to address contemporary challenges.[^cite_note-9]
