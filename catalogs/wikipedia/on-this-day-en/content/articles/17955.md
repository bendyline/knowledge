**Leipzig**[^cite_note-9] is the most populous city in the [German state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_of_Germany) of [Saxony](28395.md). The city has a population of 633,592 residents as of 31 December 2025.[^cite_note-Lstat-10] It is the [eighth-largest city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_in_Germany_by_population) in Germany and is part of the [Central German Metropolitan Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_German_Metropolitan_Region). Leipzig is located about 150 km (90 mi) southwest of Berlin, in the southernmost part of the [North German Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_German_Plain) (the [Leipzig Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Bay)), at the confluence of the [White Elster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Elster) and its tributaries [Pleiße](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plei%C3%9Fe) and [Parthe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthe).

Leipzig has been a trade city since at least the time of the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire).[^cite_note-11] [Via Regia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Regia) and the [Via Imperii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Imperii), two important medieval trade routes, intersected here, marking the city's economic importance. The [Leipzig Trade Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Trade_Fair) dates back to 1190. Between 1764 and 1945, the city was a centre of publishing.[^cite_note-idehist.uu.se-12] After the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War), Leipzig remained a major urban centre in [East Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Germany). Events in Leipzig in 1989 played a significant role in precipitating the [fall of Communism in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Communism_in_Europe), mainly through [demonstrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monday_demonstrations_in_East_Germany) starting from [St. Nicholas Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Nicholas_Church,_Leipzig). Since the early 2000s, Leipzig has experienced substantial transformation, marked by urban and economic revitalisation as well as the modernisation of its transport infrastructure.[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-Power-2016-14]

Leipzig is home to one of the [oldest universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_oldest_universities_in_continuous_operation) in Europe ([Leipzig University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_University)). It is the main seat of the [German National Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_National_Library), and the seat of the [German Music Archive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Music_Archive) and the German [Federal Administrative Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Administrative_Court_\(Germany\)). [Leipzig Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Zoological_Garden) is one of the most modern zoos in Europe and as of 2018 ranks first in Germany and second in Europe.[^cite_note-15] Leipzig's late-19th-century [Gründerzeit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gr%C3%BCnderzeit) architecture consists of around 12,500 buildings.[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17] The city's central railway terminus [Leipzig Hauptbahnhof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Hauptbahnhof) is Europe's largest railway station measured by floor area. Since [Leipzig City Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_City_Tunnel) came into operation in 2013, it has formed the centrepiece of the [S-Bahn Mitteldeutschland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-Bahn_Mitteldeutschland) (*[S-Bahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-Bahn) Central Germany*) public transit system, Germany's largest S-Bahn network.[^cite_note-18]

Leipzig has long been a major centre for music, including classical and modern [dark wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_wave). The [Thomanerchor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomanerchor) (English: St. Thomas Choir of Leipzig), a boys' choir, was founded in 1212. The [Leipzig Gewandhaus Orchestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Gewandhaus_Orchestra), established in 1743, is one of the oldest symphony orchestras in the world. Several well-known composers lived and worked in Leipzig, including [Johann Sebastian Bach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Sebastian_Bach), [Felix Mendelssohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Mendelssohn), and [Richard Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Wagner). The [University of Music and Theatre "Felix Mendelssohn Bartholdy"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Music_and_Theatre_Leipzig) was founded in 1843. The [Oper Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Opera), one of the most prominent opera houses in Germany, was founded in 1693. During a stay in [Gohlis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gohlis), which is now part of the city, [Friedrich Schiller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Schiller) wrote his poem "[Ode to Joy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ode_to_Joy)".

## Names

A once common English spelling of the city's name was **_Leipsic_**, among many variants. The [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) name **_Lipsia_** was also used in many languages and in the academic publications of the city's university.[^cite_note-19]

### Etymology

The name *Leipzig* is commonly held to derive from *lipa*, the common [Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_languages) designation for [linden trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tilia), making the city's name etymologically related to [Lipetsk, Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipetsk). Based on medieval attestations like *Lipzk* (c. 1190), the original Slavic name of the city has been reconstructed as *\*Lipьsko*, which is also reflected in similar forms in neighbouring modern Slavic languages (Sorbian/Polish *Lipsk*, Czech *Lipsko*). This has, however, been questioned by more recent [onomastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onomastics) research based on the very oldest forms like *Libzi* (c. 1015).[^cite_note-20]

The archaic [Romanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_language) name of Leipzig, *Lipsca*, is also derived from a Slavic form. [Lipscani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipscani), a historical district in the center of [Bucharest](36877.md), is named after it.[^cite_note-21]

### Epithets

Due to the etymology mentioned above, *Lindenstadt* or *Stadt der Linden* (City of Linden Trees) are common poetic epithets for the city.[^cite_note-22]

Another, somewhat old-fashioned epithet is *Pleiß-Athen* ([Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Athens) on the [Pleiße River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plei%C3%9Fe)), hinting at Leipzig's long academic and literary tradition, as the seat of one of the oldest German universities and a centre of the book trade.[^cite_note-23]

It is also referred to as "Little Paris" (*Klein-Paris*) after a line from [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_von_Goethe)'s *[Faust I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faust,_Part_One)*, which is partly set in the famous Leipzig restaurant *[Auerbachs Keller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auerbachs_Keller)*.

In 1937 the [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazis) government awarded the city the epithet *_Reichsmessestadt Leipzig_* (Reich Trade Fair City Leipzig).[^cite_note-Rolf_Jehke-24]

In 1989 Leipzig was dubbed a [Hero City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hero_City_\(Soviet_Union\)) (*Heldenstadt*), a title that the Soviet Union awarded to some of its cities for their key role in World War II. In Leipzig's case, though, this was an informal allusion to its role in the fall of the East German regime (through the [Monday demonstrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monday_demonstrations_in_East_Germany)).[^cite_note-25]

In 2013 *Die Zeit* nicknamed the city *Hypezig,* or the "Boomtown of eastern Germany", due to its vibrancy, creative scene and [startups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Startup_company).[^cite_note-26]

### Location

Leipzig is located in the [Leipzig Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Bay), the southernmost part of the [North German Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_German_Plain), which is the part of the [North European Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_European_Plain) in Germany. The city sits on the [White Elster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Elster), a river that rises in the [Czech Republic](5321.md) and flows into the [Saale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saale) south of Halle. The [Pleiße](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plei%C3%9Fe) and the [Parthe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthe) join the White Elster in Leipzig, and the large inland delta-like landscape the three rivers form is called *Leipziger Gewässerknoten*. The site is characterized by swampy areas such as the [Leipzig Riparian Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Riverside_Forest) (*Leipziger Auenwald*), though there are also some limestone areas to the north of the city. The landscape is mostly flat, though there is also some evidence of [moraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moraine) and [drumlins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drumlin).

Although there are some forest parks within the city limits, the area surrounding Leipzig is relatively unforested. During the 20th century, there were several open-pit mines in the region, many of which have been converted to lakes.[^cite_note-27]

Leipzig is also situated at the intersection of the [ancient roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_road) known as the [Via Regia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Regia) (King's highway), which traversed Germany in an east–west direction, and the [Via Imperii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Imperii) (Imperial highway), a north–south road.

Leipzig was a walled city in the Middle Ages and [the current "ring" road around the historic centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_City_Ring_Road_\(Leipzig\)) of the city follows the line of the old city walls.

### Climate

Like many cities in Eastern Germany, Leipzig has an [oceanic climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_climate) ([Köppen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification): *Cfb*), with significant [continental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_climate) influences due to its inland location. Winters are cold, with an average temperature of around 1 °C (34 °F). Summers are generally warm, averaging at 19 °C (66 °F) with daytime temperatures of 24 °C (75 °F). Precipitation in winter is about half that of the summer. The amount of sunshine differs significantly between winter and summer, with an average of around 51 hours of sunshine in December (1.7 hours per day) compared with 229 hours of sunshine in July (7.4 hours per day).[^cite_note-31]

Leipzig in the 17th century

Leipzig was first documented in 1015 in the chronicles of Bishop [Thietmar of Merseburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thietmar_of_Merseburg) as *_urbs Libzi_* (*Chronicon*, VII, 25) and endowed with city and market privileges in 1165 by [Otto the Rich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_II,_Margrave_of_Meissen). [Leipzig Trade Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Trade_Fair), started in the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), has become an event of international importance and is the oldest surviving trade fair in the world. This encouraged the growth of the [Leipzig merchant bourgeoisie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_merchant_bourgeoisie).

There are records of commercial fishing operations on the river [Pleiße](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plei%C3%9Fe) that, most likely, refer to Leipzig dating back to 1305, when the [Margrave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margrave) Dietrich the Younger granted the fishing rights to the church and convent of St Thomas.[^cite_note-34]

There were a number of [monasteries](45856.md) in and around the city, including a [Franciscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franciscans) monastery after which the [Barfußgäßchen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barfu%C3%9Fg%C3%A4%C3%9Fchen) (Barefoot Alley) is named and a monastery of Irish monks (*Jacobskirche*, destroyed in 1544) near the present day *Ranstädter Steinweg* (the old *_[Via Regia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Regia)_*).

The [University of Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Leipzig) was founded in 1409 and Leipzig developed into an important centre of German law and of the publishing industry in Germany, resulting, in the 19th and 20th centuries, with the [Reichsgericht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichsgericht) (Imperial Court of Justice) and the [German National Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_National_Library) being located here.

During the [Thirty Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty_Years'_War), two battles took place in *[Breitenfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breitenfeld,_Leipzig)*, about 8 km (5 mi) outside Leipzig city walls. The [first Battle of Breitenfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Breitenfeld_\(1631\)) took place in 1631 and [the second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Breitenfeld_\(1642\)) in 1642. Both battles resulted in victories for the Swedish-led side.

On 24 December 1701, when [Franz Conrad Romanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Conrad_Romanus) was mayor, an oil-fueled [street lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_light) system was introduced. The city employed light guards who had to follow a specific schedule to ensure the punctual lighting of the 700 lanterns.

### 19th century



[Battle of Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Leipzig), 1813

The Leipzig region was the arena of the 1813 [Battle of Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Leipzig) between [Napoleonic France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_France) and an allied coalition of [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Prussia), [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire), Austria, and Sweden. It was the largest battle in Europe before the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War) and the coalition victory ended [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon)'s presence in Germany and would ultimately lead to his first exile on [Elba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elba). The [Monument to the Battle of the Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monument_to_the_Battle_of_the_Nations) celebrating the centenary of this event was completed in 1913. In addition to stimulating German nationalism, the war had a major impact in mobilizing a civic spirit in numerous volunteer activities. Many volunteer militias and civic associations were formed, and collaborated with churches and the press to support local and state militias, patriotic wartime mobilization, humanitarian relief and postwar commemorative practices and rituals.[^cite_note-35] While over half of the [Kingdom of Saxony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Saxony) was formally ceded to Prussia, Leipzig remained part of [King Frederick Augustus I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Augustus_I_of_Saxony).

When it was made a terminus of the first German long-distance railway to [Dresden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dresden) (the capital of Saxony) in 1839, Leipzig became a hub of Central European railway traffic, with [Leipzig Hauptbahnhof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Hauptbahnhof) the largest [terminal station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_station) by area in Europe. The railway station has two grand entrance halls, the eastern one for the [Royal Saxon State Railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Saxon_State_Railways) and the western one for the [Prussian state railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_state_railways).

In the 19th century, Leipzig was a centre of the German and Saxon liberal movements.[^cite_note-36] The first German [labor party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Labour_Parties), the [General German Workers' Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_German_Workers'_Association) (*Allgemeiner Deutscher Arbeiterverein*, ADAV) was founded in Leipzig on 23 May 1863 by [Ferdinand Lassalle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Lassalle); about 600 workers from across Germany travelled to the foundation on the new railway. Leipzig expanded rapidly to more than 700,000 inhabitants. Huge *[Gründerzeit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gr%C3%BCnderzeit)* areas were built, which mostly survived both war and post-war demolition.



Augustusplatz with [Leipzig Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Opera) House, c. 1900

### 20th century

See also: [History of Leipzig from 1933 to 1939](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Leipzig_from_1933_to_1939), [Bombing of Leipzig in World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombing_of_Leipzig_in_World_War_II), and [University of Music and Theatre Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Music_and_Theatre_Leipzig)



[New Town Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Town_Hall_\(Leipzig\)) of Leipzig, built in 1905

With the opening of a fifth production hall in 1907, the [Leipziger Baumwollspinnerei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipziger_Baumwollspinnerei) became the largest [cotton mill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_mill) company on the continent, housing over 240,000 spindles. Yearly production surpassed 5 million kilograms of yarn.[^cite_note-37]

During [World War I](4764461.md), in 1917, the American Consulate was closed, and its building became a temporary place of stay for Americans and [Allied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_I) refugees from [Serbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Serbia), [Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Romania), and [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Japan).[^cite_note-usem-38]

During the 1930s and 1940s, music was prominent throughout Leipzig. Many students attended [Felix Mendelssohn Bartholdy College of Music and Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Music_and_Theatre_Leipzig) (then named Landeskonservatorium.) However, in 1944, it was closed due to [World War II](32927.md). It re-opened soon after the war ended in 1945.

On 22 May 1930, [Carl Friedrich Goerdeler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Goerdeler) was elected mayor of Leipzig. He later became an opponent of the [Nazi regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Regime).[^cite_note-39] He resigned in 1937 when, in his absence, his Nazi deputy ordered the destruction of the city's statue of [Felix Mendelssohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Mendelssohn). On [Kristallnacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristallnacht) in 1938, the 1855 [Moorish Revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moorish_Revival) [Leipzig synagogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_synagogue), one of the city's most architecturally significant buildings, was deliberately destroyed. Goerdeler was later executed by the Nazis on 2 February 1945.

Several thousand forced labourers were stationed in Leipzig during the Second World War.

Beginning in 1933, many Jewish citizens of Leipzig were members of the [Gemeinde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemeinde_\(Germany\)), a large Jewish religious community spread throughout Germany, Austria, and Switzerland. In October 1935, the Gemeinde helped found the Lehrhaus (English: a house of study) in Leipzig to provide different forms of studies to Jewish students who were prohibited from attending any institutions in Germany. Jewish studies were emphasized and much of the Jewish community of Leipzig became involved.[^cite_note-Willingham-40]

Like all other cities claimed by the Nazis, Leipzig was subject to [aryanisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryanization_\(Nazism\)). Beginning in 1933 and increasing in 1939, Jewish business owners were forced to give up their possessions and stores. This eventually intensified to the point where Nazi officials were strong enough to evict the Jews from their own homes. They also had the power to force many of the Jews living in the city to sell their houses. Many people who sold their homes emigrated elsewhere, outside of Leipzig. Others moved to Judenhäuser, which were smaller houses that acted as ghettos, housing large groups of people.[^cite_note-Willingham-40]

The Jews of Leipzig were greatly affected by the [Nuremberg Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg_Laws). However, due to the [Leipzig Trade Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Trade_Fair) and the international attention it garnered, Leipzig was especially cautious about its public image. Despite this, the Leipzig authorities were not afraid to strictly apply and enforce anti-semitic measures.[^cite_note-Willingham-40]

On 20 December 1937, after the Nazis took control of the city, they renamed it Reichsmessestadt Leipzig, meaning the "Imperial Trade Fair City Leipzig".[^cite_note-Rolf_Jehke-24] In early 1938, Leipzig saw an increase in [Zionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zionism) through Jewish citizens. Many of these Zionists attempted to flee before deportations began.[^cite_note-Willingham-40] On 28 October 1938, [Heinrich Himmler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Himmler) ordered the deportation of Polish Jews from Leipzig to Poland.[^cite_note-Willingham-40][^cite_note-41] The Polish Consulate sheltered 1,300 Polish Jews, preventing their deportation.[^cite_note-dw-42]

On 9 November 1938, as part of [Kristallnacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristallnacht), in [Gottschedstrasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottschedstrasse_\(Leipzig\)), synagogues and businesses were set on fire.[^cite_note-Willingham-40] Only a couple of days later, on 11 November 1938, many Jews in the Leipzig area were deported to the [Buchenwald Concentration Camp.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buchenwald_concentration_camp)[^cite_note-43] As [World War II](32927.md) came to an end, much of Leipzig was destroyed. Following the war, the [Communist Party of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_Germany) provided aid for the reconstruction of the city.[^cite_note-44]

In 1933, a census recorded that over 11,000 Jews were living in Leipzig. In the 1939 census, the number had fallen to roughly 4,500, and by January 1942 only 2,000 remained. In that month, these 2,000 Jews began to be deported.[^cite_note-Willingham-40] On 13 July 1942, 170 Jews were deported from Leipzig to [Auschwitz concentration camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auschwitz_concentration_camp). On 19 September 1942, 440 Jews were deported from Leipzig to [Theresienstadt concentration camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theresienstadt_concentration_camp). On 18 June 1943, the remaining 18 Jews still in Leipzig were deported from Leipzig to Auschwitz. According to records of the two waves of deportations to Auschwitz there were no survivors. According to records of the Theresienstadt deportation, only 53 Jews survived.[^cite_note-Willingham-40][^cite_note-45]



Memorial at the site of the Abtnaundorf massacre

During the [German invasion of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_invasion_of_Poland) at the start of [World War II](32927.md), in September 1939, the [Gestapo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestapo) carried out arrests of prominent local [Poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_people),[^cite_note-46] and seized the Polish Consulate and its library.[^cite_note-dw-42] In 1941, the American Consulate was also closed by order of the German authorities.[^cite_note-usem-38] During the war, Leipzig was the location of five [subcamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_subcamps_of_Buchenwald) of the [Buchenwald concentration camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buchenwald_concentration_camp), in which over 8,000 men, women, and children were imprisoned, mostly Polish, Jewish, Soviet, and French, but also Italian, Czech, and Belgian.[^cite_note-ab-47] In April 1945, most surviving prisoners were sent on [death marches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_marches_during_the_Holocaust) to various destinations in Saxony and [German-occupied Czechoslovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Czechoslovakia_\(1938%E2%80%931945\)), whereas prisoners of the Leipzig-Thekla subcamp who were unable to march were either burned alive, shot, or beaten to death by the Gestapo, [SS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS), *[Volkssturm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volkssturm)*, and German civilians in the Abtnaundorf massacre.[^cite_note-Thekla-48][^cite_note-lb-49] Some were rescued by Polish [forced laborers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forced_labour_under_German_rule_during_World_War_II) of another camp; at least 67 people survived.[^cite_note-Thekla-48][^cite_note-lb-49] 84 victims were buried on 27 April 1945, however, the total number of victims remains unknown.[^cite_note-Thekla-48][^cite_note-lb-49]



Leipzig after bombing in the Second World War

During World War II, Leipzig was [repeatedly struck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombing_of_Leipzig_in_World_War_II) by [Allied bombing raids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_bombing_during_World_War_II), beginning in 1943 and lasting until 1945. The first raid occurred on the morning of 4 December 1943, when 442 bombers of the [Royal Air Force](25679.md) (RAF) dropped a total amount of almost 1,400 tons of explosives and incendiaries on the city, destroying large parts of the city centre.[^cite_note-50] This bombing was the largest up to that time. Due to the close proximity of many of the buildings hit, a firestorm occurred. This prompted firefighters to rush to the city; however, they were unable to control the fires. Unlike the [firebombing of the neighbouring city of Dresden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombing_of_Dresden_in_World_War_II), this was a largely conventional bombing with high explosives rather than incendiaries. The resultant pattern of loss was a patchwork, rather than wholesale loss of its centre, but was nevertheless extensive.

The Allied ground advance into Germany reached Leipzig in late April 1945. The U.S. [2nd Infantry Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2nd_Infantry_Division_\(United_States\)) and U.S. [69th Infantry Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/69th_Infantry_Division_\(United_States\)) fought their way into the city on 18 April and completed its capture after fierce urban action, in which fighting was often house-to-house and block-to-block, on 19 April 1945.[^cite_note-51] In April 1945, the Mayor of Leipzig, SS-*[Gruppenführer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gruppenf%C3%BChrer)* [Alfred Freyberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Freyberg), his wife and daughter, together with Deputy Mayor and City Treasurer Ernest Kurt Lisso, his wife, daughter, and *[Volkssturm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volkssturm)* Major and former Mayor [Walter Dönicke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_D%C3%B6nicke), all committed suicide in Leipzig City Hall.

The United States turned the city over to the [Red Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Army) as it pulled back from the [line of contact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_of_contact) with Soviet forces in July 1945 to the designated [occupation zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_occupation_zone_in_Germany) boundaries. Leipzig became one of the major cities of the German Democratic Republic ([East Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Germany)).

Following the [end of World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_of_World_War_II_in_Europe) in 1945, Leipzig saw a slow return of Jews to the city.[^cite_note-Willingham-40][^cite_note-52] They were joined by large numbers of [German refugees who had been expelled from Central and Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_and_expulsion_of_Germans_\(1944%E2%80%931950\)).[^cite_note-53]



Monument to the Battle of the Nations

In the mid-20th century, the city's trade fair assumed renewed importance as a point of contact with the [Comecon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comecon) Eastern Europe economic bloc, of which East Germany was a member. At this time, trade fairs were held at a site in the south of the city, near the [Monument to the Battle of the Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monument_to_the_Battle_of_the_Nations).

The planned economy of the German Democratic Republic, however, was not kind to Leipzig. Before the Second World War, Leipzig had developed a mixture of industry, creative business (notably publishing), and services (including legal services). During the period of the German Democratic Republic, services became the concern of the state, concentrated in [East Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Berlin); capitalist business moved to [West Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Germany); and Leipzig was left only with heavy industry. To make matters worse, this industry was extremely polluting, making Leipzig an even less attractive city to live in.[^cite_note-TheProtoCity.com-2014-54] Between 1950 and the end of the German Democratic Republic, the population of Leipzig fell from 600,000 to 500,000.[^cite_note-Power-2016-14]

In October 1989, after prayers for peace at [St. Nicholas Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Nicholas_Church,_Leipzig), established in 1983 as part of the peace movement, the [Monday demonstrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monday_demonstrations_in_East_Germany) started as the most prominent mass protest against the [East German government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_East_Germany).[^cite_note-55][^cite_note-56] The [reunification of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reunification_of_Germany), however, was at first not good for Leipzig. The centrally planned heavy industry that had become the city's specialty was, in terms of the advanced economy of reunited Germany, almost completely unviable, and closed. Within only six years, 90% of jobs in industry had vanished.[^cite_note-Power-2016-14] As unemployment rocketed, the population fell dramatically; some 100,000 people left Leipzig in the ten years after reunification, and vacant and derelict housing became an urgent problem.[^cite_note-Power-2016-14]

Starting in 2000, an ambitious urban-renewal plan first stopped Leipzig's population decline and then reversed it. The plan focused on saving and improving the city's attractive historic downtown area and particularly its early 20th-century building stock, and attracting new industries, partly through infrastructure improvement. However, the renewal has led to [gentrification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentrification) of parts of the city and has not arrested the decline of Leipzig-East.[^cite_note-TheProtoCity.com-2014-54][^cite_note-Power-2016-14]

### 21st century



[Federal Administrative Court of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Administrative_Court_of_Germany)



The 153 m (502 ft) tall [City-Hochhaus Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City-Hochhaus_Leipzig) and the [Augusteum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augusteum_\(Leipzig\)) of the [University of Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Leipzig)

Leipzig is an important economic centre in Germany. Since the 2010s, the city has been celebrated by the media as a hip urban centre with a very high quality of living.[^cite_note-Michael_Fabricius-2015-57][^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59] It is often called "The new Berlin".[^cite_note-60] Leipzig is also Germany's fastest growing city.[^cite_note-61] Leipzig was the German candidate for the [2012 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics), but was unsuccessful. After ten years of construction, the [Leipzig City Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_City_Tunnel) opened on 14 December 2013. Leipzig forms the centrepiece of the [S-Bahn Mitteldeutschland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-Bahn_Mitteldeutschland) public transit system, which operates in the four German states of [Saxony](28395.md), Saxony-Anhalt, [Thuringia](31130.md), and [Brandenburg](3765.md).

Leipzig has a population of about 620,000.[^cite_note-63] In 1930, the population reached its historical peak of over 700,000. It decreased steadily from 1950 to about 530,000 in 1989. In the 1990s, the population decreased rather rapidly to 437,000 in 1998. This reduction was mostly due to outward migration and suburbanisation. After almost doubling the city area by incorporation of surrounding towns in 1999, the number stabilised and started to rise again, with an increase of 1,000 in 2000.[^cite_note-64] As of 2015, Leipzig is the fastest-growing city in Germany with over 500,000 inhabitants.[^cite_note-65] The growth of the past 10–15 years has mostly been due to inward migration. In recent years, inward migration accelerated, reaching an increase of 12,917 in 2014.[^cite_note-66]

In the years following German reunification, many people of working age took the opportunity to move to the states of the former West Germany to seek employment opportunities. This was a contributory factor to falling birth rates. Births dropped from 7,000 in 1988 to less than 3,000 in 1994.[^cite_note-67] However, the number of children born in Leipzig has risen since the late 1990s. In 2011, it reached 5,490 births resulting in a [RNI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_of_natural_increase) of −17.7 (−393.7 in 1995).[^cite_note-68]

The unemployment rate decreased from 18.2% in 2003 to 9.8% in 2014 and 7.6% in June 2017.[^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71]

The percentage of the population from an immigrant background is low compared with other German cities. As of 2012, only 5.6% of the population were foreigners, compared to the German national average of 7.7%.[^cite_note-72]

The number of people with an immigrant background (immigrants and their children) grew from 49,323 in 2012 to 77,559 in 2016, making them 13.3% of the city's population (Leipzig's population 579,530 in 2016).[^cite_note-73]

## Culture, sights, and cityscape

In the 2010s, Leipzig was often referred to as *Hypezig*, as overblown comparisons were made to 1990s and early 2000s Berlin. The affordability, diversity, and openness of the city have attracted many young people from across Europe, leading to a trendsetting alternative atmosphere, resulting in an innovative music, dance, and art scene.[^cite_note-Warum_Leipzig_der_beste_Ort_für_kreative_Experimente_ist-2019-75]

### Architecture

See also: [Architecture of Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_Leipzig) and [List of arcade galleries in Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_arcade_galleries_in_Leipzig)



*Palais Roßbach*, one of the many *[Gründerzeit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gr%C3%BCnderzeit)* buildings in Leipzig



[Mädler Passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A4dlerpassage), one of 24 covered passages in Leipzig city centre

The historic central area of Leipzig features a [Renaissance-style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_architecture) ensemble of buildings from the sixteenth century, including the old city hall in the marketplace. There are also several [baroque period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroque_architecture) trading houses and former residences of rich merchants. As Leipzig grew considerably during the economic boom of the late-nineteenth century, the town has many buildings in the [historicist style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historicism_\(art\)) representative of the *[Gründerzeit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gr%C3%BCnderzeit)* era. Approximately 35% of Leipzig's flats are in buildings of this type. The [new city hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Town_Hall_\(Leipzig\)), completed in 1905, is built in the same style.

Some 90,000 apartments in Leipzig were built in *[Plattenbau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plattenbau)* buildings during Communist rule in East Germany.[^cite_note-76] Although some of these have been demolished and fewer people live in this type of accommodation in recent years, a significant number still live in Plattenbau apartments. Grünau, for example, had about 43,600 people living in this sort of accommodation in 2016.[^cite_note-77]

The [St. Paul's Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paulinerkirche,_Leipzig) was destroyed by the Communist government in 1968 to make room for a new main building for the university. After some debate, the city decided to establish a new, mainly secular building at the same location, called [Paulinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paulinum_\(University_of_Leipzig\)), which was completed in 2012. Its architecture alludes to the look of the former church and it includes space for religious use by the faculty of theology, including the original altar from the old church and two newly built organs.

Many commercial buildings were built in the 1990s as a result of tax breaks after German reunification.

### Museums and the arts

One of the highlights of the city's contemporary arts was the [Neo Rauch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo_Rauch) retrospective opening in April 2010 at the [Leipzig Museum of Fine Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_der_bildenden_K%C3%BCnste). This is a show devoted to the father of the [New Leipzig School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Leipzig_School)[^cite_note-78] of artists. According to *The New York Times*,[^cite_note-The_New_York_Times-2010-79] this scene "has been the toast of the contemporary art world" for the past decade. In addition, there are eleven galleries in the so-called [Spinnerei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipziger_Baumwollspinnerei).[^cite_note-80]

The [Grassi Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grassi_Museum) complex contains three more of Leipzig's major collections:[^cite_note-81] the [Ethnography Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Museum_of_Ethnography), [Applied Arts Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Museum_of_Applied_Arts), and [Musical Instrument Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Musical_Instruments_of_the_University_of_Leipzig) (the last of which is run by the University of Leipzig). The university also runs the [Museum of Antiquities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Antiquities_of_the_University_of_Leipzig).[^cite_note-82]

Founded in March 2015, the [G2 Kunsthalle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G2_Kunsthalle?action=edit\&redlink=1) houses the Hildebrand Collection.[^cite_note-83] This private collection focuses on the so-called [New Leipzig School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Leipzig_School). Leipzig's first private museum dedicated to contemporary art in Leipzig after the turn of the millennium is located in the city centre close to the famous [St. Thomas Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Thomas_Church,_Leipzig) on the third floor of the former GDR processing centre.[^cite_note-84] Also dedicated to the contemporary art is the [Galerie für Zeitgenössische Kunst Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galerie_f%C3%BCr_Zeitgen%C3%B6ssische_Kunst_Leipzig).[^cite_note-85]

Other museums in Leipzig include the following:

- The [German Museum of Books and Writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Museum_of_Books_and_Writing) is the world's oldest museum of its kind, founded in 1884.

- The [Bach Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bach_Museum_Leipzig) at the [St. Thomas Church Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Thomas_Church_Square).

- The Egyptian Museum of the University of Leipzig located in the [Kroch High-rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kroch_High-rise) comprises a collection of about 7,000 artefacts from several millennia.

- The [Schillerhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Schiller) is the house where Schiller lived in summer 1785.

- The [Zeitgeschichtliches Forum Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeitgeschichtliches_Forum_Leipzig) (Forum of Contemporary History) shows the history of the German division and the everyday life in the socialist German Democratic Republic.

- [Naturkundemuseum Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturkundemuseum_Leipzig) is the city's natural history museum.

- The [Leipzig Panometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Panometer) is a visual panorama displayed inside a former gasometer, accompanied by a thematic exhibition.

- The "Museum in der Runden Ecke" is the best-known museum in the city. It deals with the operation of the [Stasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stasi) State Security of the former East Germany.

- [Johann Sebastian Bach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Sebastian_Bach) lived from 1723 until his death in Leipzig. The [Bach Archive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bach_Archive) is an institution for the documentation and research of his life and work.

- [Mendelssohn House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendelssohn_House,_Leipzig), home of Felix Mendelssohn from 1845 until his death in 1847.

- [Schumann House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schumann_House,_Leipzig), home of Robert and Clara Schumann from 1840 to 1844.

- The [Museum of Antiquities of Leipzig University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Antiquities_of_Leipzig_University) and a small museum regarding he youth of Richard Wagner in the [Old St Nicholas School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_St_Nicholas_School,_Leipzig).

- The [Saxon Psychiatric Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxon_Psychiatric_Museum) is a small museum dealing with the history of lunatic asylums and psychiatry.

- The [Unikatum Children's Museum, Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unikatum_Children's_Museum,_Leipzig) opened in 2010 in [Plagwitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plagwitz).

### Main sights

- [Leipzig Zoological Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Zoological_Garden) is one of the most modern zoos in Europe, with approximately 850 different animal species. It houses the world's largest zoological facilities for [primates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primate) (Pongoland). Gondwanaland is the world's largest indoor rainforest hall.

- [Monument to the Battle of the Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monument_to_the_Battle_of_the_Nations) (*Völkerschlachtdenkmal*) ([Battle of the Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Leipzig) Monument): one of the largest monuments in Europe, built to commemorate the victorious battle against Napoleonic troops.

- [Bundesverwaltungsgericht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Administrative_Court_of_Germany): Germany's federal administrative court was the site of the [Reichsgericht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichsgericht), the highest state court between 1888 and 1945.

It is also Germany's tallest town hall.

- [Old Town Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Town_Hall_\(Leipzig\)) on the [Market square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markt_\(Leipzig\)): the old city hall was built in 1556 and houses a museum of the city's history.

- [City-Hochhaus Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City-Hochhaus_Leipzig): built in 1972, the city's tallest habitable building is one of the top 25 [tallest buildings in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_Germany).

- The [Augusteum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augusteum_\(Leipzig\)) and [Paulinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paulinum_\(University_of_Leipzig\)) at [Augustusplatz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustusplatz) form the new main campus of the [University of Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Leipzig).

### Parks and lakes

Leipzig is well known for its large parks. The *Leipziger Auwald* ([riparian forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riparian_forest)) lies mostly within the city limits. [Neuseenland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuseenland) is an area south of Leipzig where old open-cast mines are being converted into a huge lake district. It is planned to be finished in 2060.

- [Leipzig Botanical Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Botanical_Garden) is the oldest of its kind in Germany. It contains a total of some 7,000 plant species, of which nearly 3,000 species comprise ten special collections.

- [Johannapark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannapark) and [Clara-Zetkin-Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clara-Zetkin-Park_\(Leipzig\)) are the most prominent parks in the Leipzig city centre ([Leipzig-Mitte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig-Mitte)).

- *[Leipziger Auwald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Riverside_Forest)* covers a total area of approx. 2,500 hectares. The Rosental is a park in the north of the forest and borders Leipzig Zoo.

- The [Leipzig Wildlife Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Wildlife_Park) in [Connewitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connewitz), showing 25 species.

- The [Lene Voigt Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lene_Voigt_Park) on the refurbished site of the former train station [Leipzig Eilenburger Bahnhof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Eilenburger_Bahnhof) opened in 2004.

### Music

#### Baroque to Modern

[Johann Sebastian Bach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Sebastian_Bach) spent the longest phase of his career in Leipzig, from 1723 until his death in 1750, conducting the [Thomanerchor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomanerchor) (St. Thomas Church Choir), at the [St. Thomas Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Thomas_Church,_Leipzig), the [St. Nicholas Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Nicholas_Church,_Leipzig), and the [Paulinerkirche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paulinerkirche,_Leipzig), the university church of Leipzig (destroyed in 1968). The composer [Richard Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Wagner) was born in Leipzig in 1813, in the [Brühl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Br%C3%BChl_\(Leipzig\)). [Robert Schumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Schumann) was also active in Leipzig music, having been invited by [Felix Mendelssohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Mendelssohn) when the latter established Germany's first musical [conservatoire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservatoire) in the city in 1843. [Gustav Mahler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Mahler) was second conductor (working under [Artur Nikisch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artur_Nikisch)) at the [Leipzig Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oper_Leipzig) from June 1886 until May 1888, and achieved his first significant recognition while there by completing and publishing [Carl Maria von Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Maria_von_Weber)'s opera [Die Drei Pintos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Drei_Pintos). Mahler also completed his own [1st Symphony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._1_\(Mahler\)) while living in Leipzig.

Today the conservatory is the [University of Music and Theatre Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Music_and_Theatre_Leipzig).[^cite_note-89] A broad range of subjects are taught, including artistic and teacher training in all orchestral instruments, voice, interpretation, coaching, piano [chamber music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamber_music), orchestral conducting, choir conducting, and [musical composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_composition) in various musical styles. The drama departments teach acting and [scriptwriting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramaturgy).

The [Bach-Archiv Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bach-Archiv_Leipzig), an institution for the documentation and research of the life and work of Bach (and also of the [Bach family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bach_family)), was founded in Leipzig in 1950 by [Werner Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Neumann_\(musicologist\)). The Bach-Archiv organizes the prestigious [International Johann Sebastian Bach Competition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Johann_Sebastian_Bach_Competition), initiated in 1950 as part of a music festival marking the bicentennial of Bach's death. The competition is now held every two years in three changing categories. The Bach-Archiv also organizes performances, especially the international festival [Bachfest Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachfest_Leipzig) and runs the [Leipzig Bach Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Bach_Museum).

The city's musical tradition is also reflected in the worldwide fame of the [Leipzig Gewandhaus Orchestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gewandhausorchester), under its chief conductor [Andris Nelsons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andris_Nelsons), and the Thomanerchor.

The [MDR Leipzig Radio Symphony Orchestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MDR_Leipzig_Radio_Symphony_Orchestra) is Leipzig's second largest symphony orchestra. Its current chief conductor is [Kristjan Järvi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristjan_J%C3%A4rvi). Both the Gewandhausorchester and the MDR Leipzig Radio Symphony Orchestra make use of in the [Gewandhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gewandhaus) concert hall.

For over sixty years Leipzig has been offering a "school concert"[^cite_note-90] programme for children in Germany, with over 140 concerts every year in venues such as the Gewandhaus and over 40,000 children attending.

#### Contemporary

Leipzig is known for its independent music scene and subcultural events. Leipzig has for thirty years been home to the [Wave-Gotik-Treffen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave-Gotik-Treffen) (WGT), which is currently the world's largest Gothic festival, where thousands of fans of [goth music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goth_music) gather in the early summer. The first Wave Gotik Treffen was held at the Eiskeller club, today known as [Conne Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conne_Island), in the [Connewitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connewitz) district. [Mayhem's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayhem_\(band\)) notorious album [Live in Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_in_Leipzig) was also recorded at the Eiskeller club.

Leipzig Pop Up was an annual music trade fair for the independent music scene as well as a music festival taking place on [Pentecost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentecost) weekend.[^cite_note-91] Its most famous indie-labels are Moon Harbour Recordings (House) and Kann Records (House/Techno/Psychedelic). Several venues offer live music frequently, including the [Moritzbastei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moritzbastei),[^cite_note-92] Tonelli's, and Noch Besser Leben.

*[Die Prinzen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Prinzen)* ("The Princes") is a German band founded in Leipzig. With almost six million records sold, they are one of the most successful German bands.

The cover photo for the [Beirut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beirut_\(band\)) band's 2005 album *[Gulag Orkestar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulag_Orkestar)*, according to the sleeve notes, was stolen from a Leipzig library by Zach Condon.

The city of Leipzig is also the birthplace of [Till Lindemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Till_Lindemann), best known as the lead vocalist of [Rammstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rammstein) and [Bill Kaulitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Kaulitz) and Tom Kaulitz, best known as the main members of [Tokio Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokio_Hotel).

### Food and drink

- An all-season local dish is [Leipziger Allerlei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipziger_Allerlei), a stew consisting of seasonal vegetables and [crayfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crayfish).

- [Gose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gose) is a locally brewed top-fermenting [sour beer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sour_beer) that originated in the [Goslar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goslar) region and became popular in 18th-century Leipzig.

### Football



The [Red Bull Arena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Bull_Arena_\(Leipzig\)) from above. Home of RB Leipzig.



[Bruno-Plache-Stadion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruno-Plache-Stadion) is the home stadion of [1. FC Lokomotive Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1._FC_Lokomotive_Leipzig).

The [German Football Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Football_Association) (DFB) was founded in Leipzig in 1900. The city was the venue for the [2006 FIFA World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_FIFA_World_Cup) draw, and hosted four first-round matches and one match in the round of 16 in the [central stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zentralstadion_\(Leipzig\)).

[VfB Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VfB_Leipzig) won the first national Association football championship in 1903. The club was dissolved in 1946 and the remains reformed as SG Probstheida. The club was eventually reorganized as football club 1. FC Lokomotive Leipzig in 1966. 1. FC Lokomotive Leipzig has had a glorious past in international competition as well, having been champions of the [1965–66 Intertoto Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1965%E2%80%9366_Intertoto_Cup), semi-finalists in the [1973–74 UEFA Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973%E2%80%9374_UEFA_Cup), and runners-up in the [1986–87 European Cup Winners' Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1986%E2%80%9387_European_Cup_Winners'_Cup).

The [current Chemie Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BSG_Chemie_Leipzig_\(1997\)) from the [Leutzsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leutzsch) district, although legally founded in 1997 and started playing in 2008, can trace its roots to the 19th Century. The [Chemie Leipzig founded in 1950](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BSG_Chemie_Leipzig_\(1950\)) dates back to the prewar identity rooted in the establishment of [Britannia Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britannia_Leipzig?action=edit\&redlink=1) in 1899 and its successor [TuRa Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TuRa_Leipzig). During the socialist era, the traditions of the club were continued in the [East German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_German) teams through a complicated history of mergers and name changes before the emergence of [FC Sachsen Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FC_Sachsen_Leipzig) in 1990, following [German reunification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_reunification), which continued the clubs traditions.[^cite_note-100]

[Red Bull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Bull) took over a local 5th division football club [SSV Markranstädt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SSV_Markranst%C3%A4dt) in May 2009, having previously been denied the right to buy into [FC Sachsen Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FC_Sachsen_Leipzig) in 2006. The club was renamed [RB Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RB_Leipzig) and came up through the ranks of German football, winning promotion to the [Bundesliga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundesliga), the highest division of German football in 2016.[^cite_note-101] The club finished runners-up in its first-ever Bundesliga season and made its debut in the [UEFA Champions League](44220.md) in 2017 and the Semi-Final in 2020.

RB Leipzig won the [DFB-Pokal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DFB-Pokal) football cup twice, in 2022 and 2023.

### University

[Leipzig University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_University), founded 1409, is one of Europe's oldest universities. [Karl Bücher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_B%C3%BCcher), a German economist, founded the *Institut für Zeitungswissenschaften* (Institute for Newspaper Science) at the University of Leipzig in 1916. It was the first institute of its kind to be established in Europe, and it marks the commencement of academic study of media communication in Germany.[^cite_note-111]

[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Wilhelm_Leibniz), a philosopher and mathematician, was born in Leipzig in 1646, and attended the university from 1661 to 1666. Nobel Prize laureate [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg) worked at the university as a physics professor (from 1927 to 1942), as did Nobel Prize laureates [Gustav Ludwig Hertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Ludwig_Hertz) (physics), [Wilhelm Ostwald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Ostwald) (chemistry), and [Theodor Mommsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Mommsen) ([Nobel Prize in Literature](23385442.md)). The 2022 [Nobel Prize in Physiology and Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_and_Medicine) went to [Svante Pääbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Svante_P%C3%A4%C3%A4bo), an honorary professor at the university. Other former university staff include mineralogist [Georg Agricola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Agricola), writer [Gotthold Ephraim Lessing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gotthold_Ephraim_Lessing), philosopher [Ernst Bloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Bloch), founder of [psychophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychophysics) [Gustav Theodor Fechner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Theodor_Fechner), and founder of modern psychology, [Wilhelm Wundt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Wundt). The university's notable former students include writers [Johann Wolfgang Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_Goethe) and [Erich Kästner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_K%C3%A4stner), philosopher [Friedrich Nietzsche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Nietzsche), political activist [Karl Liebknecht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Liebknecht), and composer [Richard Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Wagner). [Angela Merkel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Merkel), former German chancellor, studied physics at Leipzig University.[^cite_note-112] The university has about 30,000 students.

A part of Leipzig University is the [German Institute for Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Institute_for_Literature) which was founded in 1955 under the name "Johannes R. Becher-Institut". Many noted writers have graduated from this school, including [Heinz Czechowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinz_Czechowski), [Kurt Drawert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Drawert?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Adolf Endler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Endler), [Ralph Giordano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Giordano_\(writer\)), [Kerstin Hensel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerstin_Hensel), [Sarah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Kirsch_\(poet\)) and [Rainer Kirsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainer_Kirsch), [Angela Krauß](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Krau%C3%9F), [Erich Loest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Loest), and [Fred Wander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Wander). After its closure in 1990 the institute was refounded in 1995 with new teachers.

### Visual arts and theatre

The Academy of Visual Arts (*[Hochschule für Grafik und Buchkunst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hochschule_f%C3%BCr_Grafik_und_Buchkunst)*) was established in 1764. Its 600 students (as of 2018) are enrolled in courses in painting and graphics, book design/graphic design, photography and media art. The school also houses an Institute for Theory.

The [University of Music and Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Music_and_Theatre_Leipzig) offers a broad range of subjects ranging from training in orchestral instruments, voice, interpretation, coaching, piano chamber music, orchestral conducting, choir conducting, and musical composition to acting and scriptwriting.



Main building of [Leipzig University of Applied Sciences (HTWK)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_University_of_Applied_Sciences)

### University of Applied Science

The [Leipzig University of Applied Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_University_of_Applied_Science) (HTWK)[^cite_note-113] has approximately 6,200 students (as of 2007) and is (as of 2007) the second biggest institution of higher education in Leipzig. It was founded in 1992, merging several older schools. As a university of applied sciences (German: *Fachhochschule*) its status is slightly below that of a university, with more emphasis on the practical parts of education. The HTWK offers many engineering courses, as well as courses in computer science, mathematics, business administration, librarianship, museum studies, and social work. It is mainly located in the south of the city.

### Leipzig Graduate School

The private [Leipzig Graduate School of Management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HHL_Leipzig_Graduate_School_of_Management), (in German *Handelshochschule Leipzig (HHL)*), is the oldest business school in Germany. According to [The Economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Economist), HHL is one of the best schools in the world, ranked at number six overall.[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115]

### Lancaster University Leipzig

Branch campus of [Lancaster University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancaster_University), it is the first public UK university with a campus in Germany. [Lancaster University Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancaster_University_Leipzig) was founded in 2020 and currently has a diverse international student body with more than 45 nationalities.

## Economy

The city is a location for automobile manufacturing by [BMW](3772.md) and [Porsche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porsche) in large plants north of the city. In 2011 and 2012 [DHL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DHL) transferred the bulk of its European air operations from [Brussels Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels_Airport) to [Leipzig/Halle Airport](5454926.md). [Kirow Ardelt AG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirow_Leipzig?action=edit\&redlink=1), the world market leader in [breakdown cranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crane_\(railroad\)), is based in Leipzig. The city also houses the [European Energy Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Energy_Exchange), the leading energy exchange in Central Europe. [VNG – Verbundnetz Gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VNG_%E2%80%93_Verbundnetz_Gas) AG, one of Germany's large natural gas suppliers, is headquartered at Leipzig. In addition, inside its larger metropolitan area, Leipzig has developed an important petrochemical centre.

Some of the largest employers in the area (outside of manufacturing) include software companies such as [Spreadshirt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spreadshirt) and the various schools and universities in and around the Leipzig/[Halle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halle,_Saxony-Anhalt) region. The University of Leipzig attracts millions of [euros](9472.md) of investment yearly and celebrated its 600th birthday in 2009.

Leipzig also benefits from world-leading medical research (Leipzig Heart Centre) and a growing biotechnology industry.[^cite_note-119] [Bio City Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bio_City_Leipzig), established in 2003, is a [biotechnology](4502.md) [business incubator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_incubator).

Many bars, restaurants, and stores in the downtown area are patronized by German and foreign tourists. Leipzig Main Train Station is the location of a shopping mall.[^cite_note-120] Leipzig is one of Germany's most visited cities with over 3 million overnight stays in 2017.[^cite_note-121]

In 2010, Leipzig was included in the top 10 cities to visit by *[The New York Times](30680.md)*,[^cite_note-The_New_York_Times-2010-79] and ranked 39th globally out of 289 cities for innovation in the 4th Innovation Cities Index published by Australian agency 2thinknow.[^cite_note-122] In 2015, Leipzig have among the 30 largest German cities the third best prospects for the future.[^cite_note-123] In recent years Leipzig has often been nicknamed the "Boomtown of eastern Germany" or "Hypezig".[^cite_note-Handelsblatt-2013-124] As of 2013 it had the highest rate of population growth of any German city.[^cite_note-Wiemann-2014-125]

## Transport

Founded at the crossing of [Via Regia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Regia) and [Via Imperii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Imperii), Leipzig has been a major interchange of inter-European traffic and commerce since medieval times. After the [Reunification of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reunification_of_Germany), immense efforts to restore and expand the traffic network have been undertaken and left the city area with an excellent infrastructure.

### Air



Leipzig/Halle Airport, hub of DHL

[Leipzig/Halle Airport](5454926.md) is the international commercial airport of the region. It is located at the Schkeuditzer Kreuz junction northwest of Leipzig, halfway between the two major cities. The easternmost section of the new Erfurt-Leipzig/Halle line under construction gave the airport a long-distance railway station, which was also integrated into the ICE network when the railway line was completed in 2015.

Passenger flights are operated to and from the major German hub airports, European metropolises and holiday destinations, especially to the Mediterranean region and North Africa. The airport is of international importance in the cargo sector. In Germany, it ranks second behind Frankfurt am Main, fifth in Europe and 26th worldwide (as of 2011). [DHL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DHL) uses the airport as its central European hub. It is also the home base of the freight airlines [Aerologic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerologic) and [European Air Transport Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Air_Transport_Leipzig).

The former military airport near [Altenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altenburg), Thuringia, called [Leipzig–Altenburg Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig%E2%80%93Altenburg_Airport), about a half-hour drive from Leipzig, was served by [Ryanair](262878.md) until 2010.

[Berlin Brandenburg Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Brandenburg_Airport) can be reached from Leipzig in one and a half hours by train since December 2025.[^cite_note-148]

### Railways



Leipzig Hauptbahnhof is the main hub of the tram and railway network and the world's largest railway station by floor area.



Inside Leipzig Hauptbahnhof

Opened in 1915, [Leipzig Hauptbahnhof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Hauptbahnhof) (lit. *main station*) is the largest overhead railway station in Europe in terms of its built-up area. At the same time, it is an important supra-regional junction in the [Intercity-Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercity-Express) (ICE) and [Intercity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercity_\(Deutsche_Bahn\)) network of the [Deutsche Bahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Bahn) as well as a connection point for [S-Bahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-Bahn) and regional traffic in the Halle/Leipzig area.

In Leipzig the Intercity Express routes (Hamburg–)Berlin–Leipzig–Nuremberg–Munich and Dresden–Leipzig–Erfurt–Frankfurt am Main–(Wiesbaden/Saarbrücken) intersect. Leipzig is also the starting point for the intercity lines Leipzig-Halle (Saale)–Magdeburg–Hannover–Dortmund–Köln and –Bremen–Oldenburg(–Norddeich Mole). Both lines complement each other at hourly intervals and also stop at Leipzig/Halle Airport. The only international connection is the daily EuroCity Leipzig-Prague.

Most major and medium-sized towns in Saxony and southern Saxony-Anhalt can be reached without changing trains. There are also direct connections via regional express lines to Falkenberg/Elster-Cottbus, Hoyerswerda, and Dessau-Magdeburg as well as Chemnitz. Neighbouring Halle (Saale) can be reached via three S-Bahn lines, two of which run via Leipzig/Halle Airport. The surrounding area of Leipzig is served by numerous regional and S-Bahn lines.

The city's railway connections are currently being greatly improved by major construction projects, particularly within the framework of the [German Unity transport projects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Reunification_Transport_Projects). The line to Berlin has been extended and has been passable at 200 km/h (120 mph) since 2006. On 13 December 2015, the high-speed line from Leipzig to Erfurt, designed for 300 km/h (190 mph), was put into operation. Its continuation to Nuremberg followed in December 2017. This integration into the high-speed network considerably reduced the journey times of the ICE from Leipzig to Nuremberg, Munich, and Frankfurt am Main. The Leipzig-Dresden railway line, which was the first German long-distance railway to go into operation in 1839, is also undergoing expansion for 200 km/h. The most important construction project in regional transport was the four-kilometer-long [city tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City-Tunnel_\(Leipzig\)), which went into operation in December 2013 as the main line of the [S-Bahn Mitteldeutschland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-Bahn_Mitteldeutschland).

There are freight stations in the districts of Wahren and Engelsdorf. In addition, a freight traffic centre has been set up near the Schkeuditzer Kreuz junction for goods handling between road and rail, as well as a freight station on the site of the DHL hub at Leipzig/Halle Airport.

### Suburban trains

See also: [S-Bahn Mitteldeutschland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-Bahn_Mitteldeutschland)



[Leipzig Wilhelm-Leuschner-Platz station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Wilhelm-Leuschner-Platz_station), August 2016

Leipzig is the core of the S-Bahn Mitteldeutschland line network. Together with the tram, six of the ten lines form the backbone of local public transport and an important link to the region and the neighbouring Halle. The main line of the S-Bahn consists of the underground S-Bahn stations Hauptbahnhof, Markt, Wilhelm-Leuschner-Platz, and [Bayerischer Bahnhof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayerischer_Bahnhof) leading through the City Tunnel as well as the above-ground station Leipzig MDR. There are a total of 30 S-Bahn stations in the Leipzig city area. Endpoints of the S-Bahn lines include Wurzen, Zwickau, Dessau, and Lutherstadt Wittenberg. Two lines run to Halle, one of them via Leipzig/Halle Airport.

With the timetable change in December 2004, the networks of Leipzig and Halle were combined to form the Leipzig-Halle S-Bahn. However, this network only served as a transitional solution and was replaced by the S-Bahn Mitteldeutschland on 15 December 2013. At the same time, the main line tunnel, marketed as the Leipzig City Tunnel, went into operation. The tunnel, which is almost four kilometres long, crosses the entire city centre from the main railway station to the Bavarian railway station. The S-Bahn stations are up to 22 metres underground. This construction was the first to create a continuous north–south axis, which had not existed until now due to the north-facing terminus station. The connection to the south of the city and the federal state will thus be greatly improved.

### Tramway and buses

See also: [Trams in Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Leipzig)



Tram at Friedrich-List-Platz

The [Leipziger Verkehrsbetriebe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipziger_Verkehrsbetriebe), existing since 1 January 1917, operate a total of 15 tram lines and 47 bus lines in the city.

The total length of the tram network is 146 km (91 mi), making it the largest in Saxony ahead of [Dresden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dresdner_Verkehrsbetriebe) (134.4 km (83.5 mi)) and the second largest in Germany after [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Berlin) (196 km (122 mi)).

The longest line in the Leipzig network is line 11, which connects Schkeuditz with Markkleeberg over 22 kilometres and is the only tram line in Leipzig to run in three tariff zones of the Central German Transport Association.

Night bus lines N1 to N9 and the night tram N17 operate in the night traffic. On Saturdays, Sundays, and holidays the tram line N10 and the bus line N60 also operate. The central transfer point between the bus and tram lines as well as to the S-Bahn is Leipzig Central Station.

### Bicycle

Like most German cities, Leipzig has a traffic layout designed to be [bicycle-friendly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycle-friendly). There is an extensive cycle network. In most of the one-way central streets, cyclists are explicitly allowed to cycle both ways. A few cycle paths have been built or declared since 1990. According to the data from the 2021/22 [traffic count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_count), [the Saxons' Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Saxons'_Bridge) has the highest traffic occupancy with over 15,000 cyclists per day in cycling in Leipzig.[^cite_note-149]

Since 2004 there is a [bicycle-sharing system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycle-sharing_system). Bikes can be borrowed and returned via smartphone app or by telephone. Since 2018, the system has enabled flexible borrowing and returning of bicycles in the inner city; in this zone, bicycles can be handed in and borrowed from almost any street corner. Outside these zones, there are stations where the bikes are waiting. The current locations of the bikes can be seen via the app. There are cooperation offers with the Leipzig public transport companies and car sharing in order to offer as complete a mobility chain as possible.

### Road



Leipzig's road network

Several federal motorways pass by Leipzig: the [A 14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundesautobahn_14) in the north, the [A 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundesautobahn_9) in the west, and the [A 38](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundesautobahn_38) in the south. The three motorways form a triangular partial ring of the double ring Mitteldeutsche Schleife around Halle and Leipzig. To the south toward Chemnitz, the [A 72](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundesautobahn_72) is also partly under construction.

The [federal roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundesstra%C3%9Fe) [B 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundesstra%C3%9Fe_2), [B 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundesstra%C3%9Fe_6), [B 87](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundesstra%C3%9Fe_87), [B 181](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundesstra%C3%9Fe_181?action=edit\&redlink=1), and [B 184](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundesstra%C3%9Fe_184?action=edit\&redlink=1) lead through the city area.

The ring road (Innenstadtring), which corresponds to the course of the old city fortification, surrounds the city centre of Leipzig, which today is largely traffic-calmed.

Leipzig has a dense network of [carsharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carsharing) stations. Additionally, since 2018 there is also a stationless car sharing system in Leipzig. Here the cars can be parked and booked anywhere in the inner city without having to define a specific car or period in advance. Finding and booking is done via a smartphone app.

Leipzig is one of the few cities in Germany with [vehicle for hire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle_for_hire) services that can be booked via a [mobile app](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_app). In contrast to taxicab services, the start and destination must be defined beforehand and other passengers can be taken along at the same time if they share a route.

### Long-distance buses

In March 2018, the [Leipzig Long-Distance Bus Terminal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_Long-Distance_Bus_Terminal) opened a few steps outside of the Hauptbahnhof building on its east side.[^cite_note-150]

In addition to a large number of national lines, several international lines also serve Leipzig. The cities of Bregenz, Budapest, Milan, Prague, Sofia, and Zurich, among others, can be reached without having to change trains. Around 30,000 journeys and 1.5 million passengers a year are expected at the new bus station.

Some lines also use Leipzig/Halle Airport, located at the A 9/A 14 motorway junction, and [Leipziger Messe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipziger_Messe) for a stop. Passengers can take the S-Bahn from there to the city centre.

### Water



Boats at the [Elsterflutbett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Elster)

In the first half of the 20th century, the construction of the [Elster-Saale canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elster-Saale_canal), [White Elster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Elster), and [Saale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saale) was started in Leipzig in order to connect to the network of waterways. The outbreak of the Second World War stopped most of the work, though some may have continued through the use of [forced labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forced_labor). The [Lindenauer port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindenauer_port?action=edit\&redlink=1) was almost completed but not yet connected to the Elster-Saale and [Karl Heine Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Heine_Canal) respectively. The Leipzig rivers (White Elster, [New Luppe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neue_Luppe), [Pleiße](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plei%C3%9Fe), and [Parthe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthe)) in the city have largely artificial river beds and are supplemented by some channels. These waterways are suitable only for small leisure boat traffic.

Through the renovation and reconstruction of existing [mill races](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mill_race) and watercourses in the south of the city and flooded disused [open cast mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_cast_mines), the city's navigable water network is being expanded. A link between Karl Heine Canal and the disused Lindenauer port was opened in 2015. Still more work was scheduled to complete the Elster-Saale canal. Such a move would allow small boats to reach the [Elbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elbe) from Leipzig. The intended completion date has been postponed because of an unacceptable cost-benefit ratio.
