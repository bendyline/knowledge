The **prime minister of Japan** ([内閣](573784.md)総理大臣, *Naikaku Sōri Daijin*; Japanese pronunciation: [\[naꜜi.ka.kɯ | soː.ɾʲi daꜜi.dʑiɴ\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Japanese))[^cite_note-daijirin-3] is the [head of government](37570.md) of [Japan](15573.md). The [prime minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_minister) chairs the [Cabinet of Japan](573784.md) and has the ability to select and dismiss its ministers of state. The prime minister also serves as the [commander-in-chief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander-in-chief) of the [Japan Self Defence Forces](2785204.md).&#x20;

The [National Diet](163847.md) (parliament) acts as an [electoral college](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_college) and both houses nominate the prime minister from among their members (typically from among the members of the [House of Representatives](217778.md)). Each house conducts a ballot under the [run-off](25932.md) system, though the nominee of the House of Representatives prevails if the two houses disagree on the nomination. They are then formally appointed by the [emperor](10110.md). The prime minister must retain the confidence of the House of Representatives to remain in office. Conventionally, the prime minister is almost always the leader of the majority party in the House of Representatives or the leader of the senior partner in the governing coalition, though there have been cabinet prime ministers from junior coalition partners. The prime minister lives and works at the [Naikaku Sōri Daijin Kantei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naikaku_S%C5%8Dri_Daijin_Kantei) (Prime Minister's Official Residence) in [Nagatachō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagatach%C5%8D), [Chiyoda, Tokyo](47836792.md), close to the [National Diet Building](925262.md).&#x20;

Before the adoption of the [Meiji Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Constitution), Japan had in practice no written [constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution). Under the [Yōrō Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y%C5%8Dr%C5%8D_Code) enacted in 752, the *[Daijō-daijin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daij%C5%8D-daijin)* was the head of the *[Daijō-kan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daij%C5%8D-kan)* (Department of State), the highest organ of Japan's pre-modern Imperial government during the [Heian period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heian_period) and until briefly under the Meiji Constitution. The office was replaced with the office of prime minister on 22 December 1885 with the appointment of [Itō Hirobumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It%C5%8D_Hirobumi) to the new position. The Meiji Constitution was adopted in 1890, which gave the emperor the powers to appoint and dismiss the prime minister and cabinet members. During this period, the Prime Minister and his [Cabinet](573784.md) were not necessarily chosen from the elected [members of parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Members_of_parliament). The [modern constitution](62699.md), adopted in 1947, transferred formal executive powers from the emperor to the prime minister, as well as designating the Diet as the nominator of the prime minister instead of the emperor.&#x20;

The prime minister exercises control and supervision over the entire executive branch and appoints all Cabinet ministers, with the power to dismiss them any time. They present bills to the Diet on behalf of the [Cabinet](573784.md), and sign laws and Cabinet orders along with other members of the Cabinet. They also have the power to call an early election by advising the emperor to dissolve the House of Representatives. Powers of the prime minister have grown significantly in the 21st century. The prime minister is one of the world's most powerful political leaders in modern times.[^cite_note-4] As the leader of the [world's fourth largest economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_\(nominal\)), the prime minister holds significant domestic and international leadership, being the leader of a prominent member state of the [G7](1196634.md) and [G20](2206608.md).&#x20;

As of 2026, [sixty-six people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_prime_ministers_of_Japan) (sixty-five men and one woman) have served as prime minister. The [longest-serving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_prime_ministers_of_Japan_by_time_in_office) prime minister was [Shinzo Abe](2634434.md), who served over eight years across two non-consecutive terms, and the shortest-serving was [Prince Naruhiko Higashikuni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Naruhiko_Higashikuni), who served fifty-four days. [Sanae Takaichi](8125532.md) succeeded [Shigeru Ishiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shigeru_Ishiba) as prime minister on 21 October 2025, following the [2025 Liberal Democratic Party presidential election](80506510.md); she is the first woman to serve as prime minister.&#x20;

## Designation

### Abbreviations

In Japanese, due to the special nature of the work of the head of government, the prime minister's titles vary depending on context, sometimes demonstrating their role. Since the inception of the [cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_\(government\)) system, the prime minister is known in Japanese as *Naikaku Sōri-Daijin* (内閣総理大臣) whenever they are referred to as the head of the Cabinet; however, this title is usually abbreviated to *Sōri-Daijin* (総理大臣). Other abbreviations include *Sōri* (総理), *Shushō* (首相), or *Saishō* (宰相).[^cite_note-5]&#x20;

### English notation

The official [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) rendering is "Prime Minister".[^cite_note-6] Before the introduction of the cabinet system, the translation "Grand Minister" was sometimes historically used, as was the translated [German](11867.md) term "[Minister President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_President) of the State".&#x20;

## History

See also: [List of prime ministers of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_prime_ministers_of_Japan) and [List of prime ministers of Japan by time in office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_prime_ministers_of_Japan_by_time_in_office)



[Itō Hirobumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It%C5%8D_Hirobumi), the first Prime Minister of Japan

Before the adoption of the [Meiji Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Constitution), Japan had in practice no written [constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution). Originally, a Chinese-inspired legal system known as *[ritsuryō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritsury%C5%8D)* was enacted in the late [Asuka period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asuka_period) and early [Nara period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nara_period). It described a government based on an elaborate and rational [meritocratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meritocracy) bureaucracy, serving, in theory, under the ultimate authority of the [emperor](10110.md); although in practice, real power was often held elsewhere, such as in the hands of the [Fujiwara clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujiwara_clan), who intermarried with the [imperial family](3177105.md) in the [Heian period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heian_period), or by the ruling *[shōgun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sh%C5%8Dgun)*. Theoretically, the last *ritsuryō* code, the [Yōrō Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y%C5%8Dr%C5%8D_Code) enacted in 752, was still in force at the time of the [Meiji Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration).[^cite_note-7]&#x20;

Under this system, the *[Daijō-daijin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daij%C5%8D-daijin)* (太政大臣; Chancellor of the Realm)[^cite_note-8] was the head of the *[Daijō-kan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daij%C5%8D-kan)* (Department of State), the highest organ of Japan's pre-modern Imperial government during the [Heian period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heian_period) and until briefly under the Meiji Constitution with the appointment of [Sanjō Sanetomi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanj%C5%8D_Sanetomi) in 1871. The office was replaced in 1885 with the appointment of [Itō Hirobumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It%C5%8D_Hirobumi) to the new position of Minister President of State,[^cite_note-9] four years before the enactment of the Meiji Constitution, which mentions neither the Cabinet nor the position of Prime Minister explicitly.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11] It took its current form with the adoption of the [Constitution of Japan](62699.md) in 1947.&#x20;

To date, sixty-six people have served in this position. The longest-serving prime minister to date is [Shinzo Abe](2634434.md), who served in two non-consecutive terms for 8 years, 267 days: from 26 September 2006 until 26 September 2007, and from 26 December 2012 until 16 September 2020.[^cite_note-CBS-Abe-off-resigns-12] The shortest-serving prime minister to date is [Prince Naruhiko Higashikuni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Naruhiko_Higashikuni), who served for fifty-four days: from 17 August until 9 October 1945.&#x20;

## Appointment



The document appointing [Noboru Takeshita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noboru_Takeshita) as prime minister. The [Privy Seal of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privy_Seal_of_Japan) and signature of Crown Prince [Akihito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akihito) (then temporarily performing state acts during the illness of Emperor [Hirohito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hirohito)) can be seen in the centre, and the signature of the outgoing prime minister [Yasuhiro Nakasone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasuhiro_Nakasone) on the left

The prime minister is nominated by both houses of the [Diet](163847.md), before the conduct of any other business. Each house conducts a ballot under the [run-off](25932.md) system. If the two houses choose different individuals, then a joint committee of both houses is appointed to agree on a common nominee. Ultimately, however, if the two houses do not agree, or if the House of Councillors does not make a nomination within 10 days after the House of Representatives vote, the nominee of the House of Representatives is deemed to be that of the Diet. Therefore, the House of Representatives can theoretically ensure the appointment of any prime minister it wants.[^cite_note-13] The nominee is then formally [appointed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Investiture) to office and presented with their appointment letter by the [emperor](10110.md) at the [Tokyo Imperial Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Imperial_Palace).[^cite_note-14]&#x20;

Conventionally, since the prime minister must maintain the confidence of the Diet to stay in office, they are almost always the leader of the majority party in the House of Representatives or the leader of the senior partner in the governing coalition. But there have been three cabinet prime ministers from junior coalition partners ([Hitoshi Ashida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitoshi_Ashida): 1948, [Morihiro Hosokawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morihiro_Hosokawa): 1993 and [Tomiichi Murayama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomiichi_Murayama): 1994), a few [minority governments](271434.md) (most recently the [Hata Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hata_Cabinet) in 1994 and at least numerically the [Second Hashimoto Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Hashimoto_Cabinet) of 1996 during its first year, but with an [extra-cabinet cooperation (閣外協力, *kakugai kyōryoku*)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_and_supply) agreement with two parties, sufficient to ensure safe majorities for most government initiatives), and several cabinets with a majority in the House of Representatives, but without legislative majority of their own (most recently the [DPJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Party_of_Japan)-led cabinets, [Kan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kan_Cabinet) and [Noda Cabinets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noda_Cabinet) after the [2010 upper house election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Japanese_House_of_Councillors_election); cf. *Nejire Kokkai*/"twisted Diets").&#x20;

## Role

### Constitutional roles

- Exercises "control and supervision" over the entire executive branch.

  [^cite_note-16]

- Presents bills to the Diet on behalf of the [Cabinet](573784.md).

  [^cite_note-ReferenceA-17]

- Signs laws and Cabinet orders (along with other members of the Cabinet).

  [^cite_note-18]

- Appoints all Cabinet ministers, and can dismiss them at any time.

  [^cite_note-19]

- May permit legal action to be taken against Cabinet ministers.

  [^cite_note-20]

- Must make reports on domestic and foreign relations to the Diet.

  [^cite_note-ReferenceA-17]

- Must report to the Diet upon demand to provide answers or explanations.

  [^cite_note-21]

- May advise the emperor to dissolve the House of Representatives.

  [^cite_note-22]

### Statutory roles

- Presides over meetings of the Cabinet.

  [^cite_note-23]

- [Commander-in-chief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander-in-chief) of the [Japan Self-Defense Forces](2785204.md).

  [^cite_note-24]

  [^cite_note-25]

- May override a court injunction against an administrative act upon showing of cause.

  [^cite_note-26]

Unlike most of their counterparts in constitutional monarchies, the prime minister is both *de jure* and *de facto* chief executive. In most other constitutional monarchies, the monarch is at least nominal chief executive, while being bound by convention to act on the advice of the cabinet. In contrast, the Constitution of Japan explicitly vests executive power in the Cabinet, of which the prime minister is the leader; this greatly enhances the prime minister's position compared to prime ministers in other parliamentary democracies. Their countersignature is required for all laws and Cabinet orders. While most ministers in parliamentary democracies have some freedom of action within the bounds of [cabinet collective responsibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_collective_responsibility), the Japanese Cabinet is effectively an extension of the prime minister's authority.&#x20;

## Retirement honours and emoluments























All eleven living former prime ministers, left to right, top to bottom: [Shigeru Ishiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shigeru_Ishiba), [Fumio Kishida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fumio_Kishida), [Yoshihide Suga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshihide_Suga), [Yoshihiko Noda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshihiko_Noda), [Naoto Kan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naoto_Kan), [Yukio Hatoyama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukio_Hatoyama), [Tarō Asō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tar%C5%8D_As%C5%8D), [Yasuo Fukuda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasuo_Fukuda), [Junichiro Koizumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junichiro_Koizumi), [Yoshirō Mori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshir%C5%8D_Mori) and [Morihiro Hosokawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morihiro_Hosokawa)

Until the mid-1930s, the prime minister of Japan was normally granted a hereditary [peerage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peerage) (*[kazoku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazoku)*) prior to leaving office if they had not already been ennobled. Titles were usually bestowed in the ranks of [count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count), [viscount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscount) or [baron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron), depending on the relative accomplishments and status of the prime minister. The two highest ranks, [marquess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marquess) and [prince](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince), were only bestowed upon highly distinguished statesmen, and were not granted to a prime minister after 1928. The last prime minister who was a peer was Baron [Kijūrō Shidehara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kij%C5%ABr%C5%8D_Shidehara), who served as Prime Minister from October 1945 to May 1946. The peerage was abolished when the [Constitution of Japan](62699.md) came into effect in May 1947.&#x20;

Certain eminent prime ministers have been awarded the [Order of the Chrysanthemum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_Chrysanthemum), typically in the degree of Grand Cordon. The highest honour in the Japanese honours system, the Collar of the Order of the Chrysanthemum, has only been conferred upon select prime ministers and eminent statesmen; the last such award to a living prime minister was to [Saionji Kinmochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saionji_Kinmochi) in 1928. More often, the Order of the Chrysanthemum has been a posthumous distinction; both the Collar and Grand Cordon of the order were last awarded posthumously to former prime minister [Shinzo Abe](2634434.md) in July 2022.[^cite_note-31]&#x20;

After relinquishing office, the prime minister is normally accorded the second or senior third rank in the court order of precedence, and is usually raised to the senior second rank posthumously. Certain distinguished prime ministers have been posthumously raised to the first rank; the last such award was to Eisaku Sato in 1975, Yasuhiro Nakasone in 2019, and Shinzo Abe in 2022. Since the 1920s, following their tenure in office, prime ministers have typically been conferred with the Grand Cordon of the [Order of the Paulownia Flowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_Paulownia_Flowers) (until 2003 a special higher class of the Order of the Rising Sun), depending on tenure and eminence; however, honours may be withheld due to misconduct or refusal on the part of the prime minister (for example, [Kiichi Miyazawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiichi_Miyazawa)).&#x20;

The Prime Minister also awards individuals in recognition of their accomplishments in sport, entertainment, and other fields. Some of the awards and commendations offered include the Prime Minister's Award, created by Eisaku Satō in 1966, and the [People's Honour Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People%27s_Honour_Award), created by Takeo Fukuda in 1977. Additionally, the PM also presents the [Prime Minister's Trophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Professional_Sports_Grand_Prize) on behalf of the Japan Professional Sports Association and the Monodzukuri Nippon award on behalf of the Japanese Manufacturing Association.&#x20;
