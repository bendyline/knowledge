The **prime minister of New Zealand** ([Māori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_language): *Te pirimia o Aotearoa*) is the [head of government](37570.md) of [New Zealand](4913064.md). The incumbent prime minister, [Christopher Luxon](62303596.md), leader of the [New Zealand National Party](333149.md), took office on 27 November 2023.[^cite_note-Jan2023-2]

The prime minister (informally abbreviated to PM) ranks as the most senior [government minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministers_in_the_New_Zealand_Government). They are responsible for chairing meetings of [Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_of_New_Zealand); allocating posts to ministers within the [government](8899161.md); acting as the spokesperson for the government; and providing [advice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advice_\(constitutional_law\)) to the [sovereign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy_of_New_Zealand) or the sovereign's representative, the [governor-general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor-General_of_New_Zealand). They also have ministerial responsibility for the [Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_the_Prime_Minister_and_Cabinet_\(New_Zealand\)), which is based in the [Beehive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beehive_\(New_Zealand\)) in Wellington.

The office exists by a long-established [convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_\(political_norm\)), which originated in New Zealand's former colonial power, the then [United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland). The convention stipulates that when the office becomes vacant, the governor-general must select as prime minister the person most likely to command the support, or confidence, of the [House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_House_of_Representatives). This individual is typically the [parliamentary leader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_leader) of the [political party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_political_parties_in_New_Zealand) that holds the largest number of seats in that house.[^cite_note-3] The prime minister and Cabinet are collectively [accountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_accountability) for their actions to the governor-general, to the House of Representatives, to their political party, and ultimately to the national electorate.

Originally the head of government was titled ["colonial secretary"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_Secretary_of_New_Zealand) or "[first minister](183220.md)". This was changed in 1869 to "[premier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premier)". That title remained in use for more than 30 years, until [Richard Seddon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Seddon) changed it to "[prime minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_minister)" in 1900 during his tenure in the office.[^cite_note-title-4] Following the declaration of New Zealand as a [Dominion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominion_of_New_Zealand) in 1907, the term prime minister was formally adopted. The Māori title for the office is *pirimia*, and is derived from the English *premier*.[^cite_note-5] New Zealand prime ministers are [styled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Style_\(form_of_address\)) as "[The Right Honourable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Right_Honourable)", a privilege they retain for life.[^cite_note-rt_hon-6]

[Forty-two people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_prime_ministers_of_New_Zealand) (thirty-nine men and three women) have served as prime minister, the first being [Henry Sewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Sewell), who took office on 7 May 1856 as premier. The longest-serving prime minister was [Richard Seddon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Seddon), who served over 13 years, and the shortest-serving was Sewell, who served two weeks.

## Appointment and tenure

The governor-general appoints a prime minister, like other [ministerial positions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministers_in_the_New_Zealand_Government), on behalf of the [monarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy_of_New_Zealand). By the conventions of [responsible government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Responsible_government), the governor-general will call to form a government the individual most likely to receive the support, or confidence, of a majority of the elected [members of parliament](98066.md#New_Zealand) (MPs).[^cite_note-cabinetmanual_pm-7] In making this appointment, convention requires the governor-general to act on the outcome of the [electoral process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_New_Zealand) and subsequent discussions between political parties by which the person who will lead the government as prime minister is identified.[^cite_note-cabinetmanual_pm-7][^cite_note-GG-8] In practice, the position typically falls to an MP who is the [parliamentary leader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_leader) of the largest political party among those forming the government.[^cite_note-te_ara-9][^cite_note-10] The prime minister may lead a [coalition government](6036.md) and/or a [minority government](271434.md) dependent on support from smaller parties during [confidence and supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_and_supply) votes.[^cite_note-11]



[Christopher Luxon](62303596.md) reads the [oath of allegiance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oath_of_Allegiance_\(New_Zealand\)), 27 November 2023

Once appointed and sworn in by the governor-general, the prime minister remains in the post until dismissal, resignation,[^cite_note-12] or death in office.[^cite_note-14] They, like all ministers, hold office "[during the pleasure of the Governor-General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/At_the_Governor-General's_pleasure)", so theoretically, the governor-general can dismiss a prime minister at any time; however, convention heavily circumscribes the power to do so.[^cite_note-15] The governor-general retains [reserve powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_power#New_Zealand)[^cite_note-16] to dismiss a prime minister in certain circumstances, such as those pertaining to a [no-confidence motion](208101.md) against the government in the [House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_House_of_Representatives).[^cite_note-17]

Where a prime minister, and by extension, the government, can no longer command the confidence of the house, either by losing a confidence vote or as the result of an election, convention dictates that they should tender their resignation to the governor-general. Under the [Constitution Act 1986](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_Act_1986), general elections are required every three years, setting the maximum period a prime minister can serve without their [mandate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_\(politics\)) being renewed.[^cite_note-18]

## Responsibilities and powers

See also: [New Zealand Government](8899161.md) and [Constitution of New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_New_Zealand)



Prime Minister [Christopher Luxon](62303596.md) (foreground, left of centre) chairs his first meeting of Cabinet, November 2023

The office of prime minister is not defined by [codified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codification_\(law\)) laws but by unwritten customs known as [constitutional conventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_convention_\(political_custom\)) which developed in Britain and which New Zealand replicated. These conventions depend for the most part on the underlying principle that the prime minister and fellow ministers must not lose the confidence of the democratically elected component of parliament, the House of Representatives. The prime minister is the leader of the [Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_of_New_Zealand) (itself a body existing by convention) and takes a coordinating role.[^cite_note-te_ara-9]

The *[Cabinet Manual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_Manual_\(New_Zealand\))* provides an outline of the prime minister's powers and responsibilities.[^cite_note-cabinetmanual_pm-7]

### Principal adviser to the sovereign

By constitutional convention, the prime minister holds formal power to [advise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advice_\(constitutional_law\)) the sovereign. This means that as long as the prime minister has the confidence of parliament, they alone may advise the monarch on:[^cite_note-cabinetmanual_pm-7]

- appointment or [recall](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/recall) of the governor-general

  [^cite_note-recall-19]

- amendments to the [Letters Patent Constituting the Office of Governor-General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_Patent_Constituting_the_Office_of_Governor-General_of_New_Zealand), which most recently occurred in 2006

- the conferment of [New Zealand honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_royal_honours_system) (except for honours in the personal gift of the monarch)

### Principal adviser to the governor-general

As head of government, the prime minister alone has the right to advise the governor-general to:

- appoint, dismiss, or accept the resignation of ministers

  [^cite_note-cabinetmanual_pm-7]

- [dissolve parliament](652293.md) and [issue a writ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writ_of_election) for an election to be held.

  [^cite_note-20]

  [^cite_note-cabinetmanual_pm-7]

  &#x20;The governor-general may reject the advice to dissolve parliament if the prime minister has recently lost a vote of confidence (that is, the governor-general would be using their [reserve powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_power)), but as of 2023 none has done so.

  [^cite_note-21]

### Head of government

Convention regards the prime minister as "[first among equals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_among_equals)".[^cite_note-te_ara-9] A prime minister does hold the most senior post in government, but must also adhere to any decisions taken by Cabinet, as per the convention of [collective ministerial responsibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_collective_responsibility). The actual ability of a prime minister to give direct orders is largely limited; most of the position's power comes about through other means, such as:

- the ability to set the Cabinet agenda, thereby controlling items for discussion

  [^cite_note-cabinetmanual_pm-7]

  [^cite_note-23]

- The ability to appoint and dismiss ministers, and to allocate [portfolios](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/portfolio)

  [^cite_note-ministers-25]

- the influence a prime minister is likely/assumed to have as leader of the dominant political party, which may afford more direct control over subordinates than is attached to the prime ministerial role

  [^cite_note-Cross-22]

- The power gained simply from being central to most significant decision-making, and from being able (as of right) to comment on and criticise any decisions taken by other ministers

Since the 1996 implementation of [the MMP electoral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_system_of_New_Zealand), the role of the prime minister in negotiating and maintaining relationships with [support parties](6036.md) has increased, placing some constraints on prime ministerial abilities.[^cite_note-\:0-26]

### Other roles and functions

Prime ministers also take on additional portfolios (to prioritise policy areas).[^cite_note-te_ara-9] Historically, 19th-century premiers looked after the colonial-secretary and finance portfolios. As New Zealand developed, the role of [minister of finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_Finance_\(New_Zealand\)) became too big; Prime Minister [Sir Robert Muldoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Muldoon) came under criticism for taking on the finance portfolio during his time in office (1975–1984), as it resulted in a large concentration of power in the hands of one individual.[^cite_note-te_ara-9]

Before 1987 it was common for prime ministers to take the role of [minister of foreign affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_Foreign_Affairs_\(New_Zealand\)), so they could represent New Zealand on the international stage.[^cite_note-\:0-26] More recent prime ministers have taken portfolios relevant to their interests, or to promote specific areas they saw as important. For example, [David Lange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lange) took the education portfolio in his second term; [Helen Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_Clark) took the role of [minister for arts, culture and heritage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Arts,_Culture_and_Heritage); [John Key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Key) served as [minister of tourism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_Tourism_\(New_Zealand\)); and [Jacinda Ardern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacinda_Ardern) became minister for child-poverty reduction.[^cite_note-te_ara-9]

Although no longer likely to be the minister of foreign affairs, the prime minister remains responsible for welcoming foreign heads of government, visiting leaders overseas, and attending [Commonwealth Heads of Government Meetings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_Heads_of_Government_Meeting).[^cite_note-\:0-26]

Conventionally, the prime minister is the responsible minister for the [Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_the_Prime_Minister_and_Cabinet_\(New_Zealand\)) (DPMC; founded in 1990), which has the task of supporting the policy agenda of Cabinet through policy advice and the coordination of the implementation of key government programmes.[^cite_note-\:0-26][^cite_note-Support-28]

Before 2014, the prime minister was also responsible for the [New Zealand Security and Intelligence Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Security_Intelligence_Service) (NZSIS) and for the [Government Communications Security Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Communications_Security_Bureau) (GCSB).[^cite_note-\:0-26] In 2014, Prime Minister John Key gave himself the new portfolio of [National Security and Intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_National_Security_and_Intelligence_\(New_Zealand\)) and delegated responsibility for SIS and GCSB to other ministers. He also expanded the role of DPMC in [security](240468.md) and intelligence. This model has been followed by subsequent prime ministers.[^cite_note-29]

## Privileges of office

### Salary and perquisites

Under the Remuneration Authority Act 1977, and the Members of Parliament (Remuneration and Services) Act 2013, a prime minister's salary is determined annually by the Remuneration Authority, an independent body established by parliament to set salaries for members of parliament and other government officials.[^cite_note-30] MPs' salaries were temporarily reduced during the [COVID-19 pandemic in New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_New_Zealand).[^cite_note-31] As of July 2024, the prime minister's salary is set at [NZ\$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NZ\$)498,300. In addition, like all other ministers and MPs, the prime minister receives annual allowances for travel and lodging, as do the prime minister's spouse and children.[^cite_note-32]



[Premier House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premier_House) in Wellington is the prime minister's residence.

The incumbent prime minister's official residence is [Premier House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premier_House), Tinakori Road, Wellington. There the prime minister hosts receptions and events for New Zealand and overseas guests.[^cite_note-33] Unlike the residences of certain other heads of government (e.g. the [White House](33057.md) and [10 Downing Street](226294.md)), Premier House does not serve as the government headquarters; the location of the prime minister's office is [the Beehive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beehive_\(New_Zealand\)), in the [parliament precinct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Parliament_Buildings) a short distance away.[^cite_note-Support-28] The prime minister's governmental work is supported by the non-partisan Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet. The separate Private Office of the Prime Minister provides advice and support on political party matters.[^cite_note-Support-28]

The [style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Style_\(form_of_address\)) of "[The Right Honourable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Right_Honourable)" (abbreviated to "The Rt Hon") is always granted to the prime minister upon taking office. Former prime ministers retain this style for the remainder of their lives.[^cite_note-rt_hon-6] The written form of address for the head of government should use their full parliamentary title as applicable: *The Right Honourable \[name], \[post-nominal letters], Prime Minister of New Zealand*.[^cite_note-34] It is also traditional for the monarch to bestow a [knighthood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knighthood) or [damehood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damehood) on prime ministers after they leave office,[^cite_note-35] and two prime ministers were knighted while still in office (namely [Sir Keith Holyoake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Holyoake) in 1970,[^cite_note-36] and [Sir Robert Muldoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Muldoon) in 1983[^cite_note-37]).

### Security and transport

The [Dignitary Protection Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dignitary_Protection_Service) (DPS) is a special branch of the [New Zealand Police](406756.md) that is charged with protecting the prime minister and their family, as well as members of parliament, members of the judiciary, and visiting foreign dignitaries when required.[^cite_note-38]

The DPS provides the prime minister with transport; they are driven in the [BMW 7 Series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BMW_7_Series_\(G11\)) 730LD and 750LI, the latter of which is armoured.[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40] Although usually flown domestically on regularly scheduled [Air New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_New_Zealand) flights, the prime minister also makes use of [Royal New Zealand Air Force](190903.md) aircraft, usually [Boeing 757](151995.md).[^cite_note-41] The 757 aircraft, which are used for international travel, has been upgraded with work stations, internal air stairs, and military communications capabilities.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43] The 757 fleet is set to be replaced by 2028.[^cite_note-44]

### After office

Former officeholders are entitled to annuity and travel payments for the rest of their lives. Former prime ministers who held the office for no less than two years are entitled to a yearly rate of \$10,900 for each complete year the person held office, with a maximum of \$54,500 payable annually.[^cite_note-45] Former prime ministers, when travelling within New Zealand, are eligible to be paid if the travel is for fulfilling commitments related to his or her role as a former prime minister.[^cite_note-46]

Should a serving or former prime minister die, they are accorded a [state funeral](714370.md#New_Zealand) (subject to the approval of the family). Two prime ministers who died in office were buried in mausoleums: [William Massey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Massey) (died 1925) in the [Massey Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massey_Memorial) in [Wellington](33804.md), and [Michael Joseph Savage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Joseph_Savage) (died 1940) in the Savage Memorial at [Bastion Point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bastion_Point) in [Auckland](18660332.md).[^cite_note-47]

## History



[Henry Sewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Sewell), regarded as New Zealand's first premier

Assuming that [Henry Sewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Sewell) is counted as the first prime minister, [42 individuals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_prime_ministers_of_New_Zealand) have held the office since it was established.[^cite_note-48] Some of these people have held it on several separate occasions, with the record for maximum number of times being shared between [William Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Fox_\(New_Zealand_politician\)) and [Harry Atkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Atkinson) (both of whom served four times). The longest that anyone has served in the office is 13 years, a record set by [Richard Seddon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Seddon). The first holder of the office, Henry Sewell, led the country for the shortest total time; his only term lasted just 13 days. The shortest term belonged to Harry Atkinson, whose third term lasted only seven days, but Atkinson served longer in total than did Sewell.[^cite_note-trivia-13] The youngest was [Edward Stafford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Stafford_\(politician\)), who was appointed premier in 1856, at 37 years, 40 days old.[^cite_note-Coulter-49] The oldest was [Walter Nash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Nash), who was 78 years old when he left office in 1960 (and 75 upon taking office in 1957).[^cite_note-trivia-13]

It is regarded that all New Zealand prime ministers thus far have been [Pākehā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81keh%C4%81) ([New Zealand European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_European)), and mostly of [British](19097669.md) and [Irish](775859.md) descent. There was persistent speculation during his lifetime that [Norman Kirk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Kirk) (Prime Minister from 1972 to 1974) was [Māori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people) and had [Kāi Tahu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C4%81i_Tahu) ancestry; he never publicly identified himself as such, and there is no substantial evidence for the claim.[^cite_note-50]

New Zealand is one of the few countries in the world to have had three female heads of government and one of only three countries to have had a female head of government directly succeed another.[^cite_note-51] The first female prime minister was [Jenny Shipley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jenny_Shipley) of the [National Party](333149.md), who replaced [Jim Bolger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Bolger) in late 1997; Shipley was succeeded by [Helen Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_Clark) in 1999. [Jacinda Ardern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacinda_Ardern), the second female leader of the [Labour Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Labour_Party) after Clark, became prime minister in 2017.[^cite_note-Coulter-49]

### Early leaders

On becoming the [Colony of New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colony_of_New_Zealand) in 1841, the country was directly governed by a [governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor-General_of_New_Zealand), appointed by the [Colonial Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_Office) in Britain. [Self-government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-governing_colony) was established in 1853, following the [New Zealand Constitution Act 1852](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Constitution_Act_1852), and the [First Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1st_New_Zealand_Parliament) met on 24 May 1854.[^cite_note-52]

The origins of the office of prime minister are disputed. Use of the words *[prime minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_minister)* as a descriptive term dates back to the First Parliament, where they are applied to [James FitzGerald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_FitzGerald_\(New_Zealand_politician\)) and [Thomas Forsaith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Forsaith).[^cite_note-McLean-53] FitzGerald and Forsaith had no official titles, however, and New Zealand had not yet obtained self-government. As such, they are not usually considered prime ministers in any substantive sense.[^cite_note-McLintock1966-54]

The first person to be formally appointed to a position of executive leadership was [Henry Sewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Sewell),[^cite_note-McLintock1966-54] who formed a brief [ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_\(collective_executive\)) in April 1856, at the beginning of the [Second Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2nd_New_Zealand_Parliament). Despite his formal leadership role, however, his only actual title was "[colonial secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_Secretary_of_New_Zealand)",[^cite_note-McLean-53] a position comparable to a [minister of internal affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_Internal_Affairs_\(New_Zealand\)). His successor, [William Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Fox_\(New_Zealand_politician\)), was also given a formal leadership role but was not a colonial secretary. In 1864 when [Frederick Weld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Weld) became the sixth person appointed to formal leadership, a substantive leadership title, "premier", appeared. Weld's successor, [Edward Stafford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Stafford_\(politician\)), briefly changed the title to "first minister", but it was soon restored to premier during the second tenure of Fox in 1869.[^cite_note-title-4] From that point, the title "premier" was used almost exclusively for the remainder of the 19th century. Nevertheless, in the Schedule of the [Civil List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_List) of 1873, provision was made for the salary of the head of government "being the Prime Minister".[^cite_note-title-4]

Initially, premiers acted as mere advisers to the governor—with the governor at times a very active partner. This began to change during the first tenure of Edward Stafford. Stafford met with his ministers and made decisions outside of the [Executive Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Council_of_New_Zealand), which was chaired by the governor, thus establishing the modern convention of [cabinet government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_government).[^cite_note-55] Stafford also clashed with the governor over control of [native](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people) affairs, which was eventually to fall within the premier's powers.[^cite_note-governors-56]

### Party leadership



[Richard Seddon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Seddon) styled himself "Prime Minister" at the turn of the 20th century

Premiers were initially supported by unorganised factions based on personal interests, and their governments were short-lived.[^cite_note-57] The political position of the premier was enhanced by the development of modern [political parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_political_parties_in_New_Zealand).[^cite_note-Johansson2013-58] Premier [John Ballance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ballance) organised the first formal party in New Zealand, the [Liberal Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Liberal_Party), forming the [Liberal Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Government_of_New_Zealand) in 1891.[^cite_note-59] There was little real [parliamentary opposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_opposition) until 1909, when [William Massey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Massey) organised his [conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historic_conservatism_in_New_Zealand) faction to form the [Reform Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reform_Party_\(New_Zealand\)). Thereafter political power centred on parties and their leaders.[^cite_note-party_leadership-60] (Subsequent governments were led by prime ministers from the Reform, [United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Party_\(New_Zealand\)), [Labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Labour_Party) and [National](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_National) parties.[^cite_note-Johansson2013-58]) Although not every government would have a large majority, the [party system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party_system) and tight control of party members by [whips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whip_\(politics\)) helped heads of government to direct the passage of legislation in the House of Representatives. In 1893, the premier gained the ability to restrict the term of appointments to the [Legislative Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Legislative_Council).[^cite_note-61]

After 1900, [Richard Seddon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Seddon), the incumbent head of government, used the title of "prime minister". The change of title was reflected in the *[New Zealand Official Yearbook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_official_publications)* of that year.[^cite_note-title-4] Seddon's immediate successor, [William Hall-Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Hall-Jones), was the first to be sworn in as "prime minister", in 1906.[^cite_note-62]

The expanding power of the prime minister was kept in check by the need to build consensus with other leading members of the Cabinet and of the governing party, including those who represented various ideological wings of the party. Other institutions, including Parliament itself and the wider state bureaucracy, also acted as limits on prime ministerial power; in 1912 [Thomas Mackenzie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Mackenzie) was the last prime minister to lose power through an unsuccessful [confidence motion](208101.md) in the House of Representatives.[^cite_note-trivia-13]

### Towards modern leadership

One change brought about by the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War) was direct participation in governing the British Empire. Previously, New Zealand prime ministers had attended occasional colonial and imperial conferences, but they otherwise communicated with London through the governor (a position then appointed by the British government). In 1917, British prime minister [David Lloyd George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lloyd_George) offered the New Zealand prime minister a seat in the [Imperial War Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_War_Cabinet), the British Empire's wartime coordinating body. In 1919, Prime Minister William Massey signed the [Treaty of Versailles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Versailles) on behalf of New Zealand, signalling the [independence of New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_of_New_Zealand) within the empire, although Massey downplayed the event as an ardent imperialist.[^cite_note-party_leadership-60]

Constitutional conventions adopted in 1930, following the [Imperial Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1930_Imperial_Conference) held that year, increased the domestic and international prestige of the prime minister. The [Statute of Westminster 1931](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Westminster_1931) confirmed that Dominions had exclusive power to make their laws. New Zealand initially resisted greater autonomy and did not [adopt the statute until 1947](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Westminster_Adoption_Act_1947). Increasingly New Zealand began to act independently in foreign affairs. During the 1940s the prime minister's profile rose as New Zealand signed several international treaties.[^cite_note-party_leadership-60] In 1967, [Keith Holyoake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Holyoake) became the first New Zealand prime minister to select candidates for the position of governor-general without any involvement of the British government. Holyoake advised the monarch, [Queen Elizabeth II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Elizabeth_II), to appoint [Sir Arthur Porritt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Porritt,_Baron_Porritt), the first New Zealand-born governor-general.[^cite_note-governors-56]



The 37th and 38th prime ministers of New Zealand, pictured in 2009: [John Key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Key) with his predecessor, [Helen Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_Clark).

After the [mixed-member proportional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed-member_proportional) (MMP) system was [introduced in 1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_reform_in_New_Zealand), prime ministers have had to manage minority governments, although the Ward government formed in 1928 and the Forbes government formed in 1930 are two earlier minority government examples. The Forbes-led United and Reform coalition from 1931 to 1935 was the country's first coalition government outside of wartime; and [George Forbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Forbes_\(New_Zealand_politician\)) is the only New Zealand parliamentary leader of a junior coalition party to have served as prime minister. The skill of MMP management was exemplified by [Helen Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_Clark)'s nine years as prime minister (1999–2008), when her Labour government remained in power thanks to a range of [confidence-and-supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence-and-supply) agreements with five smaller parties.[^cite_note-63]

Until the premiership of Helen Clark, it was customary for senior members of the legislature, executive and judiciary—including the prime minister—to be appointed to the [Privy Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privy_Council_of_the_United_Kingdom), granting them the style "Right Honourable". This practice was discontinued at the same time as the abolition of knighthoods and damehoods in 2000 from the [New Zealand royal honours system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_royal_honours_system). National's [John Key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Key) became prime minister in 2008 and moved to restore titular honours,[^cite_note-64] but did not resume appointments to the Privy Council, meaning Key was styled "The Honourable". On 3 August 2010, the Queen granted the style "Right Honourable" to the offices of prime minister, governor-general, [speaker of the House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speaker_of_the_New_Zealand_House_of_Representatives) and [chief justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Justice_of_New_Zealand), to be held for life by those appointed to these roles.[^cite_note-65]

On 21 June 2018, Labour's [Jacinda Ardern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacinda_Ardern) became the first prime minister of New Zealand (and second elected head of government in the world) to give birth while in office.[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67] Ardern was also the first prime minister to lead a single-party [majority government](465640.md) since the introduction of MMP, doing so from 2020 to 2023.[^cite_note-68]

## Deputy prime minister

Main article: [Deputy Prime Minister of New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deputy_Prime_Minister_of_New_Zealand)

An office titled "deputy prime minister" has existed since 1949.[^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70] The deputy typically holds important ministerial portfolios and, by convention, becomes [acting prime minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acting_prime_minister) in the absence or incapacity of the prime minister.[^cite_note-71] The deputy is commonly a member of the same party as the prime minister, but not necessarily so; in coalition governments, the parliamentary leader of a junior party may be offered the post.[^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73] [David Seymour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Seymour), leader of [ACT New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACT_New_Zealand), has been deputy prime minister since 31 May 2025.[^cite_note-74]
