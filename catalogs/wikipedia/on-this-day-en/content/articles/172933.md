The **Transportation Security Administration** (**TSA**) is an agency of the [United States Department of Homeland Security](58236.md) (DHS) that has authority over the security of transportation systems within and connecting to the United States. It was created as a response to the [September 11 attacks](5058690.md) to improve [airport security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airport_security) procedures and consolidate air travel security under a combined [federal law enforcement](16252377.md) and [regulatory agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulatory_agency).[^cite_note-3]

The TSA develops key policies to protect the U.S. transportation system, including highways, railroads, bus networks, [mass transit systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_transport), [ports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port), [pipelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport), and [intermodal freight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermodal_freight_transport) facilities. It fulfills this mission in conjunction with other federal, [state](18618239.md), [local](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_government) and foreign government partners. However, the TSA's primary mission is [airport security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airport_security) and the prevention of [aircraft hijacking](2075.md). It is responsible for screening passengers and baggage at more than 450 U.S. airports, employing screening officers, [explosives detection dog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explosive_detection_dog) handlers, and [bomb technicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bomb_disposal#EOD) in airports, and armed [Federal Air Marshals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Air_Marshal_Service) and [Federal Flight Deck Officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Flight_Deck_Officer) on aircraft.[^cite_note-4]

At first a part of the [Department of Transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Transportation), the TSA became part of DHS in March 2003 and is headquartered in [Springfield, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springfield,_Virginia). As of the fiscal year 2023, the TSA operated on a budget of approximately \$9.70 billion and employed over 47,000 Transportation Security Officers, Transportation Security Specialists, Federal Air Marshals, and other security personnel.

The TSA has screening processes and regulations related to passengers and [checked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Checked_baggage) and [carry-on luggage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_luggage), including [identification verification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_document), [pat-downs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frisking), [full-body scanners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millimeter_wave_scanner), and [explosives screening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puffer_machine). Since its inception, the agency has been subject to criticism and controversy regarding the effectiveness of various procedures, as well as incidents of baggage theft, data security, and allegations of prejudicial treatment towards certain ethnic groups.[^cite_note-5]

## History and mission



TSA's seal when first established under the [Department of Transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Transportation)



Historical TSA design used on TSO uniform patch, coin, and Year of Service pins

The TSA was created largely in response to the terrorist attacks of [September 11, 2001](5058690.md), which revealed weaknesses in existing airport security procedures.[^cite_note-6] At the time, a myriad of [private security companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_security_companies) managed air travel security under contract to individual airlines or groups of airlines that used a given airport or terminal facility.[^cite_note-7] Proponents of placing the government in charge of airport security, including Transportation Secretary [Norman Mineta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Mineta), argued that only a single federal agency could best protect passenger aviation.

Congress agreed, and authorized the creation of the TSA in the [Aviation and Transportation Security Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_and_Transportation_Security_Act), which was signed into law by President [George W. Bush](3414021.md) on November 19, 2001.[^cite_note-8] Bush nominated [John Magaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Magaw) as [undersecretary of transportation for security](#Leadership) on December 10, and he was confirmed by the Senate the following January. The agency was initially placed under the [United States Department of Transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Transportation) but was moved to the [Department of Homeland Security](58236.md) when that department was formed on March 9, 2003.

The new agency's effort to hire screeners to begin operating security checkpoints at airports represents a case of a large-scale staffing project completed over a short period. The only effort in U.S. history that came close to it was the testing of recruits for the armed forces in [World War II](32927.md). During the period from February to December 2002, 1.7 million applicants were assessed for 55,000 screening jobs.[^cite_note-9]

Private screening did not disappear entirely under the TSA, which allows airports to opt-out of the federal screening and hire firms to do the job instead. Such firms must still get TSA approval under its [Screening Partnership Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screening_Partnership_Program) (SPP) and follow TSA procedures.[^cite_note-10] Among the handful of U.S. airports with privately operated checkpoints are [San Francisco International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_International_Airport), [Kansas City International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansas_City_International_Airport), [Greater Rochester International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Rochester_International_Airport), [Tupelo Regional Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupelo_Regional_Airport), [Key West International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_West_International_Airport), and [Charles M. Schulz–Sonoma County Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_M._Schulz%E2%80%93Sonoma_County_Airport).[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12] However, the bulk of airport screening in the U.S. is done by the TSA's 46,661 (as of FY 2018) Transportation Security Officers (TSOs). [^cite_note-Gayden-Commercial-Aviation-101-13] They examine passengers and their baggage, and perform other security duties within airports, including controlling entry and exit points, and monitoring the areas near their checkpoints.

In August 2017, the [General Services Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Services_Administration) announced a new headquarters for the TSA would be built in [Springfield, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springfield,_Virginia). The new, 625,000-square-foot headquarters was built near the outskirts of [Fort Belvoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Belvoir) and the [Franconia-Springfield Metro station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franconia-Springfield_Station), and cost \$316 million.[^cite_note-14]

### Leadership

When the TSA was part of the [Department of Transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Transportation), the head of the agency was referred to as the undersecretary of transportation for security. Following the move to the [Department of Homeland Security](58236.md) in March 2003, the position was reclassified as the administrator of the Transportation Security Administration.

There have been eight administrators and nine acting administrators in the TSA's 24-year history. Several have come to the job after previously serving as Coast Guard [flag officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_officer), including Loy, Neffenger, and Pekoske.

Following the passage of the [FAA Reauthorization Act of 2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FAA_Reauthorization_Act_of_2018), which included a provision known as the TSA Modernization Act, the administrator's term was set as a five-year term retroactive to the start of current administrator David Pekoske's term. It also made the deputy administrator a [politically appointed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_appointments_in_the_United_States) position.[^cite_note-15]

### Organizational structure

At the helm of the TSA is the **administrator**, who leads the organization's efforts in safeguarding the nation's airports, railways, seaports, and other critical transportation infrastructure. Assisting the administrator is a deputy administrator, whose role is to provide support and guidance in executing the agency's mission. In addition, the TSA benefits from the expertise and leadership of several deputy assistant administrators and other executive officers, who contribute their knowledge and skills to various aspects of the agency's operations. Together, this structured leadership team forms the backbone of the TSA, working collectively to uphold and enhance the security of the nation's transportation networks. The executive assistant administrator for law enforcement is also the executive director of the [Federal Air Marshal Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Air_Marshal_Service).

On September 11, 2018, the TSA adopted a new flag representing its core values and founding principles. The design features a white, graphically stylized American eagle sitting centrally located inside rings of red and white against a field of blue, with its dynamically feathered wings outstretched in a pose signifying protection, vigilance, and commitment. The eagle's wings, which break through the red and white containment rings, indicate freedom of movement. There are nine stars and 11 rays emanating out from the top of the eagle to reference September 11. Part of the TSA budget comes from a \$5.60 per-passenger fee, also known as the [September 11](5058690.md) Security Fee, for each one-way air-travel trip originating in the United States, not to exceed \$11.20 per round-trip. In 2020, this passenger fee totaled \$2.4 billion or roughly 32% of the budget allocated by Congress that year.[^cite_note-35]

Additionally, a small portion of TSA's budget comes from the loose change and small denomination cash left behind by travelers at airport security checkpoints, which TSA has been allowed to retain since 2005 under Section 44945 of title 49, United States Code. From FY 2008 through FY 2018, a total of \$6,904,035.98 has been left behind, including a record \$960,105.49 in FY 2018.[^cite_note-36] In fiscal year 2019, \$926,030.44 was unclaimed.[^cite_note-37]

### Employees

Among the types of TSA employees are:[^cite_note-dhs.gov-38]

- Transportation Security Officers: The TSA employs around 47,000 Transportation Security Officers (TSOs). They screen people and property and control entry and exit points in airports. They also watch several areas before and beyond checkpoints.

  [^cite_note-39]

  [^cite_note-40]

  &#x20;TSOs do not carry weapons, do not have [arrest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrest) powers, and are generally not permitted to use force.

  [^cite_note-41]

  

  Badge of a Transportation Security Officer

  Transportation Security Officers (TSOs) provide security and protection for air travelers, airports, and aircraft. This includes:

  - Operating various screening equipment and technology to identify dangerous objects in baggage, cargo, and passengers, and preventing those objects from being transported onto aircraft.

  - Performing searches and screening, which may include physical interaction with passengers (e.g., pat-downs, a search of property, etc.).

  - Controlling terminal entry and exit points.

  - Interacting with the public, giving directions, and responding to inquiries.

  - Maintaining focus and awareness while working in a stressful environment which includes noise from alarms, machinery and people, crowd distractions, time pressure, and disruptive and angry passengers, to preserve the professional ability to identify and locate potentially life-threatening or mass destruction devices, and to make effective decisions in both crisis and routine situations.

  - Engaging in the continuous development of critical thinking skills, necessary to mitigate actual and potential security threats, by identifying, evaluating, and applying appropriate situational options and approaches. This may include the application of risk-based security screening protocols that vary based on program requirements.

  - Retaining and implementing knowledge of all applicable Standard Operating Procedures, demonstrating responsible and dependable behavior, and is open to change and adapts to new information or unexpected obstacles.

    [^cite_note-usajobs.gov-42]

  The key requirements for employment are:[^cite_note-usajobs.gov-42]

  - Be a U.S. Citizen or U.S. National at time of application submission

  - Be at least 18 years of age at time of application submission

  - Pass a Drug Screening and Medical Evaluation

  - Pass a background investigation including a credit and criminal check

  - No default on \$7,500 or more in delinquent debt (but for some bankruptcies)

  - Selective Service registration required

A handful of airports also have a retention bonus of up to 35%.[^cite_note-44]

- [Federal Flight Deck Officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Flight_Deck_Officer) (FFDOs) are the airline pilots working for the U.S. airlines, who are sworn and deputized as federal [law enforcement officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_enforcement_officers) (FLEOs) to carry out the law enforcement duties within their specific jurisdictions (flight deck) and only from the time their aircraft doors are closed and until they are opened. FFDOs do not have arrest powers but are authorized to carry a federally issued firearm and use force (including deadly force). While the program is voluntary, only active [part 121](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Aviation_Regulations#Part_121) airline pilots are eligible for the FFDO program. FFDO's are trained by the [Federal Air Marshal Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Air_Marshal_Service) and deputized by the [Department of Homeland Security](58236.md). Their primary goal is to work with (or without) the FAM team to defend the flight deck from [hijacking](2075.md), criminal violence, or any other terrorist threats to their aircraft.

### Uniforms

In 2008, TSA officers began wearing new uniforms that have a [royal blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_blue) duty shirt, dark blue (almost black) pants, and black belt.[^cite_note-65] The first airport to introduce the new uniforms was [Baltimore/Washington International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltimore/Washington_International_Airport). Starting on September 11, 2008, all TSOs began wearing the new uniform. One stripe on the outer edge of each [shoulder board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoulder_board) denotes a TSO, two stripes a Lead TSO, and three a Supervisory TSO.

Officers are issued badges and shoulder boards after completing a trainee period including 3-week academy at the [Federal Law Enforcement Training Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Law_Enforcement_Training_Center) (FLETC) in [Glynco, Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glynco,_Georgia).



A Transportation Security Officer shoulder board

### Personnel system and pay

The personnel system used by TSA was originally based on the FAA's personnel system.[^cite_note-66]

Unlike a majority of the federal government, TSA employees are not on the [General Schedule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Schedule) pay system. Instead, a [pay band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pay_band) system with performance-based increases was used.

In June 2021, Secretary of Homeland Security [Alejandro Mayorkas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alejandro_Mayorkas) issued a memorandum which directed the TSA Administrator among other items to pay employees "at a level that is no less than that of their counterparts on the General Schedule pay scale." It also directed that TSOs be offered expanded [collective bargaining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_bargaining) rights mirroring [title 5 of the United States Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_5_of_the_United_States_Code), and appeals of adverse actions to the [Merit Systems Protection Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merit_Systems_Protection_Board).[^cite_note-67]

In July 2023, a new pay system mirroring the General Schedule was introduced following appropriations made by the [Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consolidated_Appropriations_Act,_2023). For TSOs, the career ladder begins at the D band and progresses to F band. Lead TSOs and supervisory TSOs are at the F and G band levels.[^cite_note-68] Career ladders are also being worked on for non-TSOs.[^cite_note-69]

In 2011, former TSA Administrator John Pistole granted limited collective bargaining rights to Transportation Security Officers following a union election between two federal employee unions, the [American Federation of Government Employees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Federation_of_Government_Employees) and the [National Treasury Employees Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Treasury_Employees_Union).[^cite_note-70] In May 2024, an expanded collective bargaining agreement with a 7-year term was signed between TSA and AFGE.[^cite_note-71]

In February 2025, Secretary of Homeland Security [Kristi Noem](27663933.md) issued a memorandum that cancelled the 2024 collective bargaining agreement. AFGE called it a "retaliatory action" for challenging President [Donald Trump](4848272.md)'s [mass layoffs of the federal workforce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_United_States_federal_mass_layoffs) and vowed to fight it.[^cite_note-72]

### Incidents

#### 2013 Los Angeles airport shooting

Main article: [2013 Los Angeles International Airport shooting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_Los_Angeles_International_Airport_shooting)

On Friday, November 1, 2013, TSA officer Gerardo I. Hernandez, age 39, was shot and killed by a lone gunman at the Los Angeles International Airport. Law enforcement officials identified the suspect as 23-year-old Paul Anthony Ciancia, who was shot and wounded by law enforcement officers before being taken into custody.[^cite_note-73] Ciancia was wearing fatigues and carrying a bag containing a hand-written note that said he "wanted to kill TSA and pigs". Hernandez was the first TSA officer to be killed in the line of duty.[^cite_note-74]

#### 2015 New Orleans airport attack

On March 21, 2015, 63-year-old Richard White entered the [Louis Armstrong New Orleans International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Armstrong_New_Orleans_International_Airport) armed with six [Molotov cocktails](20922.md), a gasoline lighter, and a [machete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machete). White began assaulting passengers and TSA officers by spraying them with a can of wasp killer, then started swinging his machete. A TSA agent blocked the machete with a piece of luggage, as White ran through a metal detector. A [Jefferson Parish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jefferson_Parish,_Louisiana) deputy sheriff shot and killed White as he was chasing a TSA officer with his machete.[^cite_note-USAToday-75]

#### COVID-19 pandemic in the United States

The TSA continued working throughout the [COVID-19 pandemic in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_coronavirus_pandemic_in_the_United_States). As of December 31, 2020, the TSA cumulatively had 4,978 federal employees test positive for [COVID-19](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronavirus_disease_2019): 4,219 of those employees recovered, and 12 died as a result of the virus.[^cite_note-tsa-covid-76]

#### Collaboration with ICE

In January 2026, TSA head Ha Nguyen McNeill confirmed that the TSA was helping [ICE](216537.md) "check against" passenger information.[^cite_note-77]

### Passenger and carry-on screening

#### Pre-screening

See also: [Secure Flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secure_Flight)

The TSA checks passenger information like name, sex, and date of birth seventy-two hours before departure for any matches on the [No Fly List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Fly_List) or [Selectee List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_Security_Screening_Selection).[^cite_note-78] At the checkpoint, passengers 18 and older are required to show a valid ID to be processed by Credential Authentication Technology. This is used to match passenger information with their flight reservation and [secure flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secure_Flight) status without a boarding pass.[^cite_note-79] Valid forms of identification include [passports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passport) from the U.S. or a foreign government, state or tribal-issued photo identification, or military ID. Passengers that do not have ID are often still allowed to fly if their identity can be verified through alternate means satisfying a [certificate of identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Certificate_of_identity).[^cite_note-identification-80]

##### REAL ID requirements

Passed by [Congress](31756.md) in 2005, the [Real ID Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_ID_Act) established minimum security standards for state-issued [driver's licenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Driver's_license) and [identification cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_document) and prohibits federal agencies, like TSA, from accepting licenses and identification cards for official purposes from states that do not meet these standards.[^cite_note-\:0-81] Implementation has been deferred for two decades by DHS, and enforcement begun on May 7, 2025.[^cite_note-82]

#### Screening

At the airport security checkpoint, passengers are screened to ensure they are not carrying prohibited items. These include most sorts of sharp objects, many sporting goods such as baseball bats and hockey sticks, guns or other weapons, many sorts of tools, flammable liquids (except for conventional lighters), many forms of chemicals and paint.[^cite_note-86] In addition, passengers are limited to 3.4 [US fluid ounces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_fluid_ounce) (100 ml) of almost any liquid or gel, which must be presented at the checkpoint in a clear, one-quart [zip-top bag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zipper_storage_bag).[^cite_note-87] These restrictions on liquids were a reaction to the [2006 transatlantic aircraft plot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_transatlantic_aircraft_plot).

The number of passengers who have been detected bringing firearms onto airplanes in their carry-on bags has increased in recent years, from 976 in 2009 to 4,239 in 2018, according to the TSA. Indeed, a new record high for firearms found in carry-on bags has been set every year since 2008.[^cite_note-88] In 2010 an anonymous source told ABC News that [undercover agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_team) managed to bring weapons through security nearly 70 percent of the time at some major airports.[^cite_note-quinn-89] Firearms can be legally checked in checked luggage on domestic flights.[^cite_note-90]

In some cases, government leaders, members of the US military and law-enforcement officials are allowed to bypass security screening.[^cite_note-AP112310-91][^cite_note-92]

##### Pat-downs

See also: [Frisking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frisking)

The new pat-down procedures, which were originally not made public,[^cite_note-93] "routinely involve the touching of buttocks and [genitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genitals)"[^cite_note-94][^cite_note-95][^cite_note-96] as well as breasts.[^cite_note-97] These procedures were controversial, and in a November 2010 poll, 50% of those polled felt that the new pat-down procedures were too extreme, with 48% feeling them justified.[^cite_note-98] A number of publicized incidents created a public outcry against the invasiveness of the pat-down techniques,[^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100][^cite_note-101] in which women's breasts and the genital areas of all passengers are patted.[^cite_note-Sharkey-102] Pat-downs are carried out by agents of the same gender as the passenger.[^cite_note-103]

Concerns were raised as to the constitutionality of the new screening methods by organizations such as the [American Civil Liberties Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_Liberties_Union).[^cite_note-104] As of April 2011, at least six lawsuits were filed for violation of the [Fourth Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution).[^cite_note-105][^cite_note-106] [George Washington University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington_University_Law_School) law professor [Jeffrey Rosen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_Rosen_\(legal_academic\)) has supported this view, saying "there's a strong argument that the TSA's measures violate the Fourth Amendment, which prohibits unreasonable searches and seizures."[^cite_note-107] Concerns were also raised about the effects of these pat-downs on victims of sexual assault.[^cite_note-108] In January 2014, Denver police launched an investigation against a screener at Denver International Airport over what the passenger stated was an intrusive patdown.[^cite_note-109]

##### Full body scanners

Main article: [Full body scanner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_body_scanner)

See also: [Backscatter X-ray § Health effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backscatter_X-ray#Health_effects), and [Millimeter wave scanner § Possible health effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millimeter_wave_scanner#Possible_health_effects)



The screen operators of millimeter wave scanners now see.

&#x20;TSA has used two kinds of full body imaging technology since first deploying them in airports in 2010. Previously [backscatter X-ray scanners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backscatter_X-ray) were used which produced [ionizing radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionizing_radiation). After criticism the agency now uses only [millimeter wave scanners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millimeter_wave_scanner) which use [non-ionizing radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-ionizing_radiation).[^cite_note-110] The TSA refers to both systems as Advanced Imaging Technologies or AIT. Critics sometimes refer to them as "naked scanners," though operators no longer see images of the actual passenger, which has been replaced by a stick figure with boxes indicating areas of concern identified by the machine.[^cite_note-111][^cite_note-\:1-112] In 2022, TSA announced it will allow passengers to select the gender marker of their choice and alter algorithms used by the machines to be inclusive of transgender, nonbinary, and gender-nonconforming individuals. Previously the agency required screeners to select a male or female button based on a brief glance at the passenger as they entered the machine.[^cite_note-113]



Screenshot from a [millimeter wave scanner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millimeter_wave_scanner) before they were replaced by stick figure avatars

Passengers are directed to hold their hands above their heads for a few seconds while front and back images are created.[^cite_note-114] If the machine indicates an anomaly to the operator, or if other problems occur, the passenger is required to receive a pat-down of that area.



An older Rapiscan [backscatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_backscatter) X-ray scanner



[X-ray backscatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_backscatter) technology produces an image that resembles a chalk etching.

[^cite_note-115]

Full-body scanners have also proven controversial due to privacy and health concerns. The [American Civil Liberties Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_Liberties_Union) has called the scanners a "virtual strip search."[^cite_note-Puzzanghera-116] Female passengers have complained that they are often singled out for scanning, and a review of TSA records by a local CBS affiliate in Dallas found "a pattern of women who believe that there was nothing random about the way they were selected for extra screening."[^cite_note-117]

The TSA states on their website that they have "implemented strict measures to protect passenger privacy which is ensured through the anonymity of the image,"[^cite_note-118] and additionally states that these technologies "cannot store, print, transmit or save the image, and the image is automatically deleted from the system after it is cleared by the remotely located security officer."[^cite_note-119] This claim, however, was proven false after multiple incidents involving leaked images. The machines do in fact have the ability to "save" the images and while this function is purported to be "turned off" by the TSA in screenings, TSA training facilities have the save function turned on.[^cite_note-120][^cite_note-121]

As early as 2010, the TSA began to test scanners that would produce less intrusive "stick figures".[^cite_note-122] In February 2011, the TSA began testing new software on the millimeter-wave machines already used at Amsterdam's [Schiphol Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schiphol_Airport) that automatically detects potential threats on a passenger without the need for having an officer review actual images. Instead, one generic figure is used for all passengers and small yellow boxes are placed on areas of the body requiring additional screening.[^cite_note-\:1-112] The TSA announced in 2013 that the [Rapiscan's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapiscan_Systems) backscatter scanners would no longer be used since the manufacturer of the machines could not produce "privacy software" to abstract the near-nude images that agents view and turn them into stick-like figures. The TSA continues to use other full-body scanners.[^cite_note-123]

Health concerns have been raised about both scanning technologies. With regards to exposure to [radiation](25856.md) emitted by backscatter X-rays, and there are fears that people will be exposed to a "dangerous level of radiation if they get backscattered too often". [Ionizing radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionizing_radiation) is considered a non-threshold [carcinogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcinogen), but it is difficult to quantify the risk of low radiation exposures.[^cite_note-124] Active millimeter wave scanners emit radiation which is non-ionizing, does not have enough energy to directly damage [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA), and is not known to be [genotoxic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genotoxic).[^cite_note-Cancer.org-125][^cite_note-Ryan_2000-126][^cite_note-carcinogenesis-127]

#### TSA PreCheck

This section is an excerpt from [TSA PreCheck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TSA_PreCheck).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=TSA_PreCheck\&action=edit)]



TSA PreCheck logo

[TSA PreCheck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TSA_PreCheck) is a [trusted traveler program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trusted_Traveler_Programs) that allows some people to receive expedited security screening at airports in the [United States](3434750.md).[^cite_note-128]

The program, branded as TSA Pre✓, was initiated in December 2013 and is administered (except for enrollment) by the United States Transportation Security Administration.

Travelers who can take advantage of TSA PreCheck are members of certain [frequent flyer programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequent_flyer_programs), members of [Global Entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Entry), [Free and Secure Trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_and_Secure_Trade), [NEXUS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NEXUS), and [SENTRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SENTRI), [members of the U.S. Military](32212.md), and cadets and midshipmen of the [United States service academies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_service_academies)[^cite_note-129][^cite_note-130] It works for traveling on domestic itineraries and some international ones.

As of August 2026, 97 airlines participate in the program,[^cite_note-131] which is available at more than 200 airports.[^cite_note-132]

#### Changes

##### Large printer cartridges ban

After the October 2010 [cargo planes bomb plot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_cargo_plane_bomb_plot), in which cargo containing [laser printers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_printer) with [toner cartridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toner_cartridge) filled with explosives were discovered on separate cargo planes, the U.S. prohibited passengers from carrying certain printer cartridges on flights.[^cite_note-washingtonpost3-133] The TSA said it would ban toner and [ink cartridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ink_cartridge) weighing over 16 ounces (453 grams) from all passenger flights.[^cite_note-134][^cite_note-pcmag1-135] The ban applies to both carry-on bags and checked bags, and does not affect average travelers, whose toner cartridges are generally lighter.[^cite_note-pcmag1-135]

##### November 2010 enhanced screening procedures

Beginning in November 2010, TSA added new enhanced screening procedures. Passengers are required to choose between an enhanced [patdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patdown) (allowing TSOs to more thoroughly check areas on the body such as waistbands, groin, and inner thigh)[^cite_note-AP112310-91] or instead to be imaged by the use of a [full body scanner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_body_scanner) (that is, either [backscatter X-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backscatter_X-ray) or [millimeter wave detection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millimeter_wave_scanner) machines) in order to fly. These changes were made in reaction to the [Umar Farouk Abdulmutallab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umar_Farouk_Abdulmutallab) bombing attempt.[^cite_note-136]

##### Reverse screenings

In April 2016, TSA Administrator, Peter V. Neffenger told a Senate committee that small airports had the option to use "reverse screening" – a system where passengers are not screened before boarding the aircraft at departure, but instead are screened upon arrival at the destination. The procedure is intended to save costs at airports with a limited number of flights.[^cite_note-137]

##### March 2017 electronic device restrictions

On March 21, 2017, the TSA banned electronic devices larger than [smartphones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smartphone) from being carried on flights to the U.S. from 10 specific airports located in Muslim-majority countries. The order cited intelligence that "indicates that terrorist groups continue to target commercial aviation and are aggressively pursuing innovative methods to undertake their attacks, to include smuggling explosive devices in various consumer items".[^cite_note-ars-tsa17ban-138][^cite_note-CBC-nsa17ban-139] The restrictions were ended in July following changes in screening procedures at the specified airports.

##### Reactions

After the November 2010 initiation of enhanced screening procedures of all airline passengers and flight crews, the [US Airline Pilots Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Airline_Pilots_Association) issued a press release stating that pilots should not submit to full-body scanners because of unknown radiation risks and calling for strict guidelines for pat-downs of pilots, including evaluation of their fitness for duty after the pat-down, given the stressful nature of pat-downs.[^cite_note-Sharkey-102][^cite_note-140] Two airline pilots filed suit against the procedures.[^cite_note-141]

In March 2011, two [New Hampshire](21134.md) [state representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Hampshire_House_of_Representatives) introduced proposed legislation that would criminalize as [sexual assault](218407.md) invasive TSA pat-downs made without [probable cause](146751.md).[^cite_note-142][^cite_note-143][^cite_note-144] In May 2011, the [Texas House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_House_of_Representatives) passed a bill that would make it illegal for Transportation Security Administration officials to touch a person's genitals when carrying out a [patdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frisking). The bill failed in the [Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Senate) after the [Department of Justice](52563.md) threatened to make Texas a [no-fly zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No-fly_zone) if the legislation passed.[^cite_note-145][^cite_note-146] In the [United States House of Representatives](19468510.md), [Ron Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ron_Paul) introduced the [American Traveler Dignity Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Traveler_Dignity_Act?action=edit\&redlink=1) (H.R.6416),[^cite_note-147] but it stalled in committee.[^cite_note-148]

On July 2, 2010, the [Electronic Privacy Information Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Privacy_Information_Center) (EPIC) filed a lawsuit in federal court asking to halt the use of full-body scanners by the TSA on [Fourth amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) grounds, and arguing that the TSA had failed to allow a public notice and rulemaking period. In July 2011, the D.C. Circuit court of appeals ruled that the TSA did violate the [Administrative Procedure Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Administrative_Procedure_Act_\(United_States\)) by failing to allow a public notice and comment rule-making period. The Court ordered the agency to "promptly" undertake a public notice and comment rulemaking. In July 2012, EPIC returned to court and asked the court to force enforcement; in August, the court granted the request to compel the TSA to explain its actions by the end of the month.[^cite_note-149] The agency responded on August 30, saying that there was "no basis whatsoever for (The DC Circuit Court's) assertion that TSA has delayed implementing this court's mandate," and said it was awaiting approval from the Department of Homeland Security before the hearings take place. The TSA also said that it was having "staffing issues" regarding the issue, but expects to begin hearings in February 2013.[^cite_note-150] The comment period began on March 25, 2013[^cite_note-151][^cite_note-regulations-152] and closed on June 25, 2013, with over 90% of the comments against the scanners.[^cite_note-regulations-152] As of October 2015, no report has been issued.

Two separate Internet campaigns promoted a "National Opt-Out Day," the day before [Thanksgiving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thanksgiving), urging travelers to "opt out" of the scanner and insist on a pat-down.[^cite_note-153] The enhanced pat-down procedures were also the genesis of the "[Don't touch my junk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don't_touch_my_junk)" [meme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_meme).[^cite_note-154]

### Checked baggage



TSA lock with symbol and general key access



3D printed master keys for [Travel Sentry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel_Sentry) locks

In order to be able to search passenger baggage for security screening, the TSA will cut or otherwise disable locks they cannot open themselves. The agency authorized two companies to create [padlocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padlock), lockable straps, and luggage with built-in locks that can be opened and relocked by tools and information supplied by the lock manufacturers to the TSA. These are [Travel Sentry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel_Sentry) and [Safe Skies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safe_Skies).[^cite_note-155] TSA agents sometimes cut these locks off instead of opening them, and TSA received over 3,500 complaints in 2011 about locks being tampered with.[^cite_note-156] Travel journalist and *[National Geographic Traveler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Geographic_Traveler)* editor Christopher Elliott describes these locks as "useless" at protecting the goods within,[^cite_note-157] whereas SmarterTravel wrote in early 2010 that the "jury is out on their effectiveness", while noting how easy they are to open.[^cite_note-158]

In November 2014, The *Washington Post* inadvertently published a photograph of all seven of the TSA master keys in an article[^cite_note-159] about TSA baggage handling. The photograph was later removed from the original article, but it still appears in some syndicated copies.[^cite_note-160] In August 2015, this gained the attention of news sites.[^cite_note-dangers-161] Using the photograph, security researchers and members of the public have been able to reproduce working copies of the master keys using [3D printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_printing) techniques.[^cite_note-162][^cite_note-163] The incident has prompted discussion about the security implications of using master keys.[^cite_note-dangers-161]

### Non-airport regulation

While most known for their role in airports, the TSA is also responsible for other transportation related regulations, including those without passengers. For example, the TSA was responsible for setting up cybersecurity regulations after the [Colonial Pipeline ransomware attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_Pipeline_ransomware_attack) in May 2021. As of August 2022, they issued revised cybersecurity directives for oil and gas providers more focused on performance-based measures, following extensive input from federal regulators and private industry stakeholders.[^cite_note-164]

## Criticism and controversy

### Effectiveness of screening procedures



The logo of TSA's [red team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_team) which includes a [black swan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_swan_theory)

Undercover operations to test the effectiveness of airport screening processes are routinely carried out by the TSA's Office of Investigations,[^cite_note-Elias2010-165] TSA's [red team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_team),[^cite_note-166] and the [Department of Homeland Security](58236.md) [Inspector General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inspector_General)'s office. Some measures employed by the TSA have been accused of being ineffective and fostering a false sense of safety.[^cite_note-167][^cite_note-168] This led security expert [Bruce Schneier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Schneier) to coin the term *[security theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_theater)* to describe those measures.[^cite_note-169]

A 2004 report by the [Department of Homeland Security Office of Inspector General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Homeland_Security_Office_of_Inspector_General) found that TSA officials had collaborated with [Covenant Aviation Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covenant_Aviation_Security) (CAS) at [San Francisco International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_International_Airport) to alert screeners to undercover tests.[^cite_note-170] From August 2003 until May 2004, precise descriptions of the undercover personnel were provided to the screeners. The handing out of descriptions was then stopped, but until January 2005 screeners were still alerted whenever undercover operations were being undertaken.[^cite_note-171] When no wrongdoing on the part of CAS was found, the contract was extended for four years. Some CAS and TSA workers received disciplinary action, but none were fired.[^cite_note-sfgate2006-172][^cite_note-173]

A report on undercover operations conducted in October 2006 at [Newark Liberty International Airport](248492.md) was leaked to the press. The screeners had failed 20 of 22 undercover security tests, missing numerous guns and bombs. The [Government Accountability Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Accountability_Office) had previously pointed to repeated covert test failures by TSA personnel.[^cite_note-174][^cite_note-175] Revealing the results of covert tests is against TSA policy, and the agency responded by initiating an internal probe to discover the source of the leak.[^cite_note-176]

In July 2007, the *[Times Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Times_Union_\(Albany\))* of [Albany, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albany,_New_York) reported that TSA screeners at [Albany International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albany_International_Airport) failed multiple covert security tests conducted by the TSA. Among them was a failure to detect a fake bomb.[^cite_note-177]

In December 2010, ABC News Houston reported in an article about a man who accidentally took a forgotten gun through airport security, that "the failure rate approaches 70 percent at some major airports".[^cite_note-quinn-89]

In June 2011, TSA fired 36 screeners at the [Honolulu airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honolulu_International_Airport) for regularly allowing bags through without being inspected.[^cite_note-178]

In 2011, an artist, Geoff McGann, was detained by the TSA, arrested, and charged for wearing a watch which contained visible wiring and fuse-like elements, despite containing no explosive ingredients.[^cite_note-179]

In March 2012, American attorney Jonathan Corbett published video demonstrating a vulnerability in TSA's body scanners that would allow metallic objects to pass undetected.[^cite_note-180] TSA downplayed, though did not deny, the vulnerability,[^cite_note-181] and researchers later confirmed its existence.[^cite_note-182]

In May 2012, a report from the [Department of Homeland Security Office of Inspector General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Homeland_Security_Office_of_Inspector_General) stated that the TSA "does not have a complete understanding" of breaches at the nation's airports, with some hubs doing very little to fix or report security breaches. These findings will be presented to Congress.[^cite_note-183] Rep. [Darrell Issa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darrell_Issa), then-chairman of the [House Oversight and Government Reform Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_Oversight_and_Government_Reform_Committee), and Rep. [John Mica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Mica), then-chairman of the [House Transportation and Infrastructure Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_Transportation_and_Infrastructure_Committee), were reported in 2012 to have had several joint hearings concerning the cost and benefits of the various safety programs including full-body scanners, the [Transportation Worker Identification Credential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_Worker_Identification_Credential) (TWIC), and the behavior detection program, among others.[^cite_note-184]

A 2015 investigation by the Homeland Security Inspector General revealed that undercover investigators were able to smuggle banned items through checkpoints in 95% of their attempts.[^cite_note-185]

#### Unintended consequences of screening enhancements

Two studies by a group of [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University) researchers asserted that increased airport security may have increased road fatalities, as would-be air travelers decide to drive and are exposed to the far greater risk of dying in a car accident.[^cite_note-cornell1-186][^cite_note-cornell2-187] In 2005, the researchers looked at the immediate aftermath of the attacks of September 11, 2001, and found that the change in passenger travel modes led to 242 added driving deaths per month.[^cite_note-cornell1-186] In all, they estimated that about 1,200 driving deaths could be attributed to the short-term effects of the attacks. The study attributes the change in traveler behavior to two factors: fear of terrorist attacks and the wish to avoid the inconvenience of strict security measures; no attempt is made to estimate separately the influence of each of these two factors.

In 2007, the researchers studied the specific effects of a change to security practices instituted by the TSA in late 2002. They concluded that this change reduced the number of air travelers by 6%, and estimated that consequently, 129 more people died in car accidents in the fourth quarter of 2002.[^cite_note-cornell2-187] Extrapolating this rate of fatalities, *New York Times* contributor [Nate Silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nate_Silver) remarked that this is equivalent to "four fully loaded Boeing 737s crashing each year."[^cite_note-188] The 2007 study also noted that strict airport security hurts the airline industry; it was estimated that the 6% reduction in the number of passengers in the fourth quarter of 2002 cost the industry \$1.1 billion in lost business.[^cite_note-Blalock2007-189]

### Sexual assaults

In April 2015, NBC Denver news ran a story on two related employees coordinating amongst themselves to falsely flag attractive passengers for groping. According to NBC, while the TSA fired the employees, it took steps to protect the identity of the employees, which NBC suggested was an effort to shield them from state prosecution. The state prosecutor eventually declined to charge the individuals, as none of the unknowingly groped passengers had come forward to complain. Following the incident, Time magazine ran a story quoting a former TSA employee, who claimed groping is business as usual.[^cite_note-190][^cite_note-191]

In August 2015, a TSA agent was charged for sexually assaulting a Korean exchange student at New York LaGuardia Airport. After the woman complied with his order to go into the restroom for further screening, the agent assaulted her. TSA in a press release after firing the worker stated passengers should be aware it does not screen people after they pass through security — this despite TSA having dogs in secure areas sniffing luggage for contraband that would require a human inspection.[^cite_note-192][^cite_note-193]

In 2021, former TSA agent Jonathan Lomeli was convicted of assault after an incident where he tricked a woman into showing her breasts under the guise of it being part of a security screening.[^cite_note-194]

### Smuggling drugs and weapons

In 2012, a number of people including TSA employees were arrested in Los Angeles Airport after they were found to be a part of a drug smuggling gang.[^cite_note-195]

In 2021, a TSA employee was arrested at JFK Airport after she tried to smuggle guns through a metal detector.[^cite_note-196]

### Baggage theft



Notice of Baggage Inspection

The TSA has been criticized for an increase in baggage theft after its inception.[^cite_note-197] Reported thefts include both valuable and [dangerous goods](1476975.md), such as laptops, jewelry,[^cite_note-exposed-198] guns,[^cite_note-199] and knives.[^cite_note-pleadguilty-200] Such thefts have raised concerns that the same access might allow bombs to be placed aboard aircraft.[^cite_note-AirportInsecurity-201]

In 2004, over 17,000 claims of baggage theft were reported.[^cite_note-exposed-198] As of 2004, 60 screeners had been arrested for baggage theft,[^cite_note-exposed-198] a number which had grown to 200 screeners by 2008.[^cite_note-tipsswipe-202] 11,700 theft and damage claims were reported to the TSA in 2009, a drop from 26,500 in 2004, which was attributed to the installation of cameras and conveyor belts in airports.[^cite_note-203] A total of 25,016 thefts were reported over the five-year period from 2010 to 2014.[^cite_note-hidden-204]

As of 2011, the TSA employed about 60,000 screeners in total (counting both baggage and passenger screening)[^cite_note-205] and approximately 500 TSA agents had been fired or suspended for stealing from passenger luggage since the agency's creation in November 2001. The airports with the most reported thefts from 2010 to 2014 were [John F. Kennedy International Airport](97218.md), followed by [Los Angeles International Airport](18131.md) and [Orlando International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlando_International_Airport).[^cite_note-hidden-204]

In 2008, an investigative report by [WTAE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WTAE-TV) in Pittsburgh discovered that despite over 400 reports of baggage theft, about half of which the TSA reimbursed passengers for, not a single arrest had been made.[^cite_note-wtae-206] The TSA does not, as a matter of policy, share baggage theft reports with local police departments.[^cite_note-wtae-206]

In September 2012, ABC News interviewed former TSA agent Pythias Brown, who admitted to stealing more than \$800,000 worth of items during his employment with the agency. Brown stated that it was "very convenient to steal", and that poor morale within the agency led agents to steal from passengers.[^cite_note-207]

In September 2023, NBC Miami ran a story regarding 3 TSA employees who were arrested for grand theft after being filmed on security cameras stealing cash and other goods from handbags.[^cite_note-208]

The TSA has also been criticized for not responding properly to theft and failing to reimburse passengers for stolen goods. For example, between 2011 and 2012, passengers at [Hartsfield–Jackson Atlanta International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartsfield%E2%80%93Jackson_Atlanta_International_Airport) reported \$300,000 in property lost or damaged by the TSA. The agency only reimbursed \$35,000 of those claims.[^cite_note-209] Similar statistics were found at Jacksonville International Airport – passengers reported \$22,000 worth of goods missing or damaged over the course of 15 months. The TSA only reimbursed \$800 total of this amount.[^cite_note-210]

### Data security incidents

#### Employee records lost or stolen

In 2007, an unencrypted computer [hard drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_drive) containing [Social Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Security_Administration) numbers, bank data, and payroll information for about 100,000 employees was lost or stolen from TSA headquarters. Kip Hawley alerted TSA employees to the loss, and apologized for it. The agency asked the [FBI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI) to investigate. There were no reports that the data was later misused.[^cite_note-211][^cite_note-212]

#### Unsecured website

&#x20;In 2007, [Christopher Soghoian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Soghoian), a blogger and security researcher, said that a TSA website was collecting private passenger information in an unsecured manner, exposing passengers to [identity theft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_theft).[^cite_note-213] The website allowed passengers to dispute their inclusion on the [No Fly List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Fly_List). The TSA fixed the website several days after the press picked up the story.[^cite_note-214] The U.S. House [Committee on Oversight and Government Reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_on_Oversight_and_Government_Reform) investigated the matter,[^cite_note-215] and said the website had operated insecurely for more than four months, during which more than 247 people had submitted personal information.[^cite_note-216] The report said the TSA manager who awarded the contract for creating the website was a high-school friend and former employee of the owner of the firm that received the contract.[^cite_note-217] It noted:

> &#x20;Neither Desyne nor the Technical Lead on the traveler redress website have been sanctioned by TSA for their roles in the deployment of an insecure website. TSA continues to pay Desyne to host and maintain two major web-based information systems. TSA has taken no steps to discipline the Technical Lead, who still holds a senior program management position at TSA.[^cite_note-218]

In December 2009, someone within the TSA posted a sensitive manual titled "Screening Management [SOP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Operating_Procedures)" on secret airport screening guidelines to an obscure URL on the FedBizOpps website. The manual was taken down quickly, but the breach raised questions about whether security practices had been compromised.[^cite_note-219] Five TSA employees were placed on [administrative leave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Administrative_leave) over the manual's publication, which, while [redacted](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/redact), had its [redaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanitization_\(classified_information\)) easily removed.[^cite_note-220]

### Other criticisms

Other common criticisms of the agency have also included assertions that TSA employees have slept on the job,[^cite_note-221][^cite_note-222][^cite_note-223][^cite_note-224] bypassed security checks,[^cite_note-225] and failed to use good judgment and common sense.[^cite_note-226][^cite_note-227][^cite_note-228]

TSA agents are also accused and convicted of having mistreated passengers; having sexually harassed passengers;[^cite_note-229][^cite_note-230][^cite_note-231][^cite_note-232] having used invasive screening procedures, including touching the genitals, along with those of children;[^cite_note-233] misusing body scanners to ogle female passengers;[^cite_note-234] having searched passengers or their belongings for items other than weapons or explosives;[^cite_note-235] and having stolen from passengers.[^cite_note-wtae-206][^cite_note-236][^cite_note-237][^cite_note-238][^cite_note-239][^cite_note-240][^cite_note-241][^cite_note-242] The TSA fired 28 agents and suspended 15 others after an investigation determined they failed to scan checked baggage for explosives.[^cite_note-243]

The TSA was also accused of having spent lavishly on events unrelated to airport security,[^cite_note-244] having wasted money in hiring,[^cite_note-Traveler0307-245] and having had [conflicts of interest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conflict_of_interest).[^cite_note-246]

The TSA was accused of having performed poorly at the [2009 Presidential Inauguration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_inauguration_of_Barack_Obama) viewing areas, which left thousands of ticket holders excluded from the event in overcrowded conditions, while those who had arrived before the checkpoints were in place avoided screening altogether.[^cite_note-247][^cite_note-248]

In 2013, dozens of TSA workers were fired or suspended for illegal gambling at [Pittsburgh International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_International_Airport),[^cite_note-249] and eight TSA workers were arrested in connection with stolen parking passes at [Dallas/Fort Worth International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallas/Fort_Worth_International_Airport).

A 2013 [GAO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GAO) report showed a 26% increase in misconduct among TSA employees between 2010 and 2012, from 2,691 cases to 3,408.[^cite_note-250] Another GAO report said that there is no evidence that the Screening of Passengers by Observation Techniques (SPOT) behavioral detection program, with an annual budget of hundreds of millions of dollars, is effective.[^cite_note-251]

A 2013 report by the Department of Homeland Security Inspector General's Office charged that TSA was using criminal investigators to do the job of lower-paid employees, wasting millions of dollars a year.[^cite_note-252]

On December 3, 2013, the United States House of Representatives passed the [Transportation Security Acquisition Reform Act (H.R. 2719; 113th Congress)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_Security_Acquisition_Reform_Act_\(H.R._2719;_113th_Congress\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) in response to criticism of the TSA's acquisition process as wasteful, costly, and ineffective.[^cite_note-techreform1-253][^cite_note-18_July_2013-254] If the bill became law, it would require the TSA to develop a comprehensive technology acquisition plan and present regular reports to Congress about its successes and failures to adhere to this plan. An April 2013 report from the [Department of Homeland Security Office of Inspector General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Homeland_Security_Office_of_Inspector_General) indicated that the TSA had 17,000 items with an estimated cost of \$185.7 million stored in its warehouses on May 31, 2012.[^cite_note-oigreportonwarehouse-255] The auditors found that "TSA stored unusable or obsolete equipment, maintained inappropriate safety stock levels, and did not develop an inventory management process that systematically deploys equipment."[^cite_note-oigreportonwarehouse-255]

In January 2014, [Jason Edward Harrington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jason_Edward_Harrington), a former TSA screener at [O'Hare International Airport](248449.md), said that fellow staff members assigned to review body scan images of airline passengers routinely joked about fliers' weight, attractiveness, and penis and breast sizes. According to Harrington, screeners would alert each other to attractive female passengers with the code phrase "Hotel Papa" so that staff would have an opportunity to view the passengers' nude form in body scanner monitors and retaliated against rude flyers by delaying them at the checkpoint. TSA Administrator John Pistole responded by saying that all the scanners had been replaced and the screening rooms were disabled. He did not deny that the behaviors described by Harrington took place.[^cite_note-256]

In May 2016, actress [Susan Sarandon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Sarandon) claimed that during the entire time of the Bush administration she was "harassed every time I came into the country". She said that she hired two lawyers to contact the TSA to determine why she had been targeted but that she assumed it was because she was critical of the Bush administration. She said the harassment stopped after her attorneys followed up a second time with the TSA.[^cite_note-257]

In July 2018, a case heard in the [Third Circuit Appeals Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Third_Circuit) ruled that TSA agents are not "investigative or law enforcement officers" and thus are not liable under the [Federal Tort Claims Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Tort_Claims_Act) (FTCA). The case extended from a woman who had been detained and arrested by TSA in 2006 but later the criminal charges were acquitted in court; she had sought damages under the FTCA for damages related to the false arrest and related matters.[^cite_note-258]

An [ACLU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACLU) study found that the TSA disproportionately targets Arabs, Muslims and Latinos, despite DHS claims to the contrary.[^cite_note-259]

### Public opinion

A CBS telephone poll of 1137 people published on November 15, 2010, found that 81% percent of those polled approved TSA's use of full-body scans.[^cite_note-260] An ABC/Washington Post poll conducted by Langer Associates and released November 22, 2010, found that 64% of Americans favored the full-body X-ray scanners, but that 50% think the "enhanced" pat-downs go too far; 37% felt so strongly. Besides, the poll states opposition is lowest among those who fly less than once a year.[^cite_note-261] A later poll by [Zogby International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zogby_International) found 61% of likely voters oppose the new measures by TSA.[^cite_note-262] In 2012, a poll conducted by the Frequent Business Traveler organization found that 56% of frequent fliers were "not satisfied" with the job the TSA was doing. 57% rated the TSA as doing a "poor job," and 34% rated it "fair." Only 1% of those surveyed rated the agency's work as excellent.[^cite_note-263] On the contrary, a 2018 Rasmussen Reports telephone poll of 1,000 Adult Americans found that 45% of respondents had an opinion of the TSA ranging from somewhat favorable to very favorable, while 39% had an unfavorable opinion.[^cite_note-264]

### Investigations of the TSA

In 2013, The Office of Inspector General published a report titled "TSA's Actions Insufficient to Address Inspector General Recommendations to Improve its Office of Inspection". The report touched upon several topics of misconduct but the main focus of the report was of the TSA criminal investigators who received a premium on their pay despite not meeting the minimum qualification to be eligible for this pay.[^cite_note-265]

The TSA Office of Accountability Inspection Act of 2015 published by the Committee of Commerce, Science, and Transportation, was based on a report of an investigation that found issues with the TSA. The act also followed up the Office of Inspector General's 2013 report, mandating that the TSA should comply with Federal Regulation and correct the wage of the TSA's Criminal Investigators.[^cite_note-266] Had no action been taken this misuse of funds was estimated to cost taxpayers, in a span of five years, \$17 million.[^cite_note-267]

In response, the TSA contracted a consulting firm to assist the TSA with the Office of Inspector General recommendations. However, the Office of Inspector Generals has found the TSA's response lacking as they have yet to fix a majority of the issues brought up in the report.[^cite_note-268]

### Calls for abolition

Numerous groups and figures have called for the abolition of the TSA in its current form by persons and groups which include Sen. [Rand Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rand_Paul)[^cite_note-269] (R-KY), Rep. [John Mica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Mica)[^cite_note-270] (R-FL), The [Cato Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cato_Institute),[^cite_note-271] [Downsize DC Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downsize_DC_Foundation),[^cite_note-272] [FreedomWorks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FreedomWorks),[^cite_note-273] and opinion columnists from *[Forbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forbes),*[^cite_note-274] *[Fox News](11121.md)*,[^cite_note-275] *[National Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Review),*[^cite_note-276] *[USA Today](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USA_Today)*,[^cite_note-277] *[Vox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vox_\(website\))*,[^cite_note-278] *[The Washington Examiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Examiner)*,[^cite_note-279] and *[The Washington Post](102226.md)*.[^cite_note-280]

The TSA's critics frequently cite the agency as "ineffective, invasive, incompetent, inexcusably costly, or all four" as their reasons for seeking its abolition.[^cite_note-281] Those seeking to abolish the TSA have cited the improved efficacy and cost of screening provided by qualified private companies in compliance with federal guidelines.[^cite_note-282]
