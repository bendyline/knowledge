The **United States dollar** ([symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar_sign): **\$**; [currency code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_4217): **USD**[^cite_note-7]) is the official [currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency) of the [United States](3434750.md) (U.S.) and [several other countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_use_of_the_U.S._dollar). The U.S. [Coinage Act of 1792](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinage_Act_of_1792) established the [dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar) as the base [unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_account), with each dollar divided into 100 [cents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cent_\(currency\)) ([symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cent_sign): **¢**).[^cite_note-8] U.S. banknotes are issued in the form of [Federal Reserve Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Note), popularly called greenbacks due to their predominantly green color.

The U.S. dollar was originally defined under a [bimetallic standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bimetallism) of 371.25 grains (24.057 g) (0.7734375 troy ounces) fine silver or, from [1834](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinage_Act_of_1834),[^cite_note-\:4-9] 23.22 grains (1.505 g) fine gold, or \$20.67 per [troy ounce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troy_ounce). The [Gold Standard Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_Standard_Act) of 1900 linked the dollar solely to gold. From 1934, its equivalence to gold was revised to \$35 per troy ounce. In 1971 all links to gold were repealed.[^cite_note-10] The U.S. dollar became an important international [reserve currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_currency) after the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War), and displaced British [sterling](270673.md) as the world's primary [reserve currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_currency) by the [Bretton Woods Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bretton_Woods_Agreement) towards the end of the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War). The dollar is the [most widely used currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Template\:Most_traded_currencies) in [international transactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_transactions),[^cite_note-11] and a [free-floating currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free-floating_currency). It is also the official currency in several countries and the [*de facto* currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_facto_currency) in many others,[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13] with [Federal Reserve Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Notes) (and, in a few cases, U.S. coins) used in circulation.

The [monetary policy of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetary_policy_of_the_United_States) is conducted by the [Federal Reserve System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_System), which acts as the nation's [central bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_bank). As of February 10, 2021, physical currency in circulation amounted to US\$2.10 [trillion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trillion), \$2.05 trillion of which is in Federal Reserve Notes (the remaining \$50 billion is in the form of [coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coins_of_the_United_States_dollar) and older-style [United States Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Note)).[^cite_note-14] As of January 1, 2025, the Federal Reserve estimated that the total amount of currency in circulation was approximately US\$2.37 [trillion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trillion).[^cite_note-15]

## Overview

### In the Constitution

[Article I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_One_of_the_United_States_Constitution), [Section 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_One_of_the_United_States_Constitution#Section_8:_Powers_of_Congress) of the [U.S. Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Constitution) provides that [Congress](31756.md) has the power "to [coin money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coining_\(mint\))".[^cite_note-\:1-16] Laws implementing this power are currently codified in [Title 31](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_31_of_the_United_States_Code) of the [U.S. Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Code), under Section 5112, which prescribes the forms in which the United States dollars should be issued. These coins are both designated in the section as [legal tender](267793.md) in payment of debts. The [Sacagawea dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacagawea_dollar) is one example of the [copper alloy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_alloy) dollar, in contrast to the [American Silver Eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Silver_Eagle) which is pure [silver](27119.md). Section 5112 also provides for the [minting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minting_coins) and [issuance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_creation) of other coins, which have values ranging from [one cent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-cent_coin) ([U.S. Penny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Penny)) to 100 dollars.[^cite_note-\:0-17] These other coins are more fully described in [Coins of the United States dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coins_of_the_United_States_dollar).

[Article I, Section 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_One_of_the_United_States_Constitution#Section_9:_Limits_on_Congress) of the Constitution provides that "a regular Statement and Account of the Receipts and Expenditures of all public Money shall be published from time to time",[^cite_note-18] which is further specified by Section 331 of Title 31 of the U.S. Code.[^cite_note-19] The sums of money reported in the "Statements" are currently expressed in U.S. dollars, thus the U.S. dollar may be described as the [unit of account](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_account) of the United States.[^cite_note-20] "Dollar" is one of the first words of Section 9, in which the term refers to the [Spanish milled dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_milled_dollar), or the coin worth eight [Spanish reales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_real).

### Coinage Act

&#x20;In 1792, the [U.S. Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2nd_United_States_Congress) passed the [Coinage Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinage_Act_of_1792), of which Section 9 authorized the production of various coins, including:[^cite_note-\:3-6]<sup>: 248 </sup>

> **Dollars or Units**—each to be of the value of a Spanish milled dollar as the same is now current, and to contain three hundred and seventy-one [grains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain_\(unit\)) and four sixteenth parts of a grain of pure, or four hundred and sixteen grains of standard silver.

Section 20 of the Act designates the United States dollar as the [unit of currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency) of the United States:[^cite_note-\:3-6]<sup>: 250–1 </sup>

> \[T]he money of account of the United States shall be expressed in dollars, or units...and that all accounts in the public offices and all proceedings in the courts of the United States shall be kept and had in conformity to this regulation.

### Decimal units

Unlike the [Spanish milled dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_milled_dollar), the [Continental Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Congress) and the Coinage Act prescribed a [decimal system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal) of units to go with the unit dollar, as follows: the **mill**, or one-thousandth of a dollar; the **cent**, or one-hundredth of a dollar; and the **disme** or **dime**, or one-tenth of a dollar.[^cite_note-contcongress1-1][^cite_note-21] The current relevance of these units:

- Only the [**cent**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cent_\(currency\)) (**¢**) is used as an everyday division of the dollar, with the ubiquitous exception of vehicle fuel pricing.

- [**Dime**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dime_\(United_States_coin\)) is used solely as the name of the [coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dime_\(United_States_coin\)) with the value of ten cents.

- The [**mill**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mill_\(currency\)) (**₥**) is relatively unknown but before the middle of the 20th century was familiar in matters of [sales taxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sales_taxes_in_the_United_States). It is ubiquitous in prices of [gasoline and diesel fuels](8032596.md), which are usually in the form of \$*xx.xx*9 per [gallon](12882.md) (e.g., \$3.599, commonly written as \$3.59+9⁄10).

  [^cite_note-22]

  [^cite_note-\:2-23]

The Spanish peso, or dollar, was historically divided into eight [**reales**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_real) (colloquially, *bits*) – hence *pieces of eight*. Americans also learned counting in non-decimal *bits* of 12+1⁄2 cents before 1857 when Mexican *bits* were more frequently encountered than American cents; in fact this practice survived in [New York Stock Exchange](21560.md) quotations until 2001.[^cite_note-2bits-24][^cite_note-25]

In 1854, [Secretary of the Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_the_Treasury) [James Guthrie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Guthrie_\(American_politician\)) proposed creating \$100, \$50, and \$25 gold coins, to be referred to as a *[union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_\(United_States_coin\))*, *[half union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_union)*, and *quarter union*, respectively,[^cite_note-26] thus implying a denomination of 1 Union = \$100. However, no such coins were ever struck, and only patterns for the \$50 half union exist.

When currently issued in circulating form, denominations less than or equal to a dollar are emitted as [U.S. coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._coins), while denominations greater than or equal to a dollar are emitted as [Federal Reserve Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Note), disregarding the following special cases:

- Gold coins issued for [circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulation_\(currency\)) until the 1930s, up to the value of \$20 (known as the *[double eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_eagle)*)

- Bullion or commemorative [gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_coin), [silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_coin), [platinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platinum_coin), and [palladium coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palladium_coin) valued up to \$100 as legal tender (though worth far more as [bullion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullion)).

- Civil War paper currency issue in denominations below \$1, i.e. fractional currency, sometimes pejoratively referred to as *[shinplasters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinplaster)*.

### Etymology

Further information: [Dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar)

In the 16th century, Count [Hieronymus Schlick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hieronymus_Schlick) of [Bohemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemia) began minting coins known as *joachimstalers*, named for [Joachimstal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%A1chymov), the valley in which the silver was mined. In turn, the valley's name is titled after Saint [Joachim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joachim), whereby *thal* or *tal*, a cognate of the English word *[dale](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/dale#English)*, is [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language) for 'valley.'[^cite_note-NatGeo-27] The *joachimstaler* was later shortened to the German *[taler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thaler)*, a word that eventually found its way into many languages, including:[^cite_note-NatGeo-27] *tolar* ([Czech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_language), [Slovak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovak_language) and [Slovenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovenian_language)); *daler* ([Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_language) and [Swedish](26689.md)); *talar* ([Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_language)); *dalar* and *daler* ([Norwegian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_language)); *daler* or *daalder* ([Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language)); *[talari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_talari)* ([Ethiopian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Ethiopia)); *tallér* ([Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_language)); *tallero* ([Italian](14708.md)); *دولار* ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language)); and *[dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar)* ([English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language)).

Though the [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_people) pioneered in modern-day [New York](8210131.md) in the 17th century the use and the counting of money in silver dollars in the form of German-Dutch *[reichsthalers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichsthaler)* and native Dutch *[leeuwendaalders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeuwendaalder)* ('lion dollars'), it was the ubiquitous [Spanish American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_America) eight-real coin which became exclusively known as the *dollar* since the 18th century.[^cite_note-28]

### Nicknames

See also: [Slang terms for money § United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slang_terms_for_money#United_States)

The [colloquialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colloquialism) *[**buck(s)**](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/buck)* (much like the British *quid* for the [pound sterling](270673.md)) is often used to refer to dollars of various nations, including the U.S. dollar. This term, dating to the 18th century, may have originated with the colonial [leather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leather) trade, or it may also have originated from a [poker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poker) term.[^cite_note-29]

[***Greenback***](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Note) is another nickname, originally applied specifically to the 19th-century [Demand Note](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demand_Note) dollars, which were printed black and green on the backside, created by [Abraham Lincoln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln) to finance the [North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_\(American_Civil_War\)) for the [Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War).[^cite_note-30] It is still used to refer to the U.S. dollar (but not to the dollars of other countries). The term *greenback* is also used by the financial press in other countries, such as [Australia](4689264.md),[^cite_note-BI-31] [New Zealand](4913064.md),[^cite_note-MW-32] [South Africa](17416221.md),[^cite_note-Reuters-33] and [India](14533.md).[^cite_note-BT-34]

Other well-known names of the dollar as a whole in denominations include ***greenmail***, ***green***, and ***dead presidents***, the latter of which referring to the deceased presidents pictured on most bills. Dollars in general have also been known as ***bones*** (e.g. "twenty bones" = \$20). The newer designs, with portraits displayed in the main body of the obverse (rather than in [cameo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameo_\(carving\)) insets), upon paper color-coded by denomination, are sometimes referred to as ***bigface*** notes or ***[Monopoly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopoly_\(game\)) money***.

***[Piastre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piastre)*** was the original French word for the U.S. dollar, used for example in the French text of the [Louisiana Purchase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_Purchase). Though the U.S. dollar is called *dollar* in Modern French, the term *piastre* is still used among the speakers of [Cajun French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cajun_French) and [New England French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England_French), as well as speakers in [Haiti](13373.md) and other [French Caribbean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Caribbean) islands.

Nicknames specific to denomination:

- The [quarter dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarter_\(United_States_coin\)) coin is known as *two bits*, alluding the dollar's origins as the "piece of eight" (bits or *reales*).

  [^cite_note-2bits-24]

- The [\$1 bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_one-dollar_bill) is nicknamed *buck* or *single*.

- The infrequently used [\$2 bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_two-dollar_bill) is sometimes called *deuce*, *Tom*, or *Jefferson* (after [Thomas Jefferson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson)).

- The [\$5 bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_five-dollar_bill) is sometimes called *Lincoln* (after [Abraham Lincoln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln)), *fin*, *fiver*, or *five-spot*.

- The [\$10 bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_ten-dollar_bill) is sometimes called *sawbuck*, *ten-spot*, or *Hamilton* (after [Alexander Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Hamilton)).

- The [\$20 bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_twenty-dollar_bill) is sometimes called *double sawbuck*, *Jackson* (after [Andrew Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Jackson)), or *[double eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_eagle)*.

- The [\$50 bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_fifty-dollar_bill) is sometimes called a *yardstick*, or a *grant*, after President [Ulysses S. Grant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_S._Grant).

- The [\$100 bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_one-hundred-dollar_bill) is called *Benjamin*, *Benji*, *Ben*, or *Franklin*, referring to its portrait of [Benjamin Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin). Other nicknames include *C-note* (C being the [Roman numeral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_numerals) for 100), *century note*, or *bill* (e.g. *two bills* = \$200).

- Amounts or multiples of \$1,000 are sometimes called *[grand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1000_\(number\)#Notation)* in colloquial speech, abbreviated in written form to *G*, *K*, or *k* (from [*kilo*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilo-); e.g. \$10k = \$10,000). Likewise, a *large* or *stack* can also refer to a multiple of \$1,000 (e.g. "fifty large" = \$50,000).

### Dollar sign

Main article: [Dollar sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar_sign)



[Spanish silver eight-real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_real) or [peso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peso) of 1768

The symbol [\$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar), usually written before the numerical amount, is used for the U.S. dollar (as well as for many other currencies). The sign was perhaps the result of a late 18th-century evolution of the [scribal abbreviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scribal_abbreviation) ***p<sup>s</sup>*** for the [peso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peso), the common name for the Spanish dollars that were in wide circulation in the [New World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_World) from the 16th to the 19th centuries. The ***p*** and the ***s*** eventually came to be written over each other giving rise to ***\$***.[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38]

Another popular explanation is that it is derived from the [Pillars of Hercules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pillars_of_Hercules) on the [Spanish coat of arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_coat_of_arms) of the Spanish dollar. These Pillars of Hercules on the silver Spanish dollar coins take the form of two vertical bars (**||**) and a swinging cloth band in the shape of an ***S***.[^cite_note-39]

Yet another explanation suggests that the dollar sign was formed from the capital letters ***U*** and ***S*** written or printed one on top of the other. This theory, popularized by novelist [Ayn Rand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayn_Rand) in *[Atlas Shrugged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_Shrugged)*,[^cite_note-40] does not consider the fact that the symbol was already in use before the formation of the United States.[^cite_note-41]

## History

See also: [History of the United States dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_United_States_dollar)

### Origins: the Spanish dollar

The U.S. dollar was introduced at par with the Spanish-American silver dollar (or *Spanish peso*, *Spanish milled dollar*, *eight-real coin*, *piece-of-eight*). The latter was produced from the rich silver mine output of [Spanish America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_America), was minted in [Mexico City](18987.md), [Potosí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potos%C3%AD) (Bolivia), [Lima](85423.md) (Peru), and elsewhere, and was in wide circulation throughout the Americas, Asia, and Europe from the 16th to the 19th centuries. The minting of machine-milled Spanish dollars since 1732 boosted its worldwide reputation as a trade coin and positioned it to be the model for the new currency of the United States.

Even after the [United States Mint](358818.md) commenced issuing coins in 1792, locally minted *dollars* and *cents* were less abundant in circulation than [Spanish American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_America) *pesos* and *reales*; hence Spanish, Mexican, and American dollars all remained legal tender in the United States until the [Coinage Act of 1857](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinage_Act_of_1857). In particular, colonists' familiarity with the Spanish two-*real quarter peso* was the reason for issuing a quasi-decimal [25-cent quarter dollar coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarter_\(United_States_coin\)) rather than a 20-cent coin.

For the relationship between the [Spanish dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_dollar) and the individual state colonial currencies, see [Connecticut pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connecticut_pound), [Delaware pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware_pound), [Georgia pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgia_pound), [Maryland pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland_pound), [Massachusetts pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_pound), [New Hampshire pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Hampshire_pound), [New Jersey pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_pound), [New York pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_pound), [North Carolina pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Carolina_pound), [Pennsylvania pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania_pound), [Rhode Island pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhode_Island_pound), [South Carolina pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Carolina_pound), and [Virginia pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_pound).

### Coinage Act of 1792

See also: [Coinage Act of 1792](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinage_Act_of_1792)



[Alexander Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Hamilton) finalized the details of the 1792 Coinage Act and the establishment of the U.S. Mint.

On July 6, 1785, the [Continental Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Congress) resolved that the money unit of the United States, the dollar, would contain 375.64 [grains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain_\(unit\)) of fine silver; on August 8, 1786, the Continental Congress continued that definition and further resolved that the money of account, corresponding with the division of coins, would proceed in a [decimal ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimalisation), with the sub-units being mills at 1⁄1000 of a dollar, cents at 1⁄100 of a dollar, and dimes at 1⁄10 of a dollar.[^cite_note-contcongress1-1]

After the adoption of the [United States Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Constitution), the U.S. dollar was defined by the [Coinage Act of 1792](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinage_Act_of_1792). It specified a "dollar" based on the [Spanish milled dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_dollar) to contain 371+4⁄16 [grains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain_\(unit\)) of fine silver, or 416.0 grains (26.96 g) of "standard silver" of fineness 371.25/416 = 89.24%; as well as an "eagle" to contain 247+4⁄8 grains of fine gold, or 270.0 grains (17.50 g) of 22 [karat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karat) or 91.67% fine gold.[^cite_note-42] [Alexander Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Hamilton) arrived at these numbers based on a treasury assay of the average fine silver content of a selection of worn Spanish dollars, which came out to be 371 grains. Combined with the prevailing gold-silver ratio of 15, the standard for gold was calculated at 371/15 = 24.73 grains fine gold or 26.98 grains 22K gold. Rounding the latter to 27.0 grains finalized the dollar's standard to 24.75 grains of fine gold or 24.75 × 15 = 371.25 grains = 24.0566 grams = 0.7735 troy ounces of fine silver.

The same coinage act also set the value of an eagle at 10 dollars, and the dollar at 1⁄10 eagle. It called for silver coins in denominations of 1, 1⁄2, 1⁄4, 1⁄10, and 1⁄20 dollar, as well as gold coins in denominations of 1, 1⁄2 and 1⁄4 eagle. The value of gold or silver contained in the dollar was then converted into relative value in the economy for the buying and selling of goods. This allowed the value of things to remain fairly constant over time, except for the influx and outflux of gold and silver in the nation's economy.[^cite_note-43]

Though a [Spanish dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_dollar) freshly minted after 1772 theoretically contained 417.7 grains of silver of fineness 130/144 (or 377.1 grains fine silver), reliable assays of the period in fact confirmed a fine silver content of 370.95 grains (24.037 g) for the average Spanish dollar in circulation.[^cite_note-44] The new U.S. silver dollar of 371.25 grains (24.057 g) therefore compared favorably and was received at par with the Spanish dollar for foreign payments, and after 1803 the [United States Mint](358818.md) had to suspend making this coin out of its limited resources since it failed to stay in domestic circulation. It was only after Mexican independence in 1821 when their peso's fine silver content of 377.1 grains was firmly upheld, which the U.S. later had to compete with using a heavier 378.0 grains (24.49 g) [Trade dollar coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_dollar_\(United_States_coin\)).

### Design

The early currency of the United States did not exhibit faces of presidents, as is the custom now;[^cite_note-45] although today, by law, only the portrait of a deceased individual may appear on United States currency.[^cite_note-46] In fact, the newly formed government was against having portraits of leaders on the currency, a practice compared to the policies of European monarchs.[^cite_note-47] The currency as we know it today did not get the faces they currently have until after the early 20th century; before that "heads" side of coinage used profile faces and striding, seated, and standing figures from Greek and Roman mythology and composite Native Americans. The last coins to be converted to profiles of historic Americans were the dime (1946), the half Dollar (1948), and the Dollar (1971).

### Continental currency

See also: [Continental currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_currency)



Continental one third dollar bill (obverse)

After the [American Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolution), the [Thirteen Colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies) became independent. Freed from British monetary regulations, they each issued [£sd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C2%A3sd) paper money to pay for military expenses. The [Continental Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Congress) also began issuing "Continental Currency" denominated in Spanish dollars. For its value relative to states' currencies, see [Early American currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_American_currency#Continental_currency).

Continental currency [depreciated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depreciation_\(currency\)) badly during the war, giving rise to the famous phrase "not worth a continental".[^cite_note-48] A primary problem was that monetary policy was not coordinated between Congress and the states, which continued to issue bills of credit. Additionally, neither Congress nor the governments of the several states had the will or the means to retire the bills from circulation through taxation or the sale of bonds.[^cite_note-49] The currency was ultimately replaced by the silver dollar at the rate of 1 silver dollar to 1000 continental dollars. This resulted in the clause "No state shall... make anything but gold and silver coin a tender in payment of debts" being written into the [United States Constitution article 1, section 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_One_of_the_United_States_Constitution#Section_10:_Limits_on_the_States).

### Silver and gold standards, 19th century

From implementation of the [1792 Mint Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinage_Act_of_1792) to the 1900 implementation of the [gold standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard), the dollar was on a [bimetallic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bimetallism) silver-and-gold standard, defined as either 371.25 [grains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain_\(unit\)) (24.056 g) of fine silver or 24.75 grains of fine gold (gold-silver ratio 15).

Subsequent to the [Coinage Act of 1834](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinage_Act_of_1834) the dollar's fine gold equivalent was revised to 23.2 grains; it was slightly adjusted to 23.22 grains (1.505 g) in 1837 (gold-silver ratio ≈16). The same act also resolved the difficulty in minting the "standard silver" of 89.24% fineness by revising the dollar's alloy to 412.5 grains, 90% silver, still containing 371.25 grains fine silver. Gold was also revised to 90% fineness: 25.8 grains gross, 23.22 grains fine gold.

Following the rise in the price of silver during the [California Gold Rush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Gold_Rush) and the disappearance of circulating silver coins, the [Coinage Act of 1853](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinage_Act_of_1853) reduced the standard for silver coins less than \$1 from 412.5 grains to 384 grains (24.9 g), 90% silver per 100 cents (slightly revised to 25.0 g, 90% silver in 1873). The Act also limited the [free silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_silver) right of individuals to convert [bullion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullion) into only one coin, the silver dollar of 412.5 grains; smaller coins of lower standard can only be produced by the [United States Mint](358818.md) using its own bullion.

Summary and [links to coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coins_of_the_United_States_dollar) issued in the 19th century:

- In base metal: [1/2 cent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_cent_\(United_States_coin\)), [1 cent](164092.md), [5 cents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel_\(United_States_coin\)).

- In silver: [half dime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_dime), [dime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dime_\(United_States_coin\)), [quarter dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarter_\(United_States_coin\)), [half dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_dollar_\(United_States_coin\)), [silver dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar_coin_\(United_States\)).

- In gold: [gold \$1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_dollar), [\$2.50 quarter eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarter_eagle), [\$5 half eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_eagle), [\$10 eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eagle_\(United_States_coin\)), [\$20 double eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_eagle).

- Less common denominations: [bronze 2 cents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-cent_piece_\(United_States_coin\)), [nickel 3 cents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-cent_nickel), [silver 3 cents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-cent_silver), [silver 20 cents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty-cent_piece_\(United_States_coin\)), [gold \$3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-dollar_piece).

### Note issues, 19th century



Series of 1917 [\$1 United States Note](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_one-dollar_bill)

In order to finance the [War of 1812](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_1812), Congress authorized the issuance of [Treasury Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasury_Note_\(19th_century\)), interest-bearing short-term debt that could be used to pay public dues. While they were intended to serve as debt, they did function "to a limited extent" as money. Treasury Notes were again printed to help resolve the reduction in public revenues resulting from the [Panic of 1837](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panic_of_1837) and the [Panic of 1857](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panic_of_1857), as well as to help finance the [Mexican–American War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican%E2%80%93American_War) and the [Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War).

Paper money was issued again in 1862 without the backing of precious metals due to the [Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War). In addition to Treasury Notes, Congress in 1861 authorized the Treasury to borrow \$50 million in the form of [Demand Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demand_Notes), which did not bear interest but could be redeemed on demand for precious metals. However, by December 1861, the [Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_\(American_Civil_War\)) government's supply of specie was outstripped by demand for redemption and they were forced to suspend redemption temporarily. In February 1862 Congress passed the [Legal Tender Act of 1862](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_Tender_Cases), issuing [United States Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Notes), which were not redeemable on demand and bore no interest, but were [legal tender](267793.md), meaning that creditors had to accept them at face value for any payment except for import tariffs and interest on public debts. However, silver and gold coins continued to be issued, resulting in the depreciation of the newly printed notes through [Gresham's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gresham's_law). In 1869, the Supreme Court ruled in [Hepburn v. Griswold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepburn_v._Griswold) that Congress could not require creditors to accept United States Notes, but overturned that ruling the next year in the Legal Tender Cases. In 1875, Congress passed the [Specie Payment Resumption Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specie_Payment_Resumption_Act), requiring the Treasury to allow U.S. Notes to be redeemed for gold after January 1, 1879.

### Gold standard, 20th century



Gold [double eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_eagle) (\$20 coin), 1907

Though the dollar came under the [gold standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard) de jure only after 1900, the [bimetallic era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bimetallism) was ended de facto when the [Coinage Act of 1873](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinage_Act_of_1873) suspended the minting of the standard [silver dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar_coin_\(United_States\)) of 412.5 Troy [grains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain_\(unit\)) = 26.73 g; 0.859 ozt, the only fully legal tender coin that individuals could convert bullion into in unlimited (or [Free silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_silver)) quantities,[^cite_note-50] and right at the onset of the [silver rush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_rush) from the [Comstock Lode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comstock_Lode) in the 1870s. This was the so-called "Crime of '73".

The [Gold Standard Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_Standard_Act) of 1900 repealed the U.S. dollar's historic link to silver and defined it solely as 23.22 grains (1.505 g) of fine gold (or \$20.67 per [troy ounce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troy_ounce) of 480 grains). In 1933, gold coins were confiscated by [Executive Order 6102](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_6102) under [Franklin D. Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt), and in 1934 the standard was changed to \$35 per troy ounce fine gold, or 13.71 grains (0.888 g) per dollar.

After 1968 a series of revisions to the gold peg was implemented, culminating in the [Nixon Shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nixon_Shock) of August 15, 1971, which suddenly ended the convertibility of dollars to gold. The U.S. dollar has since floated freely on the [foreign exchange markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_exchange_market).

### Federal Reserve Notes, 20th century to present

See also: [Federal Reserve Note](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Note)



Obverse of a rare 1934 \$500 Federal Reserve Note, featuring a portrait of President [William McKinley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_McKinley)



Reverse of a \$500 Federal Reserve Note

Congress continued to issue paper money after the Civil War, the latest of which is the [Federal Reserve Note](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Note) that was authorized by the [Federal Reserve Act of 1913](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Act_of_1913). Since the discontinuation of all other types of notes ([gold certificates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_certificate_\(United_States\)) in 1933, [silver certificates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_certificate_\(United_States\)) in 1963, and [United States Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Note) in 1971), U.S. dollar notes have since been issued exclusively as Federal Reserve Notes.

### Emergence as reserve currency

Main article: [International use of the U.S. dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_use_of_the_U.S._dollar)



[John Maynard Keynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes) (right) and [Harry Dexter White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Dexter_White) at the inaugural meeting of the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md) in 1946. They were instrumental in drafting the provisions of the post-war global financial system.

The U.S. dollar first emerged as an important international [reserve currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_currency) in the 1920s, displacing the British [pound sterling](270673.md) as it emerged from the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War) relatively unscathed and since the United States was a significant recipient of wartime gold inflows. After the United States emerged as an even stronger global [superpower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superpower) during the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War), the [Bretton Woods Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bretton_Woods_Agreement) of 1944 established the U.S. dollar as the world's primary reserve currency and the only post-war currency linked to gold. Despite all links to gold being severed in 1971, the dollar continues to be the world's foremost reserve currency for international trade to this day.

The Bretton Woods Agreement of 1944 also defined the post-World War II monetary order and relations among modern-day [independent states](31769.md), by setting up a system of rules, institutions, and procedures to regulate the [international monetary system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_monetary_system). The agreement founded the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md) and other institutions of the modern-day [World Bank Group](36752.md), establishing the infrastructure for conducting international payments and accessing the global capital markets using the U.S. dollar.

The [monetary policy of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetary_policy_of_the_United_States) is conducted by the [Federal Reserve System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_System), which acts as the nation's [central bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_bank). It was founded in 1913 under the [Federal Reserve Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Act) in order to furnish an elastic currency for the United States and to supervise its banking system, particularly in the aftermath of the [Panic of 1907](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panic_of_1907).

For most of the post-war period, the [U.S. government](195149.md) has financed its own spending by borrowing heavily from the dollar-lubricated global capital markets, in debts denominated in its own currency and at minimal interest rates. This ability to borrow heavily without facing a significant [balance of payments crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balance_of_payments_crisis) has been described as the [United States](3434750.md)'s [exorbitant privilege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exorbitant_privilege).

Gold and silver coins have been previously minted for general circulation from the 18th to the 20th centuries. The last gold coins were minted in 1933. The last 90% silver coins were minted in 1964, and the last 40% silver half dollar was minted in 1970.

The [United States Mint](358818.md) currently produces circulating coins at the [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Mint) and [Denver Mints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denver_Mint), and commemorative and proof coins for collectors at the [San Francisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Mint) and [West Point Mints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Point_Mint). Mint mark conventions for these and for past mint branches are discussed in *[Coins of the United States dollar#Mint marks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coins_of_the_United_States_dollar#Mint_marks)*.

The [one-dollar coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar_coin_\(United_States\)) has never been in popular circulation from 1794 to present, despite several attempts to increase their usage since the 1970s, the most important reason of which is the continued production and popularity of the [one-dollar bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_one-dollar_bill). It has not been produced for circulation since 2012.[^cite_note-51] [Half dollar coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_dollar_\(United_States_coin\)) were commonly used currency since inception in 1794, but has fallen out of use from the mid-1960s when all silver half dollars began to be hoarded.

The [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel_\(United_States_coin\)) is the only coin whose size and composition (5 grams, 75% copper, and 25% nickel) is still in use from 1865 to today, except for wartime 1942–1945 [Jefferson nickels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jefferson_nickel) which contained silver.

Due to the [penny](164092.md)'s low value, [debate exists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penny_debate_in_the_United_States) over the penny's status as circulating coinage.[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53] In 2025, the Mint halted the production of pennies for circulation, but pennies remain in circulation as only an [act of Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Congress) can eliminate a currency.[^cite_note-Mint-54]

For a discussion of other discontinued and canceled denominations, see *[Obsolete denominations of United States currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obsolete_denominations_of_United_States_currency#Coinage)* and *[Canceled denominations of United States currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canceled_denominations_of_United_States_currency#Coinage)*.

The [U.S. Constitution](31644.md) provides that Congress shall have the power to "borrow money on the credit of the United States."[^cite_note-\:1-16] Congress has exercised that power by authorizing [Federal Reserve Banks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Banks) to issue [Federal Reserve Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Notes). Those notes are "obligations of the United States" and "shall be redeemed in lawful money on demand at the Treasury Department of the United States, in the city of Washington, District of Columbia, or at any Federal Reserve bank".[^cite_note-61] Federal Reserve Notes are designated by law as "[legal tender](267793.md)" for the payment of debts.[^cite_note-62] Congress has also authorized the issuance of [more than 10 other types of banknotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Note), including the [United States Note](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Note)[^cite_note-63] and the [Federal Reserve Bank Note](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Bank_Note). The Federal Reserve Note is the only type that remains in circulation since the 1970s. Federal Reserve Notes are printed by the [Bureau of Engraving and Printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Engraving_and_Printing) and are made from [cotton fiber paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_paper) (as opposed to [wood fiber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_fibre) used to make common paper). The "[large-sized notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large-sized_note)" issued before 1928 measured 7.42 in × 3.125 in (188.5 mm × 79.4 mm), while [small-sized notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small-sized_note) introduced that year measure 6.14 in × 2.61 in × 0.0043 in (155.96 mm × 66.29 mm × 0.11 mm).[^cite_note-64] The dimensions of the modern (small-size) U.S. currency is identical to the size of [Philippine peso](227672.md) banknotes issued under United States administration after 1903, which had proven highly successful.[^cite_note-65] The American large-note bills became known as "horse blankets" or "saddle blankets".[^cite_note-Orzano-66]

Currently printed denominations are [\$1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_one-dollar_bill), [\$2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_two-dollar_bill), [\$5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_five-dollar_bill), [\$10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_ten-dollar_bill), [\$20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_twenty-dollar_bill), [\$50](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_fifty-dollar_bill), and [\$100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_one_hundred-dollar_bill). Notes above the \$100 denomination stopped being printed in 1946 and were officially withdrawn from circulation in 1969. These notes were used primarily in inter-bank transactions or by [organized crime](22625.md); it was the latter usage that prompted President [Richard Nixon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nixon) to issue an executive order in 1969 halting their use. With the advent of electronic banking, they became less necessary. Notes in denominations of [\$500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_five-hundred-dollar_bill), [\$1,000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_one-thousand-dollar_bill), [\$5,000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_five-thousand-dollar_bill), [\$10,000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_ten-thousand-dollar_bill) (discontinued, but still legal tender); [\$100,000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_one_hundred-thousand-dollar_bill) were all produced at one time; see [large denomination bills in U.S. currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_denomination_bills_in_U.S._currency) for details. With the exception of the \$100,000 bill (which was only issued as a Series 1934 Gold Certificate and was never publicly circulated; thus it is illegal to own), these notes are now collector's items and are worth more than their face value to collectors.

Though still predominantly green, the post-2004 series incorporate other colors to better distinguish different denominations. As a result of a 2008 decision in an accessibility lawsuit filed by the [American Council of the Blind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Council_of_the_Blind), the [Bureau of Engraving and Printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Engraving_and_Printing) is planning to implement a raised tactile feature in the next redesign of each note, except the \$1 and the current version of the \$100 bill. It also plans larger, higher-contrast numerals, more color differences, and distribution of currency readers to assist the visually impaired during the transition period.[^cite_note-67]

## Monetary policy

See also: [Federal Reserve](10819.md), [Monetary policy](297032.md), [Monetary policy of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetary_policy_of_the_United_States), and [money supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_supply)



The [Headquarters of the Federal Reserve System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eccles_Building) in Washington, D.C.

The [Federal Reserve Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Act) created the [Federal Reserve System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_System) in 1913 as the [central bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_bank) of the [United States](3434750.md). Its primary task is to conduct the nation's [monetary policy](297032.md) to promote maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates in the U.S. economy. It is also tasked to promote the stability of the financial system and regulate financial institutions, and to act as [lender of last resort](921278.md).[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-79]

The [Monetary policy of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetary_policy_of_the_United_States) is conducted by the [Federal Open Market Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Open_Market_Committee), which is composed of the [Federal Reserve Board of Governors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Board_of_Governors) and 5 out of the 12 Federal Reserve Bank presidents, and is implemented by all twelve regional [Federal Reserve Banks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Bank).

[Monetary policy](297032.md) refers to actions made by central banks that determine the size and growth rate of the [money supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_supply) available in the economy, and which would result in desired objectives like low inflation, low unemployment, and stable financial systems. The economy's aggregate money supply is the total of

- **M0 money, or Monetary Base** – "dollars" in currency and [bank money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_money) balances credited to the central bank's depositors, which are backed by the central bank's assets,

- **plus M1, M2, M3 money** – "dollars" in the form of [bank money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_money) balances credited to banks' depositors, which are backed by the bank's assets and investments.

The FOMC influences the level of money available to the economy by the following means:

- **Reserve requirements** – specifies a required minimum percentage of deposits in a [commercial bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_bank) that should be held as a reserve (i.e. as deposits with the Federal Reserve), with the rest available to loan or invest. Higher requirements mean less money loaned or invested, helping keep inflation in check. Raising the **[federal funds rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_funds_rate)** earned on those reserves also helps achieve this objective.

- **Open market operations** – the Federal Reserve buys or sells [US Treasury bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Treasury_security) and other securities held by banks in exchange for reserves; more reserves increase a bank's capacity to loan or invest elsewhere.

- **Discount window lending** – banks can borrow from the Federal Reserve.

[Monetary policy](297032.md) directly affects interest rates; it indirectly affects stock prices, wealth, and currency exchange rates. Through these channels, monetary policy influences spending, investment, production, employment, and inflation in the United States. Effective monetary policy complements [fiscal policy](132645.md) to support economic growth.

The adjusted monetary base has increased from approximately \$400 billion in 1994, to \$800 billion in 2005, and to over \$3 trillion in 2013.[^cite_note-80]

When the Federal Reserve makes a purchase, it credits the seller's reserve account (with the Federal Reserve). This money is not transferred from any existing funds—it is at this point that the Federal Reserve has created new [high-powered money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-powered_money). Commercial banks then decide how much money to keep in deposit with the Federal Reserve and how much to hold as physical currency. In the latter case, the Federal Reserve places an order for printed money from the U.S. Treasury Department.[^cite_note-81] The Treasury Department, in turn, sends these requests to the Bureau of Engraving and Printing (to print new [dollar bills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_notes)) and the Bureau of the Mint (to stamp the coins).

The Federal Reserve's monetary policy objectives to keep prices stable and unemployment low is often called the *dual mandate*. This replaces past practices under a [gold standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard) where the main concern is the gold equivalent of the local currency, or under a gold exchange standard where the concern is fixing the exchange rate versus another gold-convertible currency (previously practiced worldwide under the [Bretton Woods Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bretton_Woods_Agreement) of 1944 via fixed exchange rates to the U.S. dollar).

### Ascendancy

The primary currency used for global trade between [Europe](9239.md), [Asia](689.md), and [the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Americas) has historically been the Spanish-American [silver dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_dollar), which created a global [silver standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_standard) system from the 16th to 19th centuries, due to abundant silver supplies in [Spanish America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_America).[^cite_note-82] The U.S. dollar itself was derived from this coin. The Spanish dollar was later displaced by the British [pound sterling](270673.md) in the advent of the international [gold standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard) in the last quarter of the 19th century.

The U.S. dollar began to displace the [pound sterling](270673.md) as international [reserve currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_currency) from the 1920s since it emerged from the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War) relatively unscathed and since the [United States](3434750.md) was a significant recipient of wartime gold inflows.[^cite_note-83] After the U.S. emerged as an even stronger global [superpower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superpower) during the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War), the [Bretton Woods Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bretton_Woods_Agreement) of 1944 established the post-war international monetary system, with the U.S. dollar ascending to become the world's primary reserve currency for international trade, and the only post-war currency linked to gold at \$35 per [troy ounce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troy_ounce).[^cite_note-84]

### As international reserve currency

The U.S. dollar is joined by the world's other major currencies – the [euro](9472.md), [pound sterling](270673.md), [Japanese yen](34392.md) and [Chinese renminbi](66958.md) – in the currency basket of the [special drawing rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_drawing_rights) of the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md). Central banks worldwide have huge reserves of U.S. dollars in their holdings and are significant buyers of [U.S. treasury bills and notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Treasury_security).[^cite_note-85]

Foreign companies, entities, and private individuals hold U.S. dollars in foreign deposit accounts called [eurodollars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurodollar) (not to be confused with the [euro](9472.md)), which are outside the jurisdiction of the [Federal Reserve System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_System). Private individuals also hold dollars outside the banking system mostly in the form of [US\$100 bills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_one-hundred-dollar_bill), of which 80% of its supply is held overseas.

The [United States Department of the Treasury](53667.md) exercises considerable oversight over the [SWIFT financial transfers network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SWIFT),[^cite_note-86] and consequently has a huge sway on the global [financial transactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_transaction) systems, with the ability to impose sanctions on foreign entities and individuals.[^cite_note-87]

### In the global markets

The U.S. dollar is predominantly the standard currency unit in which goods are quoted and traded, and with which payments are settled, in the global [commodity markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodity_markets).[^cite_note-88] The [U.S. Dollar Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Dollar_Index) is an important indicator of the dollar's strength or weakness versus a basket of six foreign currencies.

The [United States Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Government) is capable of borrowing trillions of dollars from the global capital markets in U.S. dollars issued by the [Federal Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_System), which is itself under U.S. government purview, at minimal interest rates, and with virtually zero default risk. In contrast, foreign governments and corporations incapable of raising money in their own local currencies are forced to issue debt denominated in U.S. dollars, along with its consequent higher interest rates and risks of default.[^cite_note-89] The United States's ability to borrow in its own currency without facing a significant balance of payments crisis has been frequently described as its [exorbitant privilege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exorbitant_privilege).[^cite_note-90]

A frequent topic of debate is whether the [strong dollar policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_dollar_policy) of the United States is indeed in America's own best interests, as well as in the best interest of the [international community](31769.md).[^cite_note-91]

Inflation value of dollar

The 6th paragraph of [Section 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinage_clause) of Article 1 of the U.S. Constitution provides that the U.S. Congress shall have the power to "coin money" and to "regulate the value" of domestic and foreign coins. Congress exercised those powers when it enacted the [Coinage Act of 1792](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinage_Act_of_1792). That Act provided for the minting of the [first U.S. dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowing_Hair_Dollar) and it declared that the U.S. dollar shall have "the value of a [Spanish milled dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_milled_dollar) as the same is now current".[^cite_note-92]

The table above shows the equivalent amount of goods that, in a particular year, could be purchased with \$1. The table shows that from 1774 through 2012 the U.S. dollar has lost about 97.0% of its buying power.[^cite_note-measuring-worth-93]

The decline in the value of the U.S. dollar corresponds to [price inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_inflation), which is a rise in the general level of prices of goods and services in an economy over a period of time.[^cite_note-94] A [consumer price index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_price_index) (CPI) is a measure estimating the average price of consumer goods and services purchased by households. The [United States Consumer Price Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Consumer_Price_Index), published by the [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Labor_Statistics), is a measure estimating the average price of consumer goods and services in the United States.[^cite_note-BLSCPI-95]<sup>: Question 1 </sup> It reflects inflation as experienced by consumers in their day-to-day living expenses.[^cite_note-BLSCPI-95]<sup>: Question 12 </sup> A graph showing the U.S. CPI relative to 1982–1984 and the annual year-over-year change in CPI is shown at right.

The value of the U.S. dollar declined significantly during wartime, especially during the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War), World War I, and World War II.[^cite_note-96] The [Federal Reserve](10819.md), which was established in 1913, was designed to furnish an "elastic" currency subject to "substantial changes of quantity over short periods", which differed significantly from previous forms of [high-powered money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-powered_money) such as gold, national banknotes, and silver coins.[^cite_note-97] Over the very long run, the prior gold standard kept prices stable—for instance, the price level and the value of the U.S. dollar in 1914 were not very different from the price level in the 1880s. The Federal Reserve initially succeeded in maintaining the value of the U.S. dollar and price stability, reversing the inflation caused by the First World War and stabilizing the value of the dollar during the 1920s, before presiding over a 30% deflation in U.S. prices in the 1930s.[^cite_note-central-banking-then-and-now-98]

Under the [Bretton Woods system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bretton_Woods_system) established after World War II, the value of gold was fixed to \$35 per ounce, and the value of the U.S. dollar was thus anchored to the value of gold. Rising government spending in the 1960s, however, led to doubts about the ability of the United States to maintain this convertibility, gold stocks dwindled as banks and international investors began to convert dollars to gold, and as a result, the value of the dollar began to decline. Facing an emerging [currency crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_crisis) and the imminent danger that the United States would no longer be able to redeem dollars for gold, gold convertibility was finally terminated in 1971 by [President Nixon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nixon), resulting in the "[Nixon shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nixon_shock)".[^cite_note-inflation-historical-perspective-99]

The value of the U.S. dollar was therefore no longer anchored to gold, and it fell upon the Federal Reserve to maintain the value of the U.S. currency. The Federal Reserve, however, continued to increase the money supply, resulting in [stagflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagflation) and a rapidly declining value of the U.S. dollar in the 1970s. This was largely due to the prevailing economic view at the time that inflation and real economic growth were linked (the [Phillips curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phillips_curve)), and so inflation was regarded as relatively benign.[^cite_note-inflation-historical-perspective-99] Between 1965 and 1981, the U.S. dollar lost two thirds of its value.[^cite_note-measuring-worth-93]

In 1979, [President Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Carter) appointed [Paul Volcker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Volcker) [Chairman of the Federal Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chairman_of_the_Federal_Reserve). The Federal Reserve tightened the money supply and inflation was substantially lower in the 1980s, and hence the value of the U.S. dollar stabilized.[^cite_note-inflation-historical-perspective-99]

Over the thirty-year period from 1981 to 2009, the U.S. dollar lost over half its value.[^cite_note-measuring-worth-93] This is because the Federal Reserve has targeted not zero inflation, but a low, stable rate of inflation—between 1987 and 1997, the rate of inflation was approximately 3.5%, and between 1997 and 2007 it was approximately 2%. The so-called "[Great Moderation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Moderation)" of economic conditions since the 1970s is credited to monetary policy targeting price stability.[^cite_note-inflation-historical-perspective2-100]

There is an ongoing debate about whether central banks should target zero inflation (which would mean a constant value for the U.S. dollar over time) or low, stable inflation (which would mean a continuously but slowly declining value of the dollar over time, as is the case now). Although some economists are in favor of a zero inflation policy and therefore a constant value for the U.S. dollar,[^cite_note-central-banking-then-and-now-98] others contend that such a policy limits the ability of the central bank to control [interest rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interest_rates) and stimulate the economy when needed.[^cite_note-101]
