**Miami**[^cite_note-12] is a [coastal](89126.md) city in the southeast portion of the U.S. state of [Florida](18933066.md). It is the [second-most populous city proper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_municipalities_in_Florida) in Florida, with a population of 442,241 at the [2020 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_census).[^cite_note-2020CensusPopulationByRace-7] The [Miami metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_metropolitan_area) in [South Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Florida) has an estimated 6.39 million residents, ranking as the fifth-largest metropolitan area in the [Southeast](816925.md) and [eighth-largest metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_statistical_area#United_States) in the United States.[^cite_note-2020Pop-10] Miami has the [third-largest skyline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_the_United_States#Cities_with_the_most_skyscrapers) in the U.S. with over [300 high-rises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_Miami),[^cite_note-13] 70 of which exceed 492 ft (150 m).[^cite_note-14] It is the [county seat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_seat) of [Miami-Dade County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_County,_Florida).

Miami is a major urban center and leader in finance, commerce, culture, arts, and international trade.[^cite_note-GAWC-15][^cite_note-16] Miami's metropolitan area is the largest urban economy in Florida, with a 2023 [gross domestic product](12594.md) of \$533.674 billion.[^cite_note-GDP-11] In a 2018 [UBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UBS) study of 77 world cities, Miami was the third-richest city in the U.S. and the third-richest globally in [purchasing power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purchasing_power).[^cite_note-17] Miami is a [majority-minority city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majority_minority_in_the_United_States), with 70.2 percent of the city's population identifying as [Hispanic and Latino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic_and_Latino_Americans) as of 2020.[^cite_note-2020Hispanic-18]

[Downtown Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Downtown_Miami) has one of the largest concentrations of international banks in the U.S. and is home to several large national and international companies.[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20] The [Health District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_District_\(Miami\)) is home to several major [University of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Miami)–affiliated hospital and health facilities, including [Jackson Memorial Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackson_Memorial_Hospital), the nation's largest hospital with 1,547 beds,[^cite_note-21] the [Miller School of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_School_of_Medicine), the University of Miami's academic medical center and teaching hospital, and others engaged in health-related care and research. [PortMiami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PortMiami), the city's seaport, is the busiest [cruise](314855.md) port in the world in both passenger traffic and cruise lines.[^cite_note-22]

The Miami metropolitan area is the second-most visited city or metropolitan statistical area in the U.S. after [New York City](645042.md), with over four million visitors in 2022.[^cite_note-23] Due to its strong commercial and cultural ties to [Latin America](18524.md) and majority-[Hispanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic_and_Latino_Americans) population (at 70%), Miami has been called the "Gateway to Latin America" or even the "Capital of Latin America".[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26]

## Toponymy

Miami was named after the [Miami River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_River_\(Florida\)), derived from [Mayaimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayaimi), the historic name of [Lake Okeechobee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Okeechobee) and the Native Americans who lived around it.[^cite_note-27] Miami is sometimes colloquially referred to as *The [305](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area_codes_305,_786,_and_645)*, *Magic City*, *Gateway to the Americas*, *Gateway to Latin America*, *Capital of Latin America*,[^cite_note-Time-1] and *Vice City*.

## History

The mouth of [Miami River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_River_\(Florida\)) at [Brickell Key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brickell_Key) in February 2010

The [Tequesta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tequesta) tribe occupied the Miami area for around 2,000 years before contact with Europeans. A village of hundreds of people, dating to 500–600 BCE, was located at the mouth of the [Miami River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_River_\(Florida\)). It is believed that the entire tribe migrated to [Cuba](5042481.md) by the mid-1700s.[^cite_note-28] In 1566, admiral [Pedro Menéndez de Avilés](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_Men%C3%A9ndez_de_Avil%C3%A9s), Florida's first governor, claimed the area for [Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Empire). A Spanish [mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission_\(station\)) was constructed one year later. Florida was ruled by Spain for centuries—aside from a brief British interlude (1763–1783) that ended with [Britain](31717.md) losing the territory back to Spain—until Spain ceded it to the United States in 1821, in exchange for U.S. recognition of Spanish sovereignty in [Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Texas) and the resolution of the border dispute along the [Sabine River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabine_River_\(Texas%E2%80%93Louisiana\)). In 1836, the U.S. built [Fort Dallas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Dallas) on the banks of the Miami River as part of their development of the [Florida Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Territory) and their attempt to suppress and remove the [Seminoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminole). As a result, the Miami area became a site of fighting in the [Second Seminole War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Seminole_War).

Miami is noted as the only major city in the United States founded by a woman. [Julia Tuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_Tuttle), a local [citrus grower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citrus) and a wealthy [Cleveland](5951.md) native, was the original owner of the land upon which Miami was built.[^cite_note-29] In the late 19th century, the area was known as "Biscayne Bay Country", and reports described it as a promising wilderness and "one of the finest building sites in Florida".[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31] The [Great Freeze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Freeze) of 1894–1895 hastened Miami's growth, as the crops there were the only ones in Florida that survived. Julia Tuttle subsequently convinced [railroad tycoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_magnate) [Henry Flagler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Flagler) to extend his [Florida East Coast Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_East_Coast_Railway) to the region, for which she became known as "the mother of Miami".[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33] Miami was officially incorporated as a city on July 28, 1896, with a population of just over 300.[^cite_note-34]

In the early twentieth century, Miami's development was deeply shaped by waves of migrant labor, particularly from the Caribbean. Scholars note that Bahamian immigrants formed a critical portion of Miami's construction and service workforce during the city's formative decades, contributing essential labor to roadbuilding, hotel construction, and other urban expansion projects (Shell-Weiss, 2005). Their role in Miami's labor force helped anchor the city's early economic growth and laid a foundation for its later multicultural identity.[^cite_note-35] During the early 20th century, migrants from the [Bahamas](3451.md) and African-Americans constituted 40 percent of the city's population.[^cite_note-Concrete-36]<sup>: 25 </sup> When landlords began to rent homes to African-Americans around Avenue J, what would later become NW Fifth Avenue, a gang of white men with torches marched through the neighborhood and warned the residents to move or be bombed.[^cite_note-Concrete-36]<sup>: 33 </sup> Miami's economy was heavily based on seasonal tourism and construction work, which created a volatile job market of low-wage, temporary employment and contributed to pronounced income inequality even as the city promoted itself as a booming leisure destination.[^cite_note-37]

Despite the city's image of resort-style prosperity, workers and activists during the interwar period organized around unemployment and labor exclusion, helping to lay early foundations for labor mobilization in South Florida.[^cite_note-38] Miami prospered during the 1920s with an increase in population and development in infrastructure as northerners moved to the city. The legacy of [Jim Crow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Crow_laws) was embedded in these developments. Miami's chief of police at the time, [H. Leslie Quigg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._Leslie_Quigg), did not hide the fact that he, like many other white Miami police officers, was a member of the [Ku Klux Klan](16779.md). Unsurprisingly, these officers enforced social codes far beyond the written law. Quigg, for example, "personally and publicly beat a colored bellboy to death for speaking directly to a white woman".[^cite_note-Concrete-36]<sup>: 53 </sup>[^cite_note-39] The collapse of the [1920s Florida land boom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1920s_Florida_land_boom), the [1926 Miami hurricane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1926_Miami_hurricane), and the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression) in the 1930s slowed development. When [World War II](32927.md) began, Miami became a base for U.S. defense against [German submarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-boat) due to its prime location on the southern coast of Florida. This brought an increase in Miami's population; 172,172 people lived in the city by 1940. The [city's nickname](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicknames_of_Miami), *The Magic City*, came from its rapid growth, which was noticed by winter visitors who remarked that the city grew so much from one year to the next that it was like magic.[^cite_note-MDInfocenter-40]

After [Fidel Castro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fidel_Castro) rose to power in [Cuba](5042481.md) following the [Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Revolution) in 1959, many Cubans sought refuge in Miami, further increasing the city's population. Miami's national profile expanded dramatically in the 1970s, particularly in 1972.[^cite_note-SFMN_1972-41] The region hosted both the [Democratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1972_Democratic_National_Convention) and [Republican National Conventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1972_Republican_National_Convention) in the [1972 presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1972_United_States_presidential_election). The [Miami Dolphins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Dolphins) also made history with their [undefeated "perfect" season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1972_Miami_Dolphins_season). The area's educational and cultural institutions also developed significantly in this period, positioning the city to service a larger and increasingly international population.[^cite_note-SFMN_1972-41]

Miami developed new businesses and cultural amenities as part of the [New South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_South) in the 1980s and 1990s. At the same time, South Florida weathered social problems related to [drug wars](1181646.md), immigration from [Haiti](13373.md) and Latin America, and the widespread destruction of [Hurricane Andrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Andrew).[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-MDInfocenter-40] Racial and cultural tensions sometimes sparked, but Miami developed in the latter half of the 20th century as a major international, financial, and cultural center. It is the second-largest U.S. city with a Spanish-speaking majority (after [El Paso, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Paso,_Texas)), and the largest city with a [Cuban-American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban-American) plurality.[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-autogenerated1-44]

## Geography

Miami and its suburbs are located on a broad plain between the [Everglades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everglades) to the west and [Biscayne Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biscayne_Bay) to the east, which extends from [Lake Okeechobee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Okeechobee) southward to [Florida Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Bay). The elevation of the area averages at around 6 ft (1.8 m)[^cite_note-45] above sea level in most neighborhoods, especially near the coast. The highest points are found along the [Miami Rock Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Rock_Ridge), which lies under most of the eastern Miami metro. The main portion of Miami is on the shores of Biscayne Bay, which contains several hundred natural and artificial [barrier islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrier_island), the largest of which contains [Miami Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Beach,_Florida) and [South Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Beach). The [Gulf Stream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_Stream), a warm [ocean current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_current), runs northward just 15 miles (24 km) off the coast, allowing Miami's climate to stay warm and mild all year.

### Geology

The surface bedrock under the Miami area is called [Miami oolite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_oolite) or Miami limestone. This bedrock is covered by a thin layer of soil, and is no more than 50 feet (15 m) thick. Miami limestone formed as the result of the drastic changes in sea level associated with recent [glacial periods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glacial_period), or [ice ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_age). Beginning some 130,000 years ago, the [Sangamonian Stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sangamonian) raised sea levels to approximately 25 feet (8 m) above the current level. All of southern Florida was covered by a shallow sea. Several parallel lines of reef formed along the edge of the submerged Florida [plateau](538005.md), stretching from the present Miami area to what is now the [Dry Tortugas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_Tortugas).[^cite_note-geology-46]

The area behind this reef line was a large lagoon. Miami limestone formed throughout the area from the deposition of oolites and the shells of [bryozoans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryozoans). Starting about 100,000 years ago, the [Wisconsin glaciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisconsin_glaciation) began lowering sea levels, exposing the floor of the lagoon. By 15,000 years ago, the sea level had dropped 300 to 350 feet (90 to 110 m) below the current level. The sea level rose quickly after that, stabilizing at the current level about 4,000 years ago, leaving the [mainland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland) of South Florida just [above sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Above_mean_sea_level).[^cite_note-geology-46]

Beneath the plain lies the [Biscayne Aquifer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biscayne_Aquifer), a natural underground source of fresh water that extends from southern [Palm Beach County](73647.md) to [Florida Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Bay). It comes closest to the surface around the cities of [Miami Springs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Springs,_Florida) and [Hialeah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hialeah,_Florida).[^cite_note-47] Most of the [Miami metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_metropolitan_area) obtains its drinking water from the Biscayne Aquifer. As a result of the aquifer, it is not possible to dig more than 15 to 20 ft (5 to 6 m) beneath the city without hitting water, which impedes underground construction, though some [underground parking garages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underground_parking_in_South_Florida) exist. For this reason, the [mass transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapid_transit) systems in and around Miami are elevated or [at-grade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_grading).[^cite_note-geology-46]

Most of the western fringes of Miami border the [Everglades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everglades), a tropical marshland covering most of the southern portion of Florida. [Alligators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_alligator) that live in the marshes have ventured into Miami communities and onto major highways.[^cite_note-geology-46]

The historic district of [Downtown Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Downtown_Miami) is one of the city's oldest with buildings constructed as far back as 1896.

Miami is split roughly into north, south, west, and Downtown areas. [Downtown Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Downtown_Miami), which is on the eastern side includes the neighborhoods of [Brickell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brickell), [Virginia Key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_Key), [Watson Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watson_Island), and [PortMiami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PortMiami). Downtown Miami is Florida's largest and most influential business district, with many major banks, courthouses, financial headquarters, cultural and tourist attractions, schools, parks, and a large residential population. [Brickell Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brickell_Avenue) has the largest concentration of international banks in the United States. Just northwest of Downtown is the [Health District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_District_\(Miami\)), which is Miami's center for hospitals, [research institutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_institute) and [biotechnology](4502.md), with institutions such as [Jackson Memorial Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackson_Memorial_Hospital), [Bascom Palmer Eye Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bascom_Palmer_Eye_Institute), and the [University of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Miami)'s [Miller School of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_School_of_Medicine).[^cite_note-neighborhoods-49]

The southern area of Miami includes the neighborhoods of [Coral Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral_Way), [The Roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Roads), and [Coconut Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coconut_Grove). Coral Way is a historic residential neighborhood built in 1922 between Downtown and [Coral Gables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral_Gables,_Florida), and is home to many old homes and tree-lined streets. Coconut Grove, settled in 1825, and annexed into Miami in 1925, is a historic neighborhood with narrow, winding roads and a heavy tree canopy.[^cite_note-neighborhoods-49][^cite_note-50] It is the location of Miami's City Hall at [Dinner Key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinner_Key), the former [Coconut Grove Playhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coconut_Grove_Playhouse), [CocoWalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CocoWalk), and the [Coconut Grove Convention Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coconut_Grove_Convention_Center). It is home to many [nightclubs](19321330.md), bars, restaurants, and [bohemian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemianism) shops, which are popular with local [college students](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_student). Coconut Grove is known for its many parks and gardens, such as [Vizcaya Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vizcaya_Museum_and_Gardens), [The Kampong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Kampong), [The Barnacle Historic State Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Barnacle_Historic_State_Park), and numerous other historic homes and estates.[^cite_note-neighborhoods-49]

The western area of Miami includes the neighborhoods of [Little Havana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Havana), [West Flagler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Flagler), and [Flagami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flagami). Although at one time a mostly Jewish neighborhood, today western Miami is home to immigrants from mostly Central America and [Cuba](5042481.md). The west central neighborhood of [Allapattah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allapattah) is a multicultural community of many ethnicities.[^cite_note-neighborhoods-49]

The northern side of Miami includes [Midtown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midtown_Miami), a district with a great mix of diversity ranging from Caribbeans to Central Americans, South Americans and Europeans. The [Edgewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgewater_\(Miami\)) neighborhood of Midtown is mostly composed of high-rise residential towers and is home to the [Adrienne Arsht Center for the Performing Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrienne_Arsht_Center_for_the_Performing_Arts). [Wynwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wynwood_Art_District) is an art district with ten galleries in former warehouses, as well as a large outdoor mural project. There are wealthy residents in the [Design District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Design_District) and the [Upper Eastside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Eastside), which has many 1920s homes as well as examples of [Miami Modern architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Modern_architecture) in the MiMo Historic District.[^cite_note-51] The northern side of Miami also has notable African-American and [Caribbean](18956035.md) immigrant communities, including [Little Haiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Haiti), [Overtown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overtown_\(Miami\)) (home of the [Lyric Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyric_Theater_\(Miami\))), and [Liberty City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberty_City_\(Miami\)).[^cite_note-neighborhoods-49]

### Climate

Main article: [Climate of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_Miami)



A summer afternoon thunderstorm rolling into Miami from the [Everglades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everglades), July 2006

Miami has a [tropical monsoon climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_monsoon_climate) ([Köppen climate classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) *Am*)[^cite_note-Miami,_Florida-52][^cite_note-53] with hot and wet summers and warm and dry winters.

Miami's sea-level elevation, coastal location, position just above the [Tropic of Cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropic_of_Cancer), and proximity to the [Gulf Stream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_Stream) shape its climate. Average winter high temperatures, from December to March, range from 76.4–80.3 °F (24.7–26.8 °C). January is the coolest month with an average daily temperature of 68.2 °F (20.1 °C). Low temperatures fall below 50 °F (10 °C) about 3 to 4 nights during the winter season, after the passage of [cold fronts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_front) that produce what little rainfall that falls in the winter.

There are two basic seasons in Miami, a hot and wet season from May to October, and a warm and dry season from November to April. During the hot and wet season, daily thundershowers occur in the humid unstable air masses. The wet season in Miami is defined as the period during which the average daily [dew point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dew_point) temperature is above 70 °F (21 °C). The rainy season typically begins on the first day that occurs, or within a few days later.[^cite_note-weather.gov-54]

Daily rainfall in Miami decreases sharply when the average daily dew point falls to 70 °F (21 °C) or below. In some years, a stalled front to the south of the Florida peninsula may cause rains to continue for a few more days. From 1956 to 1997, the date summer began ranged from April 16 to June 3, with a median date of May 21. In those same years, the date summer ended ranged from September 24 to November 1, with a median date of October 17.[^cite_note-weather.gov-54]

During summer, temperatures range from the mid-80s to low 90s °F (29–35 °C) and are accompanied by high humidity. The heat is often relieved in the afternoon by thunderstorms or a [sea breeze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_breeze) that develops off the Atlantic Ocean. Much of the year's 61.9 inches (1,572 mm) of rainfall occurs during this period. [Dew points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dew_point) in the warm months range from 71.9 °F (22.2 °C) in June to 73.7 °F (23.2 °C) in August.[^cite_note-NOAA-55]

Historical temperature extremes range from 27 °F (−2.8 °C) on February 3, 1917, to 100 °F (38 °C) on July 21, 1942 and August 18, 2026.[^cite_note-56][^cite_note-57] While Miami has never [recorded snowfall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snow_in_Florida) at any official weather station since records have been kept, [snow flurries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snow_flurry) fell in some parts of Miami on January 19, 1977.[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60] The coldest daytime maximum temperature on record is 45 °F (7 °C) in December 1989 during the [December 1989 United States cold wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/December_1989_United_States_cold_wave). The warmest overnight low measured is 84 °F (29 °C) on several occasions.[^cite_note-NOAA-55]

[Hurricane season](8282374.md) officially runs from June 1 to November 30, although hurricanes can develop beyond those dates. The most likely time for Miami to be hit is during the peak of the [Cape Verde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Verde_hurricane) season, which is mid-August to the end of September.[^cite_note-61] Although tornadoes are uncommon in the area, one struck in [1925](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1925_Miami_tornado) and another in [1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Miami_tornado).[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63] Around 40% of homes in Miami are built upon [floodplains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floodplain) and are considered as flood-risk zones.[^cite_note-64]

Miami falls within the [Department of Agriculture's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Agriculture) 10b/11a plant [hardiness zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardiness_zone).[^cite_note-65]

A report issued by [Resources for the Future](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resources_for_the_Future) in 2020 stated that, due to global [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise), Miami is one of the cities in the world most vulnerable to damage caused by storms and [coastal flooding](31546082.md).[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67] Historical tide-gauge observations show a measurable upward trend in sea level along Florida's coastline, and projections estimate that Miami may experience significantly higher flooding frequency by mid-century[^cite_note-68] which in Miami is projected to be 21 inches (53 cm) to 40 inches (100 cm) by 2070, will lead to an increase in storm damage, more intense flooding, and will threaten Miami's water supply.[^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71] Other potential impacts of climate change include higher hurricane wind speeds and severe thunderstorms, which can bring about hail or tornadoes.[^cite_note-\:1-72] Some protective efforts are in place, including nourishing beaches and adding protective barriers, raising buildings and roads that are vulnerable, and restoring natural habitats such as [wetlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wetland).[^cite_note-\:1-72] Miami Beach has invested \$500 million to protect roads, buildings, and water systems.[^cite_note-\:1-72] Real estate prices in Miami already reflect the increase in prices for real estate at a higher elevation within the city compared to real estate at a lower elevation.[^cite_note-73]

Miami is the largest city in [South Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Florida), the second-largest city in [Florida](18933066.md), and is the anchor of the largest metropolitan area in Florida: the [Miami metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_metropolitan_area), which has over 6 million residents. Despite Miami being home to less than a fourteenth (1/14) of the population of the metro area, it is an outlier compared to its neighbors, being over twice the size of the next-largest city in the metro: [Hialeah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hialeah). Miami has approximately a sixth of the population of its own county, [Miami-Dade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_County), which is the state's largest.

Miami had rapid growth in the first half of the twentieth century. Its population grew from 1,681 in the [1900 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1900_United_States_census) to 249,276 in the [1950 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1950_United_States_census). This made it Florida's largest city, a title it retained until the [Jacksonville Consolidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacksonville_Consolidation), when the city of Jacksonville absorbed most of [Duval County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duval_County,_Florida), nearly tripling its population. Since then, Miami has retained its spot as Florida's second-largest city.

Throughout the latter half of the twentieth century, Miami experienced a certain amount of stagnation in its population, with expansion slowing during the 1950s and 1960s before nearly halting in the next three decades as suburbanization occurred. Miami grew by 34.3% in the 1950s and 1960s as its population reached 334,859 at the [1970 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1970_United_States_census). In the next three decades, it only grew 8.2%. By the time of the [2000 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_United_States_census), Miami's population stood at 362,470.

In the 2000s and 2010s, spurred by high-rise construction in Downtown Miami, Edgewater, and Brickell, Miami's population began to grow quickly once more.[^cite_note-DowntownGrowth1-87] An estimate by the American Community Survey found that the downtown population, from Brickell north to Midtown Miami, grew nearly 40% between 2010 and 2018.[^cite_note-DowntownGrowth2-88] From 2000 to 2010, Miami's population grew by 10.2% and reached 399,457 in 2010. In the early 2010s, Miami's population crossed a milestone of 400,000 people. In the [2020 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_census), it had grown by a further 10.7%, up to a population of 442,241.

In 1970, the Census Bureau reported Miami's population as 45.3% Hispanic, 32.9% non-Hispanic White, and 22.7% Black.[^cite_note-90] Miami's explosive population growth has been driven by internal migration from other parts of the country, up until the 1960s. From 1970 to 2000, population growth in Miami was stagnant, as [non-Hispanic White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Hispanic_White) Miamians left and significant immigration from Latin America, particularly [Cuba](5042481.md), made up the balance.[^cite_note-91][^cite_note-92] Miami's Hispanic majority solidified itself in this period of time, and in 1985, Miami elected its first Cuban-born mayor, [Xavier Suarez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xavier_Suarez).

According to the U.S. Census Bureau's 2024 population estimates, Miami-Dade county continues to experience steady growth, driven largely by domestic migration and ongoing international immigration. The county's population in 2024 reflects its status as one of the most diverse metropolitan regions in the United States, with large Hispanic, Caribbean, and Latin American communities contributing to its demographic profile (U.S Census Bureau, 2024)[^cite_note-93]

The non-Hispanic Black population of the city of Miami peaked in 1990 at almost 90,000, making up nearly a quarter of the population of Miami. Since then, Miami's non-Hispanic Black population has experienced a precipitous and steady decline. In the 2020 census, it was 52,447, only 11.7% of the population. Reasons for this include high costs in areas such as [Liberty City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberty_City,_Miami) and [Little Haiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Haiti), compounded with [gentrification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentrification).[^cite_note-94][^cite_note-95]

The non-Hispanic White population began to rebound in the twenty-first century, as the monolithically Hispanic areas in the Western and Central parts of Miami experienced population stagnation. This caused them to begin to be outweighed by migration into the Downtown region, from Latin America and the rest of the United States. This caused the non-Hispanic White population to rise from a nadir of 11.8% at the time of the [2000 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_United_States_census) to 11.9% at the time of the [2010 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_States_census). After this, the non-Hispanic White population grew significantly faster than Miami as a whole did during the 2010s. In the [2020 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_census), non-Hispanic Whites were 14.0% of the population of Miami and numbered 61,829, the highest number since the 1980s. The non-Hispanic White population of Miami surpassed the non-Hispanic Black population of Miami in the 2010s.

## Economy



[Brickell Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brickell_Avenue) has the largest concentration of international banks in the nation.



Downtown Miami's [Brickell Financial District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brickell) serves as an example of the city's recent waves of "[Manhattanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattanization)".

Miami is a major center of commerce and finance and has a strong [international business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_business) community. According to the 2020 ranking of world cities undertaken by the [Globalization and World Cities Research Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globalization_and_World_Cities_Research_Network) (GaWC) based on the level of presence of global corporate service organizations, Miami is considered a Beta + level [world city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_city), along with [Atlanta](3138.md), [Dallas](53838.md), and [Houston](13774.md). However, according to the U.S. census between 2015 and 2019, Miami lacks in terms of owner-occupied housing, computer and internet usage, education of bachelor's degree or higher, median household income, per capita income, while having a higher percentage of persons in poverty.[^cite_note-\:0-139][^cite_note-140] In 2013, Miami had a Gross Metropolitan Product of \$257 billion, ranking 11th in the United States and 20th worldwide in GMP.[^cite_note-141][^cite_note-142]

Several large companies are headquartered in Miami and the Miami area, including but not limited to [Akerman LLP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akerman_LLP),[^cite_note-143] [Alienware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alienware),[^cite_note-144] [Arquitectonica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arquitectonica),[^cite_note-145] [Brightstar Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brightstar_Corporation), [Celebrity Cruises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celebrity_Cruises),[^cite_note-146] [Carnival Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnival_Corporation_&_plc),[^cite_note-147] [Duany Plater-Zyberk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duany_Plater-Zyberk),[^cite_note-148] [Greenberg Traurig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenberg_Traurig), Inktel Direct, [Lennar Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lennar_Corporation), [Norwegian Cruise Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Cruise_Line), [Oceania Cruises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceania_Cruises), [OPKO Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OPKO_Health), [Parkjockey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkjockey), [RCTV International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RCTV_International),[^cite_note-149] [Royal Caribbean International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Caribbean_International), [Sitel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sitel), [Southern Wine & Spirits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Wine_&_Spirits),[^cite_note-150] [Telemundo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telemundo), [Vector Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_Group), [Watsco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watsco) and [World Fuel Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Fuel_Services). In 2022, hedge fund [Citadel LLC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citadel_LLC) and [market maker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_maker) [Citadel Securities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citadel_Securities) relocated their headquarters from Chicago to Miami.[^cite_note-ChicagoBusinessExodus1-151][^cite_note-ChicagoBusinessExodus2-152][^cite_note-ChicagoBusinessExodus3-153] Over 1,400 multinational firms are located in Miami, with many major global organizations headquartering their Latin American operations (or regional offices) in the city including [Walmart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walmart).[^cite_note-154] Companies based in nearby cities or unincorporated areas of [Miami-Dade County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_County,_Florida) include, [Benihana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benihana), [Burger King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burger_King), [Carnival Cruise Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnival_Cruise_Line), [Navarro Discount Pharmacies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navarro_Discount_Pharmacies), [Perry Ellis International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perry_Ellis_International), [Ryder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryder), [Sedano's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedano's), [UniMás](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UniM%C3%A1s), and [U.S. Century Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Century_Bank).[^cite_note-155][^cite_note-156][^cite_note-157][^cite_note-158][^cite_note-159][^cite_note-160][^cite_note-161]

Miami is a major television production center, and the most important city in the United States for Spanish-language media. [Telemundo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telemundo) and [UniMás](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UniM%C3%A1s) have their headquarters in the Miami area. [Univisión Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Univision) and [Telemundo Global Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telemundo_Global_Studios) produce much of the original programming for their respective parent networks, such as [telenovelas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telenovela), news, sports, and talk shows. In 2011, 85% of Telemundo's original programming was filmed in Miami.[^cite_note-162] Miami is a significant music recording center, with the [Sony Music Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sony_Music_Latin) headquarters in the city,[^cite_note-163] and the famed [Criteria Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criteria_Studios) in the city limits as well, along with many other smaller record labels. The famed [461 Ocean Boulevard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/461_Ocean_Boulevard) in nearby Miami Beach was a recording location for [Eric Clapton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Clapton) and the [Bee Gees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bee_Gees), among other artists. Miami also attracts many artists for music video and film shoots.

During the mid-2000s, Miami witnessed its largest real estate boom since the [Florida land boom of the 1920s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_land_boom_of_the_1920s), and the city had well over a hundred approved high-rise construction projects. However, only 50 were actually built.[^cite_note-164] A rapid wave of [Manhattanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattanization), or high-rise construction, led to fast population growth in Miami's inner neighborhoods, with [Downtown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Downtown_Miami), [Brickell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brickell) and [Edgewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgewater_\(Miami\)) becoming the fastest-growing areas of Miami. Miami currently has the seven tallest, as well as fifteen of top twenty, [skyscrapers in the state of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_Florida), with the tallest being the 868-foot (265 m) [Panorama Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panorama_Tower).[^cite_note-emporis-165]

The [housing market crash of 2007](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_housing_bubble) caused a foreclosure crisis in the area.[^cite_note-166] Like other metro areas in the United States, crime in Miami is localized to specific neighborhoods.[^cite_note-167]

[Miami International Airport](128968.md) ([IATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IATA_airport_code): MIA) and [PortMiami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PortMiami) are among the nation's busiest ports of entry, especially for cargo from [South America](26769.md) and the [Caribbean](18956035.md). PortMiami is the world's busiest cruise port. Miami International Airport is the busiest airport in Florida and the largest gateway between the United States and Latin America.[^cite_note-168] Due to its strength in international business, finance and trade, Miami has among the largest concentration of international banks in the country, primarily along [Brickell Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brickell_Avenue) in [Brickell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brickell), Miami's financial district. Miami was the host city of the 2003 [Free Trade Area of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Trade_Area_of_the_Americas) negotiations.

Miami is the home to the [National Hurricane Center](255317.md) and the headquarters of the [United States Southern Command](1590932.md), responsible for military operations in [Central](6121.md) and South America. Miami is also an industrial center, especially for stone quarrying and warehousing. These industries are centered largely on the western fringes of Miami near [Doral](109422.md) and [Hialeah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hialeah,_Florida).[^cite_note-169]

In the 2012 census, Miami had the [fourth highest percentage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_lowest-income_places_in_the_United_States) of family incomes below the federal poverty line out of all large cities in the United States, behind [Detroit](8687.md), [Cleveland](5951.md), and [Cincinnati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cincinnati), respectively. Miami is also one of the very few cities in the U.S. where the local government has gone bankrupt, in 2001.[^cite_note-170]

The Little Fire Ant, *[Wasmannia auropunctata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasmannia_auropunctata)*, is an [invasive agricultural pest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasive_agricultural_pest) in parts of Miami.[^cite_note-LFA-171]

### PortMiami

Main article: [PortMiami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PortMiami)



[PortMiami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PortMiami), the world's largest cruise ship port and headquarters for many of the world's largest cruise companies, seen from the docked *[MSC Magnifica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MSC_Magnifica)* cruise ship in March 2024.

[PortMiami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PortMiami) in Miami is the largest cruise ship port in the world.[^cite_note-miamiport-172] It has retained its status as the number one cruise and passenger port in the world for well over a decade, accommodating the largest cruise ships and the major cruise lines. In 2017, the port served 5,340,559 cruise passengers.[^cite_note-portreport-173] The port is one of the nation's busiest cargo ports, importing 9,162,340 tons of cargo in 2017.[^cite_note-portreport-173]

Among [North American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American) ports, it ranks second to [New Orleans](53842.md)' [Port of South Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_South_Louisiana) in cargo tonnage imported from [Latin America](18524.md). The port sits on 518 acres (2 km<sup>2</sup>) and has seven passenger terminals. China is the port's number one import country and number one export country. Miami has the world's largest amount of cruise line headquarters, home to [Carnival Cruise Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnival_Cruise_Line), [Celebrity Cruises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celebrity_Cruises), [Norwegian Cruise Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Cruise_Line), [Oceania Cruises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceania_Cruises), and [Royal Caribbean International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Caribbean_International). In 2014, the [Port of Miami Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Miami_Tunnel) opened, connecting the [MacArthur Causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacArthur_Causeway) to PortMiami.[^cite_note-174]

### Tourism and conventions



The [Royal Caribbean International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Caribbean_International) headquarters at [PortMiami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PortMiami)

Tourism is one of the Miami's largest private-sector industries, accounting for more than 144,800 jobs in [Miami-Dade County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_County,_Florida).[^cite_note-175] Miami's frequent portrayal in music, film, and popular culture has made Miami and its landmarks recognizable worldwide. In 2016, it attracted the second-highest number of foreign tourists of any city in the United States, after New York City, and is among the top 20 cities worldwide by international visitor spending. More than 15.9 million visitors arrived in Miami in 2017, adding \$26.1 billion to the economy.[^cite_note-176] With a large hotel infrastructure and the newly renovated [Miami Beach Convention Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Beach_Convention_Center), Miami is a popular destination for annual conventions and conferences.[^cite_note-177]

Some of the most popular tourist destinations in Miami include [South Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Beach), [Lincoln Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Road), [Bayside Marketplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayside_Marketplace), [Downtown Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Downtown_Miami), and [Brickell City Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brickell_City_Centre). The [Art Deco District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Beach_Architectural_District) in [Miami Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Beach,_Florida) is reputed as one of the most glamorous in the world for its [nightclubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nightclubs), beaches, historical buildings, and shopping. Annual events such as the [Miami Open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Open_\(tennis\)), [Art Basel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Basel), the [Winter Music Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_Music_Conference), the [South Beach Wine and Food Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_Network_South_Beach_Wine_and_Food_Festival), and [Mercedes-Benz Fashion Week Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercedes-Benz_Fashion_Week_Miami) attract millions to the metropolis every year.

### Venues

Miami is home to many entertainment venues, theaters, museums, parks, and performing arts centers. The [Adrienne Arsht Center for the Performing Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrienne_Arsht_Center_for_the_Performing_Arts) is the home of the [Florida Grand Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Grand_Opera) and the second-largest performing arts center in the United States after [Lincoln Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Center_for_the_Performing_Arts) in New York City.[^cite_note-194] The center attracts many large-scale operas, ballets, concerts, and musicals from around the world.

Other performing arts venues in Miami include the [Olympia Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympia_Theater_\(Miami\)), [Wertheim Performing Arts Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wertheim_Performing_Arts_Center), the [Fair Expo Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_Expo_Center), the [Tower Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_Theater_\(Miami,_Florida\)), and the [Bayfront Park Amphitheater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayfront_Park).[^cite_note-195]

Miami's first boat-in [movie theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movie_theater) opened in 2020.[^cite_note-miamiherald.com-196]

### Events

Miami is home to a number of annual festivals, such as the [Calle Ocho Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calle_Ocho_Festival), the largest Latin music festival in the country, which has run since 1978.[^cite_note-197][^cite_note-198]

Another celebrated event is the [Miami International Film Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_International_Film_Festival), taking place every year for 10 days around the first week of March, during which independent international and American films are screened across Miami. Miami has over a half dozen independent film theaters.[^cite_note-199]

The Miami Jewish Film Festival (MJFF) was established in 1996.[^cite_note-200] It is an annual event held in January that screens a variety of films relating to Jewish history and culture. As of 2025, the festival awards a number of film awards: Critics Jury Prize, Next Wave Jury Prize, Kadima Jury Prize, Torchbearer Award, Emerging Filmmaker Award, and the Audience Awards for Best Narrative Film, Documentary Film, and Short Film. The Miami Jewish Film Club holds special screenings throughout the year.[^cite_note-201]

Miami is a major fashion center, home to models and some of the top modeling agencies in the world. Miami hosts many fashion shows and events, including the annual [Miami Fashion Week](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Fashion_Week) and the [Mercedes-Benz Fashion Week Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercedes-Benz_Fashion_Week_Miami), held in the [Wynwood Art District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wynwood_Art_District).[^cite_note-202][^cite_note-203][^cite_note-204]

### Cuisine

The cuisine of Miami is a reflection of its diverse population, with a heavy influence from [Latin American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_American_cuisine), [Caribbean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caribbean_cuisine), [Soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_food), and [Jewish cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_cuisine). By combining them with mainstream [American cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_cuisine), it has spawned a unique [South Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Florida) style of cooking known as [Floribbean cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floribbean_cuisine). It is widely available throughout Miami and South Florida and can be found in restaurant chains such as [Pollo Tropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollo_Tropical).

Cuban immigrants in the 1960s originated the [Cuban sandwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_sandwich) and brought [medianoche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medianoche), [Cuban espresso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_espresso), [Bistec de palomilla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bistec_de_palomilla), and [croquetas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croquette), all of which have grown in popularity among all Miamians and have become symbols of the city's varied cuisine. Today, these are part of the local culture and can be found throughout the city at window cafés, particularly outside of supermarkets and restaurants.[^cite_note-210][^cite_note-211] Some of these locations, such as the [Versailles restaurant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Versailles_restaurant) in [Little Havana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Havana), are landmark eateries of Miami. Located on the Atlantic Ocean, and with a long history as a [seaport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port), Miami is also known for its seafood, with many seafood restaurants located along the [Miami River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_River_\(Florida\)) and in and around Biscayne Bay.[^cite_note-212] The city is also the headquarters of restaurant chains such as [Burger King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burger_King) and [Benihana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benihana).

### Dialect

Main article: [Miami accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_accent)

The Miami area has a unique dialect of [American English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English), commonly called the "[Miami accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_accent)", that is widely spoken. The accent developed among second- or third-generation [Hispanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic_and_Latino_Americans), including [Cuban Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Americans), whose first language was English. Some [non-Hispanic white](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Hispanic_whites), [black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_people) and [other races](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Race_and_ethnicity_in_the_United_States) who were born and raised in the Miami area have tended to adopt it as well.[^cite_note-213]

It is based on a fairly [standard American accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_American) but with some changes, very similar to dialects in the [Mid-Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-Atlantic_\(United_States\)), especially those in the [New York area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City_English) and [Northern New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_English), including [New York Latino English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Latino_English). Unlike [Virginia Piedmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piedmont_region_of_Virginia), Coastal Southern American, Northeast American dialects and [Florida Cracker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Cracker) dialect, "Miami accent" is [rhotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhoticity_in_English). It incorporates a rhythm and pronunciation heavily influenced by Spanish, where rhythm is [syllable-timed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isochrony#syllable_timing).[^cite_note-214]

This is a native dialect of English, not learner English or [interlanguage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlanguage). It is possible to differentiate this variety from an interlanguage spoken by second-language speakers, in that the "Miami accent" does *not* generally display the following features: there is no [addition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epenthesis) of /ɛ/ before initial consonant clusters with /s/, speakers do not confuse /dʒ/ with /j/, (e.g., *Yale* with *jail*), and /r/ and /rr/ are pronounced as [alveolar approximant \[ɹ\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alveolar_approximant) instead of [alveolar tap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alveolar_tap) \[ɾ] or [alveolar trill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alveolar_trill) \[r] in Spanish.[^cite_note-215][^cite_note-216][^cite_note-217][^cite_note-218]

## Beaches and parks



[Bayfront Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayfront_Park) on [Biscayne Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biscayne_Bay), February 2017

The City of Miami has various lands operated by the [National Park Service](161535.md), the Florida Division of Recreation and Parks, and the City of Miami Department of Parks and Recreation.

Miami's tropical weather allows for year-round outdoor activities.[^cite_note-243] Miami has numerous marinas, rivers, bays, canals, and the Atlantic Ocean, which make boating, canoeing, sailing, and fishing popular outdoor activities. Biscayne Bay has numerous [coral reefs](87410.md) that make [snorkeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snorkeling) and [scuba diving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scuba_diving) popular. There are over 80 parks and gardens in the city.[^cite_note-244] The largest and most popular parks are [Bayfront Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayfront_Park) and [Museum Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_Park_\(Miami\)) (located in the heart of [Downtown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Downtown_Miami) and the location of the [Miami-Dade Arena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_Arena) and [Bayside Marketplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayside_Marketplace)), [Tropical Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_Park), [Peacock Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peacock_Park), [Virginia Key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_Key), and [Watson Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watson_Island).[^cite_note-245][^cite_note-246][^cite_note-247][^cite_note-248][^cite_note-249]

Other popular cultural destinations in or near Miami include [Zoo Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoo_Miami),[^cite_note-250] [Jungle Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungle_Island),[^cite_note-251] the [Miami Seaquarium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Seaquarium),[^cite_note-252] [Monkey Jungle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monkey_Jungle),[^cite_note-253] [Coral Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral_Castle),[^cite_note-254] [Charles Deering Estate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Deering_Estate),[^cite_note-255] [Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairchild_Tropical_Botanic_Garden), and [Key Biscayne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_Biscayne).[^cite_note-256][^cite_note-257]

The City of Miami was analyzed to have a median park size of 2.6 acres, park land as percent of city area of 6.5%, 87% of residents living within a 10-minute walk of a park, \$48.39 spending per capita of park services, and 1.3 playgrounds per 10,000 residents.[^cite_note-258]

## Law and government

Main article: [Government of the City of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_the_City_of_Miami)

See also: [List of mayors of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mayors_of_Miami)



[Miami City Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_City_Hall), located at [Dinner Key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinner_Key) in [Coconut Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coconut_Grove), is home to Miami's primary administrative offices.



Miami's new logo

The government of the City of Miami uses the [mayor-commissioner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor-council_government) type of system. The city commission consists of five commissioners that are elected from single member districts. The city commission constitutes the governing body with powers to pass ordinances, adopt regulations, and exercise all powers conferred upon the city in the city charter. The mayor is elected at large and appoints a city manager. The City of Miami is governed by Mayor [Eileen Higgins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eileen_Higgins) and 5 city commissioners that oversee the five districts in the city.[^cite_note-259]

The commission's regular meetings are held at [Miami City Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_City_Hall), which is located at 3500 Pan American Drive on [Dinner Key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinner_Key) in the neighborhood of [Coconut Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coconut_Grove,_Miami,_Florida). In the [United States House of Representatives](19468510.md), Miami is represented by [Republicans](32070.md) [Maria Elvira Salazar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Elvira_Salazar) and [Mario Diaz-Balart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Diaz-Balart), along with [Democrat](5043544.md) [Frederica Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederica_Wilson).

## Politics

For much of the 20th century, Miami and its surrounding area, [Miami-Dade County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_County,_Florida), were solidly [Democratic](5043544.md), reflecting the city's diverse population and liberal political leanings. However, in recent election cycles, a notable shift has occurred. The 2020 presidential election marked a turning point, with Trump making substantial gains among Hispanic voters, especially within the Cuban-American community. This shift was reflected in Miami-Dade County, where Trump only lost the county by seven points and lost the city by 19 points, a 21-point shift to the right from 2016.[^cite_note-260] Then in the [2022 Florida gubernatorial election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Florida_gubernatorial_election), [Ron DeSantis](36729527.md) won the county for the first time for a [Republican](32070.md) candidate since 2002 and only lost the city of Miami by one-and-a-half points.[^cite_note-261] That trend continued in the [2024 presidential election in Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_States_presidential_election_in_Florida), with Trump winning the county by eleven points and losing the city by less than a point,[^cite_note-Country-262] a rare occurrence for a Republican presidential candidate. This was the first time a Republican candidate had won Miami-Dade County since [1988](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_United_States_presidential_election_in_Florida), a major political transformation in what had traditionally been a Democratic stronghold.[^cite_note-263] Trump came within 1,000 votes of flipping the city of Miami.[^cite_note-264]

In the 1970s and 1980s, the [Non-Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Group) had a dominant influence on local government affairs.[^cite_note-The_38-265]

### Primary and secondary schools

Main article: [Miami-Dade County Public Schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_County_Public_Schools)



[Miami Senior High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Senior_High_School), founded in 1903, was Miami's first high school.

Public schools in Miami are governed by [Miami-Dade County Public Schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_County_Public_Schools), which is the largest school district in Florida and the fourth-largest in the United States. In September 2008 it had a student enrollment of 385,655 and over 392 schools and centers. The district is the largest minority public school system in the country, with 60% of its students being of Hispanic origin, 28% [Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_American) or [West Indian American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Indian_American), 10% [White (non-Hispanic)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_American) and 2% non-white of other minorities.[^cite_note-287]

The Miami city limits is home to several key high schools: [Design and Architecture High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design_and_Architecture_High_School), [MAST Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MAST_Academy), [Coral Reef High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral_Reef_High_School), and the [New World School of the Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_World_School_of_the_Arts). M-DCPS is also one of a few public school districts in the United States to offer optional [bilingual education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilingual_education) in Spanish, French, German, [Haitian Creole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Creole), and [Mandarin Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Chinese).

Miami is home to several well-known Roman Catholic, Jewish and non-denominational private schools. The [Archdiocese of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archdiocese_of_Miami) operates the area's Catholic private schools, which include [Immaculata-Lasalle High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immaculata-Lasalle_High_School) (in the Miami city limits), [St. Theresa School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Theresa_School_\(Coral_Gables,_Florida\)) (Coral Gables), [Monsignor Edward Pace High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monsignor_Edward_Pace_High_School) (Miami Gardens), and [St. Brendan High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Brendan_High_School) (in Westchester), among numerous other Catholic elementary and high schools.[^cite_note-288][^cite_note-289][^cite_note-290] [Archbishop Curley-Notre Dame High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_Curley-Notre_Dame_High_School) was in the Miami city limits until its closure in 2016.[^cite_note-291]

Catholic preparatory schools operated by religious orders in the area are [Belen Jesuit Preparatory School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belen_Jesuit_Preparatory_School) (Tamiami) and [Christopher Columbus High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Columbus_High_School_\(Miami-Dade_County,_Florida\)) (Westchester) for boys and [Carrollton School of the Sacred Heart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrollton_School_of_the_Sacred_Heart) (Miami city limits) and [Our Lady of Lourdes Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_Lady_of_Lourdes_Academy) (Ponce-Davis) for girls.[^cite_note-292][^cite_note-293][^cite_note-294][^cite_note-295]

### Expressways and roads



[Venetian Causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_Causeway) (left) and [MacArthur Causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacArthur_Causeway) (right) connect [Downtown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Downtown_Miami) and [South Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Beach).



[State Road 886](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_State_Road_886), also known as Port Boulevard, connects [Downtown Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Downtown_Miami) and [PortMiami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PortMiami) over [Biscayne Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biscayne_Bay).

Miami's road system is based along the numerical Miami grid where [Flagler Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flagler_Street) forms the east–west [baseline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseline_\(surveying\)) and [Miami Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Avenue) forms the north–south [meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_\(geography\)). The corner of Flagler Street and Miami Avenue is in the middle of Downtown in front of the Downtown [Macy's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macy's) (formerly the [Burdine's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burdine's) headquarters). The Miami grid is primarily numerical so that, for example, all street addresses north of Flagler Street and west of Miami Avenue have "NW" in their address. Because its point of origin is in Downtown, which is close to the coast, the "NW" and "SW" quadrants are much larger than the "SE" and "NE" quadrants. Many roads, especially major ones, are also named (e.g., [Tamiami Trail/SW 8th St](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamiami_Trail)), although, with exceptions, the number is in more common usage among locals.

With few exceptions, within this grid north–south roads are designated as Courts, Roads, Avenues or Places (often remembered by their acronym), while east–west roads are streets, Terraces, Drives or occasionally Ways. Major roads in each direction are located at one mile intervals. There are 16 [blocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_block) to each mile on north–south avenues, and 10 blocks to each mile on east–west streets. Major north–south avenues generally end in "7" – e.g., 17th, 27th, 37th/Douglas Aves., 57th/Red Rd., 67th/Ludlam, 87th/Galloway, etc., all the way west beyond 177th/Krome Avenue. One prominent exception is 42nd Avenue, LeJeune Road, located at the half-mile point instead.

Major east–west streets to the south of Downtown are multiples of 16, though the beginning point of this system is at SW 8th St, one half-mile south of Flagler ("zeroth") Street. Thus, major streets are at 8th St., 24th St./Coral Way, 40th St./Bird, 56th/Miller, 72nd/ Sunset, 88th/N. Kendall, 104th (originally S. Kendall), 120th/Montgomery, 136th/Howard, 152nd/Coral Reef, 168th/Richmond, 184th/Eureka, 200th/Quail Roost, 216th/Hainlin Mill, 232nd/Silver Palm, 248th/Coconut Palm, etc., well into the 300s. Within the grid, odd-numbered addresses are generally on the north or east side, and even-numbered addresses are on the south or west side.

All streets and avenues in [Miami-Dade County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_County) follow the Miami grid, with a few exceptions, most notably in [Coral Gables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral_Gables,_Florida), [Hialeah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hialeah), Coconut Grove and [Miami Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Beach,_Florida). One neighborhood, [The Roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Roads), is named as such because its streets run off the Miami grid at a 45-degree angle, and therefore are all named roads.

[Miami-Dade County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_County) is served by four Interstate Highways ([I-75](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_75_\(Florida\)), [I-95](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_95_in_Florida), [I-195](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_195_\(Florida\)), [I-395](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_395_\(Florida\))) and several U.S. Highways including [U.S. Route 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_1_\(Florida\)), [U.S. Route 27](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_27_\(Florida\)), [U.S. Route 41](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_41_\(Florida\)), and [U.S. Route 441](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_441_\(Florida\)).

Miami has six major [causeways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causeway) that span over Biscayne Bay connecting the western mainland, with the eastern barrier islands along the Atlantic Ocean. The [Rickenbacker Causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rickenbacker_Causeway) is the southernmost causeway and connects Brickell to [Virginia Key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_Key) and [Key Biscayne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_Biscayne,_Florida). The [Venetian Causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_Causeway) and [MacArthur Causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacArthur_Causeway) connect [Downtown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Downtown_Miami) with [South Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Beach). The [Julia Tuttle Causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_Tuttle_Causeway) connects [Midtown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midtown_Miami) and [Miami Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Beach). The [79th Street Causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_State_Road_934) connects the [Upper East Side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Eastside,_Miami,_Florida) with [North Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Beach_\(Miami_Beach\)). The northernmost causeway, the [Broad Causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broad_Causeway), is the smallest of Miami's six causeways and connects [North Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Miami,_Florida) to [Bay Harbor Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_Harbor_Islands) and [Bal Harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bal_Harbour,_Florida).

### Public transportation

See also: [Metrobus (Miami-Dade County)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrobus_\(Miami-Dade_County\)), [Metromover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metromover), and [Miami-Dade Transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_Transit)



Miami's [Metrorail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrorail_\(Miami-Dade_County\)) is the city's [rapid transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapid_transit) system and connects Miami with its outlying suburbs.



[Tri-Rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tri-Rail) is Miami's [commuter rail](6286.md) that runs north–south from Miami's suburbs in [West Palm Beach](109634.md) to [Miami International Airport](128968.md).

[Public transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_transportation) in Miami is operated by [Miami-Dade Transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_Transit) and [SFRTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Florida_Regional_Transportation_Authority), and includes [commuter rail](6286.md) ([Tri-Rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tri-Rail)), heavy-rail [rapid transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapid_transit) ([Metrorail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrorail_\(Miami-Dade_County\))), an elevated [people mover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People_mover) ([Metromover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metromover)), and buses ([Metrobus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_Transit#Metrobus)). Miami has Florida's highest transit ridership as about 17% of Miamians use transit on a daily basis.[^cite_note-313] The average Miami public transit commute on weekdays is 90 minutes, while 39% of public transit riders commute for more than 2 hours a day. The average wait time at a public transit stop or station is 18 minutes, while 37% of riders wait for more than 20 minutes on average every day. The average single trip distance with public transit is 7.46 mi (12 km), while 38% travel more than 8.08 mi (13 km) in each direction.[^cite_note-314]

Miami's heavy-rail rapid transit system, [Metrorail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrorail_\(Miami-Dade_County\)), is an elevated system comprising two lines and 23 stations on a 24.4-mile (39.3 km)-long line. Metrorail connects the urban western suburbs of [Hialeah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hialeah,_Florida), [Medley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medley,_Florida), and inner-city Miami with suburban [The Roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Roads), [Coconut Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coconut_Grove), [Coral Gables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral_Gables,_Florida), [South Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Miami), and urban [Kendall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kendall,_Florida) via the central business districts of [Miami International Airport](128968.md), the [Health District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_District_\(Miami\)), and Downtown. A free, elevated [people mover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People_mover), [Metromover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metromover), operates 21 stations on three different lines in greater Downtown Miami, with a station at roughly every two blocks of Downtown and Brickell. Several expansion projects are being funded by a transit development sales tax surcharge throughout Miami-Dade County.[^cite_note-315]

[Tri-Rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tri-Rail), a commuter rail system operated by the [South Florida Regional Transportation Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Florida_Regional_Transportation_Authority) (SFRTA), runs from [Miami International Airport](128968.md) northward to [West Palm Beach](109634.md), making eighteen stops throughout [Miami-Dade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_County), [Broward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broward_County), and [Palm Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm_Beach_County) counties.[^cite_note-316]

The [Miami Intermodal Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Intermodal_Center) is a massive transportation hub servicing [Metrorail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrorail_\(Miami-Dade_County\)), [Amtrak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amtrak), [Tri-Rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tri-Rail), [Brightline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brightline), [Metrobus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_Transit#Metrobus), [Greyhound Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greyhound_Lines), [taxis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxicab), [rental cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rental_cars), [MIA Mover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIA_Mover), private automobiles, bicycles and pedestrians adjacent to Miami International Airport. Miami Intermodal Center was completed in 2010, and is serving about 150,000 commuters and travelers in the Miami area. Phase I of [MiamiCentral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MiamiCentral) Station was completed in 2012, and the Tri-Rail part of Phase II was completed in 2015, but the construction of the Amtrak part remains delayed.

Two new light rail systems, Baylink and the Miami Streetcar, have been proposed and are currently in the planning stage. BayLink would connect Downtown with [South Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Beach), and the Miami Streetcar would connect Downtown with [Midtown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midtown_Miami).

Miami is the southern terminus of [Amtrak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amtrak)'s Atlantic Coast services, running two lines, the [Silver Meteor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_Meteor) and the [Silver Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_Star_\(Amtrak_train\)), both terminating in New York City. The Miami Amtrak Station is located in the suburb of [Hialeah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hialeah) near the [Tri-Rail/Metrorail Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tri-Rail_and_Metrorail_transfer_station) on NW 79 St and NW 38 Ave. Current construction of the [MiamiCentral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MiamiCentral) Station will move all Amtrak operations from its current out-of-the-way location to a centralized location with [Metrorail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Metrorail), [MIA Mover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIA_Mover), [Tri-Rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tri-Rail), [Miami International Airport](128968.md), and the [Miami Intermodal Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Intermodal_Center) all within the same station closer to Downtown. The station was expected to be completed by 2012,[^cite_note-317] but experienced several delays and was later expected to be completed in late 2014,[^cite_note-318] again pushed back to early 2015.[^cite_note-MICjanuary-319]

### Airports

See also: [Miami International Airport](128968.md), [Fort Lauderdale-Hollywood International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Lauderdale-Hollywood_International_Airport), and [Palm Beach International Airport](287471.md)



[Miami International Airport](128968.md), the nation's 10th-largest airport

[Miami International Airport](128968.md) serves as the primary international airport of the Greater Miami Area. One of the busiest international airports in the world because of its centric location, Miami International Airport caters to over 45 million passengers a year. The airport is a major hub and the largest international gateway for [American Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Airlines).[^cite_note-miami-airport.com-320]

Miami International is the second busiest airport by passenger traffic in Florida, the United States' third-largest international port of entry for foreign air passengers, behind New York's [John F. Kennedy International Airport](97218.md) and [Los Angeles International Airport](18131.md). The airport's extensive international route network includes non-stop flights to over seventy international cities in North and [South America](26769.md), [Europe](9239.md), [Africa](5334607.md), [Asia](689.md), and the [Middle East](19323.md).[^cite_note-miami-airport.com-320]

Nearby [Fort Lauderdale–Hollywood International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Lauderdale%E2%80%93Hollywood_International_Airport) and [Palm Beach International Airport](287471.md) also serve commercial traffic in the Miami area.[^cite_note-321] [Miami-Opa Locka Executive Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Opa_Locka_Executive_Airport) in [Opa-locka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opa-locka,_Florida) and [Miami Executive Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Executive_Airport) in an unincorporated area southwest of Miami serve general aviation traffic in the Miami area.
