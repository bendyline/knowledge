The **economy of Hungary** is a [developing](78449.md),[^cite_note-devgroup-1] high-income [mixed economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_economy) that is the [53rd-largest economy in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_\(PPP\)) (out of 188 countries measured by [IMF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IMF)) with \$265.037 billion annual output,[^cite_note-imf2-29] and ranks [41st in the world in terms of GDP per capita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_\(PPP\)_per_capita) measured by [purchasing power parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purchasing_power_parity). [Hungary](13275.md) has a [very high human development index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_Human_Development_Index) and a skilled [labour force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_force), with the [22nd lowest income inequality by Gini index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_income_equality) in the world. Hungary has an export-oriented [market economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_economy) with a heavy emphasis on [foreign trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_trade); thus the country is the [35th largest export economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_exports) in the world. The country had more than \$100 billion of exports in 2015, with a high [trade surplus](4810.md) of \$9.003 billion, of which 79% went to the [European Union](9317.md) (EU) and 21% was extra-EU trade.[^cite_note-30] Hungary's productive capacity is more than 80% [privately owned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_sector), with [39.1% overall taxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_tax_revenue_as_percentage_of_GDP), which funds the country's [welfare economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_state). On the expenditure side, [household consumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Household_consumption) is the main component of [GDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GDP) and accounts for 50% of its total, followed by gross fixed [capital formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_formation) with 22% and [government expenditure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_expenditure) with 20%.[^cite_note-31]

In 2015 Hungary attracted \$119.8 billion in [FDI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_direct_investment) and invested more than \$50 billion abroad.[^cite_note-32] As of 2015, the key trading partners of Hungary were Germany, Austria, Romania, Slovakia, France, Italy, Poland and the Czech Republic.[^cite_note-33] Major industries include food processing, pharmaceuticals, motor vehicles, information technology, chemicals, metallurgy, machinery, electrical goods, and tourism (in 2014 Hungary received 12.1 million international tourists).[^cite_note-34] Hungary is the largest electronics producer in [Central and Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_and_Eastern_Europe). [Electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics) manufacturing and research are among the main drivers of [innovation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innovation) and economic growth in the country. In the past 20 years Hungary has also grown into a major center for [mobile technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_technology), [information security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_security), and related hardware research.[^cite_note-35]

The [employment rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employment_rate) in the economy was 68.7% in January 2017,[^cite_note-ksh.hu-36] while the employment structure shows the characteristics of [post-industrial economies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-industrial_society). An estimated 63.2% of the employed workforce work in the service sector, industry contributed by 29.7%, while agriculture employed 7.1%. The unemployment rate was 3.8% in September–November 2017,[^cite_note-ReferenceA-37] down from 11% during the [Great Recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession). Hungary is part of the [European single market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_single_market), which represents more than 448 million consumers. Several domestic commercial policies are determined by agreements among European Union members and by EU legislation.

Large [Hungarian companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_companies_of_Hungary) are included in the [BUX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BUX), the Hungarian stock market index listed on [Budapest Stock Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budapest_Stock_Exchange). Well-known companies include [Graphisoft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphisoft), [Magyar Telekom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magyar_Telekom), [MBH Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MBH_Bank), [MOL Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOL_Group), [Opus Global](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opus_Global), [OTP Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OTP_Bank), [RÁBA Automotive Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%A1ba_\(company\)), [Gedeon Richter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gedeon_Richter_\(company\)) and [Zwack Unicum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zwack). Hungary also has a large number of specialised [small and medium enterprises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_and_medium_enterprise), for example many automotive industry suppliers and technology [start ups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Startup_company).[^cite_note-38]

[Budapest](36787.md) is the financial and business capital of Hungary. The capital is a significant economic hub, classified as an Beta [world city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_city) in the study by the [Globalization and World Cities Research Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globalization_and_World_Cities_Research_Network) and it is the second fastest-developing [urban economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_economy) in Europe. The per capita GDP in the city increased by 2.4% and employment by 4.7% compared to the previous year, 2014.[^cite_note-39] On the national level, Budapest is the [primary city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primate_city) of Hungary for business, accounting for 39% of the national income. The city had a [gross metropolitan product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_metropolitan_product) of more than \$100 billion in 2015, making it one of the largest regional economies in the [European Union](9317.md).[^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41] Budapest is also among the top 100 GDP performing cities in the world, as measured by [PricewaterhouseCoopers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PricewaterhouseCoopers). In a global city competitiveness ranking by the [Economist Intelligence Unit](15303116.md), Budapest is ranked above [Tel Aviv](31453.md), [Lisbon](18091.md), [Moscow](19004.md) and [Johannesburg](58893.md), among others.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43]

Hungary maintains its own currency, the [Hungarian forint](229795.md) (HUF), although the economy fulfills the [Maastricht criteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maastricht_criteria) with the exception of public debt. The ratio of public debt to GDP is [significantly below the EU average](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_public_debt) at 66.4% in 2019. The [Hungarian National Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_National_Bank) was founded in 1924, after the dissolution of the [Austro-Hungarian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_Empire). It is currently focusing on price stability, with an inflation target of 3%.[^cite_note-44]

The economy of Hungary is a high-income mixed economy, and a member of the European Union's single market. In recent years, it has become one of the faster-growing economies in the EU, transitioning towards an export-oriented market economy with a strong focus on foreign trade and investment, particularly in the automotive and electronics sectors.

According to the International Monetary Fund (IMF), Hungary's estimated annual output was \$219 billion (nominal GDP) in 2024, ranking it as the 57th-largest economy in the world. In terms of GDP per capita measured by purchasing power parity (PPP), it ranked 42nd globally at approximately \$47,213.[^cite_note-IMF_WEO_Apr20242-45]

Hungary maintains a very high Human Development Index, ranking 47th in the 2023/24 report, and possesses a skilled labour force.[^cite_note-46] The country has one of the lowest income inequalities in the EU, with a Gini coefficient of 29.6 in 2023.[^cite_note-47] The economy is heavily reliant on exports, primarily to other EU nations. In 2023, its goods exports reached €149.2 billion, generating a significant trade surplus of €9.8 billion.[^cite_note-48] On the expenditure side, household consumption accounts for approximately 50% of GDP, followed by gross fixed capital formation (26%) and government expenditure (18%).[^cite_note-49]

Key industries include automobile manufacturing, battery production, electronics, pharmaceuticals, and information technology. After facing the highest inflation in the EU in 2023 (averaging 17.1%), policy measures successfully brought the rate down to a forecasted 3.5% for 2025.[^cite_note-IMF_WEO_Apr20242-45] The unemployment rate remained low at 4.1% in early 2025, while the government debt-to-GDP ratio was projected to be around 72.0%.[^cite_note-IMF_WEO_Apr20242-45] Budapest, the capital, serves as the nation's primary financial and business hub.

The Hungarian economy prior to [World War II](32927.md) was primarily oriented toward agriculture and small-scale manufacturing. Hungary's strategic position in [Europe](9239.md) and its relative high lack of natural resources also have dictated a traditional reliance on foreign trade. For instance, its largest car manufacturer, [Magomobil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magomobil) (maker of the *Magosix*), produced a total of a few thousand units.[^cite_note-50] In the early 1920s the textile industry began to expand rapidly, by 1928 it became the most important industry in the foreign trade of Hungary exporting textile goods worth more than 60 million pengős in that year. Companies like [MÁVAG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%81VAG) exported locomotives to [India](14533.md) and [South-America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South-America), its [locomotive no. 601](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%81V_Class_601) was the largest and most powerful in [Europe](9239.md) at the time.

### Post-war Hungarian communism

Further information: [Hungarian People's Republic § Economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_People's_Republic#Economy), and [Goulash Communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goulash_Communism)

From the late 1940s, the [Communist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist) government started to nationalize the industry. At first, only factories with more than 100 workers were nationalized; later, this limit was reduced to only 10. In the agriculture, the government started a successful program of [collectivization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectivization_in_Hungary). From the early 1950s, more and more new factories were built. This rapid and forced industrialization followed the standard [Stalinist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalinist) pattern in an effort to encourage a more self-sufficient economy. Most economic activity was conducted by state-owned enterprises or cooperatives and state farms. In 1968, [Stalinist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalinist) self-sufficiency was replaced by the "[New Economic Mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Economic_Mechanism)", which reopened Hungary to foreign trade, gave limited freedom to the workings of the market, and allowed a limited number of small businesses to operate in the services sector.&#x20;

In this era great social progress was made, for example the population between 20 and 24 years old with less than 8 years of education decreased from 71.2% in 1949 to 4.9% in 1984, the annual consumption of meat and fish increased from 35 kilograms in 1950 to 78 in 1984, the percentage of homes with electricity increased from 46.6% in 1949 to 99% in 1984, in the same period the percentage of homes with running water increased from 17.1% to 76.6%, the number of cars per 1,000 people increased from 3 in 1960 to 122 in 1984, in the case of televisions from 10 to 276, in the case of washing machines from 45 to 317, in the case of refrigerators from 4 to 328, infant mortality (per 1,000 live births) fell from 91 in 1949 to 20.4 in 1984.[^cite_note-auto3-51]

Per capita national income grew by 343% from 1950 to 1983 (4.6% per year), real wages by 136% and real incomes by 251% over the same period, meanwhile real value of per capita social benefits rose by 432% from 1960 to 1980. From 1949 to 1984, Hungary's GDP grew as fast as that of countries like Spain.[^cite_note-auto3-51][^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53]

Also poverty was greatly reduced, although from 1962 to 1982 the price index rose by 96%, people with less than 800 forints were 55% in 1962 and after 20 years only 6.4% of those with less than 1800 forints. In 1987, inequality was minimal, with a Gini coefficient of 0.21, and poverty was the same, with a 1% poverty rate (poverty line: \$120 PPP per person per month).[^cite_note-auto3-51][^cite_note-54]

Although Hungary enjoyed one of the most liberal and economically advanced economies of the former [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc) even though at the end of the 1980s state employment represented 94% of the total, both agriculture and industry began to suffer from a lack of investment in the 1970s, and Hungary's net foreign debt rose significantly—from \$1 billion in 1973 to \$15 billion in 1993—due largely to consumer subsidies and unprofitable state enterprises. In the face of economic stagnation, Hungary opted to liberalize further by passing a joint venture law, instating an income tax, and joining the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md) ([IMF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IMF)) and the [World Bank](36752.md). By 1988, Hungary had developed a [two-tier banking system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-tier_banking_system), and had enacted significant corporate legislation that paved the way for the ambitious market-oriented reforms of the post-communist years.

### Transition to a market economy (1990–1995)



ING headquarters in Budapest

After the fall of communism, the former Eastern Bloc had to transition from a one-party, centrally [planned economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planned_economy) to a market economy with a [multi-party political system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-party_political_system). With the [collapse of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collapse_of_the_Soviet_Union), the Eastern Bloc countries suffered a significant loss in both markets for goods, and subsidizing from the Soviet Union.[^cite_note-55] Hungary, for example, "lost nearly 70% of its export markets in Eastern and Central Europe." The loss of external markets in Hungary left "800,000 unemployed people because all the unprofitable and unsalvageable factories had been closed."[^cite_note-es-56] Another form of Soviet subsidizing that greatly affected Hungary after the fall of communism was the loss of social welfare programs. Because of the lack of subsidies and a need to reduce expenditures, many social programs in Hungary had to be cut in an attempt to lower spending. As a result, many people in Hungary suffered incredible hardships during the transition to a market economy. Following [privatization](24661.md) and tax reductions on Hungarian businesses, unemployment suddenly rose to 12% in 1991 (it was 1.7% in 1990 ), gradually decreasing until 2001. Economic growth, after a fall in 1991 to −11.9%, gradually grew until the end of the 1990s at an average annual rate of 4.2%. With the stabilization of the new market economy Hungary experienced growth in [foreign investment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_direct_investment),[^cite_note-megginson01-57][^cite_note-mihalyi01-58] with a "cumulative foreign direct investment totaling more than \$60 billion since 1989."[^cite_note-59]

The [Antall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antall) government of 1990–94 began market reforms with price and trade liberation measures, a revamped tax system, and a nascent market-based banking system. By 1994, however, the costs of government overspending and hesitant privatization had become clearly visible. Cuts in consumer subsidies led to increases in the price of food, medicine, transportation services, and energy. Reduced exports to the former Soviet bloc and shrinking industrial output contributed to a sharp decline in GDP. Unemployment rose rapidly to about 12% in 1993. The external debt burden, one of the highest in Europe, reached 250% of annual export earnings, while the budget and current account deficits approached 10% of GDP. The [devaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devaluation) of the currency (in order to support exports), without effective stabilization measures, such as [indexation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indexation) of wages, provoked an extremely high inflation rate, that in 1991 reached 35% and slightly decreased until 1994, growing again in 1995. In March 1995, the government of Prime Minister [Gyula Horn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyula_Horn) implemented an austerity program, coupled with aggressive privatization of state-owned enterprises and an export-promoting exchange raw regime, to reduce indebtedness, cut the current account deficit, and shrink public spending. By the end of 1997 the consolidated public sector deficit decreased to 4.6% of GDP—with public sector spending falling from 62% of GDP to below 50%—the current account deficit was reduced to 2% of GDP, and government debt was paid down to 94% of annual export earnings.



[Megyeri Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megyeri_Bridge)

The Government of Hungary no longer requires IMF financial assistance and has repaid all of its debt to the fund. Consequently, Hungary enjoys favorable borrowing terms. Hungary's sovereign foreign currency debt issuance carries investment-grade ratings from all major credit-rating agencies, although recently the country was downgraded by Moody's, S\&P and remains on negative outlook at Fitch. In 1995 Hungary's currency, the Forint (HUF), became convertible for all current account transactions, and subsequent to [OECD](33853117.md) membership in 1996, for almost all capital account transactions as well. Since 1995, Hungary has pegged the forint against a basket of currencies (in which the U.S. dollar is 30%), and the central rate against the basket is devalued at a preannounced rate, originally set at 0.8% per month, the Forint is now an entirely free-floating currency. The government privatization program ended on schedule in 1998: 80% of GDP is now produced by the private sector,[^cite_note-\:0-14] and foreign owners control 70% of financial institutions, 66% of industry, 90% of telecommunications, and 50% of the trading sector.



Kőröshegy Viaduct

After Hungary's GDP declined about 18% from 1990 to 1993 and grew only 1%–1.5% up to 1996, strong [export performance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Export_performance) propelled GDP growth to 4.4% in 1997, with other macroeconomic indicators similarly improving. These successes allowed the government to concentrate in 1996 and 1997 on major structural reforms such as the implementation of a fully funded pension system (partly modelled after [Chile's pension system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chile_pension_system) with major modifications), reform of higher education, and the creation of a national treasury. Remaining economic challenges include reducing fiscal deficits and inflation, maintaining stable external balances, and completing structural reforms of the tax system, health care, and local government financing. Recently, the overriding goal of Hungarian economic policy has been to prepare the country for entry into the European Union, which it joined in late 2004.&#x20;

Prior to the change of regime in 1989, 65% of Hungary's trade was with [Comecon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comecon) countries. By the end of 1997, Hungary had shifted much of its trade to the West. Trade with EU countries now comprises 80% of the total.[^cite_note-\:1-60] Germany is Hungary's single most important trading partner. The US has become Hungary's sixth-largest export market, while Hungary is ranked as the 72nd largest export market for the U.S. Bilateral trade between the two countries increased 46% in 1997 to more than \$1 billion. The U.S. has extended to Hungary most-favored-nation status, the Generalized System of Preferences, Overseas Private Investment Corporation insurance, and access to the [Export-Import Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Export-Import_Bank_of_the_United_States).

With about \$18 billion in foreign direct investment (FDI) since 1989, Hungary has attracted over one-third of all FDI in central and eastern Europe, including the former Soviet Union. Of this, about \$6 billion came from American companies. Foreign capital is attracted by skilled and relatively inexpensive labor, tax incentives, modern infrastructure, and a good telecommunications system.[^cite_note-61]

By 2006 Hungary's economic outlook had deteriorated. Wage growth had kept up with other nations in the region; however, this growth has largely been driven by increased government spending. This resulted in the budget deficit ballooning to over 10% of GDP and inflation rates predicted to exceed 6%. [Nouriel Roubini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nouriel_Roubini), an economist in the Clinton administration, said that "Hungary is an accident waiting to happen."[^cite_note-62]

### Privatization

In January 1990, the State Privatization Agency (SPA, *Állami Vagyonügynökség*) was established to manage the first steps of [privatization](24661.md). Because of Hungary's \$21.2 billion [foreign debt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_debt), the government decided to sell state property instead of distributing it to the people for free.[^cite_note-3541privatization_in_hun-63] The SPA was attacked by populist groups because several companies' management had the right to find buyers and discuss sale terms with them thus "stealing" the company. Another reason for discontent was that the state offered large tax subsidies and environmental investments, which sometimes cost more than the selling price of the company. Along with the acquisition of companies, foreign investors launched many "greenfield investments".[^cite_note-3541privatization_in_hun-63]

The center-right [Hungarian Democratic Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Democratic_Forum) government of 1990–1994 decided to abolish agricultural co-operatives by splitting them up and giving machinery and land to their former members.[^cite_note-3458strucutural_problems-64] The government also introduced a Recompensation Law which offered vouchers to people who had owned land before it was nationalized in 1948. These people (or their descendants) could exchange their vouchers for land previously owned by agricultural co-operatives, who were forced to give up some of their land for this purpose.[^cite_note-3458strucutural_problems-64]

Small stores and retail businesses were privatized between 1990 and 1994, however, [greenfield investments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenfield_investment) by foreign retail companies like [Tesco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesco), [Cora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cora_\(hypermarket\)) and [IKEA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IKEA) had a much bigger economic impact.[^cite_note-3541privatization_in_hun-63] Many public utilities, including the national telecommunications company [Matáv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mat%C3%A1v), the national oil and gas conglomerate [MOL Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOL_Group), and electricity supply and production company [MVM Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MVM_Group) were privatized as well.[^cite_note-4991privatisation_in_hun-65]

Though most banks were sold to foreign investors, the largest bank, [National Savings Bank (OTP)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OTP_Bank), remained Hungarian-owned. 20%–20% of the shares were sold to foreign institutional investors and given to the Social Security organizations, 5% were bought by employees, and 8% was offered at the [Budapest Stock Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budapest_Stock_Exchange).[^cite_note-5955political_economy_of-66]

Reaching 1995, Hungary's fiscal indices deteriorated: foreign investment fell as well as judgement of foreign analysts on economic outlook.[^cite_note-bokrosinterview-67] Due to high demand in import goods, Hungary also had a high trade deficit[^cite_note-exttrade-68] and budget gap, and it could not reach an agreement with the [IMF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IMF), either.[^cite_note-bokrosinterview-67][^cite_note-vg_hu-69] After not having a [minister of finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_finance) for more than a month, prime minister [Gyula Horn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyula_Horn) appointed [Lajos Bokros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lajos_Bokros) as Finance Minister on 1 March 1995. He introduced a string of austerity measures (the "[Bokros Package](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bokros_package)") on 12 March 1995 which had the following key points: one-time 9% devaluation of the forint, introducing a constant sliding devaluation, 8% additional customs duty on all goods except for energy sources, limitation of growth of wages in the public sector, simplified and accelerated privatization. The package also included welfare cutbacks, including abolition of free higher education and dental service; reduced family allowances, child-care benefits, and maternity payments depending on income and wealth; lowering subsidies of pharmaceuticals, and raising retirement age.

These reforms not only increased investor confidence,[^cite_note-usgovcikk-70] but they were also supported by the IMF and the World Bank,[^cite_note-konyv-71] however, they were not welcome widely by the Hungarians; Bokros broke the negative record of popularity: 9% of the population wanted to see him in an "important political position"[^cite_note-median_bokros-72] and only 4% were convinced that the reforms would "improve the country's finances in a big way"[^cite_note-bokrosinterview-67]

In 1996, the Ministry of Finance introduced a new pension system instead of the fully state-backed one: private pension savings accounts were introduced, which were 50% social security based and 50% funded.[^cite_note-konyv-71]

In 2006 Prime Minister [Ferenc Gyurcsány](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferenc_Gyurcs%C3%A1ny) was reelected on a platform promising economic "reform without austerity". However, after the elections in April 2006, the Socialist coalition under Gyurcsány unveiled a package of austerity measures which were designed to reduce the budget deficit to 3% of GDP by 2008.[^cite_note-\:2-73] Because of the austerity program, the economy of Hungary slowed down in 2007.[^cite_note-\:2-73]

### Great Recession

Declining exports, reduced domestic consumption and fixed asset accumulation affected Hungary during the [Great Recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession), making the country enter a severe recession of −6.4%, one of the worst economic contractions in its history.

On 27 October 2008, Hungary reached an agreement with the [IMF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IMF) and [EU](9317.md) for a rescue package of US\$25 billion, aiming to restore financial stability and investors' confidence.[^cite_note-8078hungary_receives_res-74]

Because of the uncertainty of the crisis, banks gave less loans which led to a decrease in investment. This along with price-awareness and fear of bankruptcy led to a fallback in consumption which then increased job losses and decreased consumption even further. Inflation did not rise significantly, but real wages decreased.[^cite_note-7679economy_and_society_-75]

The fact that the euro and the Swiss franc are worth a lot more in forints than they were before affected a lot of people. According to The Daily Telegraph, "statistics show that more than 60 percent of Hungarian mortgages and car loans are denominated in foreign currencies".[^cite_note-9940global_financial_cri-76] After the election in 2010 of the new [Fidesz](404647.md)-party government of Prime Minister [Viktor Orbán](750582.md), Hungarian banks were forced to allow the conversion of foreign-currency mortgages to the forint.[^cite_note-bloomberg-20111230-77] The new government also nationalized \$13 billion of private pension-fund assets, which could then be used to support the government debt position.[^cite_note-bloomberg-20111222-78]

### Post-recession

The economy showed signs of recovery in 2011 with decreasing tax rates and a moderate 1.7 percent GDP growth.[^cite_note-79]

From November 2011 to January 2012, all three major [credit rating agencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_rating_agencies) downgraded Hungarian debt to a non-investment speculative grade, commonly called "junk status".[^cite_note-bbc-20111222-80][^cite_note-guardian-20111225-81][^cite_note-bbc-20120106-82] In part this is because of political changes creating doubts about the independence of the [Hungarian National Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_National_Bank).[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-bbc-20111222-80][^cite_note-guardian-20111225-81]

European Commission President [José Manuel Barroso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Manuel_Barroso) wrote to Prime Minister [Viktor Orbán](750582.md) stating that new central bank regulations, allowing political intervention, "seriously harm" Hungary's interests, postponing talks on a financial aid package. Orbán responded "If we don't reach an agreement, we'll still stand on our own feet."[^cite_note-bloomberg-20111230-77]

The [European Commission](9974.md) launched legal proceedings against Hungary on 17 January 2012. The procedures concern Hungary's [central bank law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_National_Bank), the retirement age for judges and prosecutors and the independence of the data protection office, respectively.[^cite_note-84][^cite_note-85] One day later Orbán indicated in a letter his willingness to find solutions to the problems raised in the infringement proceedings.[^cite_note-86] On 18 January he participated in plenary session of the [European Parliament](9581.md) which also dealt with the Hungarian case. He said "Hungary has been renewed and reorganised under European principles". He also said that the problems raised by the European Union can be resolved "easily, simply and very quickly". He added that none of the EC's objections affected Hungary's new constitution.[^cite_note-87][^cite_note-88]

Following the mild recession of 2012, the GDP picked up again from 2014, and based on the commission's Winter 2015 forecast it was projected to have accelerated to 3.3%. The more dynamic economic performance attributed to a moderately growing domestic demand and supported the growth of gross fixed capital formation. The surge (3.8% in the first half of 2014), however was only achieved via temporary measures and factors, such as the stepped-up absorption of EU-funds and the central bank's Funding for Growth Scheme, which subsidised loans for small-and medium-sized enterprises.[^cite_note-89] The fundaments of growth didn't considerably change in 2015 as well – the government supported EU-fund transfers along with the moderately successful central bank loans of economic revitalization – fueled the fair GDP growth.

### During and after the COVID-19 pandemic

The Hungarian GDP, GDP per capita, living standards and wages had been steadily rising until the start of the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md), when just like the rest of Europe, the stats above tanked. GDP fell to \$155 billion, GDP PPP has fallen to \$322 Billion, GDP per capita to \$15,855, inflation slightly rose to 4.54%.

National debt rose considerably, to around 80% Debt-to-GDP from the previous 60–65%.

The country was hard hit, unemployment was also higher than average until 2021 when the lockdowns ended. The GDP, GDP per capita, GDP PPP, unemployment and national debt have all recovered to and beyond their pre-COVID values. On the other hand, [inflation](38286.md) has risen to the record levels, reaching 24.5% in December 2022, being the highest in Europe.[^cite_note-90]

## Natural resources

Main article: [Geography of Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Hungary)



Topographic map of Hungary

Hungary's total land area is 93,030 km<sup>2</sup> along with 690 km<sup>2</sup> of water surface area which altogether makes up 1% of Europe's area.

Nearly 75% of Hungary's landscape consists of flat plains. Additional 20% of the country's area consists of foothills whose altitude is 400 m at the most; higher hills and water surface makes up the remaining 5%.

The two flat plains that take up three-quarters of Hungary's area are the [Great Hungarian Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Hungarian_Plain) and the [Little Hungarian Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Hungarian_Plain). Hungary's most significant natural resource is arable land. About 83% of the country's total territory is suitable for cultivation;[^cite_note-6601the_hungarian_agricu-91] of this portion, 75% (around 50% of the country's area) is covered by arable land, which is an outstanding ratio compared to other EU countries.[^cite_note-6601the_hungarian_agricu-91] Hungary lacks extensive domestic sources of energy and raw materials needed for further industrial development.

19% of the country is covered by forests. These are located mainly in the foothills such as the [North Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Hungarian_Mountains) and the [Transdanubian Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transdanubian_Mountains), and the [Alpokalja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpokalja). The composition of forests is various; mostly oak or beech, but the rest include fir, willow, acacia and plane.



Medicinal bath in [Hévíz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%A9v%C3%ADz)

In European terms, Hungary's underground water reserve is one of the largest. Hence the country is rich in brooks and hot springs as well as medicinal springs and spas; as of 2003, there are 1250 springs that provide water warmer than 30 °C.[^cite_note-6827a_h_v_zfelt_r_s_te-92] 90% of Hungary's drinking water is mostly retrieved from such sources.[^cite_note-6435magyarorsz_g_term_sz-93]

The major rivers of Hungary are the [Danube](21209639.md) and the [Tisza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tisza). The Danube also flows through parts of [Germany](11867.md), [Austria](26964606.md), [Slovakia](26830.md), [Serbia](29265.md), and [Romania](25445.md). It is navigable within Hungary for 418 km. The Tisza River is navigable for 444 km in the country. Hungary has three major lakes. [Lake Balaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Balaton), the largest, is 78 km long and from 3 to 14 km wide, with an area of 592 km<sup>2</sup>. Lake Balaton is Central Europe's largest lake and a prosperous tourist spot and recreation area. Its shallow waters offer summer bathing and during the winter its frozen surface provides facilities for winter sports. Smaller bodies of water include [Lake Velence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Velence) (26 km<sup>2</sup>) in Fejér County and [Lake Fertő](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Fert%C5%91) (82 km<sup>2</sup> within Hungary).

The waters of the country are in danger, since more water is going out of the county than in. In 2022, a severe drought which has impacted all of Europe is endangering among others, Lake Velence, Lake Balaton, the Danube and Tisza rivers. The flow of the aforementioned rivers have been modified in the late 19th century, and while it prevented larger floods and helped water transport, it has its downsides, such as the inability to support large-scale watering of crops, worsening the drought of 2022. Large-scale reforms and rebuilding of the Alföld water system is needed, but no such plans are being announced by the government as of 2022.

### Agriculture

See also: [Honey production in Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honey_production_in_Hungary) and [Hungarian wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_wine)



[Tokaj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokaj-Hegyalja) vineyard with ripening grapes

Agriculture accounted for 4.3% of GDP in 2008[^cite_note-1404value_and_distributi-95] and along with the food industry occupied roughly 7.7% of the labor force.[^cite_note-7495number_of_employed_p-96] These two figures represent only the primary agricultural production: along with related businesses, agriculture makes up about 13% of the GDP.[^cite_note-6601the_hungarian_agricu-91] Hungarian agriculture is virtually self-sufficient and due to traditional reasons export-oriented:[^cite_note-6601the_hungarian_agricu-91] exports related to agriculture make up 20–25% of the total. About half of Hungary's total land area is agricultural area under cultivation; this ratio is prominent among other EU members.[^cite_note-6601the_hungarian_agricu-91] This is due to the country's favorable conditions including [continental climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_climate) and the [plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Hungarian_Plain) that make up about half of Hungary's landscape. The most important crops are wheat, corn, sunflower, potato, sugar beet, canola and a wide variety of fruits (notably apple, peach, pear, grape, watermelon, plum etc.). Hungary has [several wine regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_wine) producing among others the worldwide famous white dessert wine [Tokaji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokaji) and the red [Bull's Blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bull's_Blood). Another traditional world-famous alcoholic drink is the fruit brandy *[pálinka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C3%A1linka)*.

Mainly cattle, pigs, poultry and sheep are raised in the country. The livestock includes the [Hungarian Grey Cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Grey_Cattle) which is a major tourist attraction in the [Hortobágy National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hortob%C3%A1gy_National_Park). An important component of the country's gastronomic heritage is [foie gras](230149.md) with about 33,000 farmers engaged in the industry. Hungary is the second largest world producer and the biggest exporter of foie gras (exporting mainly to [France](5843419.md)).

Another symbol of Hungarian agriculture and cuisine is the *[paprika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paprika)* (both sweet and hot types). The country is one of the leading paprika producers of the world with [Szeged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szeged) and [Kalocsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalocsa) being the centres of production.

Hungary produced, in 2018, 7.9 million tons of [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize) (15th largest producer in the world); 5.2 million tons of [wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat); 1.8 million tons of [sunflower seed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunflower_seed) (8th largest producer in the world); 1.1 million tons of [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley); 1 million tons of [rapeseed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapeseed) (14th largest producer in the world); 941 thousand tons of [sugar beet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_beet), which is used to produce [sugar](27712.md) and [ethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol); 674 thousand tons of [apple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple); 539 thousand tons of [grape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grape); 330 thousand tons of [potato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato); 330 thousand tons of [triticale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triticale); in addition to smaller productions of other agricultural products.[^cite_note-97]

### Industry

See also: [Category\:Manufacturing in Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Manufacturing_in_Hungary)

The main sectors of Hungarian industry are heavy industry (mining, metallurgy, machine and steel production), energy production, mechanical engineering, chemicals, food industry and automobile production. The industry is leaning mainly on processing industry and (including construction) accounted for 29.32% of GDP in 2008.[^cite_note-8704elemz_i_reakci_k_az_-98] Due to the sparse energy and raw material resources, Hungary is forced to import most of these materials to satisfy the demands of the industry. Following the transition to market economy, the industry underwent restructuring and remarkable modernization. The leading industry is machinery, followed by chemical industry (plastic production, [pharmaceuticals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmaceutical)), while mining, [metallurgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallurgy) and textile industry seemed to be losing importance in the past two decades. In spite of the significant drop in the last decade, Hungary's food industry is still giving up to 14% of total industrial production and amounts to 7–8% of the country's exports.[^cite_note-6904food_industry-99]

Nearly 50% of energy consumption is dependent on imported energy sources. Gas and oil are transported through pipelines from Russia forming 72% of the energy structure, while nuclear power produced by the [nuclear power station of Paks](1835713.md) accounts for 53,6%.

### Services

The tertiary (service) sector is the largest component of the Hungarian economy, accounting for approximately 65.5% of GDP in 2023 and employing over 63% of the workforce.[^cite_note-Eurostat_sector_contribution2-111] Located in the heart of Central Europe, the country's geostrategic position has been a key factor in the growth of its service sector, attracting investment in logistics, finance, and business services.

Hungary's economy is heavily reliant on foreign trade. In 2023, the total value of goods exports was €149.2 billion, while imports stood at €139.4 billion, resulting in a trade surplus of €9.8 billion. The vast majority of this trade is with other European Union member states, with Germany being the most significant trading partner.[^cite_note-KSH_trade_20232-112]

#### Healthcare

Main article: [Healthcare in Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthcare_in_Hungary)



Total health spending as a percentage of GDP for Hungary compared amongst various other first world nations from 2005 to 2008

Hungary has a tax-funded [universal health care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_health_care) system, organized by the state-owned National Healthcare Fund ([Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_language): *Országos Egészségbiztosítási Pénztár (OEP)*). Health insurance is not directly paid for by children, mothers or fathers with baby, students, [pensioners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pensioner), [people with socially poor background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty), [handicapped people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handicapped_people) (including physical and mental disorders),[^cite_note-117] [priests](23707.md) and other church employees.[^cite_note-118] Health in Hungary can be described with a rapidly growing [life expectancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_expectancy) and a very low [infant mortality rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant_mortality_rate) (4.9 per 1,000 [live births](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_birth_\(human\)) in 2012).[^cite_note-KSH-119] Hungary spent 7.4% of the GDP on health care in 2009 (it was 7.0% in 2000), lower than the average of the [OECD](33853117.md). Total health expenditure was 1,511 US\$ per capita in 2009, 1,053 US\$ governmental-fund (69.7%) and 458 US\$ private-fund (30.3%)[^cite_note-120] but has now risen to 2047 US\$ per capita (as per 2018 data), roughly a 33% increase total, with the government funding 1439 US\$ (70.3%) of the total versus the private funding 608 US\$ (29.7%).[^cite_note-121] This amount totals to 6.6% of the country's total GDP, roughly a percent decrease overall.[^cite_note-122]

Main article: [Energy in Hungary](32999260.md)

See also: [Renewable energy in Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_energy_in_Hungary), [MOL (company)](2964253.md), [Panrusgáz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panrusg%C3%A1z), [Category\:Petroleum in Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Petroleum_in_Hungary), [Category\:Natural gas in Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Natural_gas_in_Hungary), and [List of power stations in Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_power_stations_in_Hungary)

## Socioeconomic characteristics

### Brain drain

Hungary has one of the highest levels of citizen [emigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emigration) inside the EU,[^cite_note-auto2-146] particularly among [young people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_people). Of those immigrating 44% are under 30 and 77% are under 40. Most who leave the country and migrate to [western](33800.md) or [central Europe](5188.md).[^cite_note-auto2-146] Current immigration is exceedingly high, with 239,924 Hungarians immigrating abroad since 2010,[^cite_note-auto1-147][^cite_note-auto2-146] peaking in 2015. Young women tend to be one of the main groups emigrating.[^cite_note-148] [^cite_note-auto2-146] Common destination countries include [Germany](11867.md), [Austria](26964606.md), and the [United Kingdom](31717.md).[^cite_note-auto2-146][^cite_note-auto1-147]

Common reasons for immigrating include low [wages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wage) when compared to the rest of the EU.[^cite_note-149] According to the [Hungarian Central Statistical Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Central_Statistical_Office) the number of Hungarian students enrolled in [university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University) decreased by 25% from 2003 to 2013, roughly a third of those immigrating are college graduates leading to [brain drain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain_drain).[^cite_note-150] Compounding this is that the [Hungarian Forint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Forint) tends to be less stable then the [Euro](9472.md) and often suffers from high levels of inflation.[^cite_note-151][^cite_note-152] Other than economic reasons, familial, social, and educational factors also contribute.[^cite_note-153][^cite_note-154] The high rate of immigration coupled with low birth rates (1.59 [births per woman](515898.md)) results in Hungary's aging and decreasing [population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_\(human_biology\)).[^cite_note-155][^cite_note-156]

### Social stratification

See also: [Family policy in Hungary § Current provisions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_policy_in_Hungary#Current_provisions)

As most post-communist countries, Hungary's economy is affected by its social stratification in terms of income and wealth, age, gender and racial inequalities.[^cite_note-157]



[Lorenz curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorenz_curve) of [Denmark](76972.md), Hungary, and [Namibia](21292.md)

Hungary's [Gini coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gini_coefficient) of 0.269[^cite_note-distribution_of_inco-158] ranks 11th in the world.[^cite_note-a_dollar_a_day_pov-159] The graph on the right shows that Hungary is close in equality to the world-leader [Denmark](76972.md). The highest 10% of the population gets 22.2% of the incomes.[^cite_note-distribution_of_inco-158] According to the business magazine *[Napi Gazdaság](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napi_Gazdas%C3%A1g)*, the owner of the biggest fortune, 300 billion HUF, is [Sándor Demján](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A1ndor_Demj%C3%A1n).[^cite_note-richest_hungarian_co-160] On the other hand, the lowest 10% gets 4% of the incomes. Considering the standard EU indicators (Percentage of the population living under 60% of the per capita median income), 13% of the Hungarian population is stricken by poverty.[^cite_note-national_programme_f-161] According to the [Human Development Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Development_Report), the country's [HPI-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Poverty_Index) value is 2.2% (3rd among 135 countries),[^cite_note-human_development_re-162] and its [HDI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HDI) value is 0.879 (43rd out of 182).[^cite_note-human_development_re-162]



[Population pyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_pyramid) of Hungary

The fertility rate in Hungary, just like in many European countries, is very low: 1.34 children/women (205th in the world)[^cite_note-key_development_data-138] Life expectancy at birth is 73.3 years.,[^cite_note-key_development_data-138] while the expected number of healthy years is 57.6 for females and 53.5 for males. The average life expectancy overall is 73.1 years.[^cite_note-healthy_life_years_a-163]

Hungary's [GDI (gender-related development index)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender-related_Development_Index) value of 0.879[^cite_note-human_development_re-162] is 100% of its HDI value (3rd best in the world).[^cite_note-human_development_re-162] 55.5% of the female population (between 15 and 64) participate in the labour force, and the ratio of girls to boys in primary and secondary education is 99%.[^cite_note-key_development_data-138]

Ethnic inequality, which strikes primarily [Roma in Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roma_in_Hungary), is a serious problem. Although the definition of the Roma identity is controversial,[^cite_note-1026roma_employment_in_h-164] qualitative studies prove that the Roma employment rate decreased significantly following the [fall of Communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Communism):[^cite_note-5488inequality_and_discr-165] due to the tremendous layoffs of unskilled workers[^cite_note-9905roma_children_in_the-166] during the transition years, more than one-third of Roma were excluded from the labour market.[^cite_note-8956roma_foglalkoztat_s_-167] Therefore, this ethnic conflict is inherently interconnected with the income inequalities in the country[^cite_note-4730the_income_situation-168] – at least two-thirds of the poorest 300,000 people in Hungary are Romas.[^cite_note-4730the_income_situation-168] Furthermore, ethnic discrimination is outstandingly high, 32% of Romas experience discrimination when looking for work.[^cite_note-4150data_in_focus_report-169] Consequently, new Roma entrants to the labour market are rarely able to find employment,[^cite_note-8956roma_foglalkoztat_s_-167] which creates a motivation deficit and further reinforces [segregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_segregation) and unemployment.[^cite_note-6994a_hern_d_v_lgyi_rom_-170]

### Institutional quality

Twenty years after the change of the regime, [corruption](66220.md) remains a severe issue in Hungary.[^cite_note-5463hungary_corruption_-171] According to [Transparency International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transparency_International) Hungary, almost one-third of top managers claim they regularly bribe politicians.[^cite_note-5463hungary_corruption_-171] Most people (42%) in Hungary think that the sector most affected by bribery is the political party system.[^cite_note-1766global_corruption_ba-172] Bribery is common in the [healthcare system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthcare_in_Hungary) in the form of gratitude payment–92% of all people think that some payment should be made to the head surgeon conducting a heart operation or an obstetrician for a child birth.[^cite_note-1046hidden_in_an_envelop-173]

Another problem is the administrative burden: in terms of the ease of doing business, Hungary ranks 47th out of 183 countries in the world.[^cite_note-5276doing_business_-174] The five days' time[^cite_note-key_development_data-138] required to start a new business ranks 29th, and the country is 122nd concerning the ease of paying taxes.[^cite_note-3758paying_taxes_-175]

In accordance with the theory of the [separation of powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_of_powers), the judicial system is independent from the legislative and the executive branches.[^cite_note-4430background_note_hun-176] Consequently, courts and prosecutions are not influenced by the government. However, the legal system is slow and overburdened, which makes proceedings and rulings lengthy and inefficient.[^cite_note-3455hungary_investment_c-177] Such a justice system is hardly capable of prosecuting corruption and protecting the country's financial interests.[^cite_note-5463hungary_corruption_-171]

### Unemployment

Main article: [Unemployment in Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unemployment_in_Hungary)

This section is an excerpt from [Unemployment in Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unemployment_in_Hungary).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Unemployment_in_Hungary\&action=edit)]

[Unemployment in Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unemployment_in_Hungary) measured by the [Hungarian Central Statistical Office (Központi Statisztikai Hivatal, KSH)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Central_Statistical_Office) shows the rate of [unemployed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unemployed) individuals out of the [labor force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_force). The [European Union](9317.md)'s own statistical office, [Eurostat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostat) also makes reports and predictions about the Hungarian job market and the unemployment rate in the country. The KSH's most recent unemployment data shows the unemployment rate for men 15–74 to be 3.3% and 4.1% for women.[^cite_note-178]

Article XII in [Hungary's Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Hungary) states: '(2) Hungary seeks to create the conditions for all people who have the ability to work and want to work to get work.' [Hungarian National Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_National_Bank) (MNB)

The Hungarian organization responsible for controlling the country's monetary policy is the [Hungarian National Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_National_Bank) (Hungarian: *Magyar Nemzeti Bank*, MNB) which is the central bank in Hungary.[^cite_note-5892hungarian_national_bank-179] According to the Hungarian Law of National Bank (which became operative in 2001. – LVIII. Law about The Hungarian National Bank[^cite_note-9076t_rv_nyek_s_ogy_hat-180]), the primary objective of MNB is to achieve and maintain price stability. This aim is in line with the European and international practice.

Price stability means achieving and maintaining a basically low, but positive inflation rate. This level is around 2–2.5% according to international observations, while the [European Central Bank](9474.md) "aims at inflation rates of below, but close to 2% over the medium term".[^cite_note-1639monetary_policy-181] Since Hungary is in the process of catching up ([Balassa-Samuelson effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balassa-Samuelson_effect)), the long-term objective is a slightly higher figure, around 2.3–3.2%.[^cite_note-2817the_optimal_rate-182] Therefore, the medium term inflation target of the Hungarian National Bank is 3%.[^cite_note-4383k_zlem_ny_a_k_z_pt_v-183]

Concerning the exchange rate system, the [floating exchange rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_exchange_rate) system is in use since 26 February 2008, as a result of which HUF is fluctuating in accordance with the effects of the market in the face of the reference currency, the euro.

In Hungary, state revenue makes up 44% and expenditure makes up 45% of the GDP which is relatively high compared to other EU members.[^cite_note-8115a_gazdasa_gi_no_veke-184] This can be traced back to historical reasons such as [socialist economic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_economic) tradition as well as cultural characteristics that endorse paternalist behaviour on the state's part, meaning that people have a habitual reflex that make them call for state subsidies.[^cite_note-4811interview_with_adam-185] Some economists dispute this point, claiming that expenditure ran up to today's critical amount from 2001, during two left-wing government cycles.[^cite_note-8115a_gazdasa_gi_no_veke-184]

Along with joining the EU the country undertook the task of joining the [Eurozone](184391.md) as well. Therefore, the [Maastricht criteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maastricht_criteria) which forms the condition of joining the Eurozone acts as an authoritative guideline to Hungarian fiscal politics. Although there has been remarkable progress, recent years' statistics still point at significant discrepancies between the criteria and fiscal indices. The target date for adapting the Euro has not been fixed, either.

General government deficit has shown a drastic decline to −3.4% (2008) from −9.2% (2006).[^cite_note-3654_main_macroecon-186] According to an MNB forecast however, until 2011, the deficit will by a small margin fall short of the 3.0% criterion.[^cite_note-2834ecomoic_report-187]

Another criterion that is found lacking is the ratio of gross government debt to GDP which, since 2005, exceeds the allowed 60%.[^cite_note-3847introducing_the_eur-188] According to an [ESA95](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_System_of_Accounts) figure, in 2008 the ratio increased from 65.67% to 72.61%, which primarily results from the requisition of an IMF-arranged financial assistance package.[^cite_note-9345imf_eu_and_worl-189]

Hungary's balance of payments on its current account has been negative since 1995, around 6–8% in the 2000s[^cite_note-emenbe-190] reaching a negative peak 8.5% in 2008.[^cite_note-emenbe-190][^cite_note-6342elemze_s_a_makrogazd-191]

### Tax system

Main article: [Taxation in Hungary](43682232.md)

In Hungary, the 1988 reform of taxes introduced a comprehensive tax system which mainly consists of central and local taxes, including a [personal income tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_tax), a [corporate income tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_income_tax) and a [value added tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_added_tax).[^cite_note-9331reflection_on_the_hu-192] Among the total tax income the ratio of local taxes is solely 5% while the EU average is 30%.[^cite_note-1882ad_z_s_taxation_-193] Until 2010, the taxation of an individual was progressive, determining the tax rate based on the individual's income: with earning up to 1,900,000 forints a year, the tax was 18%, the tax on incomes above this limit was 36% since 1 July 2009.[^cite_note-2956short_summary_on_the-194]

Based on the new one-rate tax regime introduced January 2011, the overall tax rate for all income-earnings bands has been 16%. According to the income-tax returns of 2008, 14,6% of taxpayers was charged for 64,5% of the total tax burdens.[^cite_note-2758az_ad_z_k_sz_zl_k-195] Before the new corporate income tax regime, the corporate tax was fixed at 16% of the positive rateable value, with an additional tax called solidarity tax of 4%, the measure of which is calculated based on the result before tax of the company (the solidarity tax has been in use since September, 2006). The actual rateable value might be different is the two cases. From January 2011, under the new corporate income tax regime the tax rate was divided into two parts (i) corporations having income before tax below 500 million HUF (appr. USD 2.5 million) was lowered to 10% and (ii) 16% remained for all other companies until 2013. After this, the unified corporate income tax rate will be 10%, irrespectively from the size of the net income before tax. In January 2017, corporate tax was unified at a rate of 9% – the lowest in the European Union. The rate of value added tax in Hungary is 27%, the highest in Europe, since 1 January 2012.[^cite_note-7391_vi_cxxvii_t_-196]
