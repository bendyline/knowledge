# Water resources

This article is about all types of waters that are of potential use to humans. For a naturally occurring type of water resource that humans use a lot, see [Fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water).

Distribution of freshwater resources by type

[^cite_note-1]

1. [Glaciers](12463.md) (69.0%)

2. [Groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater) (30.0%)

3. Other freshwater (e.g. soil moisture) (0.70%)

4. Directly accessible water (0.30%)

**Water resources** are [natural resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_resource) of [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) that are potentially useful for humans. For example, they serve as a source of drinking [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) or [irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation) water. These resources may consist of [freshwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water) from natural sources or water produced artificially from other sources, such as [reclaimed water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reclaimed_water) ([wastewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wastewater)) or [desalinated](156787.md) water ([seawater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seawater)). Approximately 97% of the water on Earth is [salt water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saline_water), and only 3% is fresh water; slightly over two-thirds of this is frozen in [glaciers](12463.md) and [polar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_climate) [ice caps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_cap).[^cite_note-USGS_dist-2] The remaining unfrozen freshwater is found mainly as [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater), with only a small fraction present above ground or in the air.[^cite_note-3] Natural sources of fresh water include [frozen water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice), groundwater, [surface water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_water), and under river flow. People use water resources for [agricultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture), [household](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Household), and [industrial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industry_\(economics\)) activities.

Water resources are under threat from multiple issues. There is [water scarcity](15380061.md), [water pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution), [water conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_conflict), and [climate change](5042951.md). [Fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water) is in principle a [renewable resource](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_resource). However, the world's supply of [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater) is steadily decreasing. Groundwater depletion (or [overdrafting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overdrafting)) is occurring for example in [Asia](689.md), [South America](26769.md) and [North America](21139.md).

## Natural sources of fresh water

Further information: [Water distribution on Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_distribution_on_Earth)

Natural sources of [fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water) include [surface water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_water), under river flow, [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater) and [frozen water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frozen_water).[^cite_note-4]

### Surface water

Main article: [Surface water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_water)



[Lake Chungará](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Chungar%C3%A1) and [Parinacota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parinacota_Volcano) volcano in northern Chile

[Surface water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_water) refers to water that is found on the surface of the Earth. This includes but not limited to [river](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River), [lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake), ponds or [fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water) [wetland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wetland). Surface water is naturally replenished by [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation_\(meteorology\)), and naturally lost through discharge to the [oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceans), [evaporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporation), [evapotranspiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evapotranspiration), drought, climate change, population growth, [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation), land degradation, and [groundwater recharge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater_recharge). The only natural input to any surface water system is precipitation within its [watershed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin). The total quantity of water in that system at any given time is also dependent on many other factors. These factors include storage capacity in lakes, wetlands and artificial [reservoirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reservoir_\(water\)), the permeability of the [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil) beneath these storage bodies, the [runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_runoff) characteristics of the land in the watershed, the timing of the precipitation and local evaporation rates. Furthermore, other human factors such as temperature, climate change, topography, Vegetation cover, windspeed, relative humidity, and solar radiation has an effect on water loss. All of these factors also affect the proportions of water loss.[^cite_note-5]

Humans often increase storage capacity by constructing [reservoirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reservoir), rainwater harvesting systems, Managed Aquifier Recharge (MAR), Watershed Conservation, Construction of retention, and detention basins, floodplain restoration, creation of artificial rains; and decrease it by draining wetlands, land reclamation, urbanisation, river channelisation, over-abstraction of water, sedimentation by deforestation and poor reservoir management. Humans often increase runoff quantities and velocities by paving areas and channelizing the stream flow.[^cite_note-6]

Natural surface water can be augmented by importing surface water from another watershed through a [canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canal) or [pipeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport).[^cite_note-7] Other methods also includes rain harvesting, construction of dams and reservoirs, desalination, cloud seeding, and artificial recharge.

[Brazil](3383.md) is estimated to have the largest supply of [fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water) in the world with 5,661 billion m³/year followed by [Russia](25391.md), and [Canada](5042916.md),[^cite_note-8] with about 4,312 billion m³/year and 2,850 billion m³/year respectively according to rècent World Bank summaries.[^cite_note-9] However, this is renewable freshwater and not freshwater physically stored in lakes,groundwater or glaciers.

#### Water from glaciers

[Glacier](12463.md) runoff is considered to be [surface water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_water). The [Himalayas](37966.md), which are often called "The Roof of the World", contain some of the most extensive and rough high altitude areas on [Earth](9228.md) as well as the greatest area of glaciers and [permafrost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permafrost) outside of the poles. Ten of Asia's largest rivers flow from there, and more than a billion people's livelihoods depend on them. To complicate matters, temperatures there are rising more rapidly than the global average. In [Nepal](171166.md), the temperature has risen by 0.6 [degrees Celsius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celsius) over the last decade, whereas globally, the Earth has warmed approximately 0.7 degrees Celsius over the last hundred years.[^cite_note-10]

### Groundwater



Relative groundwater travel times in the subsurface

This section is an excerpt from [Groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Groundwater\&action=edit)]

[Groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater) is the [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) present beneath [Earth](9228.md)'s surface in rock and [soil pore spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pore_space_in_soil) and in the [fractures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fracture) of [rock formations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratum). About 30 percent of all readily available [fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water) in the world is groundwater.[^cite_note-11] A unit of rock or an unconsolidated deposit is called an *[aquifer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquifer)* when it can yield a usable quantity of water. The depth at which [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil) pore spaces or fractures and voids in rock become completely saturated with water is called the *[water table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_table)*. Groundwater is [recharged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater_recharge) from the surface; it may discharge from the surface naturally at [springs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_\(hydrosphere\)) and [seeps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seep_\(hydrology\)), and can form [oases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oasis) or [wetlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wetland). Groundwater is also often withdrawn for agricultural, municipal, and industrial use by constructing and operating extraction [wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_well). The study of the distribution and movement of groundwater is *[hydrogeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogeology)*, also called groundwater [hydrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrology).

Typically, groundwater is thought of as water flowing through shallow aquifers, but, in the technical sense, it can also contain [soil moisture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_moisture), [permafrost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permafrost) (frozen soil), immobile water in very low permeability [bedrock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedrock), and deep [geothermal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geothermal_activity) or [oil formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_geology) water. Groundwater is hypothesized to provide [lubrication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lubrication) that can possibly influence the movement of [faults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_\(geology\)). It is likely that much of [Earth](9228.md)'s subsurface contains some water, which may be mixed with other fluids in some instances.

#### Under river flow

Throughout the course of a river, the total volume of water transported downstream will often be a combination of the visible free water flow together with a substantial contribution flowing through rocks and sediments that underlie the river and its floodplain called the [hyporheic zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyporheic_zone). For many rivers in large valleys, this unseen component of flow may greatly exceed the visible flow. The hyporheic zone often forms a dynamic interface between surface water and groundwater from aquifers, exchanging flow between rivers and aquifers that may be fully charged or depleted. This is especially significant in [karst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karst) areas where pot-holes and underground rivers are common.[^cite_note-12]

## Artificial sources of usable water

This refers to structured systems designed by humans for the collection, storage, treatment, and the convertion of water for human use.[^cite_note-sources_of_water-13][^cite_note-14] The purpose of this system is to help mitigate the shortage of water especially in regions where there is little to no rainfall or high demand of water.[^cite_note-15] There are several artificial sources of [fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water); [treated wastewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wastewater_treatment) ([reclaimed water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reclaimed_water)), [atmospheric water generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_water_generator)[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18] and [Desalinated seawater](156787.md). Other important sources includes: rainwater harvesting systems, wells, and [boreholes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borehole), artificial recharge [ponds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pond), dams and reservoirs.[^cite_note-sources_of_water-13][^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20] It is important to consider the economic and environmental side effects of these technologies.[^cite_note-21]

### Wastewater reuse

This section is an excerpt from [Reclaimed water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reclaimed_water).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Reclaimed_water\&action=edit)]

[Water reclamation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reclaimed_water) is the process of converting [municipal wastewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewage) or sewage and [industrial wastewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_wastewater_treatment) into water that can be [reused](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reuse) for a variety of purposes. It is also called wastewater reuse, water reuse or water recycling. There are many types of reuse. It is possible to reuse water in this way in cities or for irrigation in agriculture. Other types of reuse are environmental reuse, industrial reuse, and reuse for drinking water, whether planned or not. Reuse may include [irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation) of gardens and agricultural fields or replenishing [surface water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_water) and [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater). This latter is also known as [groundwater recharge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater_recharge). Reused water also serve various needs in residences such as [toilet flushing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flush_toilet), businesses, and industry. It is possible to treat wastewater to reach [drinking water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drinking_water) standards. Injecting reclaimed water into the water supply distribution system is known as direct potable reuse. Drinking reclaimed water is not typical.[^cite_note-22] Reusing treated municipal wastewater for irrigation is a long-established practice. This is especially so in [arid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arid) countries. Reusing wastewater as part of sustainable [water management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_management) allows water to remain an alternative water source for human activities. This can reduce [scarcity](15380061.md). It also eases pressures on groundwater and other natural water bodies.[^cite_note-Reclaimed_water_Andersson-23]

There are several technologies used to treat wastewater for reuse. A combination of these technologies can meet strict treatment standards and make sure that the processed water is hygienically safe, meaning free from [pathogens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathogen). The following are some of the typical technologies: [Ozonation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozonation), [ultrafiltration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrafiltration), [aerobic treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerobic_treatment_system) ([membrane bioreactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Membrane_bioreactor)), [forward osmosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forward_osmosis), [reverse osmosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse_osmosis), and [advanced oxidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_oxidation_process),[^cite_note-Reclaimed_water_PotableReuseMembraneReview-24] or [activated carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activated_carbon).[^cite_note-Reclaimed_water_\:2-25] Some water-demanding activities do not require high grade water. In this case, wastewater can be reused with little or no treatment.

### Desalinated water

This section is an excerpt from [Desalination](156787.md).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Desalination\&action=edit)]

[Desalination](156787.md) is the artificial process by which salt water (generally [sea water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_water)) is converted to [fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water). More generally, desalination is the removal of salts and minerals from a substance.[^cite_note-26] It is possible to desalinate saltwater, especially sea water, to produce water for human consumption or irrigation, producing [brine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brine) as a by-product.[^cite_note-Desalination_PanagopoulosHaralambousLoizidou2019-27]

Interest in desalination mostly focuses on cost-effective provision of fresh water for human use. Along with recycled [wastewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wastewater), it is one of the few water resources independent of rainfall.[^cite_note-Desalination_Fischetti2007-28] As stress on the need for freshwater intensifies globally, desalination has become a key part of strategies for global water security. According to a 2019 review in *Science of the Total Environment*, around 95 million cubic meters per day of desalinated water is produced worldwide, and the demand for desalinated water is expected to grow significantly to help close the global water supply gap.

The total quantity of water available at any given time is an important consideration. Some human water users have an intermittent need for water. For example, many [farms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farm) require large quantities of water in the spring, and no water at all in the winter. ### Agriculture and other irrigation

Further information: [Sustainable Water and Innovative Irrigation Management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_Water_and_Innovative_Irrigation_Management)

This section is an excerpt from [Irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Irrigation\&action=edit)]



Irrigation of agricultural fields in [Andalusia](2736.md), Spain. Irrigation canal on the left.

[Irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation) in [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture) is the practice of applying controlled amounts of [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) to [land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land) to help grow [crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop), [landscape plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landscape_plant), and [lawns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawn). Irrigation has been a key aspect of agriculture for over 5,000 years and has been developed by many cultures worldwide. Irrigation helps to grow crops, maintain landscapes, and [revegetate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revegetation) disturbed soils in dry areas and during times of below-average rainfall. In addition to these uses, irrigation is also employed to protect crops from [frost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frost),[^cite_note-35] suppress [weed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weed) growth in [grain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain) fields, and prevent [soil consolidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_consolidation). It is also used to cool [livestock](25160767.md), reduce [dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), dispose of [sewage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewage), and support [mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining) operations. [Drainage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage), which involves the removal of surface and sub-surface water from a given location, is often studied in conjunction with irrigation. Irrigation and drainage improvements are not necessarily mutually exclusive. Often both may be required together to assure sustained high-level production of crops. For a discussion of the role of drainage practices in agriculture [^cite_note-36].

Several irrigation methods differ in how water is supplied to plants. [Surface irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_irrigation), also known as gravity irrigation, is the oldest form of irrigation and has been in use for thousands of years. In [sprinkler irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation_sprinkler), water is piped to one or more central locations within the field and distributed by overhead high-pressure water devices. [Micro-irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micro-irrigation) is a system that distributes water under low pressure through a piped network and applies it as a small discharge to each plant. Micro-irrigation uses less pressure and water flow than sprinkler irrigation. [Drip irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drip_irrigation) delivers water directly to the root zone of plants. [Subirrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subirrigation) has been used in field crops in areas with high water tables for many years. It involves artificially raising the water table to moisten the soil below the root zone of plants.

### Industries

See also: [Industrial water treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_water_treatment) and [Industrial wastewater treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_wastewater_treatment)

It is estimated that 22% of worldwide water is used in [industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_sector).[^cite_note-WBCSD_Water_Facts_&_Trends-37] Major industrial users include [hydroelectric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroelectric) dams, [thermoelectric power plants](9540.md#Other_generation_methods), which use water for [cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooling), [ore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ore) and [oil refineries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_refineries), which use water in [chemical processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_process), and manufacturing plants, which use water as a [solvent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvent). Water withdrawal can be very high for certain industries, but consumption is generally much lower than that of agriculture.

Water is used in [renewable power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_power) generation. [Hydroelectric power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroelectric_Power) derives energy from the force of water flowing downhill, driving a [turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbine) connected to a generator. This [hydroelectricity](381399.md) is a low-cost, non-polluting, [renewable energy](25784.md) source. Significantly, [hydroelectric power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroelectric_power) can also be used for [load following](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Load_following) unlike most renewable energy sources which are [intermittent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermittent_energy_source). Ultimately, the energy in a hydroelectric power plant is supplied by the sun. Heat from the sun evaporates water, which condenses as rain in higher altitudes and flows downhill. [Pumped-storage hydroelectric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pumped-storage_hydroelectricity) plants also exist, which use grid electricity to pump water uphill when demand is low, and use the stored water to produce electricity when demand is high.[^cite_note-38]

Thermoelectric power plants using [cooling towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooling_towers) have high consumption, nearly equal to their withdrawal, as most of the withdrawn water is evaporated as part of the cooling process. The withdrawal, however, is lower than in [once-through cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Once-through_cooling) systems.[^cite_note-39]

Water is also used in many large scale industrial processes, such as thermoelectric power production, oil refining, [fertilizer](37401.md) production and other [chemical plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_plant) use, and [natural gas extraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_extraction) from [shale rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shale_rock). Discharge of untreated water from industrial uses is [pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollution). Pollution includes discharged solutes and increased water temperature ([thermal pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_pollution)).[^cite_note-40]

### Drinking water and domestic use (households)

Main articles: [Water supply](684928.md), [Drinking water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drinking_water), and [Water footprint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_footprint)



Drinking water

It is estimated that 8% of worldwide water use is for domestic purposes.[^cite_note-WBCSD_Water_Facts_&_Trends-37] These include [drinking water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drinking_water), [bathing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathing), [cooking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooking), [toilet flushing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flush_toilet), cleaning, laundry and [gardening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gardening). Basic domestic water requirements have been estimated by [Peter Gleick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Gleick) at around 50 liters per person per day, excluding water for gardens.

Drinking water is water that is of sufficiently high quality so that it can be consumed or used without risk of immediate or long term harm. Such water is commonly called potable water. In most developed countries, the water supplied to domestic, commerce and industry is all of drinking water standard even though only a very small proportion is actually consumed or used in food preparation.[^cite_note-41]

844 million people still lacked even a basic drinking water service in 2017.[^cite_note-\:7-42]<sup>: 3 </sup> Of those, 159 million people worldwide drink water directly from surface water sources, such as lakes and streams.[^cite_note-\:7-42]<sup>: 3 </sup> One in eight people in the world do not have access to safe water.[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44] Unsafe drinking water leads to 1.2 million deaths per year according to the World Bank.[^cite_note-45]

## Challenges and threats

### Water scarcity

This section is an excerpt from [Water scarcity](15380061.md).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Water_scarcity\&action=edit)]

[Water scarcity](15380061.md) (closely related to water stress or [water crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_crisis_\(disambiguation\))) is the lack of any, local or economically viably transportable, sources of [fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water) resources to meet the standard water demand in a region. There are two types of water scarcity. One is *physical.* The other is *economic water scarcity*.[^cite_note-Water_scarcity_Caretta-2022a-46]<sup>: 560 </sup> Physical water scarcity is where there is not enough water to meet all demands. This includes water needed for [ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem) to function. Regions with a [desert climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert_climate) often face physical water scarcity.[^cite_note-Water_scarcity_Rijsberman-2006a-47] [Central Asia](6742.md), [West Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Asia), [North Africa](21714.md), and most of [Australia](4689264.md) are examples of arid areas. Economic water scarcity results from a lack of investment in infrastructure or technology to draw usable water from rivers, [aquifers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquifer), and other water sources. It also results from weak human capacity to meet water demand.[^cite_note-Water_scarcity_Caretta-2022a-46]<sup>: 560 </sup> Many people in [sub-Saharan Africa](27067.md) are living with economic water scarcity.[^cite_note-Water_scarcity_IWMI-2007-48]<sup>: 11 </sup>

### Water pollution



Polluted water

This section is an excerpt from [Water pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Water_pollution\&action=edit)]

[Water pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution) (or aquatic pollution) is the [contamination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contamination) of [water bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_of_water), which has a negative impact on how they can be used.[^cite_note-Water_pollution_Von_Sperling-49]<sup>: 6 </sup> It is usually caused by human activities. Water bodies include [lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake), [rivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River), [oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean), [aquifers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquifer), [reservoirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reservoir) and [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater). Water [pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollution) results when [contaminants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contaminant) mix with these water bodies. [Contaminants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contamination) can come from one of four main sources. These are [sewage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewage) discharges, industrial activities, agricultural activities, and urban runoff including [stormwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stormwater).[^cite_note-Water_pollution_Eckenfelder-50] Water pollution may affect either [surface water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_water) or [groundwater](44413707.md). This form of pollution can lead to many problems. One is the [degradation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_degradation) of [aquatic ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquatic_ecosystems). Another is spreading [water-borne diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterborne_diseases) when people use polluted water for drinking or [irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation).[^cite_note-51] Water pollution also reduces the [ecosystem services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem_service) such as [drinking water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drinking_water) provided by the water resource.

### Water conflict

This section is an excerpt from [Water conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_conflict).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Water_conflict\&action=edit)]



Ethiopia's move to fill the [GERD dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Ethiopian_Renaissance_Dam)'s reservoir could reduce [Nile](21244.md) flows by as much as 25% and devastate Egyptian farmlands.

[^cite_note-52]

[Water conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_conflict) typically refers to violence or disputes associated with access to, or control of, water resources, or the use of water or water systems as weapons or casualties of conflicts. The term *water war* is colloquially used in media for some disputes over water, and often is more limited to describing a conflict between countries, states, or groups over [the rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_right) to access water resources.[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-Water_conflict_Kameri-Mbote-2007-54] The [United Nations](31769.md) recognizes that water disputes result from opposing interests of water users, public or private.[^cite_note-Water_conflict_UNESCO-55] A wide range of water conflicts appear throughout history, though they are rarely traditional wars waged over water alone.[^cite_note-56] Instead, water has long been a source of tension and one of the causes for conflicts. Water conflicts arise for several reasons, including territorial disputes, a fight for resources, and strategic advantage.[^cite_note-57]

### Groundwater overdrafting

The world's supply of [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater) is steadily decreasing. Groundwater depletion (or [overdrafting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overdrafting)) is occurring for example in Asia, South America and North America. It is still unclear how much natural renewal [balances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_balance) this usage, and whether [ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem) are threatened.[^cite_note-62]

This section is an excerpt from [Groundwater depletion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater_depletion).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Groundwater_depletion\&action=edit)]



Within a long period of groundwater depletion in California's [Central Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Valley_\(California\)), short periods of recovery were mostly driven by extreme weather events that typically caused flooding and had negative social, environmental and economic consequences.

[^cite_note-Groundwater_depletion_NatureComms_20221219-63]

[Groundwater depletion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater_depletion) or groundwater overdrafting is the process of extracting [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater) beyond the [equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_equilibrium) yield of an [aquifer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquifer). Groundwater is one of the largest sources of [fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water) and is found underground. The primary cause of groundwater depletion is the excessive pumping of groundwater up from underground aquifers. Insufficient [recharge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater_recharge) can lead to depletion, reducing the usefulness of the aquifer for humans. Depletion can also have impacts on the environment around the aquifer, such as soil compression and [land subsidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater-related_subsidence), local climatic change, soil chemistry changes, and other deterioration of the local environment.

## Water resource management

"Water management" redirects here. For policymaking and politics of water management, see [Water resource policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_resource_policy) and [Water politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_politics).

Further information: [Research Institute for Groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_Institute_for_Groundwater) and [Water resources law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_resources_law)



Global values of water resources and human water use (excluding [Antarctica](18959138.md)). Water resources 1961-90, water use around 2000. Computed by the global freshwater model [WaterGAP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WaterGAP).

Water resource management is the activity of planning, developing, distributing and managing the optimum use of water resources. It is an aspect of [water cycle management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_cycle_management). The field of water resources management will have to continue to adapt to the current and future issues facing the allocation of water. With the growing uncertainties of global [climate change](5042951.md) and the long-term impacts of past management actions, this decision-making will be even more difficult. It is likely that ongoing climate change will lead to situations that have not been encountered. As a result, alternative management strategies, including participatory approaches and [adaptive capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptive_capacity) are increasingly being used to strengthen water decision-making.[^cite_note-64]

Ideally, water resource management planning has regard to all the competing [demands for water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demand_for_water) and seeks to allocate water on an equitable basis to satisfy all uses and demands. As with other [resource management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resource_management), this is rarely possible in practice so decision-makers must prioritise issues of sustainability, equity and factor optimisation (in that order!) to achieve acceptable outcomes. One of the biggest concerns for water-based resources in the future is the [sustainability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainability) of the current and future water resource allocation.[^cite_note-65]

[Sustainable Development Goal 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_Development_Goal_6) has a target related to water resources management: "Target 6.5: By 2030, implement integrated water resources management at all levels, including through transboundary cooperation as appropriate."[^cite_note-\:3-66][^cite_note-\:17-67]

### Sustainable water management

At present, only about 0.08 percent of all the world's fresh water is accessible. And there is ever-increasing demand for [drinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drinking_water), [manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing), [leisure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_sports) and [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture). Due to the small percentage of water available, optimizing the fresh water we have left from [natural resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_resources) has been a growing challenge around the world.

Much effort in water resource management is directed at optimizing the [use of water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_use) and in minimizing the [environmental impact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact) of water use on the natural environment. The observation of water as an integral part of the [ecosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem) is based on [integrated water resources management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_water_resources_management), based on the 1992 [Dublin Principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Statement) (see below).

Sustainable water management requires a holistic approach based on the principles of [Integrated Water Resource Management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_water_resources_management), originally articulated in 1992 at the Dublin (January) and Rio (July) conferences. The four Dublin Principles, promulgated in the [Dublin Statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Statement) are:

1. Fresh water is a finite and vulnerable resource, essential to sustain life, development and the environment;

2. Water development and management should be based on a participatory approach, involving users, planners and policy-makers at all levels;

3. Women play a central part in the provision, management and safeguarding of water;

4. Water has an economic value in all its competing uses and should be recognized as an economic good.

Implementation of these principles has guided reform of national water management law around the world since 1992.[^cite_note-68]

Further challenges to sustainable and equitable water resources management include the fact that many water bodies are shared across boundaries which may be international (see [water conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_conflict)) or intra-national (see [Murray-Darling basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murray%E2%80%93Darling_basin)).

### Integrated water resources management

See also: [Integrated Flood Management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_Flood_Management)

Integrated water resources management (IWRM) has been defined by the [Global Water Partnership (GWP)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Water_Partnership) as "a process which promotes the coordinated [development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_development) and management of water, land and related resources, in order to maximize the resultant [economic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_welfare) and [social welfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_welfare) in an equitable manner without compromising the [sustainability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainability) of vital [ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem)".[^cite_note-69]

Some scholars say that IWRM is complementary to [water security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_security) because water security is a goal or destination, whilst IWRM is the process necessary to achieve that goal.[^cite_note-\:1-70]

IWRM is a paradigm that emerged at international conferences in the late 1900s and early 2000s, although participatory water management institutions have existed for centuries.[^cite_note-\:0-71] Discussions on a holistic way of managing water resources began already in the 1950s leading up to the 1977 United Nations Water Conference.[^cite_note-72] The development of IWRM was particularly recommended in the final statement of the ministers at the International Conference on Water and the Environment in 1992, known as the [Dublin Statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Statement). This concept aims to promote changes in practices which are considered fundamental to improved [water resource management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_resource_management). IWRM was a topic of [the second World Water Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Water_Forum#2nd_World_Water_Forum:_Netherlands), which was attended by a more varied group of stakeholders than the preceding conferences and contributed to the creation of the GWP.[^cite_note-\:0-71]

In the [International Water Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Water_Association) definition, IWRM rests upon three principles that together act as the overall framework:[^cite_note-73]

1. Social equity: ensuring equal access for all users (particularly marginalized and poorer user groups) to an adequate quantity and quality of water necessary to sustain human [well-being](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-being).

2. Economic efficiency: bringing the greatest benefit to the greatest number of users possible with the available financial and water resources.

3. Ecological sustainability: requiring that [aquatic ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquatic_ecosystem) are acknowledged as users and that adequate allocation is made to sustain their natural functioning.

In 2002, the development of IWRM was discussed at [the World Summit on Sustainable Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_Summit_2002) held in Johannesburg, which aimed to encourage the implementation of IWRM at a global level.[^cite_note-74] [The third World Water Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Water_Forum#3rd_World_Water_Forum:_Japan) recommended IWRM and discussed information sharing, stakeholder participation, and gender and class dynamics.[^cite_note-\:0-71]

Operationally, IWRM approaches involve applying knowledge from various disciplines as well as the insights from diverse stakeholders to devise and implement efficient, equitable and sustainable solutions to water and development problems. As such, IWRM is a comprehensive, [participatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Participatory) planning and implementation tool for managing and developing water resources in a way that balances social and economic needs, and that ensures the [protection of ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem_protection) for future generations. In addition, in light of contributing the achievement of [Sustainable Development goals (SDGs)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_Development_Goals),[^cite_note-75] IWRM has been evolving into more sustainable approach as it considers the Nexus approach, which is a cross-sectoral water resource management. The Nexus approach is based on the recognition that "water, energy and food are closely linked through global and local water, carbon and energy cycles or chains."

An IWRM approach aims at avoiding a fragmented approach of water resources management by considering the following aspects: Enabling environment, roles of Institutions, management Instruments. Some of the cross-cutting conditions that are also important to consider when implementing IWRM are: Political will and commitment, capacity development, adequate investment, [financial stability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_stability) and sustainable cost recovery, monitoring and evaluation. There is not one correct administrative model. The art of IWRM lies in selecting, adjusting and applying the right mix of these tools for a given situation. IWRM practices depend on context; at the operational level, the challenge is to translate the agreed principles into concrete action.[^cite_note-76]

### Regional governance frameworks

When water systems cross international borders, countries must cooperate to manage them effectively. WHiles frameworks exist globaly, continental and regional entities often dictate policy alignment, resource allocation, and integrated management straegies. Africa usually serves as a prominent model due to its high density of transboundary waters.[^cite_note-\:2-77]

Water resource management in Africa is coordinated through continental frameworks aimed at balancing development with conservation. Regional cooperation is central to continental policy because over 60 percent of Africa's water resources are contained within transboundary river basins and aquifers.[^cite_note-78]

The African Ministers' Council on Water, or AMCOW, was established in 2002 via the Abuja Declaration to provide political leadership and policy direction for water and sanitation across the African Union's 55 member states.[^cite_note-79] In late 2025, AMCOW formally adopted the Africa Water Vision 2063 and Policy, which replaced the previous 2025 roadmap. This updated gramework integrates water security directly into the African Union's Agenda 2063, making shared water systems as instruments for climate resilience, economic trade, and regional peace.[^cite_note-80] The African Network of Basin Organisations, or ANBO also operates alongside AMCOW, coordinating technical capacity-building and standardizing data collection methodologies among Africa's scattered river basin entities.

To manage specific shared river and lake systems, African nations utilize specialized River Basin Organizations.[^cite_note-81] These institutions manage competing water claims, negotiate hydrological infrastructure development like hydroelectric dams, and implement environmental protection plans.

Notable examples include the Nile Basin Initiative, a cooperative partnership formed in 1999 by the Nile riparian states. It operates as a transitional institutional mechanism to foster socio-economic benefits, and equitably utilize shared resources along the Nile River.[^cite_note-82][^cite_note-83] Similarly, the Niger Basin Authority acts as one of the oldest regional water bodies on the continent, governing the Niger River basin across nine West and Central African nations. Niger Basin Authority's aim is to promote integrated development in agriculture, energy, and transport.[^cite_note-\:2-77] In southern Africa, the Zambezi Watercourse Commission provides a joint governance framework for the eight riparian states sharing the Zambezi River basin, focusing heavily on equitable utilization and sustainable water infrastructure planning.

International initiatives, such as the European Union-backed Team Europe Initiative on Transboundary Water Management in Africa, provide development assistance to these organizations. They finance long-term infrastructure and update climate-resilient water allocation plans.[^cite_note-84]

### Managing water in urban settings



Typical urban water cycle depicting drinking [water purification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_purification) and municipal [sewage treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewage_treatment) systems

This section is an excerpt from [Integrated urban water management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_urban_water_management).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Integrated_urban_water_management\&action=edit)]

[Integrated urban water management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_urban_water_management) (IUWM) is the practice of managing [freshwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freshwater), [wastewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wastewater), and [storm water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storm_water) as components of a [basin-wide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin) management plan. It builds on existing [water supply](684928.md) and [sanitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanitation) considerations within an [urban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_area) settlement by incorporating urban [water management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_management) within the scope of the entire river basin.[^cite_note-85] IUWM is commonly seen as a strategy for achieving the goals of [Water Sensitive Urban Design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_Sensitive_Urban_Design). IUWM seeks to change the impact of [urban development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_development) on the natural [water cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_cycle), based on the premise that by managing the urban water cycle as a whole; a more efficient use of resources can be achieved providing not only economic benefits but also improved social and environmental outcomes. One approach is to establish an inner, urban, water cycle loop through the implementation of reuse strategies. Developing this urban water cycle loop requires an understanding both of the natural, pre-development, water balance and the post-development water balance. Accounting for flows in the pre- and post-development systems is an important step toward limiting urban impacts on the natural water cycle.[^cite_note-Integrated_urban_water_management_CSRIO-86]

IUWM within an urban water system can also be conducted by performance assessment of any new intervention strategies by developing a holistic approach which encompasses various system elements and criteria including [sustainability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainability) type ones in which integration of water system components including [water supply](684928.md), [waste water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waste_water) and [storm water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storm_water) subsystems would be advantageous.[^cite_note-Integrated_urban_water_management_\:0-87] Simulation of [metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism) type flows in urban water system can also be useful for analysing processes in urban water cycle of IUWM.[^cite_note-Integrated_urban_water_management_\:0-87][^cite_note-88]
