**National Public Radio** (**NPR**) is an American [public broadcasting](178808.md) organization headquartered in [Washington, D.C.](108956.md), with its NPR West headquarters in [Culver City, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culver_City,_California).[^cite_note-2] It serves as a national [syndicator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_syndication) to a network of more than [1,000 public radio stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_NPR_stations) in the United States.[^cite_note-Audience-3]

Funding for NPR comes from dues and fees paid by member stations, [underwriting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underwriting_spot) from corporate sponsors, and (until 2025) annual grants from the publicly funded [Corporation for Public Broadcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporation_for_Public_Broadcasting).[^cite_note-4] Most of its member stations are owned by non-profit organizations, including [public school districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_school_district), colleges, and universities. NPR operates independently of any government or corporation, and has full control of its content.[^cite_note-5]

NPR produces and distributes both news and cultural programming. The organization's flagship shows are two [drive-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drive-time) news broadcasts: *[Morning Edition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morning_Edition)* and the afternoon *[All Things Considered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Things_Considered)*, both carried by most NPR member stations, and among the [most popular radio programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_most-listened-to_radio_programs) in The USA.[^cite_note-All_Things_Considered-6][^cite_note-Morning_Edition-7] As of March 2018, the drive-time programs attract an audience of 14.9 million and 14.7 million per week, respectively.[^cite_note-HighestRatings-8]

NPR manages the [Public Radio Satellite System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Radio_Satellite_System), which distributes its programs and other programming from independent producers and networks such as [American Public Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Public_Media) and [Public Radio Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Radio_Exchange), and which also acts as a [primary entry point for the Emergency Alert System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_Alert_System#Primary_Entry_Point_stations). Its content is also available on-demand online, on mobile networks, and in many cases, as [podcasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podcast).[^cite_note-9]

## Name

The organization's legal name is *National Public Radio* and its trademarked brand is NPR; it is known by both names.[^cite_note-Name-10] In June 2010, the organization announced that it was "making a conscious effort to consistently refer to ourselves as NPR on-air and online" because NPR is the common name for the organization and its radio hosts have used the tag line "This ... is NPR" for many years.[^cite_note-Name-10] *National Public Radio* remains the legal name of the group, however, as it has been since 1970.[^cite_note-Name-10]

## History

### 1970s



1970s logo

NPR replaced the [National Educational Radio Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Educational_Radio_Network) on February 26, 1970, following Congressional passage of the [Public Broadcasting Act of 1967](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Broadcasting_Act_of_1967).[^cite_note-JARVIK-11] This act was signed into law by President [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson), and established the [Corporation for Public Broadcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporation_for_Public_Broadcasting), which also created the [Public Broadcasting Service (PBS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Broadcasting_Service_\(PBS\)) for television in addition to NPR.

The board then hired [Donald Quayle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Quayle) to be the first president of NPR with 30 employees and 90 charter member local stations, and studios in Washington, D.C.[^cite_note-12]

NPR aired its first broadcast on April 20, 1971, covering [United States Senate](24909346.md) hearings on the ongoing [Vietnam War](32611.md) in Southeast Asia. The afternoon drive-time newscast *[All Things Considered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Things_Considered)* premiered on May 3, 1971, first hosted by [Robert Conley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Conley_\(reporter\)).[^cite_note-13] NPR was primarily a production and distribution organization until 1977, when it merged with the [Association of Public Radio Stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_of_Public_Radio_Stations). *[Morning Edition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morning_Edition)* premiered on November 5, 1979, first hosted by [Bob Edwards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Edwards).[^cite_note-14]

### 1980s

NPR suffered an almost fatal setback in 1983 when efforts to expand services created a deficit of nearly \$7 million (equivalent to \$19 million in 2022 dollars). After a Congressional investigation and the resignation of NPR's then-president [Frank Mankiewicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Mankiewicz), the Corporation for Public Broadcasting agreed to lend the network money to stave off bankruptcy.[^cite_note-15] In exchange, NPR agreed to a new arrangement whereby the annual CPB stipend that it had previously received directly would be divided among local stations instead; in turn, those stations would support NPR productions on a subscription basis. NPR also agreed to turn its satellite service into a cooperative venture (the [Public Radio Satellite System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Radio_Satellite_System)), making it possible for non-NPR shows to get national distribution. It took NPR approximately three years to pay off the debt.[^cite_note-16]



1990s logo

### 1990s

[Delano Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delano_Lewis), the president of [C\&P Telephone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C\&P_Telephone), left that position to become NPR's CEO and president in January 1994.[^cite_note-lewis1994-17] Lewis resigned in August 1998.[^cite_note-lewis1994-17][^cite_note-klose1998-18] In November 1998, NPR's board of directors hired [Kevin Klose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Klose), the director of the [International Broadcasting Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Broadcasting_Bureau), as its president and chief executive officer.[^cite_note-klose1998-18]

### 2000s

> [September 11th attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_11th_attacks) made it apparent in a very urgent way that we need another facility that could keep NPR going if something devastating happens in Washington.

— Jay Kernis, NPR's senior VP for programming

[^cite_note-nprwest2002-19]

NPR spent nearly \$13 million to acquire and equip a [West Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Coast_of_the_United_States) 25,000-square-foot (2,300 m<sup>2</sup>) production facility, **NPR West**, which opened in [Culver City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culver_City), [Los Angeles County, California](79734.md), in November 2002. With room for up to 90 employees, it was established to expand its production capabilities, improve its coverage of the western United States, and create a backup production facility capable of keeping NPR on the air in the event of a catastrophe in Washington, D.C.[^cite_note-nprwest2002-19]

In November 2003, NPR received over \$200 million from the estate of the late [Joan B. Kroc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_B._Kroc), the widow of [Ray Kroc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Kroc), founder of [McDonald's](2480627.md). This was the largest monetary gift ever to a cultural institution.[^cite_note-nyt-20][^cite_note-21]

In 2004, the Kroc gift increased NPR's budget by over 50% to \$153 million. Of the money, \$34 million was deposited in its [endowment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_endowment).[^cite_note-22] The endowment fund before the gift totaled \$35 million. NPR will use the interest from the bequest to expand its news staff and reduce some member stations' fees.[^cite_note-nyt-20]

In August 2005, NPR entered podcasting with a directory of over 170 programs created by NPR and member stations. Users downloaded NPR and other public radio podcasts 5 million times by November of that year. Ten years later, by March 2015, users downloaded podcasts produced only by NPR 94 million times,[^cite_note-23] and NPR podcasts like *Fresh Air* and the *TED Radio Hour* routinely made the [iTunes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITunes) Top Podcasts list.[^cite_note-24]

[Ken Stern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Stern) became chief executive in September 2006, reportedly as the "hand-picked successor" of CEO Kevin Klose, who gave up the job but remained as NPR's president; Stern had worked with Klose at [Radio Free Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Free_Europe).[^cite_note-washpost2008-25]

On December 10, 2008, NPR announced that it would reduce its workforce by 7% and cancel the news programs *[Day to Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_to_Day)* and *[News & Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_&_Notes)*.[^cite_note-NPRTCSPla-26] The organization indicated this was in response to a rapid drop in corporate [underwriting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underwriting_spot) during the [2008 financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_financial_crisis).[^cite_note-NPRTCSPla-26]

In the fall of 2008, NPR programming reached a record 27.5 million people weekly, according to Arbitron ratings figures. NPR stations reach 32.7 million listeners overall.[^cite_note-audiencefigures-27]

In March 2008, the NPR Board announced that Stern would be stepping down from his role as chief executive officer, following conflict with NPR's board of directors "over the direction of the organization", including issues NPR's member station managers had had with NPR's expansion into [new media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_media) "at the expense of serving" the stations that financially support NPR.[^cite_note-washpost2008-25]

As of 2009, corporate sponsorship comprised 26% of the NPR budget.[^cite_note-npr-28]

### 2010s



NPR's former headquarters at 635 [Massachusetts Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Avenue_\(Washington,_D.C.\)) NW in Washington, D.C. (demolished in 2013)



The new NPR headquarters at 1111 [North Capitol Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Capitol_Street), NE.

In October 2010, NPR accepted a \$1.8 million grant from the [Open Society Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Society_Institute). The grant is meant to begin a project called Impact of Government that was intended to add at least 100 journalists at NPR member radio stations in all 50 states by 2013.[^cite_note-NYTSoros-29] The OSI has made previous donations but does not take on-air credit for its gifts.[^cite_note-Chiu-30]

In April 2013, NPR moved from its home of 19 years (635 [Massachusetts Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Avenue_\(Washington,_D.C.\)) NW) to new offices and production facilities at 1111 North Capitol Street NE in a building adapted from the former [C\&P Telephone Warehouse and Repair Facility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chesapeake_and_Potomac_Telephone_Company_Warehouse_and_Repair_Facility).[^cite_note-31] The new headquarters—at the corner of North Capitol Street NE and L Street NW—is in the burgeoning [NoMa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NoMa) neighborhood of Washington.[^cite_note-32] The first show scheduled to be broadcast from the new studios was *[Weekend Edition Saturday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weekend_Edition_Saturday)*.[^cite_note-33] *[Morning Edition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morning_Edition)* was the last show to move to the new location.[^cite_note-34] In June 2013 NPR canceled the weekday call-in show *[Talk of the Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talk_of_the_Nation)*.[^cite_note-35]

In September 2013, some of NPR's 840 full- and part-time employees were offered a [voluntary buyout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voluntary_redundancy) plan to reduce staff by 10 percent and return NPR to a balanced budget by the 2015 [fiscal year](5752099.md).[^cite_note-36]

In December 2018, *[The Washington Post](102226.md)* reported that between 20 and 22 percent of NPR staff were classified as [temps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temporary_work), which compares to about five percent of a typical for-profit television station. Some of the temporary staff members told the newspaper that the systems were "exploitative", but NPR's president of operations said the system was in place because the station is a "media company that strives to be innovative and nimble."[^cite_note-37]

In December 2018, NPR launched a new podcast analytics technology called Remote Audio Data (RAD), which developer Stacey Goers described as a "method for sharing listening metrics from podcast applications straight back to publishers, with extreme care and respect for user privacy."[^cite_note-38]

### 2020s

In late November 2022, chief executive officer John Lansing told staffers in a memo that NPR needed to reduce spending by \$10 million during that fiscal year due to a drop in revenue from sponsors. The amount was approximately three percent of the organization's annual budget.[^cite_note-39]

In February 2023, Lansing announced in a memo that the network would be laying off approximately 10 percent of the workforce due to reduced advertising revenue. He said the annual operating budget was approximately \$300 million, and the gap would likely be between \$30 and \$32 million.[^cite_note-40]

In January 2024, NPR's board named former [Wikimedia Foundation](18618509.md) CEO [Katherine Maher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katherine_Maher) as its new CEO, effective late March.[^cite_note-41]

On January 31, 2025, a [Defense Department](7279897.md) memo announced that NPR was among the major news outlets required to move out of its longtime workspace on the Correspondents' Corridor in [the Pentagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pentagon), under a new Annual Media Rotation Program for the Pentagon Press Corps.[^cite_note-42]

Following enactment of the [rescission law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rescissions_Act_of_2025) in July 2025, CPB announced an orderly wind-down of operations and furloughs as funding was withdrawn, while public broadcasters and allies signaled legal and legislative pushback.[^cite_note-43]

In October 2025, NPR, among other major news organizations across the political spectrum, including [Associated Press](18935732.md), [Bloomberg News](3057255.md), [The Washington Post](102226.md), [The New York Times](30680.md), [CNN](62028.md), and [Fox News](11121.md) rejected new Defense Department media-access rules they said restricted independent reporting; the Pentagon Press Association signaled potential legal action.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46]

## Governance

NPR is a membership organization. Member stations are required to be non-commercial or [non-commercial educational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-commercial_educational) radio stations; have at least five full-time professional employees; operate for at least 18 hours per day; and not be designed solely to further a [religious broadcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_broadcasting) philosophy or be used for classroom [distance learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distance_learning) programming. Each member station receives one vote at the annual NPR board meetings—exercised by its designated Authorized Station Representative ("A-Rep").

To oversee the day-to-day operations and prepare its budget, members elect a board of directors. The board was previously composed of ten A-Reps, five members of the general public, and the chair of the NPR Foundation. On November 2, 2015, NPR Members approved a change in the NPR Bylaws to expand the board of directors to 23 directors, consisting of 12 Member Directors who are managers of NPR Member stations and are elected to the board by their fellow Member stations, 9 Public Directors who are prominent members of the public selected by the board and confirmed by NPR Member stations, the NPR Foundation Chair, and the NPR President & CEO.[^cite_note-NPR_Board-47] Terms are for three years and are [staggered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staggered_board_of_directors) such that some stand for election every year.[^cite_note-48]

## Funding

In 2020, NPR released a budget for FY21 anticipating revenue of \$250 million, a slight decrease from the prior year due to impacts of COVID-19. The budget anticipated \$240 million in operating expenses, plus additional debt service and capital costs that led to a cash deficit of approximately \$4 million. The budget included \$25 million in budget cuts.[^cite_note-51]

### Funding pre-2000

During the 1970s and early 1980s, the majority of NPR funding came from the federal government. Steps were taken during the Reagan administration in the 1980s to completely wean NPR from government support, but [the 1983 funding crisis](#History) forced the network to make immediate changes.

### Funding since the 2000s

According to [Corporation for Public Broadcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporation_for_Public_Broadcasting) (CPB), in 2009 11.3% of the aggregate revenues of all public radio broadcasting stations were funded from federal sources, principally through CPB;[^cite_note-CPB2009-52] in 2012, 10.9% of the revenues for Public Radio came from federal sources.[^cite_note-CPB2012-53]

In 2010, NPR revenues totaled \$180 million, with the bulk of revenues coming from programming fees, [grants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grant_\(money\)) from [foundations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundation_\(charity\)) or business entities, contributions and [sponsorships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sponsorships).[^cite_note-npr-28] According to the 2009 financial statement, about 50% of NPR revenues come from the fees it charges member stations for programming and distribution charges.[^cite_note-npr-28] Typically, NPR member stations receive funds through on-air [pledge drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pledge_drive), corporate underwriting, state and local governments, educational institutions, and the federally funded CPB. In 2009, member stations derived 6% of their revenue from federal, state and local government funding, 10% of their revenue from CPB grants, and 14% of their revenue from universities.[^cite_note-npr-28][^cite_note-NPR_Responds-54] NPR receives a small number of competitive grants from CPB and federal agencies like the Department of Education and the Department of Commerce.[^cite_note-npr-28] This funding amounts to less than 1% of revenues.[^cite_note-55]

In 2011, NPR announced the roll-out of their own online [advertising network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advertising_network), which allows member stations to run geographically [targeted advertisement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Targeted_advertisement) spots from national sponsors that may otherwise be unavailable to their local area, opening additional advertising-related revenue streams to the broadcaster.[^cite_note-56]

*Center Stage*, a mix of [native advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_advertising) and [banner ad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banner_ad) featured prominently on the NPR homepage, above-the-fold, was launched in 2013. The launch partner for *Center Stage* was [Squarespace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squarespace).[^cite_note-57]

In 2014, NPR CEO Jarl Mohn said the network would begin to increase revenue by having brands NPR views as more relevant to the audience underwrite NPR programs and requesting higher rates from them.[^cite_note-58]

For the year ended September 30, 2018, total operating revenues were \$235 million, increasing to almost \$259 million by September 2019.[^cite_note-59]

In 2023, [*Current*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_\(newspaper\)) reported that NPR partnered with [Spotify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spotify) to run targeted advertisements sold through the Spotify Audience Network platform within NPR programming, when NPR has empty slots available they otherwise were unable to sell to other advertisers directly.[^cite_note-megaphone-60]

#### Funding under the Second Trump administration

##### FCC underwriting investigation

This section is an excerpt from [PBS § FCC underwriting investigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PBS#FCC_underwriting_investigation).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=PBS\&action=edit)]

In January 2025, [Federal Communications Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Communications_Commission) (FCC) Chair [Brendan Carr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brendan_Carr) ordered an investigation of the [corporate underwriting sponsorships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underwriting_spot) of PBS and NPR member stations for possible violations of FCC regulations prohibiting noncommercial broadcasters from airing [advertisements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advertisements).[^cite_note-61][^cite_note-62] The following March, the [Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_Permanent_Subcommittee_on_Investigations) opened an inquiry into the investigation ordered by Carr.[^cite_note-63] In 1981, Congress amended the [Communications Act of 1934](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communications_Act_of_1934) to authorize noncommercial station licensees to offer services and facilities in exchange for remuneration on the condition that it not interfere with their provision of public telecommunications services.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGAO200490–97-64][^cite_note-67][^cite_note-71] In 1984, the FCC adopted a policy under the amendment allowing noncommercial stations to broadcast underwriting spots from for-profit entities that provided donations or underwriting.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGAO200490–97-64][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFCC2011315–316-72]

While the FCC prohibited underwriting spots from including certain specific content (e.g. promotion of the contributor's products, services, or business, comparative or qualitative descriptions of products, price information, [calls to action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Call_to_action_\(marketing\)), or inducements to buy, sell, or lease products), the FCC has generally given noncommercial broadcasters deference in determining compliance with its underwriting rules, placed no specific limit on the length of the underwriting spots, and permitted the use of logos and [slogans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slogan) for the purpose of identifying the contributor, business location information and phone numbers, value-neutral descriptions of [product or service lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_lining), brand and [trade names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_name), and product and service listings.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGAO200490–97-64][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFCC2011315–316-72] Also, the FCC's underwriting rules and prohibition on noncommercial broadcasters receiving advertising only apply to underwriting spots that are [broadcast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcasting_in_the_United_States) and do not apply to the websites of noncommercial broadcasters.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFCC2011315–316-72][^cite_note-75] The 1981 amendment to the Communications Act also established a temporary commission to identify alternative sources of funding for public broadcasting and study the potential for advertising revenue using limited demonstrations, but in its final report released in 1983, the commission found that the prospect of significant advertising revenue was limited and instead recommended that Congress continue providing the appropriation to Corporation for Public Broadcasting (CPB).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGAO200490–97-64]

In 2012, the CPB issued a report commissioned by Congress and that included [market analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_analysis) conducted by [Booz & Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booz_&_Company) which noted that corporate underwriting accounted for less than one-fifth of the revenue for public television and radio stations and had declined substantially due to the [Great Recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession_in_the_United_States).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBooz20121–5,_16–21-76] Along with the 1983 report and a 2007 [Government Accountability Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Accountability_Office) report about public television specifically,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGAO200490–97-64][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGAO200736,_41–44-77] the CPB report concluded that growth in such underwriting revenue was unlikely, that allowing public broadcasters to air advertisements would not offset a decline in federal funding while likely contributing to a decline in support from other sources, and that there was no alternative source of funding to the federal CPB appropriation that could sustain public broadcasting service at the same level.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBooz20121–5,_16–21,_26–33,_56–84-78] Analysis of CPB data published by the [Pew Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pew_Research_Center) in August 2023 found that underwriting revenue for NPR member stations from 2008 through 2021 was mostly flat and fell below 2009 levels in 2021 following the [COVID-19 recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_impact_of_the_COVID-19_pandemic_in_the_United_States), while corporate funding for the *[PBS News Hour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PBS_News_Hour)* ranged from 17% to 23% of total revenue from 2015 through 2022.[^cite_note-79]

##### Executive Order 14290

This section is an excerpt from [Executive Order 14290](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_14290).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Executive_Order_14290\&action=edit)]

[Executive Order 14290](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_14290), titled "Ending Taxpayer Subsidization of Biased Media", is an [executive order](210541.md) signed by U.S. president [Donald Trump](4848272.md) on May 1, 2025 to end federal funding for NPR (a radio network) and [PBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PBS) (a television network) by the [Corporation for Public Broadcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporation_for_Public_Broadcasting) (CPB) and by federal agencies, alleging biased news coverage in violation of the [Public Broadcasting Act of 1967](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Broadcasting_Act_of_1967) (PBA) and that public funding for news programming was "not only outdated and unnecessary but corrosive to the appearance of journalistic independence" in the current [U.S. media market](158546.md).[^cite_note-80][^cite_note-Executive_Order_14290_NBC_5-2-2025-81][^cite_note-82]

CPB, PBS, and NPR executives issued press releases arguing that the executive order was unlawful under the PBA and that the organizations would explore how to continue providing programming while challenging the order.[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-Executive_Order_14290_NBC_5-2-2025-81][^cite_note-84] On May 27, NPR and three public radio stations sued the Trump administration for ending their federal funding under the executive order, citing it as a violation of the [First Amendment](31653.md).[^cite_note-Executive_Order_14290_nbc-27may2025-85][^cite_note-Executive_Order_14290_guard-27may2025-86][^cite_note-Executive_Order_14290_cl-27may2025-87] On May 30, PBS sued the Trump administration for ending their federal funding under the executive order.[^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89][^cite_note-Executive_Order_14290_cl-30may2025-90]

Before the executive order was issued, the CPB filed a lawsuit against the Trump administration on April 28 after Trump attempted to fire three of the five members of the CPB's [board of directors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_of_directors),[^cite_note-91][^cite_note-92] while the CPB also filed a lawsuit against the [Federal Emergency Management Agency](73797.md) (FEMA) in March 2025 for halting their funding under the Next Generation Warning System Grant Program within the [Integrated Public Alert and Warning System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_Public_Alert_and_Warning_System).[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-FOOTNOTECRS20256–7-94][^cite_note-95]

FEMA released the funds on April 24.[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-FOOTNOTECRS20257-97] On June 8, [District of Columbia U.S. District Court](1786384.md) Judge [Randolph Moss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randolph_Moss) ruled against a [preliminary injunction](39343.md#Preliminary_injunctions) requested by the CPB in its lawsuit against the attempted director removals since the CPB changed its [by-laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/By-law#Organizational_by-laws) afterward under the District of Columbia Nonprofit Corporation Act to prevent any authority, including the President of the United States, from removing a director without a two-thirds vote of the other directors, which allowed for the directors to keep their positions.[^cite_note-98][^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100] On July 15, the Trump administration filed a separate lawsuit to remove the same CPB directors.[^cite_note-101][^cite_note-102]

On March 31, 2026, Judge Moss ruled for the plaintiffs, blocking the order on First Amendment grounds. In his ruling, Moss wrote, "It is difficult to conceive of clearer evidence that a government action is targeted at viewpoints that the president does not like and seeks to squelch."[^cite_note-103]

##### Rescissions Act of 2025

Main article: [Rescissions Act of 2025](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rescissions_Act_of_2025)

The [Rescissions Act of 2025](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rescissions_Act_of_2025) altered the financial outlook for the [Corporation for Public Broadcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporation_for_Public_Broadcasting) (CPB) by reclaiming unspent allocations and reducing advance funding. While NPR received limited direct support from the CPB, the legislation sparked concern for local member stations. Rural broadcasters were particularly vulnerable because they depended on CPB grants for a larger share of their operating budgets than urban stations.[^cite_note-104]

### Underwriting spots versus commercials

In contrast with [commercial broadcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_broadcasting), NPR's radio broadcasts do not carry traditional commercials, but has advertising in the form of brief statements from major sponsors which may include corporate [slogans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advertising_slogan), descriptions of products and services, and contact information such as website addresses and [telephone numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone_number).[^cite_note-105] These statements are called [underwriting spots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underwriting_spot) and, unlike commercials, are governed by specific FCC restrictions in addition to [truth in advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_advertising) laws; they cannot advocate a product or "promote the goods and services" of for-profit entities.[^cite_note-106] These restrictions apply only to radio broadcasts and not NPR's other digital platforms. When questioned on the subject of how corporate underwriting revenues and foundation grants were holding up during the recession, in a speech broadcast on [C-SPAN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C-SPAN) before the [National Press Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Press_Club_\(United_States\)) on March 2, 2009, then president and CEO [Vivian Schiller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivian_Schiller) stated: "underwriting is down, it's down for everybody; this is the area that is most down for us, in sponsorship, underwriting, advertising, call it whatever you want; just like it is for all of media."[^cite_note-107] Hosts of the NPR program *[Planet Money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet_Money)* stated the audience is indeed a product being sold to advertisers in the same way as commercial stations, saying: "they are not advertisers exactly but, they have a lot of the same characteristics; let's just say that."[^cite_note-108]

## Audience

According to NPR's 2022 data, 30.7 million listeners tuned into its programs each week.[^cite_note-109] This is down from its 2017 high of 37.7 million,[^cite_note-HighestRatings-8] but still well above its total of 20.9 million in 2008.[^cite_note-110] A Pew Research Center poll in 2025 found that approximately 20% of Americans regularly get their news from NPR.[^cite_note-111]

### Demographics

According to 2015 figures, 87% of the NPR terrestrial public radio audience and 67% of the NPR podcast audience is white.[^cite_note-112] According to the 2012 [Pew Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pew_Research_Center) 2012 News Consumption Survey, NPR listeners tend to be highly educated, with 54% of regular listeners being college graduates and 21% having some college.[^cite_note-Pew-113] NPR's audience is almost exactly average in terms of the sex of listeners (49% male, 51% female).[^cite_note-Pew-113]

A 2012 Pew Research Center survey found that the NPR audience leans [Democratic](5043544.md) (17% [Republican](32070.md), 37% [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_party_\(United_States\)), 43% Democratic) and politically moderate (21% conservative, 39% moderate, 36% liberal).[^cite_note-Pew-113] A late 2019 survey, also by Pew, found that NPR's audience overwhelmingly leaned Democratic. Further, 87% of those surveyed identified as Democrats, or leaning Democratic, and 12% were Republicans.[^cite_note-114]

### Trust

In 2005, a [Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Harris_Poll) telephone survey found that NPR was the most trusted news source in the United States.[^cite_note-115][^cite_note-pew21-116] A 2014 Pew poll reported that, of adults who had heard of NPR, 55% of those polled trusted it; this was a similar level of listener trust as [CNN](62028.md), [NBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NBC), and [ABC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABC_News_\(United_States\)).[^cite_note-117] A 2025 survey of likely voters found 53% trusted public media in the United States compared to 35% for the media in general.[^cite_note-118]

### Ratings

NPR stations generally subscribe to the [Nielsen rating service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nielsen_ratings), but are not included in published ratings and rankings such as *[Radio & Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_&_Records)*. NPR station listenership is measured by Nielsen in both Diary and PPM (people meter) markets. NPR stations are frequently not included in "summary level" diary data used by most advertising agencies for media planning. Data on NPR listening can be accessed using "respondent level" diary data. Additionally, all radio stations (public and commercial) are treated equally within the PPM data sets making NPR station listenership data much more widely available to the media planning community. NPR's signature morning news program, *Morning Edition*, is the network's most popular program, drawing 14.63 million listeners a week, with its afternoon newsmagazine, *All Things Considered*, a close second, with 14.6 million listeners a week according to 2017 Nielsen ratings data.[^cite_note-Growth-119] Arbitron data is also provided by [Radio Research Consortium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Research_Consortium), a non-profit corporation which subscribes to the Arbitron service and distributes the data to NPR and other non-commercial stations and on its website.[^cite_note-sfcron-120]

## Digital media

NPR's history in [digital media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_media) includes the work of an independent, for-profit company called **Public Interactive**, which was founded in 1999[^cite_note-PRINPR2008-121] and acquired by [PRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Radio_International) in June 2004, when it became a non-profit company.[^cite_note-nprDS2004-122] By July 2008, Public Interactive had "170 subscribers who collectively operate 325 public radio and television stations" and clients such as *[Car Talk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Car_Talk)*, *[The World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_\(radio_program\))*, and *[The Tavis Smiley Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tavis_Smiley_Show)*; by the end of that month, NPR acquired Public Interactive from PRI[^cite_note-PRINPR2008-121] In March 2011, NPR revealed a restructuring proposal in which Boston-based Public Interactive would become NPR Digital Services, separate from the Washington D.C.–based NPR Digital Media, which focuses on NPR-branded services. NPR Digital Services would continue offering its services to public TV stations.[^cite_note-NPRDigital2011-123]

The technical backbone of its digital news publishing system is Core Publisher, which was built on [Drupal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drupal), an open-source [content management system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content_management_system).[^cite_note-NPRDigital2011-123]



Kinsey Wilson and the npr.org crew at the 69th Annual Peabody Awards

In 2021, NPR had been dubbed as "leveraging the Twitter generation"[^cite_note-124] because of its adaptation of the popular [microblogging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microblog) service as one of its primary vehicles of information. Of NPR's Twitter followers, the majority (67%) also listened to NPR on the radio. In a 2010 survey of more than 10,000 respondents, NPR found that its Twitter followers were younger, more connected to the [social web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_web), and more likely to access content through digital platforms such as its [Peabody Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peabody_Award)-winning website npr.org, as well as [podcasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podcast), [mobile apps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_app) and more.[^cite_note-NPR_Twitter_Survey-125] As of 2014, NPR had more than one Twitter account including @NPR;[^cite_note-126] its survey found that most respondents followed between two and five NPR accounts, including topical account, show-specific accounts and on-air staff accounts.[^cite_note-NPR_Twitter_Survey-125]

In addition, NPR's Facebook page became a part of the company's foray into social media. Started by college student and fan Geoff Campbell[^cite_note-127] in 2008, the page was quickly taken over by NPR,[^cite_note-128] and grew to over to nearly 4 million fans by 2010. Facebook is a popular example of the company's then new focus on a younger audience.[^cite_note-129]

In May 2018, a group led by NPR acquired the podcasting app [Pocket Casts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pocket_Casts).[^cite_note-130] On July 16, 2021, [Automattic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automattic) acquired Pocket Casts from NPR.[^cite_note-131]

### Mobile Apps

The NPR News app was launched in 2009, first for [iOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IOS) smartphones, followed later by a version for [Android](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Android_\(operating_system\)) users. Both versions included a sections with NPR's print news, podcasts, their five-minute hourly newscasts, and recordings of stories that were aired previously.[^cite_note-132][^cite_note-133] The iOS app additionally included [live streams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livestreamed_news) of various member stations, and program guides for the stations, features that were not originally included in the Android version. The Android app was released under an [open source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_source) [Apache License](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apache_License), while the code of the iOS version remained [proprietary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proprietary_software).[^cite_note-134]



NPR One logo

In July 2014, NPR launched NPR One on iOS and Android, which aimed to make it easier for listeners to stream local NPR stations live, and listen to NPR podcasts by autoplaying content and permitting easy navigation.[^cite_note-135] Since launch, NPR has made the service available on additional channels: [Windows mobile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_mobile) devices, web browsers, [Chromecast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromecast), Apple Car Play, [Apple Watch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Watch), [Android Auto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Android_Auto), [Android Wear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Android_Wear), [Samsung Gear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samsung_Gear) S2 and S3, [Amazon Fire TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Fire_TV), and [Amazon Alexa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Alexa)–enabled devices.[^cite_note-136] *[The New York Times](30680.md)* listed NPR One as one of 2016's "best apps".[^cite_note-137]

In April 2018, the NPR app (renamed from the NPR News app), underwent a significant update that changed the [design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User_interface), [user experience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User_experience), selection of content, and added breaking news [notifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notification_system).[^cite_note-138]

In 2019, NPR planned to merge the NPR One service into the NPR app to reduce confusion.[^cite_note-139] By December 2023, the merge was complete and the NPR One app was made unavailable.[^cite_note-140][^cite_note-141]

### Public radio programs not affiliated with NPR

Many programs broadcast on U.S. public radio stations are not affiliated with NPR. If these programs are distributed by another distributor, a public radio station must also affiliate with that network to take that network's programming.

Most public radio stations are NPR member stations and many are affiliate stations of APM and PRX at the same time. The organizations have different governance structures and missions and relationships with stations.

## Controversies

Main article: [NPR controversies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NPR_controversies)

### Allegations of political or ideological bias

NPR station [WNYC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WNYC) in New York City delved into the question of purported liberal bias on its *[On the Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Media)* program in March 2011.[^cite_note-OTM_Bias-147] The program invited four conservative listeners to participate in the discussion, and highlighted two studies that assessed the issue of bias in the news media, with differing results. One study (by professors at [UCLA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCLA) and the [University of Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Missouri)), which was based on the number of liberal or conservative [think-tanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Think-tank) that were cited by a range of news outlets, found that NPR's *[Morning Edition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morning_Edition)* was somewhat liberal. The other study, by [Fairness and Accuracy in Reporting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairness_and_Accuracy_in_Reporting), counted the number of Republicans and Democrats who were heard on *Morning Edition* and *[All Things Considered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Things_Considered)*, and found "a very strong slant in favor of the GOP."[^cite_note-OTM_Bias-147]

Public radio host [Lisa Simeone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisa_Simeone), who worked for NPR from 1998 to 2002, accused NPR's [Pentagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pentagon) reporting of being "little more than Pentagon press releases."[^cite_note-148] The NPR [ombudsman](26289198.md) has described how NPR's coverage of the [Israel-Palestinian conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel-Palestinian_conflict) has been simultaneously criticized as biased by both sides.[^cite_note-149] University of Texas journalism professor and author [Robert Jensen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Jensen) has criticized NPR as taking a pro-war stance during coverage of [Iraq war protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_war_protests).[^cite_note-150] During the 2020 election, NPR declined to cover the controversy surrounding a *[New York Post](102227.md)* article on the [Hunter Biden laptop controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter_Biden_laptop_controversy), saying "...we don't want to waste the listeners' and readers' time on stories that are just pure distractions..."[^cite_note-151]

In 2024, veteran NPR journalist [Uri Berliner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uri_Berliner) stated that NPR demonstrated a left-wing bias in its reporting after the [2016 United States presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_States_presidential_election), citing NPR's approach to coverage of the Hunter Biden laptop controversy, the [Mueller special counsel investigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mueller_special_counsel_investigation), the [origin of SARS-CoV-2](66389098.md), and the [Gaza war](74998519.md).[^cite_note-152][^cite_note-153] According to Berliner, NPR's management prioritized focus on race and identity politics, while NPR simultaneously lost viewpoint diversity.[^cite_note-154] NPR editor-in-chief [Edith Chapin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edith_Chapin) claimed that NPR stood behind its work and defended its policies on inclusion.[^cite_note-155] NPR subsequently suspended Berliner for 5 days without pay, claiming that he did not secure NPR approval to work for another outlet.[^cite_note-156] Berliner subsequently resigned, citing disparagement by CEO Katherine Maher and her divisive views.[^cite_note-157]

#### Euphemisms for "torture"

In a controversial act, in 2009 NPR banned the use of the word "[torture](47702.md)" in regard to the [George W. Bush administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bush_administration)'s employment of so-called "[enhanced interrogation techniques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enhanced_interrogation_techniques)".[^cite_note-158] NPR Ombudswoman [Alicia Shepard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alicia_Shepard)'s defense of the policy was that "calling [waterboarding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterboarding) torture is tantamount to taking sides."[^cite_note-159] [UC Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UC_Berkeley_School_of_Information) Professor of Linguistics [Geoffrey Nunberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Nunberg) pointed out that virtually all media around the world, other than what he called the "spineless U.S. media", call these techniques torture.[^cite_note-160][^cite_note-161] In an article which criticized NPR and other U.S. media for their use of euphemisms for torture, independent journalist [Glenn Greenwald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenn_Greenwald) discussed what he called the enabling "corruption of American journalism":[^cite_note-162]

> This active media complicity in concealing that our Government created a systematic torture regime, by refusing ever to say so, is one of the principal reasons it was allowed to happen for so long. The steadfast, ongoing refusal of our leading media institutions to refer to what the Bush administration did as "torture" – even in the face of more than 100 detainee deaths; the use of that term by a leading Bush official to describe what was done at Guantanamo; and the fact that media outlets frequently use the word "torture" to describe exactly the same methods when used by other countries – reveals much about how the modern journalist thinks.

### *Live from Death Row* commentaries

In 1994, NPR arranged to air, on *All Things Considered*, a series of three-minute commentaries by [Mumia Abu-Jamal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mumia_Abu-Jamal), a journalist convicted of murdering [Philadelphia Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Police) officer Daniel Faulkner. They cancelled the commentaries after the [Fraternal Order of Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraternal_Order_of_Police) and members of the U.S. Congress objected.[^cite_note-WaPoAbul-Jamal-163]

### Juan Williams comments

On October 20, 2010, NPR terminated Senior News Analyst [Juan Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Williams)'s independent contract[^cite_note-WilliamsIndep-164] over a series of incidents culminating in remarks he made on the [Fox News Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_News_Channel) regarding Muslim head coverings and not feeling comfortable around women wearing them. Williams's firing, which was made abruptly without Williams being given a face-to-face meeting beforehand, was reported by *[The Washington Post](102226.md)* as being a key part of [Ellen Weiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellen_Weiss), NPR's top news executive at the time, being given an ultimatum on January 4, 2011, to either resign or be fired. On January 6, 2011, NPR announced that Weiss had quit.[^cite_note-165]

### Ronald Schiller comments

In March 2011, conservative political activist and provocateur [James O'Keefe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_O'Keefe) sent partners Simon Templar (a pen name) and Shaughn Adeleye[^cite_note-nprexec-166] to secretly record their discussion with Ronald Schiller, NPR's outgoing senior vice president for [fundraising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundraising), and an associate, in which Schiller made remarks viewed as disparaging of "the current Republican party, especially the [Tea Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tea_Party_movement)", and controversial comments regarding Palestine and funding for NPR. NPR disavowed Schiller's comments. CEO Vivian Schiller, who is not related to Ronald, later resigned over the fallout from the comments and the previous firing of Juan Williams.[^cite_note-167]

### July 4 reading of the Declaration of Independence

From 1988 to 2021,[^cite_note-BreaksDeclTrad-168][^cite_note-BreaksDeclTrad2-169] NPR broadcast an annual reading of the 1776 [United States Declaration of Independence](31874.md) over the radio.[^cite_note-170] In 2017, it began using Twitter as a medium for reading the document as well. On July 4, 2017, the 100+ tweets were met with considerable opposition, as some online supporters of [Donald Trump](4848272.md) mistakenly believed the words of the Declaration referring to [George III of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_III_of_the_United_Kingdom) were being directed towards the president. The tweets were called "trash"[^cite_note-kc-171] and were accused of being "propaganda",[^cite_note-172] condoning violence[^cite_note-kc-171] and calling for revolution.[^cite_note-173][^cite_note-174] The July 4, 2022, annual tradition was not held. Instead, referencing the recent [*Dobbs* decision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dobbs_v._Jackson_Women's_Health_Organization) and [voting rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_rights), host [Steve Inskeep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Inskeep) held a discussion on "what equality means" with two historians, contrasting Thomas Jefferson's use of "[All men are created equal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_men_are_created_equal)" in the Declaration with [his participation in slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson_and_slavery).[^cite_note-BreaksDeclTrad-168][^cite_note-BreaksDeclTrad2-169]

### Sexual harassment

In October 2017, sexual harassment charges were leveled against [Michael Oreskes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Oreskes), senior vice president of news and editorial director since 2015. Some of the accusations dated back to when he was Washington, D.C. bureau chief for *[The New York Times](30680.md)* during the 1990s, while others involved his conduct at NPR,[^cite_note-175] where eight women filed sexual harassment complaints against Oreskes.[^cite_note-176] After a report on the *Times* accusations was published in *[The Washington Post](102226.md)*, NPR put Oreskes on administrative leave, and the following day his resignation was requested.[^cite_note-177][^cite_note-178][^cite_note-washingtonpost1-179] CNN's [Brian Stelter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Stelter) reported that NPR staffers were dissatisfied with the handling of Oreskes, were demanding an external investigation, and that Oreskes poisoned the newsroom atmosphere by abusing his position to meet young women.[^cite_note-180] Oreskes resigned at the request of CEO [Jarl Mohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jarl_Mohn), was denied severance and separation benefits, and reimbursed NPR \$1,800 in expense account charges related to his meetings with women.[^cite_note-181][^cite_note-182]

In April 2026, *[Throughline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Throughline)* co-host Ramtin Arablouie left NPR amid an internal investigation. A colleague alleged Arablouie discussed his personal life during one-on-one meetings, made inappropriate comments during team meetings and flirted with guests and a contract employee. The employee said Arablouie described one producer as "a case for race mixing."[^cite_note-183]

### Elon Musk / Twitter controversy

On April 5, 2023, following [Elon Musk](909036.md)'s acquisition of the American social media platform Twitter, NPR's main Twitter account was designated as "US state-affiliated media". This label was typically reserved for foreign media outlets that directly represented the point of view of their respective governments, like Russia's [RT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT_\(TV_network\)) and China's [Xinhua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinhua).[^cite_note-\:0-184][^cite_note-\:1-185][^cite_note-\:2-186] Twitter's designation was widely considered controversial as NPR is an independent news organization that receives only a tiny fraction of its funding from the government. Twitter's previous policy had explicitly mentioned NPR, as well as the United Kingdom's [BBC](19344654.md), as examples of networks that were not considered as state-affiliated due to their editorial independence.[^cite_note-\:0-184][^cite_note-\:1-185][^cite_note-\:2-186] NPR ceased activity on its main Twitter account in response to the designation.[^cite_note-187]

On April 8, 2023, Twitter changed the designation of NPR's account from "state-affiliated" to "government-funded".[^cite_note-188] On April 10, after managing to get in contact with Musk himself, NPR reporter Bobby Allyn wrote in a tweet that the platform's owner told him he was relying on a list accessible through a Wikipedia category page, named "[Category\:Publicly funded broadcasters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Publicly_funded_broadcasters)", to determine which news organizations' accounts should be deemed as "government-funded media".[^cite_note-\:3-189][^cite_note-190]

On April 12, NPR announced that its accounts would no longer be active on Twitter,[^cite_note-\:3-189][^cite_note-\:4-191][^cite_note-\:5-192] citing the platform's "inaccurate and misleading" labeling of NPR as "government-funded media" despite the fact that it receives "less than 1 percent of its \$300 million annual budget" from the [Corporation for Public Broadcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporation_for_Public_Broadcasting).[^cite_note-\:3-189][^cite_note-\:4-191][^cite_note-193] In its last post on the platform, the network shared links to its alternative newsletters, websites and social media profiles in a thread.[^cite_note-\:4-191][^cite_note-194]

In an email to the staff explaining the decision, CEO John Lansing allowed individual NPR journalists and staffers to choose for themselves whether to keep using Twitter, while noting that "it would be a disservice to the serious work you all do here to continue to share it on a platform that is associating the federal charter for public media with an abandoning of editorial independence or standards."[^cite_note-\:3-189][^cite_note-\:5-192]

After NPR stopped posting on Twitter, Elon Musk threatened to forcefully reassign the @NPR handle to another user if NPR did not reactivate its Twitter activity.[^cite_note-195][^cite_note-196]
