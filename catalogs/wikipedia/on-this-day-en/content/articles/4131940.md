# Nuclear power in Japan



The [Kashiwazaki-Kariwa Nuclear Power Plant](11996462.md), a nuclear plant with seven units, the largest single nuclear power station in the world

[^cite_note-mycle-1]

Japan started using **nuclear power** for electricity generation in 1966, and it generated approximately a third of the country's electrical power prior to the [Fukushima accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_accident) in 2011.[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-wn-japan-3] After a near-nationwide shutdown following the accident, the share has recovered to 9% in 2025.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5]

The country's nuclear power industry was heavily influenced by the Fukushima accident, caused by the [2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_T%C5%8Dhoku_earthquake_and_tsunami). After the Fukushima accident, all reactors were shut down temporarily. As of November 2024, of the 54 nuclear reactors present in Japan before 2011, there were 33 operable reactors but only 13 reactors in 6 power plants were actually operating.[^cite_note-wn-japan-3] A total of 24 reactors are scheduled for decommissioning or are in the process of being decommissioned.[^cite_note-decom2020-6] Others are in the process of being reactivated, or are undergoing modifications aimed at improving resiliency against natural disasters; Japan's 2030 energy goals posit that at least 33 will be reactivated by a later date.[^cite_note-7]

The Fukushima accident hardened attitudes toward nuclear power. In June 2011, immediately after the accident, more than 80% of Japanese said they were [anti-nuclear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-nuclear) and distrusted government information on [radiation](25856.md),[^cite_note-refuse2011-8][^cite_note-M._V._Ramana_43-9][^cite_note-usatoday.com-10] but ten years later, in March 2021, only 11 percent of Japanese said they wanted that nuclear energy generation to be discontinued immediately. Another 49 percent were asking for a gradual exit from nuclear energy.[^cite_note-11] In February 2023, a survey by [Asahi Shimbun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asahi_Shimbun) showed that 51% of participants in Japan favored the restart of nuclear plant operations, with 42% opposed.[^cite_note-\:0-12]

## History

### Early years



Three experimental reactors installed in [Tōkai, Ibaraki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%8Dkai,_Ibaraki), played key roles in the early development of nuclear power in Japan: from left to right, JRR-1 (1957), JRR-2 (1960), and JRR-3 (1962).

Japan's nuclear development began in the 1930s with the [Japanese nuclear weapons program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_nuclear_weapons_program). After the war, the United States imposed a total ban on nuclear research during the [post-war occupation of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Japan).

Following the December 1953 *[Atoms for Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atoms_for_Peace)* speech at the UN General Assembly by U.S. President [Eisenhower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eisenhower), future Prime Minister [Yasuhiro Nakasone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasuhiro_Nakasone) and his peers from the [Kaishintō Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaishint%C5%8D) (a forerunner to the [Liberal Democratic Party](164231.md)) drafted a 235-million-yen budget (the amount symbolically referencing uranium-235). Members of the [National Diet](163847.md) took part in the 1955 Geneva Conference on the Peaceful Uses of Atomic Energy and drafted legislation governing Japan's nuclear program.[^cite_note-13]

Around the same time, the [Atomic Energy Act of 1954](4280305.md) was enacted in America, and Japan received condensed uranium from the country in 1955. Two experimental reactors, JRR-1 (built by [North American Aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Aviation)'s nuclear division) and JRR-2 (largely based on [Chicago Pile-5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Pile-5)), were constructed. A much more powerful experimental reactor JRR-3 was completed in 1962 by a consortium led by [Hitachi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitachi), the first experimental reactor to be fully designed and built in Japan.[^cite_note-14] Also in 1955, Nakasone's group drafted the [Atomic Energy Basic Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_Energy_Basic_Law), which was enacted in Japan to promote nuclear power while limiting activities strictly to peaceful purposes.[^cite_note-15] The [Japanese Atomic Energy Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Atomic_Energy_Commission) was established as a regulatory body, with its first chair, [Matsutarō Shōriki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matsutar%C5%8D_Sh%C5%8Driki), a newspaper magnate, also being a key promoter of nuclear power in Japan.

&#x20;A prototype nuclear power station, JPDR, began power generation on 26 October 1963.[^cite_note-16] Japan's first commercial nuclear power plant, the [Tōkai Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%8Dkai_Nuclear_Power_Plant), was built using main components imported from the [General Electric Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric_Company) in the UK and was connected to the grid on 25 July 1966. This reactor remains the only [Magnox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnox) reactor ever built on Japanese soil.[^cite_note-17] In 1969, the first [nuclear-powered ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear-powered_ship) in Japan (and the fourth in the world), *[Mutsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RV_Mirai#Mutsu_\(1972%E2%80%931996\))*, was launched by [Mitsubishi Heavy Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Heavy_Industries) (reactor) and [IHI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IHI_Corporation) (hull).[^cite_note-18]

#### Light water reactors



The [Onagawa Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onagawa_Nuclear_Power_Plant), a 3-unit BWR site typical of Japan's nuclear plants.

[General Electric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric) offered [light water reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_water_reactors) at a discounted price as soon as Yasuhiro Nakasone, then the Minister of Science and Technology, began revising the country's nuclear power strategy in 1961. This offer was accepted, and [Tsuruga-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsuruga_Nuclear_Power_Plant) was commissioned in March 1970 as Japan's first [BWR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling_water_reactor).[^cite_note-19] GE teamed up with Hitachi and [Toshiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toshiba) to build BWRs, while [Westinghouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westinghouse_Electric_Corporation) collaborated with Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (MHI) to promote [PWRs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressurized_water_reactor). By the late 1970s, these companies began constructing plants independently. All nuclear reactors built in Japan have been either BWRs or PWRs, with Hitachi and Toshiba (which later acquired [Westinghouse's nuclear division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westinghouse_Electric_Company) to build PWRs as well) still responsible for the former and MHI for the latter.[^cite_note-20]

Developments in nuclear power since that time have seen contributions from Japanese companies and research institutes on the same level as the other major users of nuclear power. From the early 1970s to the present, the Japanese government promoted the siting of nuclear power plants through a variety of policy instruments involving soft social control and financial incentives.[^cite_note-21]

### Later years

Japan's nuclear industry was not hit as hard by the effects of the [Three Mile Island accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Mile_Island_accident) (TMI) or the [Chernobyl disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernobyl_disaster) as some other countries. Construction of new plants remained strong throughout the 1980s and 1990s. While many new plants had been proposed, all were subsequently canceled or never brought past initial planning. Cancelled plant orders include:

- The Hōhoku Nuclear Power Plant at [Hōhoku, Yamaguchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C5%8Dhoku,_Yamaguchi) – 1994

- The Kushima Nuclear Power Plant at [Kushima, Miyazaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kushima,_Miyazaki) – 1997

- The Ashihama Nuclear Power Plant at Ashihama, Mie – 2000 (the first Project at the site in the 1970s was completed at Hamaoka as Unit 1&2)

- The [Maki Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maki_Nuclear_Power_Plant) at [Maki, Niigata (Kambara)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maki,_Niigata_\(Kambara\)) – Canceled in 2003

- The Suzu Nuclear Power Plant at [Suzu, Ishikawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzu,_Ishikawa) – 2003

Starting in the mid-1990s, there were several nuclear-related accidents and cover-ups in Japan that eroded public perception of the industry, resulting in protests and resistance to new plants. These accidents included the [Tokaimura nuclear accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokaimura_nuclear_accident), the [Mihama steam explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihama_Nuclear_Power_Plant), cover-ups after an accident at the [Monju reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monju_reactor), among others, more recently the [Chūetsu offshore earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_Ch%C5%ABetsu_offshore_earthquake) aftermath. While exact details may be in dispute, it is clear that the safety culture in Japan's nuclear industry has come under greater scrutiny.[^cite_note-Japan_cancels_nuclear_plant-22]

### 2000s



Up to 2011, Japan had steadily increased its nuclear generation over time. A dip in 2003 was due to TEPCO data falsification scandals in 2002 and caused a poor operating record.

On 18 April 2007, Japan and the United States signed the [United States-Japan Joint Nuclear Energy Action Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States-Japan_Joint_Nuclear_Energy_Action_Plan), aimed at putting in place a framework for the joint research and development of nuclear energy technology.[^cite_note-23]

Each country will conduct research into [fast reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_reactor) technology, fuel cycle technology, advanced computer simulation and modeling, small and medium reactors, safeguards and physical protection; and [nuclear waste](37257.md) management.[^cite_note-24] In March 2008, [Tokyo Electric Power Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Electric_Power_Company) announced that the start of operation of four new nuclear power reactors would be postponed by one year due to the incorporation of new earthquake resistance assessments. Units 7 and 8 of the Fukushima Daiichi plant would now enter commercial operation in October 2014 and October 2015, respectively. Unit 1 of the Higashidori plant is now scheduled to begin operating in December 2015, while unit 2 will start up in 2018 at the earliest.[^cite_note-25] As of September 2008, Japanese ministries and agencies were seeking an increase in the 2009 budget by 6%. The total requested comes to 491.4 billion Japanese yen (US\$4.6 billion), and the focuses of research are the development of the fast breeder reactor cycle, next-generation light water reactors, the [Iter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iter) project, and seismic safety.[^cite_note-26]

### Fukushima accident and aftermath



The use of nuclear power (in dark blue) in Japan declined significantly after the Fukushima accident.

Main article: [Fukushima nuclear accident](31162817.md)

An energy white paper, approved by the Japanese Cabinet in October 2011, says "public confidence in the safety of nuclear power was greatly damaged" by the Fukushima accident in March 2011, and calls for a reduction in the nation's reliance on nuclear power. It also omits a section on nuclear power expansion that was in the previous year's policy review.[^cite_note-Tsuyoshi_Inajima_and_Yuji_Okada-27] Nuclear Safety Commission Chairman Haruki Madarame told a parliamentary inquiry in February 2012 that "Japan's atomic safety rules are inferior to global standards and left the country unprepared for the Fukushima nuclear disaster last March". There were flaws in, and lax enforcement of, the safety rules governing Japanese nuclear power companies, and this included insufficient protection against tsunamis.[^cite_note-28]

On 6 May 2011, Prime Minister [Naoto Kan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naoto_Kan) ordered the [Hamaoka Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamaoka_Nuclear_Power_Plant) to be shut down as an earthquake of magnitude 8.0 or higher is likely to hit the area within the next thirty years.[^cite_note-Story-29][^cite_note-digitaljournal.com-30][^cite_note-bloomberg.com-31][^cite_note-Japan_nuke_plant_suspends_work-32][^cite_note-M._V._Ramana_44-33]

As of 27 March 2012, Japan had only one out of 54 nuclear reactors operating; the [Tomari-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomari_Nuclear_Power_Plant#Reactors_on_site), after the [Kashiwazaki-Kariwa 6](11996462.md#Reactors) was shut down.[^cite_note-ntse-34] The Tomari-3 was shut down for maintenance on 5 May, leaving Japan with no nuclear-derived electricity for the first time since 1970, when the country's then-only two reactors were taken offline for five days for maintenance.[^cite_note-Guardian-35] On 15 June 2012, approval was given to restart [Ōi Units 3 and 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Ci_Nuclear_Power_Plant)[^cite_note-36] which could take six weeks to bring them to full operation. On 1 July 2012, unit 3 of the [Ōi Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Ci_Nuclear_Power_Plant) was restarted. This reactor can provide 1,180 [MW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megawatts) of electricity.[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38] On 21 July 2012 unit 4 was restarted, also 1,180 MW.[^cite_note-39] The reactor was shut down again on 14 September 2013, again leaving Japan with no operating power reactors.[^cite_note-40]

Government figures in the 2014 Annual Report on Energy show that Japan depended on imported fossil fuels for 88% of its electricity in fiscal year 2013, compared with 62% in fiscal 2010. Without significant nuclear power, the country was self-sufficient for just 6% of its energy demand in 2012, compared with 20% in 2010. The additional fuel costs to compensate for its nuclear reactors being idled was ¥3.6 trillion. In parallel, domestic energy users have seen a 19.4% increase in their energy bills between 2010 and 2013, while industrial users have seen their costs rise 28.4% over the same period.[^cite_note-41]

In 2018 the Japanese government revised its energy plan to update the 2030 target for nuclear energy to 20%-22% of power generation by restarting reactors, compared to LNG 27%, coal 25%, renewables 23% and oil 3%. This would reduce Japan's carbon dioxide emissions by 26% compared to 2013, and increase self-sufficiency to about 24% by 2030, compared to 8% in 2016.[^cite_note-wnn-20180703-42]

Since the Fukushima nuclear accident, Japan has restarted twelve reactors and fifteen more have applied to restart, including two that are under construction. Amid the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine), Japan's Prime Minister announced the restart of nine units by winter 2022 and seven more by summer 2023.[^cite_note-\:0-12]

#### Investigations on the Fukushima accident

The [National Diet of Japan Fukushima Nuclear Accident Independent Investigation Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Diet_of_Japan_Fukushima_Nuclear_Accident_Independent_Investigation_Commission) (NAIIC) is the first independent investigation commission by the [National Diet](163847.md) in the 66-year history of Japan's constitutional government. NAICC was established on 8 December 2011 with the mission to investigate the direct and indirect causes of the Fukushima nuclear accident. NAICC submitted its inquiry report to both houses on 5 July 2012.[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44]

The 10-member commission compiled its report based on more than 1,167 interviews and 900 hours of hearings.[^cite_note-WP20120705W-45][^cite_note-NYT20120705-46] It was a six-month independent investigation, the first of its kind with wide-ranging subpoena powers in Japan's constitutional history, which held public hearings with former Prime Minister [Naoto Kan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naoto_Kan) and [Tokyo Electric Power Co](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Electric_Power_Company)'s former president [Masataka Shimizu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masataka_Shimizu), who gave conflicting accounts of the disaster response.[^cite_note-Bloomberg20120705-47] The commission chairman, Kiyoshi Kurokawa, declared with respect to the Fukushima nuclear incident: "It was a profoundly man-made disaster – that could and should have been foreseen and prevented."[^cite_note-48] He added that the "fundamental causes" of the disaster were rooted in "the ingrained conventions of Japanese culture."[^cite_note-49] The report outlines errors and willful negligence at the plant before the [2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_T%C5%8Dhoku_earthquake_and_tsunami) on 11 March 2011 and a flawed response in the hours, days, and weeks that followed. It also offers recommendations and encourages Japan's parliament to "thoroughly debate and deliberate" the suggestions.[^cite_note-CNN20120705-50]

### Post-Fukushima nuclear policy

**Japan's nuclear power reactors Timeline[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55]** \[ [view/edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Template\:Graphical_timeline_of_Japan's_power_nuclear_reactors) ]



Japan's new energy plan, approved by the Liberal Democratic Party cabinet in April 2014, calls nuclear power "the country's most important power source".[^cite_note-56] Reversing a decision by the previous Democratic Party, the government will re-open nuclear plants, aiming for "a realistic and balanced energy structure". In May 2014 the Fukui District Court blocked the restart of the Oi reactors.[^cite_note-57] In April 2015 courts blocked the restarting of two reactors at the [Takahama Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takahama_Nuclear_Power_Plant) but permitted the restart of two reactors at the [Sendai Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sendai_Nuclear_Power_Plant).[^cite_note-economist-20150425-58] The government hopes that nuclear power will produce 20% of Japan's electricity by 2030.[^cite_note-economist-20150425-58]

As of June 2015, approval was being sought from the new Nuclear Regulatory Agency for 24 units to restart, of the 54 pre-Fukushima units. The units also have to be approved by the local prefecture authorities before restarting.[^cite_note-nei-20150624-59]

In July 2015 fuel loading was completed at the [Sendai-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sendai_Nuclear_Power_Plant) nuclear plant, it restarted 11 August 2015 and was followed by unit 2 on 1 November 2015. Japan's Nuclear Regulatory Authority approved the restart of [Ikata-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikata_Nuclear_Power_Plant) which took place on 19 April 2016, this reactor is the fifth to receive approval to restart.[^cite_note-60] The [Takahama Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takahama_Nuclear_Power_Plant) unit 4 restarted in May 2017 and unit 3 in June 2017. And by 2023, Unit 1 and 2 of Takahama also restarted.[^cite_note-61]

In November 2016 Japan signed a nuclear cooperation agreement [with India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_India). Japanese nuclear plant builders saw this as potential lifeline given that domestic orders had ended following the Fukushima disaster, and India is proposing to build about 20 new reactors over the next decade. However, there is Japanese domestic opposition to the agreement, as India has not agreed to the [Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons](22107.md).[^cite_note-jt-20161111-62]

In 2014, following the failure of the prototype [Monju](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monju) [sodium-cooled fast reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium-cooled_fast_reactor), Japan agreed to cooperate in developing the French [ASTRID](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASTRID_\(reactor\)) demonstration sodium-cooled fast breeder reactor.[^cite_note-bl-20140505-63][^cite_note-mainichi-20161022-64] As of 2016, France was seeking the full involvement of Japan in the ASTRID development.[^cite_note-mainichi-20161022-64][^cite_note-asahi-20160922-65]

In 2015, the [Agency for Natural Resources and Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agency_for_Natural_Resources_and_Energy) changed the accounting provisions of the Electricity Business Act, so companies can account for decommissioning costs in ten yearly installments rather than a one-time charge. This will encourage the decommissioning of older and smaller nuclear units, most of which have not restarted since 2011.[^cite_note-wnn-20190213-66]

In 2022, during the [global energy crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021%E2%80%93present_global_energy_crisis) which greatly increased the cost of imported fossil fuels, Japan's prime minister announced the building of safer next-generation nuclear reactors and restarting idle existing plants would be considered. In 2022 ten reactors were operational producing about 5% of Japan's electricity.[^cite_note-guardian-20220825-67]

In December 2022, Japan's [Nuclear Regulation Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_Regulation_Authority) (NRA) approved a draft-rule allowing nuclear reactors to operate beyond 60 years by excluding inspection downtimes. This was part of a policy at enhancing nuclear reactor use, including restarting many, extending older units' lives, and developing new reactor technologies. In February 2023, the cabinet approved this policy and the construction of new reactors. By May 2023, a law was enacted to officially omit shutdown periods from the 60-year limit, subject to the economy minister's approval. The law also required the NRA to perform inspections every 10 years for reactors over 30 years of operation.[^cite_note-\:0-12]

## Seismicity

Japan has had a long [history of earthquakes and seismic activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismicity_in_Japan), and destructive [earthquakes](10106.md), often resulting in [tsunamis](31161.md), occur several times a century. Due to this, concern has been expressed about the particular risks of constructing and operating nuclear power plants in Japan. [Amory Lovins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amory_Lovins) has said: "An earthquake-and-tsunami zone crowded with 127 million people is an unwise place for 54 reactors".[^cite_note-68] To date, the most serious seismic-related accident has been the [Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_Daiichi_nuclear_disaster), following the [2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_T%C5%8Dhoku_earthquake_and_tsunami).

Professor [Katsuhiko Ishibashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katsuhiko_Ishibashi), one of the [seismologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismologists) who have taken an active interest in the topic, coined the term *[genpatsu-shinsai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genpatsu-shinsai)* (原発震災), from the Japanese words for "nuclear power" and "quake disaster" to express the potential worst-case catastrophe that could ensue.[^cite_note-why-worry-69][^cite_note-70] Dr [Kiyoo Mogi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiyoo_Mogi), former chair of the Japanese [Coordinating Committee for Earthquake Prediction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinating_Committee_for_Earthquake_Prediction),[^cite_note-nature-07-07-25-71] has expressed similar concerns, stating in 2004 that the issue 'is a critical problem which can bring a catastrophe to Japan through a man-made disaster'.[^cite_note-mogi-2004-72][^cite_note-73]

Warnings from Kunihiko Shimazaki, a professor of seismology at the University of Tokyo, were also ignored. In 2004, as a member of an influential cabinet office committee on offshore earthquakes, Mr. Shimazaki "warned that Fukushima's coast was vulnerable to tsunamis more than twice as tall as the forecasts of as much as five meters put forth by regulators and Tokyo Electric".[^cite_note-jap12-74] Minutes of the meeting on 19 February 2004, show that the government bureaucrats running the committee moved quickly to exclude his views from the committee's final report. He said the committee did not want to force Tokyo Electric to make expensive upgrades at the plant.[^cite_note-jap12-74]

[Hidekatsu Yoshii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidekatsu_Yoshii), a member of the [House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Representatives_of_Japan) for the [Japanese Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Communist_Party) and an [anti-nuclear campaigner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-nuclear_movement), warned in March and October 2006 about the possibility of the severe damage that might be caused by a tsunami or earthquake.[^cite_note-wsj-11-03-28-75] During a parliamentary committee in May 2010 he made similar claims, warning that the cooling systems of a Japanese nuclear plant could be destroyed by a landslide or earthquake.[^cite_note-wsj-11-03-28-75] In response, Yoshinobu Terasaka, head of the [Nuclear and Industrial Safety Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_and_Industrial_Safety_Agency), replied that the plants were so well designed that "such a situation is practically impossible".[^cite_note-wsj-11-03-28-75] Following damage at the [Kashiwazaki-Kariwa Nuclear Power Plant](11996462.md) due to the [2007 Chūetsu offshore earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_Ch%C5%ABetsu_offshore_earthquake), Kiyoo Mogi called for the immediate closure of the [Hamaoka Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamaoka_Nuclear_Power_Plant),[^cite_note-nature-07-07-25-71][^cite_note-times-07-07-19-76] which was knowingly built close to the centre of the expected [Tōkai earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%8Dkai_earthquake).[^cite_note-mogi-2004-72] Katsuhiko Ishibashi previously claimed, in 2004, that Hamaoka was "considered to be the most dangerous nuclear power plant in Japan".[^cite_note-japantimes-04-05-23-77]

The [International Atomic Energy Agency](14984.md) (IAEA) has also expressed concern. At a meeting of the [G8](59755.md)'s Nuclear Safety and Security Group, held in Tokyo in 2008, an IAEA expert warned that a strong [earthquake](10106.md) with a [magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richter_magnitude_scale#Richter_magnitudes) above 7.0 could pose a 'serious problem' for Japan's nuclear power stations.[^cite_note-wl-78] Before Fukushima, "14 lawsuits charging that risks had been ignored or hidden were filed in Japan, revealing a disturbing pattern in which operators underestimated or hid seismic dangers to avoid costly upgrades and keep operating. But all the lawsuits were unsuccessful".[^cite_note-79] Underscoring the risks facing Japan, a 2012 research institute investigation has "determined there is a 70% chance of a magnitude-7 earthquake striking the Tokyo metropolitan area within the next four years, and 98% over 30 years". The March 2011 earthquake was a magnitude 9.[^cite_note-80]

### Design standards



Horizontal acceleration experienced and design values during the 2007 and 2011 major earthquake and earthquake-tsunami events.

Between 2005 and 2007, three Japanese nuclear power plants were shaken by earthquakes that far exceeded the maximum [peak ground acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_ground_acceleration) used in their design.[^cite_note-autralian-11-03-13-81] The [tsunami](31161.md) that followed the [2011 Tōhoku earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_T%C5%8Dhoku_earthquake_and_tsunami), inundating the [Fukushima I Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_I_nuclear_accidents), was more than twice the design height,[^cite_note-82] while the ground acceleration also slightly exceeded the design parameters.[^cite_note-83]

In 2006 a Japanese government subcommittee was charged with revising the national guidelines on the earthquake-resistance of nuclear power plants, which had last been partially revised in 2001,[^cite_note-2006-seismic-guide-84] resulting in the publication of a new seismic guide – the 2006 *Regulatory Guide for Reviewing Seismic Design of Nuclear Power Reactor Facilities*.[^cite_note-2006-seismic-guide-84] The subcommittee membership included Professor Ishibashi, however his proposal that the standards for surveying active [faults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_\(geology\)) should be reviewed was rejected and he resigned at the final meeting, claiming that the review process was 'unscientific'[^cite_note-nature-07-07-25-71][^cite_note-businessweek-11-03-11-85] and the outcome rigged[^cite_note-businessweek-11-03-11-85][^cite_note-bloomberg-07-12-12-86] to suit the interests of the [Japan Electric Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Electric_Association), which had 11 of its committee members on the 19-member government subcommittee.[^cite_note-bloomberg-07-12-12-86] Ishibashi has subsequently claimed that, although the new guide brought in the most far-reaching changes since 1978, it was 'seriously flawed' because it underestimated the [design basis of earthquake ground motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_ground_acceleration).[^cite_note-why-worry-69] He has also claimed that the enforcement system is 'a shambles'[^cite_note-why-worry-69][^cite_note-autralian-11-03-13-81] and questioned the independence of the [Nuclear Safety Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Nuclear_Safety_Commission) after a senior Nuclear and Industrial Safety Agency official appeared to rule out a new review of the NSC's seismic design guide in 2007.[^cite_note-why-worry-69]

Following the publication of the new 2006 Seismic Guide, the [Nuclear and Industrial Safety Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_and_Industrial_Safety_Agency), at the request of the Nuclear Safety Commission, required the design of all existing nuclear power plants to be re-evaluated.[^cite_note-87]

### Geological surveys

The standard of geological survey work in Japan is another area causing concern. In 2008 Taku Komatsubara, a [geologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologist) at the [National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Advanced_Industrial_Science_and_Technology) alleged that the presence of active [faults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_\(geology\)) was deliberately ignored when surveys of potential new power plant sites were undertaken, a view supported by a former [topographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topography).[^cite_note-88] Takashi Nakata, a seismologist from the [Hiroshima Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiroshima_Institute_of_Technology) has made similar allegations and suggests that conflicts of interest between the Japanese nuclear industry and the regulators contribute to the problem.[^cite_note-bloomberg-07-12-12-86]

A 2011 [Natural Resources Defense Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_Resources_Defense_Council) report that evaluated the seismic hazard to reactors worldwide, as determined by the Global Seismic Hazard Assessment Program data, placed 35 of Japan's reactors in the group of 48 reactors worldwide in very high and high seismic hazard areas.[^cite_note-89]

## Nuclear accidents

See also: [List of Japanese nuclear incidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Japanese_nuclear_incidents)

In terms of consequences of radioactivity releases and core damage, the [Fukushima I nuclear accidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_I_nuclear_accidents) in 2011 were the worst experienced by the Japanese nuclear industry, in addition to ranking among the worst [civilian nuclear accidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_civilian_nuclear_accidents), though no fatalities were caused and no serious exposure of radiation to workers occurred. The [Tokaimura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokaimura) reprocessing plant fire in 1999 had 2 worker deaths, one more was exposed to radiation levels above legal limits, and over 660 others received detectable radiation doses but within permissible levels, well below the threshold to affect human health. The [Mihama Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihama_Nuclear_Power_Plant) experienced a steam explosion in one of the turbine buildings in 2004 where five workers were killed and six injured.[^cite_note-KEPCO-20070207-105]

### Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster

According to the Federation of Electric Power Companies of Japan, "by April 27 approximately 55 percent of the fuel in reactor unit 1 had melted, along with 35 percent of the fuel in unit 2, and 30 percent of the fuel in unit 3; and overheated spent fuels in the storage pools of units 3 and 4 probably were also damaged".[^cite_note-bas2011-106] The accident exceeds the 1979 [Three Mile Island accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Mile_Island_accident) in seriousness, and is comparable to the 1986 [Chernobyl disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernobyl_disaster).[^cite_note-bas2011-106] *The Economist* reports that the Fukushima disaster is "a bit like three Three Mile Islands in a row, with added damage in the spent-fuel stores",[^cite_note-econ2011-107] and that there will be ongoing impacts:

> Years of clean-up will drag into decades. A permanent exclusion zone could end up stretching beyond the plant’s perimeter. Seriously exposed workers may be at increased risk of cancers for the rest of their lives...[^cite_note-econ2011-107]

On 24 March 2011, Japanese officials announced that "radioactive iodine-131 exceeding safety limits for infants had been detected at 18 water-purification plants in Tokyo and five other prefectures". Officials said also that the fallout from the Dai-ichi plant is "hindering search efforts for victims from the March 11 earthquake and tsunami".[^cite_note-108]

Problems in stabilizing the [Fukushima Daiichi nuclear power plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_Daiichi_nuclear_power_plant) have hardened attitudes to [nuclear power](22153.md). As of June 2011, "more than 80 percent of Japanese now say they are [anti-nuclear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-nuclear) and distrust government information on [radiation](25856.md)".[^cite_note-refuse2011-8] The ongoing Fukushima crisis may spell the end of nuclear power in Japan, as "citizen opposition grows and local authorities refuse permission to restart reactors that have undergone safety checks". Local authorities are skeptical that sufficient safety measures have been taken and are reticent to give their permission – now required by law – to bring suspended nuclear reactors back online.[^cite_note-refuse2011-8][^cite_note-109]

Two government advisers have said that "Japan's safety review of nuclear reactors after the Fukushima disaster is based on faulty criteria and many people involved have conflicts of interest". Hiromitsu Ino, Professor Emeritus at the University of Tokyo, says "The whole process being undertaken is exactly the same as that used previous to the Fukushima Dai-Ichi accident, even though the accident showed all these guidelines and categories to be insufficient".[^cite_note-110]

In 2012, former prime minister [Naoto Kan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naoto_Kan) was interviewed about the Fukushima nuclear disaster, and has said that at one point Japan faced a situation where there was a chance that people might not be able to live in the capital zone including Tokyo and would have to evacuate. He says he is haunted by the specter of an even bigger nuclear crisis forcing tens of millions of people to flee Tokyo and threatening the nation's existence. "If things had reached that level, not only would the public have had to face hardships but Japan's very existence would have been in peril".[^cite_note-k12-111] That convinced Kan to "declare the need for Japan to end its reliance on atomic power and promote renewable sources of energy such solar that have long taken a back seat in the resource-poor country's energy mix".[^cite_note-k12-111]

## Nuclear waste disposal

Japanese policy is to [reprocess its spent nuclear fuel](197845.md). Originally spent fuel was reprocessed under contract in England and France, but then the [Rokkasho Reprocessing Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rokkasho_Reprocessing_Plant) was built, with operations originally expected to commence in 2007.[^cite_note-114] The policy to use recovered plutonium as mixed oxide ([MOX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOX)) reactor fuel was questioned on economic grounds, and in 2004 it was revealed the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry had covered up a 1994 report indicating reprocessing spent fuel would cost four times as much as burying it.[^cite_note-115]

In 2000, a Specified Radioactive Waste Final Disposal Act called for creation of a new organization to [manage high level radioactive waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-level_radioactive_waste_management), and later that year the Nuclear Waste Management Organization of Japan (NUMO) was established under the jurisdiction of the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry. NUMO is responsible for selecting a permanent [deep geological repository](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_geological_repository) site, construction, operation and closure of the facility for waste emplacement by 2040.[^cite_note-116][^cite_note-117] Site selection began in 2002 and application information was sent to 3,239 municipalities, but by 2006, no local government had volunteered to host the facility.[^cite_note-118] [Kōchi Prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%8Dchi_Prefecture) showed interest in 2007, but its mayor resigned due to local opposition. In December 2013 the government decided to identify suitable candidate areas before approaching municipalities.[^cite_note-jt-20140121-119]

In 2014 the head of the [Science Council of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_Council_of_Japan)’s expert panel has said Japan's seismic conditions makes it difficult to predict ground conditions over the necessary 100,000 years, so it will be impossible to convince the public of the safety of deep geological disposal.[^cite_note-jt-20140121-119]

The cost of MOX fuel had roughly quadrupled from 1999 to 2017, creating doubts about the economics of nuclear fuel reprocessing.[^cite_note-jt-20171219-120] In 2018 the [Japanese Atomic Energy Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Atomic_Energy_Commission) updated plutonium guidelines to try to reduce plutonium stockpiles, stipulating that the [Rokkasho Reprocessing Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rokkasho_Reprocessing_Plant) should only produce the amount of plutonium required for MOX fuel for Japan's nuclear power plants.[^cite_note-nei-20180802-121]

## Nuclear research and professional organizations in Japan

### Research organizations

These organizations are government-funded research organizations, though many of them have special status to give them power of administration separate from the Japanese government. Their origins date back to the [Atomic Energy Basic Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_Energy_Basic_Law), but they have been reorganized several times since their inception.

- [Japan Atomic Energy Research Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Atomic_Energy_Research_Institute) (JAERI) - 日本原子力研究所 The original nuclear energy research organization established by the Japanese government under cooperation with U.S. partners.

- Atomic Fuel Corporation - 原子燃料公社 This organization was formed along with JAERI under the Atomic Energy Basic Law and was later reorganized to be PNC.

- [Power Reactor and Nuclear Fuel Development Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_Reactor_and_Nuclear_Fuel_Development_Corporation) (PNC) - Succeeded the AFC in 1967 in order to perform more direct construction of experimental nuclear plants, and was renamed JNC in 1998.

- [Japan Nuclear Cycle Development Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Nuclear_Cycle_Development_Institute) (JNC) - 核燃料サイクル開発機構 (semi-governmental agency) Was formed in 1998 as the direct successor to the PNC. This organization operated Lojo and [Monju](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monju) experimental and demonstration reactors.

- [Japan Atomic Energy Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Atomic_Energy_Agency) (JAEA) - 日本原子力研究開発機構 This is the modern, currently operating primary nuclear research organization in Japan. It was formed by a merger of JAERI and JNC in 2005.

### Academic/professional organizations

- [Japan Atomic Industrial Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Atomic_Industrial_Forum) (JAIF) 日本原子力産業協会 is a non-profit organization, established in 1956 to promote the peaceful use of atomic energy.

  [^cite_note-126]

- The Atomic Energy Society of Japan (AESJ) 日本原子力学会 is a major academic organization in Japan focusing on all forms of nuclear power. The *Journal of Nuclear Science and Technology* is the academic journal run by the AESJ. It publishes English and Japanese articles, though most submissions are from Japanese research institutes, universities, and companies.

  [^cite_note-127]

- Japan Nuclear Technology Institute (JANTI) 日本原子力技術協会 was established to by the nuclear power industry to support and lead that industry.

  [^cite_note-128]

- [Japan Electric Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Electric_Association) (JEA) 日本電気協会 develops and publishes codes and guides for the Japanese nuclear power industry

  [^cite_note-cns-2007-129]

  &#x20;and is active in promoting nuclear power.

  [^cite_note-jea-work-130]

## Anti-nuclear movement

Main article: [Anti-nuclear power movement in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-nuclear_power_movement_in_Japan)



Anti-Nuclear Power Plant Rally on 19 September 2011 at [Meiji Shrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Shrine) complex in Tokyo. Sixty thousand people marched chanting "Sayonara nuclear power" and waving banners, to call on Japan's government to abandon nuclear power, following the Fukushima disaster.

[^cite_note-usatoday.com-10]

[^cite_note-wompro-131]



Peaceful anti-nuclear protest in Tokyo, Japan, escorted by policemen, 16 April 2011.

Long one of the world's most committed promoters of civilian [nuclear power](22153.md), Japan's nuclear industry was not hit as hard by the effects of the 1979 [Three Mile Island accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Mile_Island_accident) (USA) or the 1986 [Chernobyl disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernobyl_disaster) (USSR) as some other countries. Construction of new plants continued to be strong through the 1980s and into the 1990s. However, starting in the mid-1990s there were several nuclear related accidents and cover-ups in Japan that eroded public perception of the industry, resulting in protests and resistance to new plants. These accidents included the [Tokaimura nuclear accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokaimura_nuclear_accident), the [Mihama steam explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihama_Nuclear_Power_Plant), cover-ups after accidents at the [Monju reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monju_reactor), and more recently the [Kashiwazaki-Kariwa Nuclear Power Plant](11996462.md) was completely shut down for 21 months following an earthquake in 2007. While exact details may be in dispute, it is clear that the safety culture in Japan's nuclear industry has come under greater scrutiny.[^cite_note-Japan_cancels_nuclear_plant-22]

The negative impact of the 2011 [Fukushima nuclear disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_nuclear_disaster) has changed attitudes in Japan. Political and energy experts describe "nothing short of a nationwide loss of faith, not only in Japan’s once-vaunted nuclear technology but also in the government, which many blame for allowing the accident to happen".[^cite_note-132] Sixty thousand people marched in central Tokyo on 19 September 2011, chanting "Sayonara nuclear power" and waving banners, to call on Japan's government to abandon nuclear power, following the Fukushima disaster.[^cite_note-usatoday.com-10][^cite_note-wompro-131] Bishop of Osaka, Michael Goro Matsuura, has called on the solidarity of Christians worldwide to support this anti-nuclear campaign.[^cite_note-Sean_McDonagh-133] In July 2012, 75,000 people gathered near in Tokyo for the capital's largest anti-nuclear event. Organizers and participants said such demonstrations signal a fundamental change in attitudes in a nation where relatively few have been willing to engage in political protests since the 1960s.[^cite_note-Mure_Dickie-134]

[Anti-nuclear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-nuclear) groups include the [Citizens' Nuclear Information Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizens'_Nuclear_Information_Center), [Stop Rokkasho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stop_Rokkasho), [Hidankyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibakusha), [Sayonara Nuclear Power Plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayonara_Nuclear_Power_Plants), Women from Fukushima Against Nukes, and the [Article 9 group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_9_of_the_Japanese_Constitution). People associated with the anti-nuclear movement include: [Jinzaburo Takagi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinzaburo_Takagi), [Haruki Murakami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haruki_Murakami), [Kenzaburō Ōe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenzabur%C5%8D_%C5%8Ce), [Nobuto Hosaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobuto_Hosaka), [Mizuho Fukushima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mizuho_Fukushima), [Ryuichi Sakamoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryuichi_Sakamoto) and [Tetsunari Iida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetsunari_Iida).
