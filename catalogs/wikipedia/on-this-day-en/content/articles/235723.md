# Shopping mall

This article is about large, usually enclosed, shopping centers often anchored by department stores. For an overview of all types of shopping centers, see [Shopping center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shopping_center). For pedestrian malls, see [Pedestrian zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedestrian_zone).



The [Mall of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mall_of_America) in [Bloomington, Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomington,_Minnesota), the largest mall in the United States



The [West Edmonton Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Edmonton_Mall) in [Edmonton, Alberta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonton,_Alberta), the largest mall in Canada



[My Mall Limassol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/My_Mall_Limassol) in [Cyprus](5593.md)



[SM Seaside City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SM_Seaside_City), the second largest mall in Cebu, Philippines

A **shopping mall** (or simply **mall**) is a large indoor [shopping center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shopping_center), usually [anchored](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchor_tenant) by [department stores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_store). The term *mall* originally meant [a pedestrian promenade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedestrian_zone) with shops along it, but in the late 1960s, it began to be used as a generic term for the large enclosed shopping centers that were becoming increasingly commonplace.[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-Rielly-2] In the [United Kingdom](31717.md) and other countries, shopping malls may be called *shopping centres*.

In recent decades, malls have declined considerably in the [United States](3434750.md) and [Canada](5042916.md), partly due to the [retail apocalypse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retail_apocalypse), particularly in subprime locations, and some have closed and become so-called [dead malls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_mall).[^cite_note-3] Successful examples of [de-malling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De-malling) have seen such changes as added entertainment and experiential features, added [big-box stores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big-box_store) as anchors, or converted to other specialized shopping center formats such as [power centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_center_\(retail\)), [lifestyle centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lifestyle_centers), [factory outlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_outlet) centers, and [festival marketplaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Festival_marketplace).[^cite_note-ICSC_Characteristics-4] In Canada, shopping centres have frequently been replaced with mixed-use high-rise communities.[^cite_note-5] In many [African](5334607.md),[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7] [Asian](689.md),[^cite_note-8] [European](9239.md)[^cite_note-9] and [Latin American countries](18524.md),[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12] shopping malls continue to grow and thrive.

## Terminology



The interior structure of [Mall of Tripla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mall_of_Tripla) in [Helsinki, Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsinki)



Hoog Catharijne in [Utrecht](50333.md), [Netherlands](21148.md)

In the United States, [Persian Gulf countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_States_of_the_Persian_Gulf), and India, the term *shopping mall* is usually applied to enclosed retail structures (and is generally abbreviated to simply *mall*), while *shopping center* usually refers to open-air retail complexes; both types of facilities usually have large [parking lots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parking_lot), face major traffic [arterials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arterial_road), and have few pedestrian connections to surrounding neighborhoods.[^cite_note-Urban_Geography:_A_Global_Perspective-13] Outside of North America, the terms *shopping precinct* and *shopping [arcade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcade_\(architecture\))* are also used.

In the UK, such complexes are considered *shopping locations*; however, *shopping centre* covers many more sizes and types of centers than the North American *mall*. Other countries follow UK usage. In [Canadian English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_English), and often in Australia and New Zealand, the term *mall* may be used informally but *shopping centre* or merely *centre* will feature in the name of the complex (such as [Toronto Eaton Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toronto_Eaton_Centre)). The term *mall* is less commonly a part of the name of the complex.

## Types

Further information: [Shopping center § Types](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shopping_center#Types)

The [International Council of Shopping Centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Council_of_Shopping_Centers), based in New York City, classifies two types of shopping centers as malls: regional malls and super regional malls. A regional mall, per the International Council of Shopping Centers, is a shopping mall with 400,000 sq ft (37,000 m<sup>2</sup>) to 800,000 sq ft (74,000 m<sup>2</sup>) [gross leasable area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_leasable_area) with at least two [anchor stores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchor_store). A super-regional mall, per the International Council of Shopping Centers, is a shopping mall with over 800,000 sq ft (74,000 m<sup>2</sup>) of gross leasable area, three or more anchors, mass merchant, more variety, fashion [apparel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparel), and serves as the dominant shopping venue for the region (25 miles or 40 km) in which it is located.[^cite_note-ISCS_definitions-2015-14] Not classified as traditional malls are smaller formats such as [strip malls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strip_mall) and [neighborhood shopping centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_shopping_center), and specialized formats such as [power centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_center_\(retail\)), [festival marketplaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Festival_marketplace), and [outlet centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outlet_center).[^cite_note-ICSC_Characteristics-4]

## History

### Forerunners to the shopping mall



The [Burlington Arcade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burlington_Arcade) in London, with shop fronts inside (pictured), opened in 1819



The [Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galleria_Vittorio_Emanuele_II) interior in Milan, which opened in 1877

Shopping centers in general may have their origins in public markets and, in the Middle East, covered [bazaars](556904.md).

In 1798, the first covered shopping passage was built in Paris, the [Passage du Caire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passage_du_Caire).[^cite_note-15] In London, the [Royal Opera Arcade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/His_Majesty's_Theatre,_London#William_Taylor) opened in 1816,[^cite_note-16] and the more famous [Burlington Arcade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burlington_Arcade) opened in 1819.[^cite_note-17] Western European cities in particular built many arcade-style shopping centers. [The Arcade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Arcade) in [Providence, Rhode Island](19356538.md), built in 1828, claims to be the first shopping arcade in the United States.[^cite_note-18] The [Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galleria_Vittorio_Emanuele_II) in Milan, which opened in 1877, was larger than its predecessors, and inspired the use of the term "galleria" for many other shopping arcades and malls.[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20]

In the mid-20th century, with the rise of the [suburb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suburb) and [automobile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automobile) culture in the United States, a new style of shopping center was created away from [downtowns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downtown).[^cite_note-21] Early shopping centers designed for the automobile include [Market Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_Square_\(Lake_Forest,_Illinois\)), in [Lake Forest, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Forest,_Illinois) (1916), and [Country Club Plaza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country_Club_Plaza), in [Kansas City, Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansas_City,_Missouri) (1924).[^cite_note-Moore-22]

The suburban shopping center concept evolved further in the United States after [World War II](32927.md), with larger open-air shopping centers anchored by major department stores, such as the 550,000-square-foot (51,000 m<sup>2</sup>) [Broadway-Crenshaw Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadway-Crenshaw_Center) in [Los Angeles](18110.md), built in 1947 and anchored by a five-story [Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Broadway) and a [May Company California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Company_California).[^cite_note-times-23]

### Downtown pedestrian malls and use of term *mall*

In the late 1950s and into the 1960s, the term "shopping mall" was first used, but in the original sense of the word "mall", meaning a pedestrian promenade in the U.S., or in U.K. usage, a "shopping precinct". Early downtown pedestrianized malls included the [Kalamazoo Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalamazoo_Mall) (the first, in 1959), "Shoppers' See-Way" in [Toledo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toledo,_Ohio), [Lincoln Road Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Road_Mall) in [Miami Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Beach,_Florida), and the [Santa Monica Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Street_Promenade) (1965).[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26]

Although [Bergen Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bergen_Town_Center) opened in 1957 using the name "mall", inspiring other suburban shopping centers to rebrand themselves as malls, these types of properties were still referred to as "shopping centers" until the late 1960s.[^cite_note-howard-27]

### Enclosed malls



The original interior of [Shopping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shopping_\(Lule%C3%A5\)), built in 1955 and one of the first enclosed malls in the world, in [Luleå](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lule%C3%A5), Sweden

The enclosed shopping center, which would eventually be known as the shopping mall, did not appear in mainstream until the mid-1950s. One of the earliest examples was the [Valley Fair Shopping Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valley_Fair_Shopping_Center) in [Appleton, Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appleton,_Wisconsin),[^cite_note-28] which opened on 10 March 1955. Valley Fair featured a number of modern features, including central heating and cooling, a large outdoor parking area, semi-detached anchor stores, and restaurants. Later that year, the world's first fully enclosed shopping mall was opened in [Luleå](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lule%C3%A5), in northern Sweden (architect: [Ralph Erskine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Erskine_\(architect\))) and was named [*Shopping*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shopping_\(Lule%C3%A5\)); the region now claims the highest shopping center density in Europe.[^cite_note-29]

The idea of a regionally sized, fully enclosed shopping complex was pioneered in 1956 by the Austrian-born architect and American immigrant [Victor Gruen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Gruen).[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-Hardwick-31][^cite_note-Newton-32] This new generation of regional-size shopping centers began with the Gruen-designed [Southdale Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southdale_Center), which opened in the [Twin Cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minneapolis-St._Paul) suburb of [Edina, Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edina,_Minnesota), United States, in October 1956.[^cite_note-Hardwick-31][^cite_note-Newton-32] For pioneering the soon-to-be enormously popular mall concept in this form, Gruen has been called the "most influential architect of the twentieth century" by Canadian writer [Malcolm Gladwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_Gladwell).[^cite_note-33]

The first retail complex to be promoted as a "mall" was [Paramus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramus,_New_Jersey), [New Jersey](21648.md)'s [Bergen Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Outlets_at_Bergen_Town_Center), which opened with an open-air format on 14 November 1957,[^cite_note-34] and was later enclosed in 1973. Aside from [Southdale Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southdale_Center), significant early enclosed shopping malls were [Harundale Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harundale_Mall) (1958) in [Glen Burnie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glen_Burnie,_Maryland), [Maryland](18858.md),[^cite_note-35] [Big Town Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Town_Mall) (1959) in [Mesquite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesquite,_Texas), [Texas](29810.md), [Chris-Town Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris-Town_Mall) (1961) in [Phoenix](49121.md), [Arizona](21883824.md), and [Randhurst Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randhurst_Center) (1962) in [Mount Prospect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Prospect,_Illinois), [Illinois](14849.md).

Other early malls moved retailing away from the dense, commercial downtowns into the largely residential suburbs. This formula (enclosed space with stores attached, away from downtown, and accessible only by automobile) became a popular way to build retail across the world. Gruen himself came to abhor this effect of his new design; he decried the creation of enormous "land wasting seas of parking" and the spread of suburban sprawl.[^cite_note-DEADMALL-36][^cite_note-37]

Even though malls mostly appeared in suburban areas in the U.S., some U.S. cities facilitated the construction of enclosed malls downtown as an effort to revive city centers and allow them to compete effectively with suburban malls. Examples included [Main Place Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_Place_Tower) in Buffalo (1969) and The Gallery (1977, now [Fashion District Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fashion_District_Philadelphia)) in Philadelphia. Other cities created open-air [pedestrian malls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedestrian_malls).

In the United States, developers such as [A. Alfred Taubman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._Alfred_Taubman) of [Taubman Centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taubman_Centers) extended the concept further in 1980, with [terrazzo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrazzo) tiles at the [Mall at Short Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mall_at_Short_Hills) in [New Jersey](21648.md), indoor fountains, and two levels, allowing a shopper to make a circuit of all the stores. Taubman believed carpeting increased friction, slowing down customers, so it was removed. Fading daylight through glass panels was supplemented by gradually increased electric lighting, making it seem like the afternoon was lasting longer, which encouraged shoppers to linger.[^cite_note-tws29decbbgf-38][^cite_note-tws29decbsfe-39]

### Decline of shopping malls in the United States

See also: [Retail apocalypse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retail_apocalypse) and [Dead mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_mall)



Belz Factory Outlet Mall, an abandoned shopping mall in [Allen, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen,_Texas), United States

In the United States, in the mid-1990s, malls were still being constructed at a rate of 140 a year.[^cite_note-Millar-40] But in 2001, a [PricewaterhouseCoopers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PricewaterhouseCoopers) study found that underperforming and vacant malls, known as "greyfield" and "dead mall" estates, were an emerging problem. In 2007, a year before the [Great Recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession), no new malls were built in America, for the first time in 50 years.[^cite_note-41] [City Creek Center Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_Creek_Center) in [Salt Lake City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_Lake_City), which opened in March 2012, was the first to be built since the recession.[^cite_note-Moore-22]

Malls began to lose consumers to open-air power centers and lifestyle centers during the 1990s, as consumers preferred to park right in front of and walk directly into big-box stores with lower prices and without the [overhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhead_\(business\)) of traditional malls (i.e., long enclosed corridors).[^cite_note-Rybczynski-42][^cite_note-Neuborne-43][^cite_note-Laird_Page_69-44][^cite_note-Donnellan_Page_63-45]

Another issue was that the growth-crazed American commercial real estate industry had simply built too many nice places to shop—far more than could be reasonably justified by the actual growth of the American population, retail sales, or any other economic indicator. The number of American shopping centers exploded from 4,500 in 1960 to 70,000 by 1986 to just under 108,000 by 2010.[^cite_note-Donnellan_Page_64-46]

Thus, the number of dead malls increased significantly in the early 21st century. The economic health of malls across the United States has been in decline, as revealed by high vacancy rates. From 2006 to 2010, the percentage of malls that are considered to be "dying" by real estate experts (have a vacancy rate of at least 40%), unhealthy (20–40%), or in trouble (10–20%) all increased greatly, and these high vacancy rates only partially decreased from 2010 to 2014. In 2014, nearly 3% of all malls in the United States were considered to be "dying" (40% or higher vacancy rates), and nearly one-fifth of all malls had vacancy rates considered "troubling" (10% or higher). Some real estate experts say the "fundamental problem" is a glut of malls in many parts of the country, creating a market that is "extremely over-retailed".[^cite_note-nytimes.com-47] By the time shopping mall operator [Unibail-Rodamco-Westfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unibail-Rodamco-Westfield) decided to exit the American market in 2022, the United States had an average of 24.5 square feet of retail space per capita (in contrast to 4.5 square feet per capita in Europe).[^cite_note-Pimentel-48]

In 2019, [The Shops & Restaurants at Hudson Yards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shops_&_Restaurants_at_Hudson_Yards) opened as an upscale mall in New York City with "a '[Fifth Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_Avenue)' mix of shops", such as [H\&M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H\&M), [Zara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zara_\(retailer\)), and [Sephora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sephora) below them. This is one of the first two malls built recently, along with [American Dream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Dream_Meadowlands) in which both opened in 2019 since [City Creek Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_Creek_Center).[^cite_note-49]

Online shopping has also emerged as a major competitor to shopping malls. In the [United States](3434750.md), online shopping has accounted for an increasing share of total retail sales.[^cite_note-\:10-50] In 2013, roughly 200 out of 1,300 malls across the United States were going out of business.[^cite_note-51] To combat this trend, developers have converted malls into other uses, including attractions such as parks, movie theaters, gyms, and even fishing lakes.[^cite_note-EvansWSJ-52] In the United States, the 600,000 square foot [Highland Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highland_Mall) will be a campus for [Austin Community College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austin_Community_College_District).[^cite_note-\:0-53] In [France](5843419.md), the So Ouest mall, outside of [Paris](22989.md), was designed to resemble elegant, [Louis XV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XV)-style apartments and includes 17,000 square metres (180,000 sq ft) of green space.[^cite_note-54] The Australian mall company [Westfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westfield_Group) launched an online mall (and later a mobile app) with 150 stores, 3,000 brands, and over 1 million products.[^cite_note-Review-55]

The [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md) also significantly impacted the retail industry. Government regulations temporarily closed malls, increased entrance controls, and imposed strict public sanitation requirements.[^cite_note-56]

### Vertical malls

High land prices in populous cities have led to the concept of the "vertical mall", in which space allocated to retail is configured over a number of stories accessible by [elevators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevator) and/or [escalators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escalator) (usually both) linking the different levels of the mall. The challenge of this type of mall is to overcome the natural tendency of shoppers to move horizontally and encourage shoppers to move upwards and downwards.[^cite_note-reach-57] The concept of a vertical mall was originally conceived in the late 1960s by the Mafco Company, former shopping center development division of [Marshall Field & Co.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Field_&_Co.) The [Water Tower Place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_Tower_Place) skyscraper in [Chicago](6886.md), Illinois was built in 1975 by Urban Retail Properties. It contains a hotel, luxury condominiums, and office space and sits atop a block-long base containing an eight-level atrium-style retail mall that fronts on the [Magnificent Mile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnificent_Mile).

Vertical malls are common in densely populated conurbations in East and Southeast Asia. Hong Kong in particular has numerous examples such as [Times Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Times_Square,_Hong_Kong), [Dragon Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_Centre), [Apm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apm_\(Hong_Kong\)), [Langham Place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langham_Place_\(Hong_Kong\)),[^cite_note-reach-57] [ISQUARE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISQUARE), [Hysan Place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hysan_Place) and [The One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_One_\(shopping_centre\)).

A vertical mall may also be built where the geography prevents building outward or there are other restrictions on construction, such as historic buildings or significant [archeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archeology). The [Darwin Shopping Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darwin_Shopping_Centre) and associated malls in [Shrewsbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrewsbury), UK, are built on the side of a steep hill, around the former town walls;[^cite_note-58] consequently the shopping center is split over seven floors vertically – two locations horizontally – connected by elevators, escalators and bridge walkways. Some establishments incorporate such designs into their layout, such as Shrewsbury's former [McDonald's](2480627.md), split into four stories with multiple [mezzanines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mezzanine_\(architecture\)) which featured medieval castle vaults – complete with [arrowslits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrowslit) – in the basement dining rooms.

## Components

### Food court

Main article: [Food court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_court)

A common feature of shopping malls is a food court: this typically consists of a number of [fast food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_food) vendors of various types, surrounding a shared seating area.

### Department stores

Main articles: [Department store](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_store) and [Anchor store](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchor_store)

When the shopping mall format was developed by [Victor Gruen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Gruen) in the mid-1950s, signing larger department stores was necessary for the financial stability of the projects, and to draw retail traffic that would result in visits to the smaller stores in the mall as well. These larger stores are termed anchor stores or draw tenants. In physical configuration, anchor stores are normally located as far from each other as possible to maximize the amount of traffic from one anchor to another.

### Europe

There are a reported 222 malls in Europe. In 2014, these malls had combined sales of US\$12.47 billion. This represented a 10% bump in revenues from the prior year.[^cite_note-\:1-59]

#### U.K. and Ireland

In the United Kingdom and Ireland, both open-air and enclosed centers are commonly referred to as *shopping centres*. *Mall* primarily refers to either a shopping mall – a place where a collection of [shops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retailing#Shops_and_Stores) all adjoin a pedestrian area – or an exclusively pedestrianized street that allows shoppers to walk without interference from vehicle traffic.

The majority of British enclosed shopping centres, the equivalent of a U.S. mall, are located in city centres, usually found in old and historic shopping districts and surrounded by subsidiary open air shopping streets. Large examples include [Westquay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westquay) in [Southampton](7920751.md); [Manchester Arndale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Arndale); [Bullring Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Bull_Ring); [Liverpool One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liverpool_One); [Trinity Leeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_Leeds); Buchanan Galleries in [Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow); [St James Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_James_Quarter) in [Edinburgh](9602.md); and [Eldon Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eldon_Square_Shopping_Centre) in [Newcastle upon Tyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcastle_upon_Tyne). In addition to the inner city shopping centres, large UK [conurbations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conurbation) will also have large out-of-town "regional malls" such as the [Metrocentre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MetroCentre_\(shopping_centre\)) in [Gateshead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gateshead); [Meadowhall Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meadowhall_Centre), [Sheffield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheffield) serving [South Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Yorkshire); the [Trafford Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trafford_Centre) in [Greater Manchester](79819.md); [White Rose Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Rose_Centre) in [Leeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeds); the [Merry Hill Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merry_Hill_Shopping_Centre) near [Dudley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dudley); and [Bluewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluewater_Shopping_Centre) in [Kent](16766.md). These centres were built in the 1980s and 1990s, but planning regulations prohibit the construction of any more. Out-of-town shopping developments in the UK are now focused on [retail parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retail_park), which consist of groups of warehouse style shops with individual entrances from outdoors. Planning policy prioritizes the development of existing town centres, although with patchy success.[^cite_note-60] [Westfield London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westfield_London) ([White City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_City,_London)) is the largest shopping centre in Europe.

#### Russia

In [Russia](25705.md), on the other hand, as of 2013 a large number of new malls had been built near major cities, notably the [MEGA malls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MEGA_Family_Shopping_Centre) such as Mega Belaya Dacha mall near [Moscow](19004.md). In large part they were financed by international investors and were popular with shoppers from the emerging middle class.[^cite_note-NYT010113-61]

## Management and legal issues

### Shopping property management firms

A shopping [property management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property_management) firm is a company that specializes in owning and managing shopping malls. Most shopping property management firms own at least 20 malls. Some firms use a similar naming scheme for most of their malls; for example, [Mills Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mills_Corporation) puts "Mills" in most of its mall names and [SM Prime Holdings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SM_Prime_Holdings) of the [Philippines](23440.md) puts "SM" in all of its malls, as well as anchor stores such as The SM Store, SM Appliance Center, SM Hypermarket, SM Cinema, and SM Supermarket. In the UK, [The Mall Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mall_Fund) changes the name of any center it buys to *"The Mall (location)"*, using its pink-M logo; when it sells a mall the center reverts to its own name and branding, such as [the Ashley Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ashley_Centre) in [Epsom](174203.md).[^cite_note-62] Similarly, following its rebranding from Capital Shopping Centres, [intu Properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intu_Properties) renamed many of its centres to *"intu (name/location)"* (such as [intu Lakeside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intu_Lakeside)); again, malls removed from the network revert to their own brand (see for instance [The Glades in Bromley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Glades_\(Bromley\))).

### Legal issues

One controversial aspect of malls has been their effective displacement of traditional [main streets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_street) or [high streets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_street). Some consumers prefer malls, with their parking garages, controlled environments, and private [security guards](19283898.md), over [central business districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_business_district) (CBD) or [downtowns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downtown), which frequently have limited parking, poor maintenance, outdoor weather, and limited [police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police) coverage.[^cite_note-ODonahue-63][^cite_note-Frieden-64]

In response, a few jurisdictions, notably [California](5407.md), have expanded the right of [freedom of speech](21401843.md) to ensure that speakers will be able to reach consumers who prefer to shop, eat, and socialize within the boundaries of privately owned malls.[^cite_note-Rybczynski-42][^cite_note-Judd-65] The Supreme Court decision *[Pruneyard Shopping Center v. Robins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pruneyard_Shopping_Center_v._Robins)* was issued on 9 June 1980 which affirmed the decision of the California Supreme Court in a case that arose out of a free speech dispute between the Pruneyard Shopping Center in Campbell, California, and several local high school students.

In season 2, episode 9 of the American sitcom, *[How I Met Your Mother](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_I_Met_Your_Mother)*, it is revealed that [Robin Scherbatsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Scherbatsky) has a secret past of being a teenage Canadian pop star and, under the name Robin Sparkles, has a hit single entitled "Let's Go To The Mall".[^cite_note-141]
