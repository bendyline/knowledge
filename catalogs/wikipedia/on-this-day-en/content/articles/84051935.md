***NAZA*** is a 2026 British [documentary film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Documentary_film) written and directed by [Yuval Abraham](76256808.md) and [Rachel Szor](79369018.md). Produced by *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* and JW Films, it investigates the integration of [artificial intelligence and automated surveillance in military targeting systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI-assisted_targeting_in_the_Gaza_Strip) used by [Israel Defense Forces](39237.md) (IDF) during the ongoing [Gaza war](74998519.md) and [genocide](75668427.md). Its title is derived from *Nezek Agavi* ([Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language): נזק אגבי), the Hebrew military term for [collateral damage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collateral_damage).

The film premiered in the main competition of the [83rd Venice International Film Festival](83023289.md) on 10 September 2026, where it won the [Special Jury Prize](46786001.md) and received critical acclaim. Despite having not been widely distributed by that point, it elicited political controversy in Israel due to its accounts of the country's actions in Gaza, and prompted threats against Abraham and Szor, who are Israeli citizens. The Israeli government and the IDF rejected the claims presented in the film.

## Synopsis

Between 2023 and 2025,[^cite_note-labiennale-1] [Yuval Abraham](76256808.md) and [Rachel Szor](79369018.md) conducted nighttime interviews with 24 Israeli soldiers and intelligence officers on rooftops in [Tel Aviv](31453.md). The interviewees are shown only as dark silhouettes, with their voices distorted to protect their identities. They describe the AI system and [mobile phone surveillance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellphone_surveillance) used to identify targets in Gaza, which, according to these interviews, led to the [mass killing of Palestinian civilians](75668427.md) there. Interspersed with the interviews are shots of Tel Aviv at night.[^cite_note-Variety-2][^cite_note-\:12-3]

One of the soldiers interviewed explains the acronym "NAZA".[^cite_note-4] Every potential target – for example, an individual [Hamas](13913.md) member – is assigned a NAZA value in the AI software. This value represents the expected number of civilian fatalities in the event of an air strike on that target. Targets with values below 20 did not require special authorisation, he says, and approvals were granted for values up to 500. The combined NAZA values of all potential Hamas fighters exceeded the total population of Gaza, as detailed by an interviewee.[^cite_note-\:12-3][^cite_note-n432-5]

When the Palestinians were to be driven out of the northern part of the coastal strip, maps featured colour-coded quadrants to distinguish areas still inhabited (purple) from those that had been destroyed (white). The aim, an interviewee explains, was to turn the purple squares into white ones, "like a video game". Instructions were given to destroy as much as possible before any doubts about the legitimacy of the military operations could arise.&#x20;

"Death zones" in Gaza were designated from Tel Aviv; the Palestinians learnt where these zones lay by being shot at whenever they entered them. One of the interviewees recounts how Israeli snipers fired on starving people at a food distribution point. He illustrates the incident using a stick-figure drawing.[^cite_note-Variety-2][^cite_note-\:12-3][^cite_note-\:3-6]

Most of those interviewed describe killing from a great distance as an everyday office job. The most reliable targets were not Hamas fighters, but their homes, where their families were also sleeping. The houses were identified through AI-assisted surveillance of all mobile phones in Gaza. The intelligence service had access to the cameras and microphones of the mobile phones, including those belonging to children. An interviewee calls it a "factory of targets".&#x20;

The phrase "Where's Daddy?" was coined to describe the practice of listening in on a child's hacked mobile to determine when their father would be at home, enabling the whole family to be killed in an air strike. One man says about a 12-year-old, "You hack her phone, and then you kill her." One recounts how, via a Palestinian father's mobile phone, he listened live as the father's daughter – shown in photos on his phone – burnt to death. "You feel like God," explains one of the interviewees, "because you hear everything they say."[^cite_note-Variety-2][^cite_note-\:12-3][^cite_note-\:3-6][^cite_note-7]

The mass killing of civilians was not only accepted but also regarded as a duty and went unchallenged, several of the men say, adhering to the widespread post-[October 7](74998317.md) belief of there being "no innocents in Gaza". One of those interviewed says that killing Palestinians and carrying out the mission "felt good". Psychological support had been available, but no one in his unit had needed it. Another says, "We kill these children deliberately."[^cite_note-Variety-2][^cite_note-\:12-3]

When asked whether he had been involved in a [genocide](12441.md), one of the interviewees answers "yes" without hesitation. A member of an elite unit explains, with reference to [the Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust), that he now understood how it is possible to destroy an entire building, with all the people inside it, and yet for life to simply carry on as normal, whilst "you go on and complete your degree in clinical psychology".[^cite_note-Variety-2][^cite_note-\:12-3]

## Production

Israeli journalists and filmmakers [Yuval Abraham](76256808.md) and [Rachel Szor](79369018.md) previously co-directed *[No Other Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Other_Land)* (2024), which focused on [Israeli settler violence](47246246.md) against Palestinian communities in the [West Bank](33209.md), and won the [Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature Film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Documentary_Feature_Film), before collaborating on *NAZA*.[^cite_note-8]

The film is a British production,[^cite_note-\:2-9] co-produced by *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* and British film producer [James Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Wilson_\(producer\))'s JW Films. It is executive produced by [Jonathan Glazer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Glazer),[^cite_note-10] in association with Placeholder Films and Unseen Hand.[^cite_note-\:1-11] Abraham explained at the press conference held to mark the film's premiere in Venice that, while the events in Gaza are not the same as the Holocaust, there is a common pattern – that of dehumanisation.[^cite_note-\:12-3][^cite_note-12] This, he said, is what the film aims to show.[^cite_note-\:12-3]

## Release



Szor and Abraham at the [83rd Venice International Film Festival](83023289.md)

The film had its world premiere in the main competition of the [83rd Venice International Film Festival](83023289.md) on 10 September 2026, where it competed for the [Golden Lion](2386128.md).[^cite_note-TOI_e923-13] It was the only documentary selected for the festival's main competition, and was only added on 24 August, following consideration of safety concerns about its screening.[^cite_note-\:0-14][^cite_note-15] The film received a 25-minute ovation,[^cite_note-TOI_y442-16][^cite_note-Deadline_r495-17] the longest in the history of the Venice Film Festival.[^cite_note-18] It was announced as a late addition to the 'Perlak' sidebar section of the [74th San Sebastián International Film Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/74th_San_Sebasti%C3%A1n_International_Film_Festival).[^cite_note-19] Its North American premiere took place at the Main Slate of the [2026 New York Film Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_New_York_Film_Festival) on 26 September.[^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21]

French distribution company [MK2 Films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marin_Karmitz) will handle world sales rights.[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23] It is scheduled to be released in the United States on 30 September 2026, with *The Guardian* and MK2 opting to self-distribute.[^cite_note-24] [Elastica Films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastica_Films) secured distribution rights in Spain, programming a 9 October 2026 theatrical release in the territory.[^cite_note-25] [Dogwoof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogwoof) acquired U.K. and Irish distribution rights to the documentary, scheduling a theatrical premiere on 15 October 2026 before a general release the following day.[^cite_note-26]

*The Guardian* has announced that, following its [theatrical release](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatrical_release), the film will be made available free of charge for [streaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming) on its website in late November 2026.[^cite_note-27]

## Reception

### Box office

Released in Italy on 28 September 2026 by [I Wonder Pictures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Wonder_Pictures?action=edit\&redlink=1) (marking the film's first theatrical outing), *NAZA* grossed around €1.1 million in the first two days of its theatrical run, topping the Italian box office and expanding the screenings beyond the three days that they had originally been intended to last.[^cite_note-28] In that time, the film surpassed *[Avengers Endgame: Encore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avengers_Endgame:_Encore)*.[^cite_note-29]

### Critical response

On review aggregator [Rotten Tomatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotten_Tomatoes), the film holds an approval rating of [100%](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_films_with_a_100%25_rating_on_Rotten_Tomatoes) based on 36 reviews, with an average rating of 9.0/10.[^cite_note-30] On [Metacritic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metacritic), the film has a [weighted average score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weighted_arithmetic_mean) of 93 out of 100, based on 20 critics, indicating "universal acclaim".[^cite_note-31]

The [British Film Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Film_Institute) review concluded the film was "a devastating watch, especially having so many of your darkest thoughts and suppositions confirmed, but there’s no turning away from what’s said. In those long shots of people in apartments watching TV or doing yoga, one wonders if Abraham and Szor are showing us the voters responsible for these policies or pointing at all of us."[^cite_note-32] The film was a critic's pick in *[The New York Times](30680.md)*; reviewer [Manohla Dargis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manohla_Dargis) said it was "tough to watch, and impossible to ignore".[^cite_note-33]

### IDF response

On 1 October 2026, the [IDF](39237.md) published a response after it was given a copy of the film and watched it. According to the IDF, the late response was due to the time needed to examine the film's accusations, as well as the fact that the filmmakers had not sent the claims in advance, and had not let the IDF see the film until shortly before the statement. In the response, the IDF said the film was a "misrepresented and manipulative depiction of reality", rejecting how the film described the strike planning and denying the civilian casualty numbers it claimed, including ratios like 500 civilians to one militant, 200 to one, or 20 to one. It also stated that it never planned or approved any strike where around 500 civilians were expected to die. The Israeli military added that targets are approved only by humans, not by AI. According to the IDF, even under the assumption that the soldiers that were interviewed in the film did serve in the roles attributed to them, it was still clear from their words in the film that they were not a part of any broad decision-making process.[^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35]

### Israeli government and societal response

The film was condemned by Israeli government officials, with the IDF denying the allegations made in the film. Culture minister [Miki Zohar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miki_Zohar) called for the directors' citizenship to be revoked, accusing them of committing [treason](31292.md),[^cite_note-36] and threatened to cut the funding of any Israeli cinema showing the documentary.[^cite_note-37] Prime minister [Benjamin Netanyahu](56472.md) described the film's positive reception at the [Venice Film Festival](32497.md) as "shocking" and said the film was part of "global [antisemitic](1078.md) incitement".[^cite_note-38] He stated that he would introduce bills to revoke the filmmakers' [Israeli citizenship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israeli_citizenship) and raise defamation charges.[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40] IDF chief of staff [Eyal Zamir](61877756.md) raised the possibility of [legal action](113258.md) against the filmmakers, criticising the film as [libellous](28661.md) and of making "grave, false accusations against IDF soldiers and commanders".[^cite_note-41] On 23 September 2026, Netanyahu backed Miki Zohar's proposal and announced he is in the process of advancing two legislations, one for allowing revocation of citizenship of Israelis who defame soldiers or the state abroad, and another for raising the defamation of Israel or the IDF with penalties up to a million [shekels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israeli_new_shekel).[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43]

[Itamar Ben-Gvir](54504115.md), minister of national security, called the directors "a disgrace".[^cite_note-44] A week after the film's premiere in Venice, right-wing protestors linked to Ben Gvir's [Otzma Yehudit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otzma_Yehudit) party surrounded Rachel Szor's parents' house. The protestors made explicit calls for violence, including calls to "execute the traitors".[^cite_note-45] Many other journalists, politicians, influencers and organizers also threatened the filmmakers. In addition, protests occurred at addresses belonging to them and their colleagues after far-right journalist [Yehuda Schlesinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yehuda_Schlesinger) of [Channel 14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_14_\(Israel\)) said the filmmakers should be "pursued" or "hunted" wherever they go, encouraging people to prevent them from returning to Israel.[^cite_note-46]

### Response from Israeli filmmakers and human rights organisations

In the week following the film's premiere, over a thousand Israeli filmmakers signed a petition in support of Abraham and Szor, as a response to an "unprecedented campaign of incitement and instigation" against the filmmakers. The petition stated that "the central role of art and journalism is to hold a mirror up to the society in which they exist". The signatories included several of Israel's most prominent directors, among them [Ari Folman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ari_Folman), [Shlomi Elkabetz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shlomi_Elkabetz), [Ran Tal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ran_Tal), and [Michal Aviad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michal_Aviad).[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48]

In the same week, over 40 Israeli human rights, equality, and peace organizations also published a joint statement in support of the film's directors. The statement called "on the authorities to put an end to the wave of incitement and threats of violence" against Abraham and Szor. Signatories included [Combatants for Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combatants_for_Peace), [B'Tselem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B'Tselem), [Peace Now](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_Now), [Breaking the Silence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breaking_the_Silence_\(organization\)), and [Adalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adalah_\(legal_center\)).[^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50]

### Other

The [International Documentary Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Documentary_Association) published a letter on its website urging the Israeli government "to withdraw its threats against filmmakers Yuval Abraham and Rachel Szor" as well as calling for an "end to the public campaign of intimidation against them".[^cite_note-51]
