The **yen** ([Japanese](15606.md): 円; [symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_symbol): **[¥](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yen_sign)**; [code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_4217): **JPY**) is the official currency of Japan. It is the third-most traded currency in the [foreign exchange market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_exchange_market), after the [United States dollar](18717338.md) (USD) and the [euro](9472.md).[^cite_note-2] It is also widely used as a third [reserve currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_currency) after the USD and the euro.

The New Currency Act of 1871 introduced Japan's modern currency system, with the yen defined as 1.5 g (0.048 troy ounces) of gold, or 24.26 g (0.780 troy ounces) of silver, and divided decimally into 100 *sen* or 1,000 *rin*. The yen replaced the previous [Tokugawa coinage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_coinage) as well as the various *[hansatsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hansatsu)* paper currencies issued by feudal *han* ([fiefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fief)). The [Bank of Japan](188155.md) was founded in 1882 and given a monopoly on controlling the money supply.[^cite_note-BOJ-3]

Following [World War II](32927.md), the yen lost much of its pre-war value as Japan faced a debt crisis and hyperinflation. Under the [Bretton Woods system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bretton_Woods_system), the yen was pegged to the USD alongside other major currencies. After this system was abandoned in 1971 with the [Nixon shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nixon_shock), the short-lived [Smithsonian Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smithsonian_Agreement) temporarily reinstated a fixed exchange rate. However, since the end of that system in February 1973, the yen has been a [floating currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_exchange_rate).[^cite_note-4]

The [Ministry of Finance](870389.md) and the Bank of Japan have sometimes intervened in the currency market in recent years, to try to slow down exchange rate movements. There were intermittent interventions from 1998 to 2003 and from 2010 to 2011 to curb excessive and speculative appreciation of the yen, and again in 2022 and 2024 to slow down speculative selling of the currency.[^cite_note-5] The first two interventions were coordinated with respective countries,[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7] and the [IMF](15251.md) has repeatedly stated that Japan is "committed to a flexible exchange rate".[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10]

## Pronunciation and etymology

The name *yen* derives from the Japanese word [圓](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%9C%93) (*en*; [\[eɴ\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Japanese); lit. 'round'), which borrows its phonetic reading from [Chinese yuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_yuan), similar to [North Korean won](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Korean_won) and [South Korean won](298884.md). Originally, the Chinese had traded silver in mass called [sycees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sycee), and when [Spanish and Mexican silver coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_dollar#Asia) arrived from the [Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captaincy_General_of_the_Philippines), the Chinese called them "silver rounds" ([Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_characters): 銀圓; [pinyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin): *yínyuán*) for their circular shapes.[^cite_note-Mikami2007-11] The coins and the name also appeared in Japan. While the Chinese eventually replaced 圆; 圓 with 元,[^cite_note-12] the Japanese continued to use the same word, which was given the *[shinjitai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinjitai)* form 円 in reforms at the end of World War II.

The spelling and pronunciation "yen" is standard in [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language), because when Japan was first encountered by Europeans around the 16th century, Japanese /e/ ([え](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E3%81%88)) and /we/ ([ゑ](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E3%82%91)) were both pronounced \[je]. Accordingly, Portuguese missionaries spelled them as "ye".[^cite_note-14] By the middle of the 18th century, /e/ and /we/ came to be pronounced \[e] as in modern Japanese, although some regions retain the \[je] pronunciation. [Walter Henry Medhurst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Henry_Medhurst), who had neither been to Japan nor met any Japanese people, having consulted mainly a Japanese-Dutch dictionary, spelled some "e"s as "ye" in his *An English and Japanese, and Japanese and English Vocabulary* (1830).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMedhurst1830296-15] In the early [Meiji era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_era), the U.S. physician and translator [James Curtis Hepburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Curtis_Hepburn), following Medhurst, spelled all "e"s as "ye" in his *A Japanese and English dictionary* (1867); in Japanese, *e* and *i* are slightly [palatalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palatalization_\(phonetics\)), somewhat as in Russian.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHepburn1867-16] That was the first full-scale Japanese-English/English-Japanese dictionary, which had a strong influence on Westerners in Japan and probably prompted the spelling "yen", which appeared in the 2nd edition (1872). Hepburn revised most "ye"s to "e" in the 3rd edition (1886)[^cite_note-17] to mirror the contemporary pronunciation, except "yen".[^cite_note-18]

## History

Further information: [Japanese currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_currency)

### Early history (1868–1876)



[5 yen coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5_yen_coin) from 1870 (year 3)



[50 sen coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/50_sen_coin) from 1870 (year 3)

Although the [Edo Shogunate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_shogunate) collapsed with the [Meiji Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration) and a new government was born, there was no immediate change to the monetary system. During this unstable period, the confusion caused by this form of exchange caused economic turmoil.[^cite_note-19] The gold (counting money) system of eastern Japan and the silver (weighing money) system of the western Japan were not unified, and the difference in the gold-silver ratio caused a large amount of gold to flow overseas at the end of the Tokugawa shogunate. [Emperor Meiji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Meiji) responded to this by appointing [Ōkuma Shigenobu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Ckuma_Shigenobu) as head of Japan's monetary reform program. He worked with [Inoue Kaoru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inoue_Kaoru), [Itō Hirobumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It%C5%8D_Hirobumi), and [Shibusawa Eiichi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibusawa_Eiichi) to run the Ministry of Finance, seeking to introduce a modern monetary system into Japan. Ōkuma eventually proposed that coins, which were previously square, be made into circular discs, and that the names of the traditional currencies, [ryō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ry%C5%8D) (両), bu (分) and shu (朱), be unified into yen (円), which was accepted by the government.[^cite_note-20] Other rejected proposals included physical [weight units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_units_of_measurement#Mass) of "Fun" and "Momme" which never made it past the [pattern stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Japanese_coinage_patterns#Meiji).[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23]

The first gold yen coins consisted of 2, 5, and 20 yen coins which were struck throughout 1870. Five yen coins were first struck in gold for the Japanese government in 1870 at the [San Francisco Mint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Mint).[^cite_note-Frossard-24] During this time a new mint was being established at [Osaka](52230.md), but this did not receive the gold bullion needed for coinage until the following year.[^cite_note-25] Gold bullion was delivered from private Japanese citizens, foreigners, and the Japanese government.[^cite_note-Hisamitsu-26] Initially the government opted for silver, which would become the standard unit of value, leaving gold coinage as a subsidiary.[^cite_note-GS-27] While gold coinage could not be produced domestically in 1870, the mint at Osaka could produce silver coins, and these included denominations of 5, 10, 20, and 50 sen.[^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29] None of these coins dated "1870" circulated until the [Meiji government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_\(era\)) officially adopted the "yen" as Japan's modern unit of currency on 27 June 1871.[^cite_note-30] This Act formally stipulated the adoption of the decimal accounting system of *yen* (1, 圓), *sen* (1⁄100, 錢), and *rin* (1⁄1000, 厘). The new currency was gradually introduced beginning from July of that year.

Japanese yen-denominated paper currency was also conceived with the coins in 1870, as [Meiji Tsuho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Tsuho) notes by Italian engraver [Edoardo Chiossone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edoardo_Chiossone).[^cite_note-Chiossone-31] These were released as [fiat currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiat_currency) in denominations of 1, 2, 5, 10, 50, and 100 yen along with subsidiary notes of 10, 20, and 50 sen in 1872. Almost concurrently, the government established a series of national banks modeled after the system in the [United States](3434750.md), which issued national bank notes.

### Satsuma Rebellion and aftermath (1877–1887)

Massive inflation from the [Satsuma Rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satsuma_Rebellion) in 1877 caused a glut of non-redeemable fiat currency notes. The issuance of national fiat banknotes was ultimately suspended in 1880 by then prime minister [Matsukata Masayoshi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matsukata_Masayoshi).[^cite_note-SCP20-32][^cite_note-mono-33] New policies were put into place which included the establishment of a [centralized banking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_bank) system.[^cite_note-BOJ_History-34][^cite_note-mono-33]

The [Bank of Japan](188155.md) began operations on 10 October 1882, with the authority to print banknotes that could be exchanged for the old Government and National Bank Notes.[^cite_note-SCP29-35][^cite_note-BOJ1885-36] By May 1883, another act provided the redemption and retirement of national bank notes.[^cite_note-NB1883AND1896-37][^cite_note-Americana1-38] The National Bank Act was amended again in March 1896, providing for the dissolution of the national banks on the expiration of their charters.[^cite_note-NB1883AND1896-37] This amendment also prohibited national bank notes from circulating after December 31, 1899.[^cite_note-expired-39] In that year, Japan adopted a [gold exchange standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_exchange_standard), defining the yen as 0.75g fine gold or US\$0.4985.[^cite_note-40]

This exchange rate remained in place until Japan left the gold standard in December 1931, after which the yen fell to US\$0.30 by July 1932 and to US\$0.20 by 1933.[^cite_note-41]

It remained steady at around US\$0.30 until the start of the Pacific War on 7 December 1941, at which time it fell to US\$0.23.[^cite_note-42]

The sen and the rin were eventually taken out of circulation at the end of 1953.[^cite_note-43]

### Fixed rate of the yen to the U.S. dollar

No true exchange rate existed for the yen between 7 December 1941 and 25 April 1949; wartime inflation reduced the yen to a fraction of its prewar value.

After a period of instability, on 25 April 1949, the U.S. occupation government [fixed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_exchange_rate) the value of the yen at ¥360/US\$ through the [Dodge plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodge_Line), which was part of the [Bretton Woods system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bretton_Woods_system), to stabilize prices in the Japanese economy.[^cite_note-44]

That exchange rate was maintained until 1971, when the United States abandoned the gold standard, ending a key element of the Bretton Woods system, and setting in motion changes that eventually led to [floating exchange rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_exchange_rate) in 1973.[^cite_note-45]

### Yen and major currencies float

By 1971, the yen had become undervalued. Japanese exports were costing too little in international markets, and imports from abroad were costing the Japanese too much. This undervaluation was reflected in the [current account balance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_account_\(balance_of_payments\)), which had risen from the [deficits](4810.md) of the early 1960s, to a then-large [surplus](4810.md) of US\$5.8 billion in 1971. The belief that the yen, and several other major currencies, were undervalued motivated the United States' actions in 1971.

Following the United States' measures to devalue the USD in the summer of 1971, the Japanese government agreed to a new, fixed exchange rate as part of the [Smithsonian Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smithsonian_Agreement), signed at the end of the year. This agreement set the exchange rate at ¥308/US\$. However, the new fixed rates of the Smithsonian Agreement were difficult to maintain in the face of supply and demand pressures in the foreign-exchange market. In early 1973, the rates were abandoned, and the major nations of the world allowed their currencies to [float](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_currency).

### Yen adoption in Okinawa

After World War II the [U.S.-administered Okinawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Civil_Administration_of_the_Ryukyu_Islands) issued a higher-valued currency called the [B yen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B_yen) from 1946 to 1958, which was then replaced by the USD at the rate of US\$1 = ¥120 B. Upon the [reversion of Okinawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_Okinawa_Reversion_Agreement) to Japan in 1972 the Japanese yen then replaced the USD. In light of the USD's reduction in value from ¥360 to ¥308 just before the reversion, an unannounced "currency confirmation" took place on 9 October 1971, wherein residents disclosed their USD holdings in cash and bank accounts; US\$60 million were entitled to conversion in 1972 at the higher rate of ¥360.[^cite_note-46]

### Japanese government intervention in the currency market

In the 1970s, the Japanese government and business people were concerned that a rise in the value of the yen would hurt export growth by making Japanese products less competitive and would damage the industrial base. The government, therefore, continued to intervene heavily in foreign-exchange markets (buying or selling USDs), even after the 1973 decision to allow the yen to float.[^cite_note-\:0-47]

Despite intervention, market pressures caused the yen to continue climbing in value, peaking temporarily at an average of ¥271/US\$ in 1973, before the impact of the [1973 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis) was felt (this was retroactively called [endaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endaka), although the term was only coined in 1985). The increased costs of imported [oil](23195.md) caused the yen to depreciate to a range of ¥290/US\$ to ¥300/US\$ between 1974 and 1976. The re-emergence of trade surpluses drove the yen back up to ¥211 in 1978. This currency strengthening was again reversed by the [second oil shock in 1979](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_energy_crisis), with the yen dropping to ¥227/US\$ by 1980.[^cite_note-\:0-47]

### Yen in the early 1980s

During the first half of the 1980s, the yen failed to rise in value, though current account surpluses returned and grew quickly. From ¥221/US\$ in 1981, the average value of the yen actually dropped to ¥239/US\$ in 1985. The rise in the current account surplus generated stronger demand for yen in foreign-exchange markets, but this trade-related demand for yen was offset by other factors. A wide differential in interest rates, with United States interest rates much higher than those in Japan, and the continuing moves to [deregulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deregulation) the international flow of [capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_capital), led to a large net outflow of capital from Japan. This capital flow increased the supply of yen in foreign-exchange markets, as Japanese investors changed their yen for other currencies (mainly USDs) to invest overseas. This kept the yen weak relative to the USD and fostered the rapid rise in the Japanese trade surplus that took place in the 1980s.

### Effect of the Plaza Accord





JPY nominal and real [effective exchange rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_exchange_rate) (2005 = 100)

In 1985, a dramatic change began. Finance officials from major nations signed an agreement (the [Plaza Accord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_Accord)) affirming that the USD was overvalued (and, therefore, the yen undervalued). This agreement, and shifting supply and demand pressures in the markets, led to a rapid rise in the value of the yen. From its average of ¥239/US\$ in 1985, the yen rose to a peak of ¥128 in 1988, virtually doubling its value relative to the USD. After declining somewhat in 1989 and 1990, it reached a new high of ¥123/US\$ in December 1992. In April 1995, the yen hit a peak of under 80 yen/US\$, temporarily making Japan's economy nearly the size of that of the US.[^cite_note-48]

### Post-bubble years

The yen declined during the [Japanese asset price bubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_asset_price_bubble) and continued to do so afterwards, reaching a low of ¥134 to USD in February 2002. The Bank of Japan's policy of zero interest rates has discouraged yen investments, with the [carry trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carry_trade) of investors borrowing yen and investing in better-paying currencies (thus further pushing down the yen) estimated to be as large as US\$1 trillion.[^cite_note-49] In February 2007, *[The Economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Economist)* estimated that the yen was 15% undervalued against the USD, and as much as 40% undervalued against the euro.[^cite_note-50]

### After the global economic crisis of 2008



Comparison of the GNP-weighted nominal exchange rates: CHF and JPY versus CNY, EUR, USD, and GBP

However, this trend of depreciation reversed after the [global economic crisis of 2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession). Other major currencies, except the [Swiss franc](361362.md), have been declining relative to the yen.

On 4 April 2013, the Bank of Japan announced that they would expand their asset purchase program by US\$1.4 trillion in two years. The Bank of Japan hopes to bring Japan from deflation to inflation, aiming for 2% inflation. The amount of purchases is so large that it is expected to double the money supply, but this move has sparked concerns that the authorities in Japan are deliberately devaluing the yen to boost exports.[^cite_note-51] However, the commercial sector in Japan worried that the devaluation would trigger an increase in import prices, especially for energy and raw materials.[^cite_note-52]

### A period of rapid global inflation from 2022 onwards

Further information: [Carry trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carry_trade)

Since 2022, the yen has depreciated significantly against its peers, due to a variety of factors. Firstly, Japan's prolonged low-interest-rate policy (to tackle domestic [deflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflation)) has created a [yield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yield_\(finance\)) differential with other countries—notably the US—that have high interest rates (to tackle domestic [inflation](38286.md)), prompting investors to seek higher returns in foreign currencies. This interest rate differential directly affects the price  of the yen and serves as one of the drivers behind its depreciation. Widely held expectations of yen depreciation can become self-fulfilling prophecies, affecting the currency's exchange rate.[^cite_note-Economist_230430-53] To counter this, the Bank of Japan conducted currency interventions of more than ¥9 trillion selling the USD and buying the yen in the September–October 2022 and April–May 2024 periods respectively.[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55][^cite_note-56]

### Redenomination proposals

Numerous proposals have been made since the 1990s to [redenominate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redenomination) the yen by introducing a new unit or new yen, equal to 100 yen, and nearly worth one USD. This has not happened to date, since the yen remains trusted globally despite its low unit value, and due to the large costs of reissuing new currency and updating currency-reading hardware. The negative impact of postponing upgrades to various computer software until redenomination occurs, in particular, was also cited.[^cite_note-57]

The obverse side of all coins shows the coin's value in [kanji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanji) characters as well as the [country name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Japan) (through 1945, *Dai Nippon* (大日本; "Great Japan"); after 1945, *Nippon-koku* (日本国; "State of Japan") (except for the 5-yen coin with the country name on the reverse). The reverse side of all coins shows the year of mintage, which is not shown in [Gregorian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_calendar) years, but instead in the [regnal year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regnal_year) of the [reigning emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_era_name), with the first year of an era called *gannen* (元年). Imperial portraits have never appeared on Japanese coins, as the image of the emperor remains sacred.[^cite_note-65] The regnal year does not necessarily align with the calendar year. The first coins of the [Reiwa era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reiwa_era) were minted in July 2019.[^cite_note-66]

20 yen coin from 1870 (Meiji year 3)

In 1897, the silver 1 yen coin was demonetized and the sizes of the gold coins were reduced by 50%, with 5, 10 and 20 yen coins issued.

After the war, brass 50 sen, 1 and 5 yen were introduced between 1946 and 1948. The existing-type holed brass 5 yen was introduced in 1949, the bronze 10 yen in 1951, and the aluminum 1 yen in 1955.

In 1955 the first unholed, nickel 50 yen was introduced. In 1957, silver 100 yen pieces were introduced, followed by the holed 50 yen coin in 1959. These were replaced in 1967 by the existing cupro-nickel 100 yen along with a smaller 50 yen.[^cite_note-67]

In 1982, the first cupronickel 500 yen coin was introduced. Alongside the 5 [Swiss franc coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coins_of_the_Swiss_franc), the 500 yen coin is one of the highest-valued coin to be used regularly in the world, with a value of US\$3.19 as of January 2026.[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69] Because of its high [face value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_value), the 500 yen coin has been a favorite target for counterfeiters, resulting in the issuance in 2000 of the second nickel-brass 500 yen coin with added security features. Continued counterfeiting of the latter resulted in the issuance in 2021 of the third [bi-metallic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi-metallic_coin) 500 yen coin with more improvements in security features.

Due to the great differences in style, size, weight and the pattern present on the edge of the coin they are easy for people with visual impairments to tell apart from one another.

Commemorative coins have been minted on various occasions in base metal, silver and gold.[^cite_note-70] The first of these were silver ¥100 and ¥1,000 [Summer Olympic coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summer_Olympic_coins) issued for the [1964 games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_Summer_Olympics). In 2008 commemorative ¥500 and ¥1,000 coins were issued featuring Japan's 47 prefectures. Even though all commemorative coins can be spent like ordinary (non-commemorative) coins, they do not normally circulate, and ¥100,000 coins are treated with caution due to the discovery of counterfeits.[^cite_note-71]

The 1 yen coin is made out of 100% aluminum and can float on water if placed correctly.

### Formerly circulated coinage

#### Sen



50 sen coin from 1870 (year 3 of Meiji)

Main articles: [Half sen coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_sen_coin), [1 sen coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_sen_coin), [2 sen coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2_sen_coin), [5 sen coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5_sen_coin), [10 sen coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/10_sen_coin), [20 sen coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20_sen_coin), and [50 sen coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/50_sen_coin)

Subsidiary coins of "sen" (one hundredth of a yen) were initially introduced in 1870 with a silver alloy in denominations of 5, 10, 20 and 50 sen. Copper sen coins in denominations of half, 1, and 2 came three years later, as Japan acquired the technology needed to mint them. The removal of silver from sen coinage began in 1889, when [Cupronickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupronickel) 5 sen coins were introduced. By 1920, this included cupro-nickel 10 sen and reduced-size silver 50 sen coins. Production of the latter ceased in 1938, after which a variety of base metals were used to produce 1, 5, and 10 sen coins during the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War). While clay 5 and 10 sen coins were produced in 1945, they were not issued for circulation. As with the Rin, coins in denominations of less than 1 yen became invalid at the end of 1953 and were demonetized due to inflation.

#### Rin

Main articles: [1 rin coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_rin_coin) and [5 rin coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5_rin_coin)



One rin coin from 1873 (year 6 of Meiji)

Bronze coins worth one-one thousandth of a yen called "rin" were first introduced in 1873. One rin coins were very small, measuring 15.75 mm in diameter and 0.3 mm in thickness, and co-circulated with [mon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_mon_\(currency\)) coins of the old currency system. Their small size was eventually their undoing, and the rin was abandoned in 1884 due to unpopularity.[^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73] Five rin coins worth one-two hundredth of a yen also used a bronze alloy. These were successor coins to the equally valued [half sen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_sen_coin) coin which had been previously minted until 1888. The decision to bring back an equally valued coin was in response to rising inflation caused by [World War I](4764461.md) which led to an overall shortage of subsidiary coins. The mintage period for five rin coins was brief as they were discontinued after only four years of production due to their sharp decline in monetary value. The overall demand for subsidiary coinage ended as Japan slipped into a post-war recession. Coins worth 1 and 5 rin were eventually officially taken out of circulation at the end of 1953 and demonetized.

The issuance of yen banknotes began in 1872, two years after the currency was introduced. Denominations have ranged from ¥1 to ¥10,000; since 1984, the lowest-valued banknote is the ¥1,000 note. Before and during [World War II](32927.md), various bodies issued banknotes in yen, such as the [Ministry of Finance](870389.md) and the Imperial Japanese National Bank. The [Allied forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Commander_for_the_Allied_Powers) also issued some notes shortly after the war. Since then, the [Bank of Japan](188155.md) has been the exclusive note issuing authority. The bank has issued five series after World War II.

Japan is generally considered a cash-based society, with 38% of payments in Japan made by [cash](55336.md) in 2014.[^cite_note-74] Possible explanations are that cash payments protect one's privacy, merchants do not have to wait for payment, and it does not carry any negative connotation like credit.

At present, portraits of people from the [Meiji period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_era) and later are printed on Japanese banknotes. The reason for this is that from the viewpoint of preventing forgery, it is desirable to use a precise photograph as an original rather than a painting for a portrait.[^cite_note-75][^cite_note-76]

Series E banknotes were introduced in 2004 in ¥1000, ¥5000, and ¥10,000 denominations.

Series F banknotes were introduced on 3 July 2024. They were announced on 9 April 2019 by Finance Minister [Tarō Asō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tar%C5%8D_As%C5%8D).[^cite_note-77] The ¥1000 note features [Kitasato Shibasaburō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitasato_Shibasabur%C5%8D) and *[The Great Wave off Kanagawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Wave_off_Kanagawa)*, the ¥5000 note features [Tsuda Umeko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsuda_Umeko) and [Wisteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisteria) flowers, and the ¥10,000 note features [Shibusawa Eiichi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibusawa_Eiichi) and [Tokyo Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Station). The Ministry decided to not redesign the ¥2000 note due to low circulation. The [EURion constellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EURion_constellation) pattern is present in the Series D, E and F banknotes.

## Determinants of value

Beginning in December 1931, Japan gradually shifted from the gold standard system to the managed currency system.[^cite_note-78]

The [relative value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_value_\(economics\)) of the yen is determined in [foreign exchange markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_exchange_market) by the [economic forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics) of [supply and demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supply_and_demand).[^cite_note-79] The [supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supply_\(economics\)) of the yen in the [market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_\(economics\)) is governed by the desire of yen holders to exchange their yen for other currencies to purchase [goods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_\(economics\)), [services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_\(economics\)), or [assets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asset). The [demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demand_\(economics\)) for the yen is governed by the desire of foreigners to buy goods and services in Japan and by their interest in investing in Japan (buying yen-denominated real and financial assets).[^cite_note-80]

Since the 1990s, the Bank of Japan, the country's central bank, has kept interest rates low to spur economic growth. Short-term lending rates have responded to this monetary relaxation and fell from 3.7% to 1.3% between 1993 and 2008.[^cite_note-81] Low interest rates and low inflation, combined with a ready liquidity, prompted investors to borrow yen in Japan and invest it in other countries (such as the US) with significantly higher [bank rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_rate), a practice known as the [carry trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carry_trade). This has helped to keep the exchange rate of the yen low compared to other currencies, particularly the USD.[^cite_note-Economist_230430-53]

## Historical exchange rate

Before the war commenced, the yen traded on an average of 3.6 yen to the USD. After the war the yen went as low as ¥600/US\$ in 1947, as a result of currency overprinting in order to fund the war, and afterwards to fund the reconstruction.

When MacArthur and the US forces entered Japan in 1945, they decreed an official conversion rate of ¥15/US\$. Within 1945–1946: the rate tanked to ¥50/US\$ because of the ongoing inflation. During the first half of 1946, the rate fluctuated to ¥66/US\$, and eventually plummeting to ¥600/US\$ by 1947 because of the failure of the economic remedies. Eventually, the peg was officially moved to ¥270/US\$ in 1948 before being adjusted again from 1949 to 1971 to ¥360/US\$.

Beginning in 2022, the yen/USD rate has become increasingly weaker with each passing month. By July 2024, the price fell to upper ¥161/US\$, marking the lowest exchange rate for the yen in 37.5 years on a nominal effective exchange rate[^cite_note-88] and the lowest real effective exchange rate since the start of statistics by the [Bank of Japan](188155.md) in 1970.[^cite_note-89][^cite_note-90][^cite_note-91][^cite_note-92] One of the main reasons behind this fall was the US moving towards higher interest rates, while Japan remained "ultra-low". Other factors include the strength of the US economy and its labor market, while Japan continues to lag behind its peers to bring its economy back to its pre-pandemic size. Japan's trade balance staying in the red is also likely feeding into the weaker yen.[^cite_note-Bloom_220610-93][^cite_note-94][^cite_note-95] Interviewed by [The Asahi Shimbun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Asahi_Shimbun) in September 2022, Izuru Kato, chief economist at Totan Research, expressed concern about the sharp fall in its value since 2022.[^cite_note-Kato_220902-96] and *Moneypost* reported that the exchange rate instability had made it impossible to exchange the currency for Japanese yen at some exchange offices.[^cite_note-97] With the rate returning to ¥150 in November 2023, concern deepened as Nikkei sources expected the yen to depreciate further in the future.[^cite_note-98] *[The Economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Economist)* (London) expressed concern over the performance of the Bank of Japan, suggested that a systemic risk is posed to the world financial system.[^cite_note-Economist_231102-99] Other reports of a weakening of the Japanese yen include the [Noto Peninsula earthquake in January 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Noto_earthquake), where previous earthquakes had seen a temporary appreciation of the yen against the USD, but this earthquake reversed the trend and weakened the yen against the USD.[^cite_note-100] [Goldman Sachs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldman_Sachs) expects the Japanese yen to remain very weak in the coming months.[^cite_note-101]
