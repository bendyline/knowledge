The **Australian dollar** ([sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_sign): **\$**; [code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_4217): **AUD**; also abbreviated **A\$** or sometimes **AU\$** to distinguish it from other [dollar-denominated currencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar);[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3] and also referred to as the **dollar** or **Aussie dollar**) is the official [currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency) and [legal tender](267793.md#Australia) of [Australia](4689264.md), including [all of its external territories](4689096.md), and three independent sovereign [Pacific Island states](341111.md): [Kiribati](16674.md), [Nauru](21302.md), and [Tuvalu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuvalu).[^cite_note-RBA-4][^cite_note-nauru-5][^cite_note-tuvalu-6][^cite_note-kiribati-7] In April 2025, it was the [seventh most-traded currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Template\:Most_traded_currencies) in the [foreign exchange market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_exchange_market)[^cite_note-BIS_survey-8] and as of Q1 2024 the sixth  most-held [reserve currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_currency) in [global reserves](1015869.md).[^cite_note-IMFCOFER-9]

The Australian dollar was introduced as a [decimal currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_currency) on 14 February 1966 to replace the non-decimal [Australian pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_pound), with the conversion rate of two dollars to the pound (£A1 = A\$2). Each [dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar) is subdivided into 100 [cents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cent_\(currency\)). The \$ symbol precedes the amount. On the introduction of the currency, the \$ symbol was intended to have two strokes, but the version with one stroke has also always been acceptable.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11]

In 2023, there were A\$4.4 billion in coins[^cite_note-12] and A\$101.3 billion in notes[^cite_note-13] of Australian [currency in circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_in_circulation), or around A\$6,700 per person in Australia,[^cite_note-14] which includes cash reserves held by the banking system and cash in circulation in other countries or held as a [foreign exchange reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_exchange_reserve).

## Constitutional basis

[Section 51(xii) of the Constitution of Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_51\(xii\)_of_the_Constitution_of_Australia) gives the [Commonwealth (federal) Parliament](302298.md) the power to legislate with respect to "currency, coinage, and legal tender".[^cite_note-15] The states are not allowed to coin money, in accordance with section 115 which provides that "\[a] State shall not coin money, nor make anything but gold and silver coin a legal tender in payment of debts."[^cite_note-16] Australian circulating coins are now produced at the [Royal Australian Mint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Australian_Mint) in [Canberra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canberra).

## History

"Australian Royal" redirects here. For Australian royalty, see [Monarchy of Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy_of_Australia).

Main article: [History of Australian currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Australian_currency)

### Background

Before [Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_federation) in 1901, the six colonies that comprised Australia had separate currencies, all of which closely replicated the [British currency system](270673.md), and were usually exchangeable with each other on a one-to-one basis. Hence Federation was not seen as urgently requiring a single, unified currency. For another 10 years, colonial banknotes and coins continued to be the main circulating currencies.

In 1902, a select committee of the [House of Representatives](61565.md), chaired by [George Edwards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Edwards_\(Australian_politician\)), had recommended that Australia adopt a single, national decimal currency, with a pound divided into ten [florins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florin_\(British_coin\)) and each florin comprising 100 cents.[^cite_note-17] However, the recommendation was not acted upon.

The [Australian pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_pound) (£A) was introduced in 1910, at par with the [pound sterling](270673.md) –  £A1 = £1 (UK). Like the UK pound, it was divided into 240 pence, or 20 shillings (each comprising 12 pence). In December 1931, the Australian currency was devalued by 25%, so that one pound five shillings Australian was equivalent to one pound sterling.[^cite_note-18]

In 1937, a banking [royal commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_commissions_in_Australia), appointed by the [Lyons government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyons_government), recommended that Australia adopt "a system of decimal coinage ... based upon the division of the Australian pound into [1000 parts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mill_\(currency\))".[^cite_note-new-19][^cite_note-20] This recommendation was not accepted either.

### Adoption of the dollar

1966 government advertisement jingle on the change to decimal currency to the tune of [Click Go the Shears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Click_Go_the_Shears)

In February 1959, treasurer [Harold Holt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Holt) appointed a Decimal Currency Committee, chaired by [Walter D. Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_D._Scott), to examine the merits of decimalisation. The committee reported in August 1960 in favour of decimalisation and proposed that a new currency be introduced (from February 1963), to be modelled on South Africa's replacement of the [South African pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_African_pound) with the [rand](298686.md) (worth 10 shillings, or 1⁄2 pound). The [Menzies government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menzies_Government_\(1949%E2%80%9366\)) announced its support for decimalisation in July 1961, but delayed the process in order to give further consideration to the implementation process.[^cite_note-21] In April 1963, Holt announced that a decimal currency was scheduled to be introduced in February 1966, with a base unit equal to 10 shillings, and that a Decimal Currency Board would be established to oversee the transition process.[^cite_note-new-19]

A public consultation process was held in which over 1,000 names were suggested for the new currency. This was reduced to a shortlist of seven names: austral, crown, dollar, pound, regal, tasman and royal.[^cite_note-22] In June 1963, Holt announced that the new currency would be called the "royal". This met with widespread public disapproval, and three months later it was announced that it would instead be named the "dollar".[^cite_note-23]

The pound was replaced by the dollar on 14 February 1966[^cite_note-24] with the conversion rate of A\$2 = £A1 (so that A\$1 = 10  shillings and one cent = 1.2[d](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penny)). For example, a pre-decimal amount of nine pounds, sixteen shillings and sixpence (£A9 16s 6d) became \$19.65 in terms of dollars and cents. Since Australia was still part of the fixed-exchange [sterling area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sterling_area), the exchange rate was fixed to the [pound sterling](270673.md) at a rate of A\$1 = 8s sterling (or £1 (UK) = A\$2.50, and in turn £1 (UK) = [US\$](18717338.md) 2.80). In 1967, Australia effectively left the sterling area when the pound sterling was devalued against the US dollar from US\$2.80 to US\$2.40, but the Australian dollar chose to retain its peg to the US dollar at A\$1 = US\$1.12 (hence appreciating in value versus sterling).

The Australian dollar is legal tender in its external territories: [Christmas Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christmas_Island), [Cocos (Keeling) Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocos_\(Keeling\)_Islands), and [Norfolk Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norfolk_Island); and is also official currency in [Kiribati](16674.md), [Nauru](21302.md), and [Tuvalu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuvalu). It was legal tender in [Papua New Guinea](22943.md) until 31 December 1975 when it was replaced by the [Papua New Guinean kina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papua_New_Guinean_kina), and in [Solomon Islands](265083.md) until 1977 when it was replaced by the [Solomon Islands dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon_Islands_dollar).

## Coins

Main article: [Coins of the Australian dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coins_of_the_Australian_dollar)

In 1966, coins were introduced in denominations of 1 and 2 cents (bronze); 5, 10, and 20 cents ([cupronickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupronickel); 75% copper, 25% nickel); and 50 cents (silver, then cupronickel). The 50-cent coins in 80% silver were no longer minted after March 1968 due to the intrinsic value of the silver content rising to exceed the face value of the coins.[^cite_note-25] [Aluminium bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_bronze) (92% copper, 6% aluminium, 2% nickel) 1 dollar coins were introduced in 1984, followed by aluminium bronze 2 dollar coins in 1988, to replace the banknotes of that value. In everyday Australian parlance, these coins collectively are referred to as "gold coins". 1 and 2 cent coins were discontinued in 1991 and withdrawn from circulation in 1992; since then cash transactions have been [rounded to the nearest 5 cents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_rounding).

Australia's coins are produced by the [Royal Australian Mint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Australian_Mint), which is located in the nation's capital, [Canberra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canberra). Since opening in 1965, the Mint has produced more than 14 billion circulating coins, and has the capacity to produce more than two million coins per day, or more than 600 million coins per year.

Current Australian 5, 10 and 20 cent coins are identical in size to the former Australian, New Zealand, and British sixpence, shilling, and two shilling (florin) coins. Pre-decimal Australian coins remain legal tender for 10 cents per shilling. Before 2006 the old New Zealand 5, 10 and 20 cent coins were often mistaken for Australian coins of the same value, and vice versa, and therefore circulated in both countries. The UK replaced these coins with smaller versions from 1990 to 1993, as did New Zealand in 2006. Still, some confusion occurs with the larger-denomination coins in the two countries. Australia's \$1 coin is similar in size to New Zealand's \$2 coin, and the New Zealand \$1 coin is similar in size to Australia's \$2 coin.

With a mass of 15.55 grams (0.549 oz) and a diameter of 31.51 millimetres (1+1⁄4 in), the Australian 50-cent coin is one of the largest coins that is being circulated in the world today.

### Commemorative coins

Main article: [Australian commemorative coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_commemorative_coins)

The [Royal Australian Mint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Australian_Mint) also has an international reputation for producing quality numismatic coins. It has first issued commemorative 50-cent coins in 1970, commemorating [James Cook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cook)'s exploration along the east coast of the Australian continent, followed in 1977 by a coin for Queen Elizabeth II's [Silver Jubilee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_Jubilee_of_Elizabeth_II) (25th year of rule), the wedding of [Charles, Prince of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles,_Prince_of_Wales) and [Lady Diana Spencer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diana,_Princess_of_Wales) in 1981, the [Brisbane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brisbane) [Commonwealth Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_Games) in 1982, and the [Australian Bicentenary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Bicentenary) in 1988. Issues expanded into greater numbers in the 1990s and the 21st century, responding to collector demand. Commemorative designs have also been featured on the other circulating denominations.

In commemoration of the 40th anniversary of Australia using the [decimal currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_currency), the 2006 mint [proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_coinage) and [uncirculated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coin_grading) sets included one- and two-cent coins. In early 2013, Australia's first triangular coin was introduced to mark the 25th anniversary of the opening of Parliament House. The silver \$5 coin is 99.9% silver, and depicts Parliament House as viewed from one of its courtyards.[^cite_note-28]

## Banknotes

Main article: [Banknotes of the Australian dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banknotes_of_the_Australian_dollar)

### First series

The first paper issues of the Australian dollar were issued in 1966. The \$1, \$2, \$10 and \$20 notes had exact equivalents in the former pound notes. The \$5 note was issued in 1967, the \$50 was issued in 1973 and the \$100 was issued in 1984.[^cite_note-29]

The \$1 banknote was later replaced by [a \$1 coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_one_dollar_coin) in 1984, while the [\$2 banknote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/\$2_note_\(Australian\)) was replaced by [a smaller \$2 coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_two_dollar_coin) in 1988.[^cite_note-rbafgfysjf-mus-inflate-note-30] Although no longer printed, all previous notes of the Australian dollar remain legal tender.[^cite_note-rba-legal-31]

Shortly after the changeover, substantial counterfeiting of \$10 notes was detected.[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33] This provided an impetus for the Reserve Bank of Australia to develop new note technologies jointly with the [Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_Scientific_and_Industrial_Research_Organisation), culminating in the introduction of the first [polymer banknote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer_banknote) in 1988.[^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36]

### First polymer series

Australia was the first country to produce [polymer banknotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer_banknote),[^cite_note-37] more specifically made of [polypropylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polypropylene) polymer, which were produced by [Note Printing Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Note_Printing_Australia). These polymer notes are cleaner than paper notes, are more durable and easily recyclable.

The first polymer banknote was issued in 1988 as a \$10 note[^cite_note-38] commemorating the bicentenary of European settlement in Australia. The note depicted on one side a young male Aboriginal person in body paint, with other elements of Aboriginal culture. On the reverse side was the ship *Supply* from the First Fleet, with a background of Sydney Cove, as well as a group of people to illustrate the diverse backgrounds from which Australia has evolved over 200 years.

The first polymer series was rolled out starting 1992 and featured the following persons:

- The \$100 note features world-renowned soprano [Dame Nellie Melba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nellie_Melba) (1861–1931), and the distinguished soldier, engineer and administrator [General Sir John Monash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Monash) (1865–1931).

  [^cite_note-39]

- The \$50 note features Aboriginal writer and inventor [David Unaipon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Unaipon) (1872–1967), and Australia's first female parliamentarian, [Edith Cowan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edith_Cowan) (1861–1932).

  [^cite_note-40]

- The \$20 note features the founder of the world's first aerial medical service (the [Royal Flying Doctor Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Flying_Doctor_Service)), the [Reverend John Flynn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Flynn_\(minister\)) (1880–1951), and [Mary Reibey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Reibey) (1777–1855), who arrived in Australia as a convict in 1792 and went on to become a successful shipping magnate and philanthropist.

  [^cite_note-41]

- The \$10 note features the poets [Banjo Paterson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banjo_Paterson) (1864–1941) and [Dame Mary Gilmore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Gilmore) (1865–1962). This note incorporates micro-printed excerpts of Paterson's and Gilmore's work.

  [^cite_note-42]

- The \$5 note features [Queen Elizabeth II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_II) and [Parliament House, Canberra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_House,_Canberra), the national capital.

  [^cite_note-43]

A special centenary issue of the \$5 note in 2001 featured [Sir Henry Parkes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Parkes) and [Catherine Helen Spence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_Helen_Spence). In 2015–2016 there were petitions to feature [Fred Hollows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Hollows) on the upgraded \$5 note, but failed to push through when the new note was introduced on 1 September 2016.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45][^cite_note-new_5-46]

Australia also prints polymer banknotes for a number of other countries through [Note Printing Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Note_Printing_Australia), a wholly owned subsidiary of the Reserve Bank of Australia. Note Printing Australia prints polymer notes or simply supplies the polymer substrate[^cite_note-47] for a growing number of other countries including [Bangladesh](3454.md), [Brunei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunei), [Chile](5489.md), [Kuwait](7515890.md), [Malaysia](3607937.md), [Mexico](3966054.md), [Nepal](171166.md), [New Zealand](4913064.md), [Papua New Guinea](22943.md), [Romania](25445.md), [Samoa](27238.md), [Singapore](27318.md), [Solomon Islands](265083.md), [Sri Lanka](26750.md) and [Vietnam](202354.md). Many other countries are showing a strong interest in the new technology.

### Second polymer series

On 27 September 2012, the [Reserve Bank of Australia](492355.md) stated that it had ordered work on a project to upgrade the current banknotes. The upgraded banknotes would incorporate a number of new future proof security features[^cite_note-48] and include tactile features like Braille dots for ease of use of the visually impaired.[^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50] All persons featured on the first polymer series were retained on the second polymer series. However, following the death of Queen Elizabeth II, the government has announced that the \$5 note will be replaced with a design reflecting Indigenous history and culture.[^cite_note-51]

## Exchange rates

### Exchange rate history



The cost of one euro in Australian dollars

Prior to 1983, Australia maintained a [fixed exchange rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_exchange_rate). The Australian pound was initially at par from 1910 with the British pound or £A1 = £1 (United Kingdom); from 1931 it was devalued to £A1 = GB£0.80 (16 [shillings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shilling) sterling). This reflected its historical ties as well as a view about the stability in value of the British pound.

From 1946 to 1971, Australia maintained a peg under the [Bretton Woods system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bretton_Woods_system), a fixed exchange rate system that pegged the [U.S. dollar](18717338.md) to gold, but the Australian dollar was effectively pegged to sterling until 1967 at £1 sterling = A\$2.50 = US\$2.80 (A\$1 = 8s sterling). In 1967, Australia did not follow the pound sterling devaluation and remained fixed to the U.S. dollar at A\$1 = US\$1.12 = 9s 4d sterling.

With the breakdown of the Bretton Woods system in 1971, Australia converted the traditional peg to a [managed float regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Managed_float_regime) against the US dollar. In September 1974, Australia managed the dollar's exchange rate against a basket of currencies (called the [trade weighted index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_weighted_index) (TWI)) in an effort to reduce the fluctuations associated with its tie to the US dollar.[^cite_note-59] The daily TWI valuation was changed in November 1976 to a periodically adjusted valuation.

The highest valuation of the Australian dollar relative to the U.S. dollar was during the period of the peg to the U.S. dollar. On 9 September 1973, the peg was adjusted to US\$1.4875, the fluctuation limits being changed to US\$1.485–US\$1.490;[^cite_note-60] on both 7 December 1973 and 10 December 1973, the noon buying rate in New York City for cable transfers payable in foreign currencies reached its highest point of 1.4885 U.S. dollars to one dollar.[^cite_note-61]

1983 [ABC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Broadcasting_Corporation) news report on the first day of trading with a floating dollar

In December 1983, the [Australian Labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Labor_Party) [government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Australia) led by [Prime Minister](24117.md) [Bob Hawke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Hawke) and [Treasurer](579169.md) [Paul Keating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Keating) ended the managed exchange rate scheme in favour of a [floating exchange rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_exchange_rate) set by supply and demand on international money markets. The decision was made on 8 December 1983 and announced on 9 December 1983.[^cite_note-62]

In the two decades that followed, its highest price relative to the US dollar was US\$0.881 in December 1988. The lowest ever price of the Australian dollar after it was floated was 47.75 US cents in April 2001.[^cite_note-63] It returned to above 96 US cents in June 2008,[^cite_note-64] and reached 98.49 later that year. Although the price of the Australian dollar fell significantly from this high towards the end of 2008, it gradually recovered in 2009 to 94 US cents.

On 15 October 2010, the Australian dollar reached parity with the US dollar for the first time since becoming a freely traded currency, trading above US\$1 for a few seconds.[^cite_note-65] The currency then traded above parity for a sustained period of several days in November, and fluctuated around that mark into 2011.[^cite_note-66] On 27 July 2011, the dollar hit a record high since floating, at \$1.1080 against the US dollar.[^cite_note-67] Some commentators speculated that its high value that year was related to [Europe's sovereign debt crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_debt_crisis), and Australia's strong ties with material importers in Asia and in particular [China](5405.md).[^cite_note-68]

Since the end of China's large-scale purchases of Australian commodities in 2013, however, the Australian dollar's price in US dollar terms fell to US\$0.88 as of end-2013, and to as low as US\$0.57 in March 2020. As of 2024, it has traded at a range of US\$0.63 to US\$0.68.

### Determinants of value

In 2025, the Australian dollar was the seventh most traded currency in world [foreign exchange markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_exchange_market), accounting for 6.1% of the world's daily share (down from 6.4% in 2022)[^cite_note-BIS_survey-8] behind the [United States dollar](18717338.md), the [euro](9472.md), the [Japanese yen](34392.md), the [pound sterling](270673.md), [Renminbi](66958.md), and [Swiss franc](361362.md).

The Australian dollar is popular with currency traders, because of the comparatively high interest rates in Australia, the relative freedom of the foreign exchange market from government intervention, the general stability of [Australia's economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Australia) and political system, and the prevailing view that the Australian dollar offers diversification benefits in a portfolio containing the major world currencies, especially because of its greater exposure to Asian economies and the commodities cycle.[^cite_note-SMH1-69]

Economists posit that commodity prices are the dominant driver of the Australian dollar, and this means changes in exchange rates of the Australian dollar occur in ways opposite to many other currencies.[^cite_note-70] For decades, Australia's balance of trade has depended primarily upon commodity exports such as minerals and agricultural products. This means the Australian dollar varies significantly during the business cycle, rallying during global booms as Australia exports raw materials, and falling during recessions as mineral prices slump or when domestic spending overshadows the export earnings outlook. This movement is in the opposite direction to other reserve currencies, which tend to be stronger during market slumps as traders move value from falling stocks into cash.

The Australian dollar is a [reserve currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_currency) and one of the most traded currencies in the world.[^cite_note-SMH1-69] Other factors in its popularity include a relative lack of central bank intervention, and general stability of the Australian economy and government.[^cite_note-71] In January 2011 at the [World Economic Forum](172185.md) in [Davos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davos), [Switzerland](26748.md), [Alexey Ulyukaev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexey_Ulyukaev) announced that the [Central Bank of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Bank_of_Russia) would begin keeping Australian dollar reserves.[^cite_note-72]

## Legal tender

### Within Australia

Australian notes are [legal tender](267793.md) throughout Australia by virtue of section 36(1) of the *Reserve Bank Act 1959* without an amount limit.[^cite_note-75] Section 16 of the *Currency Act 1965* similarly provides that Australian coins intended for general circulation are also legal tender, but only for the following amounts:[^cite_note-76]

- 1c and 2c coins (withdrawn from circulation from February 1992, but still legal tender): for payments not exceeding 20c

- 5c, 10c, 20c and 50c (of any combination): for payments not exceeding \$5

- \$1 coins: for payments not exceeding \$10

- \$2 coins: for payments not exceeding \$20

- Non-circulating \$10 coins: for payments not exceeding \$100

  [^cite_note-currency_act-1]

- Coins of other denominations: no lower limit

However, being legal tender does not necessarily oblige businesses to accept cash. The Reserve Bank states that businesses can set commercial terms for a transaction that requires the use of a non-cash payment.[^cite_note-rbalegaltender-77] However, a business may technically be required to accept cash if they are taken to court, but this is usually not a viable option for consumers.[^cite_note-78]

### Outside Australia

Australian notes and coins are also legal tender in the independent sovereign states of [Kiribati](16674.md), [Nauru](21302.md), and [Tuvalu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuvalu).[^cite_note-nauru-5][^cite_note-tuvalu-6][^cite_note-kiribati-7] Nauru never had its own currency. Tuvalu and Kiribati additionally had their respective [Tuvaluan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuvaluan_dollar) and [Kiribati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiribati_dollar) dollars at par with the Australian dollar. They are legal tender in their respective countries but not in Australia. However, both countries no longer produce coinage since the 1990s and have never produced their own banknotes. As a result, the Australian dollar is the dominant currency in both countries.

Tuvalu also issues non-circulating commemorative bullion coins produced by the [Perth Mint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perth_Mint).[^cite_note-79][^cite_note-80]
