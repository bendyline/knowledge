In [seismology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismology), **doublet earthquakes**—and more generally, **multiple earthquakes** or **twin earthquakes**—were originally identified as multiple [earthquakes](10106.md) with nearly identical [waveforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waveform) originating from the same [location](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicenter).[^cite_note-1] They are now characterized as distinct earthquake sequences having two (or more) main shocks with similar/slightly different magnitudes that occurred twice (or more) in a single moment, sometimes occurring within tens of seconds, but sometimes separated by years.[^cite_note-2] The similarity of magnitude—often within 0.4 magnitude—distinguishes multiplet events from [aftershocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aftershock),[^cite_note-3] which start at about 1.2 magnitude less than the parent shock ([Båth's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%A5th's_law)) and decrease in magnitude and frequency according to known laws.[^cite_note-4]

Doublet/multiplet events also have nearly identical seismic waveforms, as they come from the same [rupture zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_\(geology\)) and stress field, whereas aftershocks, being peripheral to the main rupture, typically reflect more diverse circumstances of origin.[^cite_note-5] Multiplet events overlap in their focal fields (rupture zones), which can be up to 100 kilometers across for magnitude 7.5 earthquakes.[^cite_note-6] Doublets have been distinguished from *triggered earthquakes*, where the energy of the seismic waves triggers a distant earthquake with a different rupture zone, although it has been suggested such a distinction reflects "imprecise taxonomy" more than any physical reality.[^cite_note-7]

Multiplet earthquakes are believed to result when *[asperities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asperity_\(materials_science\))*, such as large chunks of [crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crust_\(geology\)) stuck in the rupturing fault, or irregularities or bends in the fault, temporarily impede the main rupture. Unlike a normal earthquake, where it is believed the earthquake releases enough of the [tectonic stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics) driving it that it will take decades to centuries to accumulate enough stress to drive the next earthquake (per the [elastic rebound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastic_rebound) theory), the initial multiplet quake only releases part of the pent-up stress when the rupture hits the asperity. This increases the stress across the asperity, which may fail within seconds, minutes, months, or even years.[^cite_note-8] In the [1997 Harnai earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Harnai_earthquake), the initial M<sub>w</sub> 7.0 shock was followed by an M<sub>w</sub> 6.8 shock just 19 seconds later.[^cite_note-9] The effect of such powerful shocks so close in time was to double the duration of ground shaking (bringing more structures to the point of collapse), and to double the area affected by the strongest shaking.[^cite_note-10] When a subsequent, and possibly stronger, shock comes hours or days later it may suffice to collapse structures weakened by the previous shock, with serious consequences to rescue and recovery efforts.[^cite_note-11]

Although there have been numerous earthquakes with two or even three primary shocks of such similar magnitude that picking one as the main shock can be somewhat arbitrary, it was not until the 1970s and 1980s that studies of [seismograms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismogram) showed that some of these were not simply unusually large [foreshocks](1160429.md) and aftershocks.[^cite_note-12] Other studies have shown that about 20% of very large earthquakes (magnitude above 7.5) are doublets,[^cite_note-13] and that, in some cases, 37 to 75 percent of earthquakes are multiplets.[^cite_note-14] A theoretical study found about one earthquake in 15 (\~7%) to be a doublet (using a narrow criterion of "doublet"),[^cite_note-15] but also found that in the [Solomon Islands](265083.md) six of 57 M ≥ 6.0 earthquakes were doublets, and 4 of 15 M ≥ 7.0 earthquakes, showing that approximately 10% and 25% of those quakes were doublets.[^cite_note-16]

Doublet earthquakes pose a challenge to the [characteristic earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_earthquake) model used for estimating [seismic hazard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismic_hazard).[^cite_note-17] This model assumes that faults are segmented, limiting the extent of rupturing, and therefore the maximum size of an earthquake, to the length of the segment.[^cite_note-18] Newer forecasts of seismic hazard, such as [UCERF3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCERF3), factor in a greater likelihood of multisegment ruptures, which changes the relative frequency of different sizes of earthquakes.[^cite_note-19]
