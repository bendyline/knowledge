# Military grade

For the unrelated term in reference to ranks, see [Military rank](150237.md).



A [thirteenth generation Ford F-150](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_F-Series_\(thirteenth_generation\)) used by the [United States Marine Corps](17349325.md) to promote [recruitment](3135672.md). This generation of Ford F-Series was infamously marketed as using "military-grade [aluminum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminum)", despite no such thing existing.

"**Military grade**" (alternatively "**military-grade**" or "**mil-spec**") is a [buzzword](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buzzword) commonly used in [marketing](59252.md) and [advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advertising) for [consumer goods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_goods). It is generally used to describe an item or product that satisfies a [United States Military Standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Military_Standard),[^cite_note-\:0-1][^cite_note-\:1-2] usually [MIL-STD-810](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIL-STD-810) for [stress testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_testing);[^cite_note-3] however, it is often used as a [marketing ploy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketing_ploy) to describe a product that satisfies any military standard regardless of what it is (if a standard is satisfied at all to begin with), or one that simply uses materials that are also used in [military technology](90815.md) ranging from [Kevlar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevlar) to [aluminum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminum).[^cite_note-\:2-4][^cite_note-\:3-5][^cite_note-\:4-6]

In marketing, "military grade" is meant to symbolize and evoke higher-than-usual levels of toughness, durability, efficiency, and quality, as well as the implication that the product was tested and "approved" by some (non-existent) overseeing body or is regularly used and trusted by militaries—even if none of those are true.[^cite_note-\:0-1][^cite_note-\:1-2][^cite_note-\:2-4] Products often marketed as "military grade" include [phone cases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phone_case), [flashlights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flashlight), [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics), [eyewear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyewear), and [clothing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clothing).[^cite_note-\:0-1][^cite_note-\:1-2]

Use of the term in marketing has been criticized by [military personnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_personnel) and [veterans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veteran), who note that items that are indeed "military grade"—as in actually issued by militaries to their personnel—are often procured for [cost-effectiveness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost-effectiveness_analysis) and may not always be of the highest quality and reliability.[^cite_note-\:0-1][^cite_note-\:2-4][^cite_note-\:3-5][^cite_note-\:4-6] However, a product's use of the term should not be inferred as a sign it is unreliable, as some items using the term may in fact be using it genuinely (e.g. are in fact used by the military, or are identical to military equivalents).[^cite_note-\:0-1][^cite_note-\:1-2]

## Notable uses

- In 2015, advertisements for the [thirteenth generation Ford F-Series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_F-Series_\(thirteenth_generation\)) [pickup truck](47877.md) notably described it as using "high strength, military-grade [aluminum alloy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminum_alloy)", despite no such thing actually existing. The only real similarities between the aluminum used in the F-Series and some military vehicles are their composition and heat-treating.
