**Diplomatic recognition** in [international law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_international_law) is a [unilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unilateral) declarative political act of a [state](23604120.md) that acknowledges an act or status of another state or [government](12229.md) in control of a state (may be also a recognized state). Recognition can be accorded either on a *[de facto](43594.md)* or *[de jure](43594.md)* basis. Partial recognition can occur if many sovereign states refuse to recognize an entity as a peer. Recognition can be a declaration to that effect by the recognizing government or may be implied from an act of recognition, such as entering into a [treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty) with the other state or making a [state visit](327283.md). Recognition may, but might not, have domestic and international legal consequences. If sufficient countries recognize a particular entity as a state, that state may have a right to membership in [international organizations](18201224.md), while treaties may require all existing member countries unanimously agreeing to the admission of a new member.

A vote by a country in the [United Nations](31769.md) (UN) in favour of the [membership](31969.md) of another country is an implicit recognition of that country by the country so voting, as only states may be members of the UN. On the other hand, a negative vote for UN membership does not necessarily mean non-recognition of the applicant as a state, as other criteria, requirements or special circumstances may be considered relevant for UN membership. Similarly, a country may choose not to apply for UN membership for its own reasons, as is the case with [Vatican City](13393.md), and [Switzerland](26748.md) was not a member until 2002 because of its concerns to maintain its [neutrality policy](15380880.md).

The non-recognition of particular acts of a state does not normally affect the recognition of the state itself. For example, the international rejection of the [occupation](816214.md) of particular territory by a recognized state does not imply non-recognition of the state itself, nor a rejection of a change of government by illegal means.

## Recognition of states and governments

Diplomatic recognition must be distinguished from formal recognition of states or their governments.[^cite_note-2] The fact that states do not maintain bilateral diplomatic relations does not mean that they do not recognize or treat one another as states. A state is not required to accord formal bilateral recognition to any other state, and some have a general policy of not doing so, considering that a vote for its membership of an international organisation restricted to states, such as the [United Nations](31769.md), is an act of recognition.

### History

Some consider that a state has a responsibility not to recognize as a state any entity that has attained the qualifications for statehood by a violation of basic principles of the [UN Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UN_Charter): the [UN Security Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UN_Security_Council) has in several instances ([Resolution 216](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council_Resolution_216) (1965) and [Resolution 217](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council_Resolution_217) (1965), concerning [Rhodesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodesia); [Resolution 541](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council_Resolution_541) (1983), concerning [Northern Cyprus](271409.md); and [Resolution 787](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council_Resolution_787) (1992), concerning the [Republika Srpska](347422.md)) issued Chapter VII resolutions (binding in international law) that denied their statehood and precluded recognition. In the 2010 [International Court of Justice advisory opinion on Kosovo's declaration of independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Court_of_Justice_advisory_opinion_on_Kosovo's_declaration_of_independence), the [ICJ](14918.md) ruled that "general international law contains no applicable prohibition of declarations of independence."[^cite_note-3] The Court carefully noted "that in all of those instances the Security Council was making a determination as regards the concrete situation existing at the time that those declarations of independence were made; the illegality attached to the declarations of independence thus stemmed not from the unilateral character of these declarations as such, but from the fact that they were, or would have been, connected with the unlawful [use of force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Use_of_force_in_international_law) or other egregious violations of norms of general international law, in particular, those of a peremptory character (jus cogens). In the context of Kosovo, the Security Council has never taken this position. The exceptional character of the resolutions enumerated above appears to the Court to confirm that no general prohibition against unilateral declarations of independence may be inferred from the practice of the Security Council."[^cite_note-4]

States can exercise their recognition powers either explicitly or implicitly.[^cite_note-5] The recognition of a government implies recognition of the state it governs, but even countries which have a policy of formally recognising states may not have a policy of doing the same regarding governments.

*De facto* recognition of states, rather than *de jure*, is rare. *De jure* recognition is stronger, while *de facto* recognition is more tentative and recognizes only that a government exercises control over a territory. An example of the difference is when the [United Kingdom](31717.md) recognized the [Soviet state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Soviet_Russia_and_the_Soviet_Union_\(1917%E2%80%9327\)) *de facto* in 1921, but *de jure* only in 1924. Another example is the state of [Israel](9282173.md) in 1948, whose government was immediately recognized *de facto* by the [United States](3434750.md) and three days later *de jure* by the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union). Another example is the [Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic) [of Indonesia](14579.md) which was whose government was recognized *de facto* by the [Netherlands](21148.md) in 1946 and *de jure* by the international community in 1949. Also, the [Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_China), commonly known as "[Taiwan](25734.md)", is generally recognized as *de facto* independent and sovereign, but is not universally recognized as *de jure* independent due to the complex [political status of Taiwan](66555.md) related to the United Nations' withdrawal of recognition in favor of the [People's Republic of China](5405.md) in 1971.

Renewing recognition of a government is not necessary when it changes in a normal, constitutional way (such as an [election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election) or [referendum](51582.md)), but may be necessary in the case of a [coup d'etat](25914154.md) or [revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution). Recognition of a new government by other states can be important for its long-term survival. For instance, the [Taliban](30635.md) government of the [Islamic State of Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_State_of_Afghanistan), which lasted from 1996 to 2001, was recognized only by [Pakistan](23235.md), the [United Arab Emirates](69328.md), and [Saudi Arabia](349303.md), while far more had recognized the government of ousted President [Burhanuddin Rabbani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burhanuddin_Rabbani). India's administration of [Jammu and Kashmir](61449812.md), part of a [disputed territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disputed_territory), is not recognized by either [Pakistan](23235.md) or the [People's Republic of China](5405.md), and the [Republic of Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Turkey).

Recognition can be implied by other acts, such as a visit of the [head of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_of_state), or the signing of a bilateral treaty. If implicit recognition is possible, a state may feel the need to explicitly proclaim that its acts do not constitute diplomatic recognition, like when the United States commenced its dialogue with the [Palestine Liberation Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine_Liberation_Organization) in 1988.

Formal diplomatic recognition can be used as a tool of political influence with examples including [European Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Community)'s conditional recognition of independence of former republics of [SFR Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SFR_Yugoslavia) in early 1990s dependent on new states commitment to protection of [human](13831.md) and [national minorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_minorities) rights.[^cite_note-Caplan-6]

## Derecognition of states

See also: [Non-recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-recognition)



Recognition of Kosovo by UN member states from 2008 to 2020 (total members 193)

States may withdraw diplomatic recognition of another state. States that experience derecognition are typically those excluded from the [United Nations System](216034.md), such as [Taiwan](25734.md), the [Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic](38762388.md), [Kosovo](17391.md), [Abkhazia](18933375.md), and [South Ossetia](318381.md).[^cite_note-Visoka-7] Derecognition is achieved through [checkbook diplomacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Checkbook_diplomacy), diplomatic pressure, or military threats.[^cite_note-Visoka-7] Smaller states have engaged in "rental recognition" by bartering their (de)recognition of states in exchange for aid.[^cite_note-de_Oliveira-8]

There is no clear consensus on the legality of derecognition.[^cite_note-Visoka-7][^cite_note-de_Oliveira-8][^cite_note-Visoka2-9] While certain academics argue that recognition of statehood is irrevocable under international law, others contend that this position is "outdated" and challenged by current state practices.[^cite_note-Visoka-7] There is a broader consensus that derecognition is permitted when (a) a state was created through the [use of force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Use_of_force_in_international_law) (e.g., Abkhazia and South Ossetia), or (b) a state ceases to exist (e.g., the United States' derecognition of [Armenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Republic_of_Armenia) in 1920).[^cite_note-Visoka2-9][^cite_note-10]

The doctrine of non-recognition of illegal or immoral situations, becoming more important especially in ensuring compliance with [international law](8195726.md) since the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War) with resolution (375) passed by the [UN General Assembly](31957.md), with institutionalization stemming from the 1969 [Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_Convention_on_the_Law_of_Treaties) in regard to "\[a] treaty\['s] conclusion \[that] has been procured by the threat or use of force in violation of the principles of international law embodied in the Charter of the United Nations" in Article 52 of the UN Charter, wherein the meaning of "force" "was unanimously agreed to include military force",[^cite_note-11] is called the [Stimson Doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stimson_Doctrine), finding application, for instance, in the case of [Rhodesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodesia) in 1965. Withdrawal of recognition of a government is a more severe act of disapproval than the breaking of [diplomatic relations](28165911.md).

## Recognition of governments

Besides recognizing other states, states also can recognize the governments of states. This can be problematic particularly when a new government comes to power by illegal means, such as a [coup d'état](25914154.md), or when an existing government stays in power by fixing an election. States once formally recognized both the government of a state and the state itself, but many no longer follow that practice,[^cite_note-12] even though, if diplomatic relations are to be maintained, it is necessary that there be a government with which to engage in diplomatic relations.[^cite_note-13] Countries such as the [United States](3434750.md) answer queries over the recognition of governments with the statement: "The question of recognition does not arise: we are conducting our relations with the new government."[^cite_note-14]

Several of the world's [geopolitical](163225.md) entities lack general international recognition, but wish to be recognized as [sovereign states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign_states). The degree of *de facto* control these entities exert over the territories they claim varies.

Most are subnational [regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subregion) with an [ethnic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic) or [national identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_identity) of their own that have [separated](286527.md) from the original parent [state](23604120.md). Such states are commonly referred to as "break-away" states. Some of these entities are in effect internally [self-governing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_entity) [protectorates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protectorate) that enjoy military protection and informal diplomatic representation abroad through another state to prevent forced reincorporation into their original states.

The word "control" in this list refers to *control* over the area *occupied*, not *occupation* of the area *claimed*. Unrecognized countries may have either full control over their occupied territory (such as [Northern Cyprus](271409.md)), or only partial control (such as [Western Sahara](33220.md)). In the former, the *de jure* governments have little or no influence in the areas they claim to rule, whereas in the latter they have varying degrees of control, and may provide essential services to people living in the areas.

## Other types of recognition

Further information: [Belligerency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belligerent)

Other elements that may be recognized include [occupation](816214.md) or [annexation](18950961.md) of territory, or [belligerent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belligerent) rights of a party in a conflict. Recognition of the latter does not imply recognition of a state.

Formal recognition of [belligerency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belligerent), which is rare today, signifies that the parties to the civil war or other internal conflict "are entitled to excise belligerent rights, thus accepting that the rebel group possesses sufficient [international personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_personality) to support the position of such rights and duties."[^cite_note-Solis-15] Extension of the rights of belligerency is usually done by other states, rather than by the government fighting the rebel group.[^cite_note-Solis-15] (A 1907 report by [William E. Fuller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_E._Fuller) for the [Spanish Treaty Claims Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Treaty_Claims_Commission) noted that "A parent state never formally recognizes the insurgents as belligerents, although it may in fact treat them as such by carrying on war against them in accordance with the rules and usages of international warfare."[^cite_note-16])

Examples of recognition of [belligerent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belligerent) status include:

- In 1823, the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland) recognized the Greek revolutionaries against the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md) as belligerents during the [Greek War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_War_of_Independence).

  [^cite_note-17]

- The United Kingdom issued a [proclamation of neutrality](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/British%20proclamation%20of%20neutrality%20in%20the%20American%20Civil%20War) soon after the outbreak of the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War), which "tacitly granted the [Confederacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederate_States_of_America) belligerent status, the right to contract loans and purchase supplies in neutral nations and to exercise belligerent rights on the [high seas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_seas)."

  [^cite_note-OfficeHistorian-18]

  &#x20;Another right of significance accorded to belligerents that was seen as potentially significant at the time was the right to issue [letters of marque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_of_marque).

  [^cite_note-19]

  &#x20;The British extension of belligerent recognition to the Confederacy greatly angered and concerned the United States, which strenuously and successfully worked to prevent full diplomatic recognition.

  [^cite_note-OfficeHistorian-18]

- During the [Nicaraguan Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicaraguan_Revolution), the [Andean Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andean_Group) ([Bolivia](3462.md), [Colombia](5222.md), [Ecuador](9334.md), [Peru](170691.md), and [Venezuela](32374.md)) "declared that 'a state of belligerency' existed in [Nicaragua](21362.md) and that the forces of the [Sandinista National Liberation Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandinista_National_Liberation_Front) (FSLN) represented a 'legitimate army.'"

  [^cite_note-VonGlahn-20]

  &#x20;The declaration, made over the strong U.S. opposition, stated that the Sandinistas were eligible for "treatment and prerogatives" accorded to belligerents under international law.

  [^cite_note-21]

  &#x20;This declaration allowed the Andean countries to provide arms to the FSLN.

  [^cite_note-VonGlahn-20]

- During the [Salvadoran Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvadoran_Civil_War), [France](5843419.md) and [Mexico](3966054.md) recognized the [Farabundo Martí National Liberation Front](327525.md) in [El Salvador](9356.md) as a belligerent in August 1981.

  [^cite_note-22]
