**Sharks** are a group of [elasmobranch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasmobranch) [cartilaginous fishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartilaginous_fish) characterized by a [ribless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rib) [endoskeleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endoskeleton), [dermal denticles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dermal_denticle), five to seven [gill slits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gill_slit) on each side, and [pectoral fins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pectoral_fin) that are not fused to the [head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head). Modern sharks are classified within the [division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_\(taxonomy\)) **Selachii**[^cite_note-1] and are the [sister group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sister_group) to the [Batomorphi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batoidea) ([rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batoidea) and [skates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skate_\(fish\))). Some sources extend the term "shark" as an informal category including [extinct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinction) members of [Chondrichthyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chondrichthyes) (cartilaginous fish) with a shark-like morphology, such as [hybodonts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybodonts). Shark-like chondrichthyans such as *[Cladoselache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cladoselache)* and *[Doliodus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doliodus)* first appeared in the [Devonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devonian) Period (419–359 million years), though some fossilized chondrichthyan-like scales are as old as the [Late Ordovician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordovician) (458–444 million years ago).[^cite_note-2] The earliest confirmed modern sharks (Selachii) are known from the [Early Jurassic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Jurassic) around [200](https://geoltime.github.io/?Ma=200) million years ago, with the oldest known member being *[Agaleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agaleus)*, though records of true sharks may extend back as far as the [Permian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permian).[^cite_note-3]

Sharks range in size from the small [dwarf lanternshark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_lanternshark) (*Etmopterus perryi*), a deep sea species that is only 17 centimetres (6.7 in) in length, to the [whale shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whale_shark) (*Rhincodon typus*), the largest fish in the world, which reaches approximately 12 metres (40 ft) to 18.8 metres (62 ft) in length.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5] They are found in all seas and are common to depths up to 2,000 metres (6,600 ft). They generally do not live in freshwater, although there are a few known exceptions, such as the [bull shark](277607.md) and the [river sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_shark), which can be found in both seawater and freshwater, and the [Ganges shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganges_shark), which lives only in freshwater.[^cite_note-6] Sharks have a covering of [placoid scales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placoid_scale) (denticles) that protects the skin from damage and [parasites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasite) in addition to improving their [fluid dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_dynamics). They have [numerous sets of replaceable teeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyphyodont).[^cite_note-Budker-7]

Several shark species are [apex predators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apex_predator), which are organisms that are at the top of their [food chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_chain) with select examples including the [bull shark](277607.md), [tiger shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiger_shark), [great white shark](43619.md), [mako sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isurus), [thresher sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thresher_shark), [greenland sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland_shark) and [hammerhead sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammerhead_shark). Some sharks are [filter-feeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filter-feeding) [planktivores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planktivore), such as the [whale shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whale_shark) and [basking shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basking_shark), which are among the [largest fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_fish) that ever lived.

Sharks are caught by humans for [shark meat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_meat) or [shark fins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_fin_soup). Many shark populations are threatened by human activities. Since 1970, shark populations have been reduced by 71%, mostly from [overfishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overfishing)[^cite_note-Einhorn-8] and mutilating practice such as [shark finning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_finning).[^cite_note-livescience-9][^cite_note-oceansi-10]

## Etymology

Until the 16th century,[^cite_note-11] sharks were known to mariners as "sea dogs".[^cite_note-12] This is still evidential in several species termed "[dogfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squalidae)", or the [porbeagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porbeagle).

The etymology of the word *shark* is uncertain. The most likely etymology states that the original sense of the word was that of "predator, one who preys on others" from the [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language) *schurk*, meaning 'villain, scoundrel' ([cf.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cf.) *[card shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Card_shark)*, *[loan shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loan_shark)*, etc.), which was later applied to the fish due to its predatory behaviour.[^cite_note-13] It has been argued to derive from the [Yucatec Maya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yucatec_Maya_language) word *xoc*/*xooc* (pronounced [\[ʃoːk\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Mayan)), meaning 'shark'.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15]

The oldest [total-group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_group) chondrichthyans, known as [acanthodians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acanthodii) or "spiny sharks", appeared during the Early [Silurian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silurian), around 439 million years ago.*[^cite_note-Andreev-2022-16]* The oldest confirmed members of [Elasmobranchii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasmobranchii) *sensu lato* (the group containing all cartilaginous fish more closely related to modern sharks and rays than to [chimaeras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chimaera)) appeared during the [Devonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devonian).[^cite_note-17] [Anachronistidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anachronistidae), the oldest probable representatives of Euselachii, the group containing modern sharks (Selachii) and rays (Batomorphi) to the exclusion of most extinct elasmobranch groups, date to the [Carboniferous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboniferous).[^cite_note-18] Selachii and Batomorphi are suggested by some to have diverged during the [Triassic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triassic).[^cite_note-Pough-2018-19] Fossils of the earliest true sharks may have appeared during the [Permian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permian), based on remains of "[synechodontiforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synechodontiformes)" found in the Early Permian of Russia,[^cite_note-20] but if remains of "synechodontiforms" from the Permian and Triassic are true sharks, they only had low diversity. Modern shark [orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_\(biology\)) first appeared during the Early Jurassic, and during the Jurassic true sharks underwent great diversification.[^cite_note-21] Sharks largely replaced the [hybodonts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybodontiformes), which had previously been a dominant group of shark-like fish during the Triassic and Early Jurassic.[^cite_note-Rees,_J._A._N._2008,_p._117–147-22]

Sharks belong to the division Selachii in the subclass [Elasmobranchii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasmobranchii) in the [class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_\(biology\)) [Chondrichthyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chondrichthyes). The Elasmobranchii also include [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batoidea) and [skates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skate_\(fish\)); the Chondrichthyes also include [Chimaeras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chimaera). It was thought that the sharks form a [polyphyletic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyphyly) group: some sharks are more closely related to rays than they are to some other sharks,[^cite_note-Taxonomy-24] but current molecular studies support monophyly of both groups of sharks and batoids.[^cite_note-MolBiol2014Jan-25][^cite_note-MitoDNA2017Sep-26]

The division Selachii is divided into the superorders [Galeomorphi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galeomorphi) (or Galea), and [Squalomorphi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squalomorphi) (or Squalea). The galeomorphs are the [Heterodontiformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullhead_shark), [Orectolobiformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpet_shark), [Lamniformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamniformes), and [Carcharhiniformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcharhiniformes). Lamnoids and carcharhinoids are usually placed in one [clade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clade), but recent studies show that Lamnoids and orectoloboids are a clade. Some scientists now think that Heterodontoids may be squalomorphs. The squalomorphs are divided into [Hexanchiformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexanchiformes) and Squalomorpha. The former includes [cow shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cow_shark) and [frilled shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frilled_shark), though some authors propose that both families be moved to separate orders. The Squalomorpha contains the [Squaliformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squaliformes) and the Hypnosqualea. The Hypnosqualea may be invalid. It includes the [Squatiniformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel_shark), and the Pristorajea, which may also be invalid, but includes the [Pristiophoriformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sawshark) and the [Batomorphi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batoidea).[^cite_note-Taxonomy-24][^cite_note-CompagnoFAO-27]

There are more than 500 species of sharks split across thirteen orders, including several orders of sharks that have gone extinct:[^cite_note-CompagnoFAO-27][^cite_note-WoRMS-28]

- [Carcharhiniformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcharhiniformes): Commonly known as [ground sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcharhiniformes), the order includes the [blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_shark), [tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiger_shark), [bull](277607.md), [grey reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grey_reef_shark), [blacktip reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blacktip_reef_shark), [Caribbean reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caribbean_reef_shark), [blacktail reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blacktail_reef_shark), [whitetip reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitetip_reef_shark), and [oceanic whitetip sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_whitetip_shark) (collectively called the [requiem sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Requiem_shark)) along with the [houndsharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houndshark), [catsharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scyliorhinidae), and [hammerhead sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammerhead_shark). They are distinguished by an elongated snout and a [nictitating membrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nictitating_membrane) which protects the eyes during an attack.

- [Heterodontiformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullhead_shark): They are generally referred to as the [bullhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullhead_shark) or [horn sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_shark).

- [Hexanchiformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexanchiformes): Examples from this group include the [cow sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cow_shark) and [frilled sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frilled_shark), which somewhat resembles a marine snake.

- [Lamniformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamniformes): They are commonly known as the [mackerel sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamniformes). They include the [goblin shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goblin_shark), [basking shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basking_shark), [megamouth shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megamouth_shark), the [thresher sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thresher_shark), [shortfin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortfin_mako_shark) and [longfin mako sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longfin_mako_shark), and [great white shark](43619.md). They are distinguished by their large jaws and [ovoviviparous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovoviviparity) reproduction. The Lamniformes also include the extinct giant [megalodon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megalodon), (*Otodus megalodon)*.

- [Orectolobiformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpet_shark): They are commonly referred to as the [carpet sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpet_shark), including [zebra sharks](466928.md), [nurse sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nurse_shark), [wobbegongs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wobbegong), and the [whale shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whale_shark).

- [Pristiophoriformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sawshark): These are the [sawsharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sawshark), with an elongated, toothed snout that they use for slashing their prey.

- [Squaliformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squaliformes): This group includes the [dogfish sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squalidae) and [roughsharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squalidae).

- [Squatiniformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel_shark): Also known as [angel sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel_shark), they are flattened sharks with a strong resemblance to [stingrays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stingrays) and [skates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skate_\(fish\)).

- [Echinorhiniformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echinorhinus): This group includes the [prickly shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prickly_shark) and [bramble shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bramble_shark). Phylogenetic placement of this group has been ambiguous in scientific studies.

## Anatomy

Main article: [Shark anatomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_anatomy)

Drawing of a shark labeling major anatomical features, including mouth, snout, nostril, eye, spiracle, dorsal fin spine, caudal keel, clasper, labial furrows, gill openings, precaudal pit and fins: first and second dorsal, anal, pectoral, caudal and pelvic

General anatomical features of sharks

### Teeth

Main article: [Shark tooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_tooth)

The serrated teeth of a tiger shark, used for sawing through flesh

The teeth of [tiger sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiger_shark) are oblique and serrated to saw through flesh

Shark teeth are embedded in the [gums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gingiva) rather than directly affixed to the jaw, and are constantly replaced throughout life. Multiple rows of replacement teeth grow in a groove on the inside of the jaw and steadily move forward like a [conveyor belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conveyor_belt); some sharks lose 30,000 or more teeth in their lifetime. The rate of tooth replacement varies from once every 8 to 10 days to several months. In most species, teeth are replaced one at a time as opposed to the simultaneous replacement of an entire row, which is observed in the [cookiecutter shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cookiecutter_shark).[^cite_note-30]

Tooth shape depends on the shark's diet: those that feed on [mollusks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mollusk) and [crustaceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crustacean) have dense and flattened teeth used for crushing, those that feed on fish have needle-like teeth for gripping, and those that feed on larger prey such as [mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammal) have pointed lower teeth for gripping and triangular upper teeth with [serrated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serration) edges for cutting. The teeth of plankton-feeders such as the basking shark are small and non-functional.[^cite_note-Gilbertson-31]

### Skeleton

Shark skeletons are very different from those of [bony fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osteichthyes) and [terrestrial vertebrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrapod). Sharks and other [cartilaginous fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chondrichthyes) ([skates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skate_\(fish\)) and [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batoidea)) have skeletons made of [cartilage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartilage) and [connective tissue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connective_tissue). Cartilage is flexible and durable, yet is about half the normal density of bone. This reduces the skeleton's weight, saving energy.[^cite_note-skeletoncorset-32] Because sharks do not have rib cages, they can easily be crushed under their own weight on land.[^cite_note-langoni-33]

### Jaw

The [jaws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaw) of sharks, like those of rays and skates, are not attached to the [cranium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cranium). The jaw's surface (in comparison to the shark's vertebrae and gill arches) needs extra support due to its heavy exposure to physical stress and its need for strength. It has a layer of tiny hexagonal plates called "tesserae", which are crystal blocks of calcium salts arranged as a mosaic.[^cite_note-34] This gives these areas much of the same strength found in the bony tissue found in other animals.

Generally sharks have only one layer of tesserae, but the jaws of large specimens, such as the bull shark, tiger shark, and the great white shark, have two to three layers or more, depending on body size. The jaws of a large great white shark may have up to five layers.[^cite_note-skeletoncorset-32] In the [rostrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rostrum_\(anatomy\)) (snout), the cartilage can be spongy and flexible to absorb the power of impacts.[^cite_note-35]

### Fins

Fin skeletons are elongated and supported with soft and unsegmented rays named ceratotrichia, filaments of elastic protein resembling the horny keratin in hair and feathers.[^cite_note-36] Most sharks have eight fins. Sharks can only drift away from objects directly in front of them because their fins do not allow them to move in the tail-first direction.[^cite_note-langoni-33]

### Dermal denticles

Further information: [Fish scale § Placoid scales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_scale#Placoid_scales)

The dermal denticles of a lemon shark

The dermal denticles of a [lemon shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemon_shark), viewed through a [scanning electron microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scanning_electron_microscope)&#x20;

Unlike bony fish, sharks have a complex dermal corset made of flexible collagenous fibers and arranged as a helical network surrounding their body. This works as an outer skeleton, providing attachment for their swimming muscles and thus saving energy.[^cite_note-Cartilagious-37] Their dermal teeth give them [hydrodynamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodynamic) advantages as they reduce turbulence when swimming.[^cite_note-SkinTeeth-38] Some species of shark have pigmented denticles that form complex patterns like spots (e.g. [Zebra shark](466928.md)) and stripes (e.g. [Tiger shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiger_shark)). These markings are important for [camouflage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camouflage) and help sharks blend in with their environment, as well as making them difficult for prey to detect.[^cite_note-Camouflage-39] For some species, dermal patterning returns to healed denticles even after they have been removed by injury.[^cite_note-40]

### Tails

[Tails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tail) provide thrust, making speed and acceleration dependent on tail shape. [Caudal fin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caudal_fin) shapes vary considerably between shark species, due to their evolution in separate environments. Sharks possess a [heterocercal](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/heterocercal) caudal fin in which the [dorsal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorsum_\(biology\)) portion is usually noticeably larger than the [ventral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ventral) portion. This is because the shark's [vertebral column](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertebral_column) extends into that dorsal portion, providing a greater surface area for muscle attachment. This allows more efficient [locomotion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_locomotion) among these negatively buoyant cartilaginous fish. By contrast, most bony fish possess a [homocercal](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/homocercal) caudal fin.[^cite_note-41]

Tiger sharks have a large upper [lobe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobe_\(anatomy\)), which allows for slow cruising and sudden bursts of speed. The tiger shark must be able to twist and turn in the water easily when hunting to support its varied diet, whereas the [porbeagle shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porbeagle), which hunts schooling fish such as [mackerel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mackerel) and [herring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herring), has a large lower lobe to help it keep pace with its fast-swimming prey.[^cite_note-Nelson-42] Other tail adaptations help sharks catch prey more directly, such as the thresher shark's usage of its powerful, elongated upper lobe to stun fish and squid.

## Physiology

### Buoyancy

Unlike bony fish, sharks do not have gas-filled swim bladders for buoyancy. Instead, sharks rely on a large liver filled with oil that contains [squalene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squalene), and their cartilage, which is about half the normal density of bone.[^cite_note-Cartilagious-37] Their liver constitutes up to 30% of their total body mass.[^cite_note-Collins-43] The liver's effectiveness is limited, so sharks employ [dynamic lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_lift_\(fish\)) to maintain depth while swimming. [Sand tiger sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sand_tiger_shark) store air in their stomachs, using it as a form of swim bladder. Bottom-dwelling sharks, like the [nurse shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nurse_shark), have negative buoyancy, allowing them to rest on the ocean floor.

Some sharks, if inverted or stroked on the nose, enter a natural state of [tonic immobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonic_immobility). Researchers use this condition to handle sharks safely.[^cite_note-ParttGruberTaniuchi-44]

### Respiration

Like other fish, sharks extract oxygen from seawater as it passes over their [gills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gill). Unlike other fish, shark gill slits are not covered, but lie in a row behind the head. A modified slit called a [spiracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiracle_\(vertebrates\)) lies just behind the eye, which assists the shark with taking in water during [respiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquatic_respiration) and plays a major role in bottom–dwelling sharks. Spiracles are reduced or missing in active [pelagic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelagic) sharks.[^cite_note-Gilbertson-31] While the shark is moving, water passes through the mouth and over the gills in a process known as "ram ventilation". While at rest, most sharks pump water over their gills to ensure a constant supply of oxygenated water. A small number of species have lost the ability to pump water through their gills and must swim without rest. These species are *obligate ram ventilators* and would presumably [asphyxiate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asphyxiate) if unable to move. Obligate ram ventilation is also true of some pelagic bony fish species.[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46]

The [respiratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Respiratory) and [circulatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulatory) process begins when deoxygenated [venous blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venous_blood) travels to the shark's two-chambered [heart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart). Here, the shark pumps blood to its gills via the ventral [aorta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aorta) where it branches into [afferent](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/afferent#Adjective) [branchial arteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branchial_arteries). [Gas exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_exchange) takes place in the gills and the reoxygenated blood flows into the [efferent](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/efferent#Adjective) branchial arteries, which come together to form the [dorsal aorta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorsal_aorta). The blood flows from the dorsal aorta throughout the body. The deoxygenated blood from the body then flows through the [posterior cardinal veins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posterior_cardinal_vein) and enters the posterior cardinal [sinuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinus_\(anatomy\)). From there venous blood re-enters the heart [ventricle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ventricle_\(heart\)) and the cycle repeats.[^cite_note-47]

### Thermoregulation

Most sharks are "cold-blooded" or, more precisely, [poikilothermic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poikilotherm), meaning that their internal [body temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermoregulation) matches that of their ambient environment. Members of the family [Lamnidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamnidae) (such as the [shortfin mako shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortfin_mako_shark) and the [great white shark](43619.md)) are [homeothermic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeothermy) and maintain a higher body temperature than the surrounding water. In these sharks, a strip of [aerobic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerobic_metabolism) red muscle located near the center of the body generates the heat, which the body retains via a [countercurrent exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countercurrent_exchange) mechanism by a system of [blood vessels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_vessel) called the [rete mirabile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rete_mirabile) ("miraculous net"). The [common thresher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_thresher) and [bigeye thresher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bigeye_thresher) sharks have a similar mechanism for maintaining an elevated body temperature.[^cite_note-48]

Larger species, like the whale shark, are able to conserve their body heat through sheer size when they dive to colder depths. The [scalloped hammerhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalloped_hammerhead) closes its mouth and gills when diving to depths of around 800 metres, holding its breath until it reaches warmer waters again.[^cite_note-49]

### Osmoregulation

In contrast to bony fish, with the exception of the [coelacanth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coelacanth),[^cite_note-50] the blood and other tissue of sharks and [Chondrichthyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chondrichthyes) is generally [isotonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotonicity) to their marine environments because of the high concentration of [urea](31734.md) (up to 2.5%[^cite_note-51]) and [trimethylamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trimethylamine) N-oxide (TMAO), allowing them to be in [osmotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osmotic) balance with the seawater. This adaptation prevents most sharks from surviving in freshwater, and they are therefore confined to [marine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_\(ocean\)) environments. A few exceptions exist, such as the [bull shark](277607.md), which has developed a way to change its [kidney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidney) function to excrete large amounts of urea.[^cite_note-Collins-43] When a shark dies, the urea is broken down to ammonia by bacteria, causing the dead body to gradually smell strongly of ammonia.[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53]

Research in 1930 by [Homer W. Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer_W._Smith) showed that sharks' urine does not contain sufficient sodium to avoid [hypernatremia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypernatremia), and it was postulated that there must be an additional mechanism for salt secretion. In 1960 it was discovered at the [Mount Desert Island Biological Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Desert_Island_Biological_Laboratory) in [Salsbury Cove, Maine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salsbury_Cove,_Maine) that sharks have a type of [salt gland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_gland) located at the end of the intestine, known as the "rectal gland", whose function is the secretion of chlorides.[^cite_note-54]

### Digestion

Digestion can take a long time. The food moves from the mouth to a J-shaped stomach, where it is stored and initial digestion occurs.[^cite_note-Digestion-55] Unwanted items may never get past the stomach, and instead the shark either vomits or turns its stomachs inside out and ejects unwanted items from its mouth.[^cite_note-56]

One of the biggest differences between the digestive systems of sharks and mammals is that sharks have much shorter intestines. This short length is achieved by the [spiral valve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiral_valve) with multiple turns within a single short section instead of a long tube-like intestine. The valve provides a long surface area, requiring food to circulate inside the short gut until fully digested, when remaining waste products pass into the [cloaca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloaca).[^cite_note-Digestion-55]

### Fluorescence

A few sharks appear [fluorescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorescent) under blue light, such as the [swell shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swell_shark) and the [chain catshark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_catshark), where the [fluorophore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorophore) derives from a [metabolite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolite) of [kynurenic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kynurenic_acid).[^cite_note-57]

## Senses

### Smell

Eyelevel photo of hammerhead from the front

The shape of the [hammerhead shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammerhead_shark)'s head may enhance olfaction by spacing the nostrils further apart.

Sharks have keen [olfactory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olfactory) senses, located in the short duct (which is not fused, unlike bony fish) between the anterior and posterior nasal openings, with some species able to detect as little as one [part per million](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts_per_million) of blood in seawater.[^cite_note-58] The size of the olfactory bulb varies across different shark species, with size dependent on how much a given species relies on smell or vision to find their prey.[^cite_note-Yopak-2015-59] In environments with low visibility, shark species generally have larger olfactory bulbs.[^cite_note-Yopak-2015-59] In reefs, where visibility is high, species of sharks from the family [Carcharhinidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Requiem_shark) have smaller olfactory bulbs.[^cite_note-Yopak-2015-59] Sharks found in deeper waters also have larger olfactory bulbs.[^cite_note-60]

Sharks have the ability to determine the direction of a given scent based on the timing of scent detection in each nostril.[^cite_note-61] This is similar to the method mammals use to determine direction of sound.

They are more attracted to the chemicals found in the intestines of many species, and as a result often linger near or in [sewage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewage) outfalls. Some species, such as [nurse sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nurse_shark), have external [barbels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbel_\(anatomy\)) that greatly increase their ability to sense prey.

### Sight



Eye of a [bigeyed sixgill shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bigeyed_sixgill_shark) (*Hexanchus nakamurai*)

Shark [eyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eye) are similar to the eyes of other [vertebrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertebrates), including similar [lenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_\(anatomy\)), [corneas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornea) and [retinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retina), though their eyesight is well adapted to the [marine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean) environment with the help of a tissue called [tapetum lucidum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapetum_lucidum). This tissue is behind the [retina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retina) and reflects light back to it, thereby increasing visibility in the dark waters. The effectiveness of the tissue varies, with some sharks having stronger [nocturnal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nocturnal) adaptations. Many sharks can contract and dilate their [pupils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pupil), like humans, something no [teleost fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teleost_fish) can do. Sharks have eyelids, but they do not blink because the surrounding water cleans their eyes. To protect their eyes some species have [nictitating membranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nictitating_membrane). This membrane covers the eyes while hunting and when the shark is being attacked. However, some species, including the [great white shark](43619.md) (*Carcharodon carcharias*), do not have this membrane, but instead roll their eyes backwards to protect them when striking prey. The importance of sight in shark hunting behavior is debated. Some believe that [electro-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroreception) and [chemoreception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemoreception) are more significant, while others point to the nictating membrane as evidence that sight is important, since presumably the shark would not protect its eyes were they unimportant. The use of sight probably varies with species and water conditions. The shark's field of vision can swap between [monocular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monocular_vision) and [stereoscopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereopsis) at any time.[^cite_note-62] A [micro-spectrophotometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrophotometry) study of 17 species of sharks found 10 had only [rod photoreceptors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rod_cell) and no cone cells in their [retinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retina) giving them good night vision while making them [colorblind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorblind). The remaining seven species had in addition to rods a single type of [cone photoreceptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_cell) sensitive to green and, seeing only in shades of grey and green, are believed to be effectively colorblind. The study indicates that an object's contrast against the background, rather than colour, may be more important for object detection.[^cite_note-63] [^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65]

### Hearing

Although it is hard to test the hearing of sharks, they may have a sharp [sense of hearing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearing_\(sense\)) and can possibly hear prey from many miles away.[^cite_note-66] The hearing sensitivity for most shark species lies between 20 and 1000 Hz.[^cite_note-Casper_2006-67] A small opening on each side of their heads (not the spiracle) leads directly into the [inner ear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_ear) through a thin channel. The [lateral line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateral_line) shows a similar arrangement, and is open to the environment via a series of openings called lateral line [pores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canal_pore). This is a reminder of the common origin of these two vibration- and sound-detecting organs that are grouped together as the acoustico-lateralis system. In bony fish and [tetrapods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrapod) the external opening into the inner ear has been lost.

### Electroreception

Main article: [Electroreception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroreception)

Drawing of shark head.

Electromagnetic field receptors (ampullae of Lorenzini) and motion detecting canals in the head of a shark

The [ampullae of Lorenzini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampullae_of_Lorenzini) are the electroreceptor organs. They number in the hundreds to thousands. Sharks use the ampullae of Lorenzini to detect the [electromagnetic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field) that all living things produce.[^cite_note-68] This helps sharks (particularly the [hammerhead shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammerhead_shark)) find prey. The shark has the greatest electrical sensitivity of any animal. Sharks find prey hidden in sand by detecting the [electric fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field) they produce. [Ocean currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_current) moving in the [magnetic field of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_magnetic_field) also generate electric fields that sharks can use for orientation and possibly navigation.[^cite_note-69]

### Lateral line

Main article: [Lateral line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateral_line)

This system is found in most fish, including sharks. It is a tactile sensory system which allows the organism to detect water speed and pressure changes near by.[^cite_note-70] The main component of the system is the neuromast, a cell similar to [hair cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hair_cell) present in the vertebrate ear that interact with the surrounding aquatic environment. This helps sharks distinguish between the currents around them, obstacles off on their periphery, and struggling prey out of visual view. The shark can sense frequencies in the range of 25 to 50 [Hz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertz).[^cite_note-71]

## Life history

Photo showing claspers of bottom-resting shark.

The claspers of male [spotted wobbegong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spotted_wobbegong)



Shark egg

Photo of 12 centimetres (4.7 in) egg case adjacent to ruler, the egg case is a brown ovalish shape, with a spiral band running around it from top to bottom.

The spiral [egg case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egg_case_\(Chondrichthyes\)) of a [Port Jackson shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Jackson_shark)

Shark lifespans vary by species. Most live 20 to 30 years. The [spiny dogfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiny_dogfish) has one of the longest lifespans at more than 100 years.[^cite_note-72] [Whale sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whale_sharks) (*Rhincodon typus*) may also live over 100 years.[^cite_note-73] Earlier estimates suggested the [Greenland shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland_shark) (*Somniosus microcephalus*) could reach about 200 years, but a recent study found that a 5.02-metre-long (16.5 ft) specimen was 392 ± 120 years old (i.e., at least 272 years old), making it the [longest-lived](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_longest-living_organisms) vertebrate known.[^cite_note-74][^cite_note-75]

### Reproduction

Unlike most [bony fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bony_fish), sharks are [K-selected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R/K_selection_theory) reproducers, meaning that they produce a small number of well-developed young as opposed to a large number of poorly developed young. [Fecundity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fecundity) in sharks ranges from 2 to over 100 young per reproductive cycle.[^cite_note-FAO-76] Sharks mature slowly relative to many other fish. For example, [lemon sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemon_shark) reach sexual maturity at around age 13–15.[^cite_note-77]

#### Sexual

Sharks practice [internal fertilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_fertilization).[^cite_note-Adams-78] The posterior part of a male shark's pelvic fins are modified into a pair of [intromittent organs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intromittent_organ) called [claspers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clasper), analogous to a [mammalian penis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammalian_penis), of which one is used to deliver [sperm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sperm) into the female.[^cite_note-Mating-79]

[Mating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mating) has rarely been observed in sharks.[^cite_note-80] The smaller [catsharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scyliorhinidae) often mate with the male curling around the female. In less flexible species the two sharks swim parallel to each other while the male inserts a clasper into the female's [oviduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oviduct). Females in many of the larger species have bite marks that appear to be a result of a male grasping them to maintain position during mating. The bite marks may also come from courtship behavior: the male may bite the female to show his interest. In some species, females have evolved thicker skin to withstand these bites.[^cite_note-Mating-79]

#### Asexual

There have been a number of documented cases in which a female shark who has not been in contact with a male has conceived a pup on her own through [parthenogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenogenesis).[^cite_note-81][^cite_note-82] The details of this process are not well understood, but [genetic fingerprinting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_fingerprinting) showed that the pups had no paternal genetic contribution, ruling out [sperm storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Female_sperm_storage). The extent of this behavior in the wild is unknown. Mammals are now the only major [vertebrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertebrate) group in which [asexual reproduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asexual_reproduction) has not been observed.

Scientists say that asexual reproduction in the wild is rare, and probably a last-ditch effort to reproduce when a mate is not present. Asexual reproduction diminishes [genetic diversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_diversity), which helps build defenses against threats to the species. Species that rely solely on it risk extinction. Asexual reproduction may have contributed to the [blue shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_shark)'s decline off the [Irish](147575.md) coast.[^cite_note-83]

### Brooding

Sharks display three ways to bear their young, varying by species: [oviparity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oviparity), [viviparity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viviparous) and [ovoviviparity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovoviviparity).[^cite_note-Birth-84][^cite_note-Adams-78]

#### Ovoviviparity

Most sharks are [ovoviviparous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovoviviparous), meaning that the eggs hatch in the [oviduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oviduct) within the mother's body and that the egg's [yolk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yolk) and fluids secreted by glands in the walls of the oviduct nourishes the embryos. The young continue to be nourished by the remnants of the yolk and the oviduct's fluids. As in viviparity, the young are born alive and fully functional. [Lamniforme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamniforme) sharks practice *[oophagy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oophagy)*, where the first embryos to hatch eat the remaining eggs. Taking this a step further, [sand tiger shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sand_tiger_shark) pups cannibalistically consume neighboring embryos. The survival strategy for ovoviviparous species is to [brood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broodiness) the young to a comparatively large size before birth. The [whale shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whale_shark) is now classified as ovoviviparous rather than oviparous, because extrauterine eggs are now thought to have been aborted. Most ovoviviparous sharks give birth in sheltered areas, including bays, river mouths and shallow reefs. They choose such areas for protection from predators (mainly other sharks) and the abundance of food. [Dogfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squalidae) have the longest known [gestation period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestation_period) of any shark, at 18 to 24 months. [Basking sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basking_shark) and [frilled sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frilled_shark) appear to have even longer gestation periods, but accurate data are lacking.[^cite_note-Birth-84]

#### Oviparity

Some species are [oviparous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oviparous), laying their fertilized eggs in the water. In most oviparous shark species, an [egg case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egg_case_\(Chondrichthyes\)) with the consistency of [leather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leather) protects the developing embryo(s). These cases may be corkscrewed into crevices for protection. The egg case is commonly called a *[mermaid's purse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mermaid's_purse)*. Oviparous sharks include the [horn shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_shark), [catshark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scyliorhinidae), [Port Jackson shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Jackson_shark), and [swellshark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swellshark).[^cite_note-Birth-84][^cite_note-85]



Uterus, placenta and fetus of a silvertip shark

#### Viviparity

Viviparity is the gestation of young without the use of a traditional egg, and results in live birth.[^cite_note-Carrier-2012-86] Viviparity in sharks can be placental or aplacental.[^cite_note-Carrier-2012-86] Young are born fully formed and self-sufficient.[^cite_note-Carrier-2012-86] Hammerheads, the [requiem sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Requiem_sharks) (such as the [bull](277607.md) and [blue sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_shark)), and [smoothhounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoothhound) are viviparous.[^cite_note-FAO-76][^cite_note-Birth-84]

## Behavior

The classic view describes a solitary hunter, ranging the oceans in search of food. However, this applies to only a few species. Most live far more social, sedentary, [benthic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benthic) lives, and appear likely to have their own distinct personalities.[^cite_note-87] Even solitary sharks meet for breeding or at rich hunting grounds, which may lead them to cover thousands of miles in a year.[^cite_note-88] Shark migration patterns may be even more complex than in birds, with many sharks covering entire [ocean basins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_basin).

Sharks can be highly social, remaining in large schools. Sometimes more than 100 [scalloped hammerheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalloped_hammerhead) congregate around [seamounts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seamount) and islands, e.g., in the [Gulf of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_California).[^cite_note-Collins-43] Cross-species social hierarchies exist. For example, [oceanic whitetip sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_whitetip_shark) dominate [silky sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silky_shark) of comparable size during feeding.[^cite_note-FAO-76]

When approached too closely some sharks perform a [threat display](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_threat_display). This usually consists of exaggerated swimming movements, and can vary in intensity according to the threat level.[^cite_note-89]

### Speed

In general, sharks swim ("cruise") at an average speed of 8 kilometres per hour (5.0 mph), but when feeding or attacking, the average shark can reach speeds upwards of 19 kilometres per hour (12 mph). The [shortfin mako shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortfin_mako_shark), the fastest shark and one of the fastest fish, can burst at speeds up to 50 kilometres per hour (31 mph).[^cite_note-90] The [great white shark](43619.md) is also capable of speed bursts. These exceptions may be due to the [warm-blooded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warm-blooded), or [homeothermic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeothermic), nature of these sharks' physiology. Sharks can travel 70 to 80 km in a day.[^cite_note-91]

### Intelligence

Sharks possess brain-to-body mass ratios that are similar to mammals and birds,[^cite_note-92] and have exhibited apparent curiosity and behavior resembling play in the wild.[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94]

There is evidence that juvenile lemon sharks can use observational learning in their investigation of novel objects in their environment.[^cite_note-Guttridge-95]

### Sleep

All sharks need to keep water flowing over their gills in order for them to breathe; however, not all species need to be moving to do this. Those that are able to breathe while not swimming do so by using their spiracles to force water over their gills, thereby allowing them to extract oxygen from the water. It has been recorded that their eyes remain open while in this state and actively follow the movements of divers swimming around them[^cite_note-Sleep-96] and as such they are not truly asleep.

Species that do need to swim continuously to breathe go through a process known as sleep swimming, in which the shark is essentially unconscious. It is known from experiments conducted on the [spiny dogfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiny_dogfish) that its [spinal cord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinal_cord), rather than its brain, coordinates swimming, so spiny dogfish can continue to swim while sleeping, and this also may be the case in larger shark species.[^cite_note-Sleep-96] In 2016 a [great white shark](43619.md) was captured on video for the first time in a state researchers believed was sleep swimming.[^cite_note-npr-97]

## Ecology

### Feeding

This section is about shark feeding. For the sport of shark feeding, see [Shark baiting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_baiting).

Photo of great white on surface with open jaws revealing meal.

A great white shark attacking bait at [Isla Guadalupe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isla_Guadalupe), Mexico

Most sharks are [carnivorous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnivorous).[^cite_note-Feeding-98] [Basking sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basking_shark), [whale sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whale_shark), and [megamouth sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megamouth_shark) have independently evolved different strategies for filter feeding [plankton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plankton): basking sharks practice [ram feeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ram_feeding), whale sharks use suction to take in plankton and small fishes, and megamouth sharks make [suction feeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suction_feeding) more efficient by using the [luminescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminescent) tissue inside of their mouths to attract prey in the deep ocean. This type of feeding requires [gill rakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gill_raker)—long, slender filaments that form a very efficient [sieve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sieve)—analogous to the [baleen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baleen) plates of the [great whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_whale). The shark traps the plankton in these filaments and swallows from time to time in huge mouthfuls. Teeth in these species are comparatively small because they are not needed for feeding.[^cite_note-Feeding-98]

Other highly specialized feeders include [cookiecutter sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cookiecutter_shark), which feed on flesh sliced out of other larger fish and [marine mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_mammal). Cookiecutter teeth are enormous compared to the animal's size. The lower teeth are particularly sharp. Although they have never been observed feeding, they are believed to latch onto their prey and use their thick lips to make a seal, twisting their bodies to rip off flesh.[^cite_note-Collins-43]

Some seabed–dwelling species are highly effective ambush predators. [Angel sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel_shark) and [wobbegongs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wobbegong) use camouflage to lie in wait and suck prey into their mouths.[^cite_note-99] Many [benthic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benthic) sharks feed solely on [crustaceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crustacean) which they crush with their flat [molariform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_\(tooth\)) teeth.

Other sharks feed on [squid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squid) or fish, which they swallow whole. The [viper dogfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viper_dogfish) has teeth it can point outwards to strike and capture prey that it then swallows intact. The [great white](43619.md) and other large predators either swallow small prey whole or take huge bites out of large animals. [Thresher sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thresher_shark) use their long tails to stun shoaling fishes, and [sawsharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sawshark) either stir prey from the seabed or slash at swimming prey with their tooth-studded [rostra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rostrum_\(anatomy\)).

The [bonnethead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonnethead) shark is the only known omnivorous species. Its main prey is crustaceans and mollusks, but it also eats a large amount of seagrass, and is able to digest and extract nutrients from about 50% of the seagrass it consumes.[^cite_note-100]

Many sharks, including the [whitetip reef shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitetip_reef_shark) are cooperative feeders and hunt in packs to herd and capture elusive prey. These social sharks are often migratory, traveling huge distances around [ocean basins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_basin) in large schools. These migrations may be partly necessary to find new food sources.[^cite_note-101]

### Range and habitat

Sharks are found in all seas. They generally do not live in fresh water, with a few exceptions such as the [bull shark](277607.md) and the [river shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_shark) which can swim both in seawater and freshwater.[^cite_note-ADW_(Bull_sharks)-102] Sharks are common down to depths of 2,000 metres (7,000 ft), and some live even deeper, but they are almost entirely absent below 3,000 metres (10,000 ft). The deepest confirmed report of a shark is a [Portuguese dogfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_dogfish) at 3,700 metres (12,100 ft).[^cite_note-103]

## Relationship with humans

### Attacks

Main article: [Shark attack](427288.md)

Photo of sign.

A sign warning about the presence of sharks in [Salt Rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_Rock), South Africa

Photo of snorkeler with shark in shallow water.

Snorkeler swims near a [blacktip reef shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blacktip_reef_shark). In rare circumstances involving poor visibility, blacktips may bite a human, mistaking it for prey. Under normal conditions they are harmless and shy.

In 2006, the [International Shark Attack File](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Shark_Attack_File) (ISAF) undertook an investigation into 96 alleged shark attacks, confirming 62 of them as unprovoked attacks and 16 as provoked attacks. The average number of fatalities worldwide per year between 2001 and 2006 from unprovoked shark attacks is 4.3.[^cite_note-104]

Contrary to popular belief, only a few sharks are dangerous to humans. Out of more than 470 species, only four have been involved in a significant number of fatal, unprovoked attacks on humans: the [great white](43619.md), [oceanic whitetip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_whitetip_shark), [tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiger_shark), and [bull sharks](277607.md).[^cite_note-isaf-105][^cite_note-reefquest-106] These sharks are large, powerful predators, and may sometimes attack and kill people. Despite being responsible for attacks on humans, they have all been filmed without using a protective cage.[^cite_note-107]

The perception of sharks as dangerous animals has been popularized by publicity given to a few isolated unprovoked attacks, such as the [Jersey Shore shark attacks of 1916](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jersey_Shore_shark_attacks_of_1916), and through popular fictional works about shark attacks, such as the *[Jaws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaws_\(film\))* film series. *Jaws* author [Peter Benchley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Benchley), as well as [*Jaws*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaws_\(film\)) director [Steven Spielberg](26940.md), later attempted to dispel the image of sharks as man-eating monsters.[^cite_note-108]

To help avoid an unprovoked attack, humans should not wear jewelry or metal that is shiny and refrain from splashing around too much.[^cite_note-109]

In general, sharks show little pattern of attacking humans specifically, part of the reason could be that sharks prefer the blood of fish and other common preys.[^cite_note-110] Research indicates that when humans do become the object of a shark attack, it is possible that the shark has mistaken the human for species that are its normal prey, such as seals.[^cite_note-bbc_2018-111][^cite_note-112] This was further proven in a recent study conducted by researchers at the California State University's Shark Lab. According to footage caught by the Lab's drones, juveniles swam right up to humans in the water without any bite incidents. The lab stated that the results showed that humans and sharks can co-exist in the water.[^cite_note-113]

### In captivity

Main article: [Sharks in captivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharks_in_captivity)

Photo showing visitors in shadow watching whale shark in front of many other fish.

A [whale shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whale_shark) in [Georgia Aquarium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgia_Aquarium)

Until recently, only a few [benthic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benthic) species of shark, such as [hornsharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_shark), [leopard sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopard_shark) and [catsharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scyliorhinidae), had survived in aquarium conditions for a year or more. This gave rise to the belief that sharks, as well as being difficult to capture and transport, were difficult to care for. More knowledge has led to more species (including the large [pelagic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelagic) sharks) living far longer in captivity, along with safer transportation techniques that have enabled long-distance transportation.[^cite_note-114] The great white shark had never been successfully held in captivity for long periods of time until September 2004, when the [Monterey Bay Aquarium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monterey_Bay_Aquarium) successfully kept a young female for 198 days before releasing her.

Most species are not suitable for home aquaria, and not every species sold by [pet stores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pet_store) are appropriate. Some species can flourish in home saltwater aquaria.[^cite_note-sharksathomeafm-115] Uninformed or unscrupulous dealers sometimes sell juvenile sharks like the [nurse shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nurse_shark), which upon reaching adulthood is far too large for typical home aquaria.[^cite_note-sharksathomeafm-115] Public aquaria generally do not accept donated specimens that have outgrown their housing. Some owners have been tempted to [release](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introduced_species) them.[^cite_note-sharksathomeafm-115] Species appropriate to home aquaria represent considerable spatial and financial investments as they generally approach adult lengths of 3 feet (90 cm) and can live up to 25 years.[^cite_note-sharksathomeafm-115]

### In culture



Shark-themed [nose art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nose_art), made popular by the [Flying Tigers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_Tigers) (pictured), is commonly seen on military aircraft.

#### In Hawaii

Sharks figure prominently in [Hawaiian mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_mythology). Stories tell of men with shark jaws on their back who could change between shark and human form. A common theme was that a shark-man would warn beach-goers of sharks in the waters. The beach-goers would laugh and ignore the warnings and get eaten by the shark-man who warned them. [Hawaiian](13270.md) mythology also includes many shark [gods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God). Among a fishing people, the most popular of all [aumakua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aumakua), or deified ancestor guardians, are shark aumakua. [Kamaku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamaku?action=edit\&redlink=1) describes in detail how to offer a corpse to become a shark. The body transforms gradually until the [kahuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kahuna) can point the awe-struck family to the markings on the shark's body that correspond to the clothing in which the beloved's body had been wrapped. Such a shark aumakua becomes the family pet, receiving food, and driving fish into the family net and warding off danger. Like all aumakua it had evil uses such as helping kill enemies. The ruling chiefs typically forbade such sorcery. Many Native Hawaiian families claim such an aumakua, who is known by name to the whole community.[^cite_note-116]

[Kamohoali'i](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamohoalii) is the best known and revered of the shark gods, he was the older and favored brother of [Pele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pele_\(deity\)),[^cite_note-117] and helped and journeyed with her to Hawaii. He was able to assume all human and fish forms. A summit cliff on the crater of [Kilauea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilauea) is one of his most sacred spots. At one point he had a *[heiau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heiau)* (temple or shrine) dedicated to him on every piece of land that jutted into the ocean on the island of [Molokai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molokai). Kamohoali'i was an ancestral god, not a human who became a shark and banned the eating of humans after eating one herself.[^cite_note-oahustories-118][^cite_note-soh-119] In Fijian mythology, [Dakuwaqa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dakuwaqa) was a shark god who was the eater of lost souls.

#### In American Samoa

On the island of [Tutuila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutuila) in [American Samoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Samoa) (a [U.S. territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territories_of_the_United_States)), there is a location called [Turtle and Shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turtle_and_Shark) (*Laumei ma Malie*) which is important in [Samoan culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Samoa)—the location is the site of a legend called *O Le Tala I Le Laumei Ma Le Malie*, in which two humans are said to have transformed into a turtle and a shark.[^cite_note-American_Samoa_NPS-120][^cite_note-121][^cite_note-122] According to the [U.S. National Park Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._National_Park_Service), "Villagers from nearby [Vaitogi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaitogi,_American_Samoa) continue to reenact an important aspect of the legend at Turtle and Shark by performing a ritual song intended to summon the legendary animals to the ocean surface, and visitors are frequently amazed to see one or both of these creatures emerge from the sea in apparent response to this call."[^cite_note-American_Samoa_NPS-120]

#### In popular culture

Main article: [Sharks in popular culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharks_in_popular_culture)

In contrast to the complex portrayals by Hawaiians and other Pacific Islanders, the European and Western view of sharks has historically been mostly of fear and malevolence.[^cite_note-Dean-123] Sharks are used in popular culture commonly as eating machines, notably in the *[Jaws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaws_\(novel\))* novel and the [film of the same name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaws_\(film\)), along with its [sequels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaws_\(franchise\)#Films).[^cite_note-124] Sharks are threats in other films such as *[Deep Blue Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_Blue_Sea_\(1999_film\))*, *[The Reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Reef_\(2010_film\))*, and [others](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_killer_shark_films), although they are sometimes used for comedic effect such as in *[Finding Nemo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finding_Nemo)* and the *[Austin Powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austin_Powers_\(film_series\))* series. Sharks tend to be seen quite often in cartoons whenever a scene involves the ocean. Such examples include the *[Tom and Jerry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_and_Jerry)* cartoons, *[Jabberjaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabberjaw)*, and other shows produced by Hanna-Barbera. They also are used commonly as a clichéd means of killing off a character that is held up by a rope or some similar object as the sharks swim right below them, or the character may be standing on a [plank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walking_the_plank) above shark infested waters.

#### Popular misconceptions

A popular myth is that sharks are immune to disease and [cancer](105219.md), but this is not scientifically supported. Sharks have been known to get cancer.[^cite_note-125][^cite_note-126] Both diseases and [parasites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasites) affect sharks. The evidence that sharks are at least resistant to cancer and disease is mostly [anecdotal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anecdotal) and there have been few, if any, scientific or [statistical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical) studies that show sharks to have heightened immunity to disease.[^cite_note-127] Other apparently false claims are that fins prevent [cancer](105219.md)[^cite_note-128] and treat [osteoarthritis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osteoarthritis).[^cite_note-129] No scientific proof supports these claims; at least one study has shown shark cartilage of no value in cancer treatment.[^cite_note-130]

### Fishery

In 2008, it was estimated that nearly 100 million sharks were being killed by people every year, due to commercial and recreational fishing.[^cite_note-131][^cite_note-sharkfinsoupcnn-132] In 2021, it was estimated that the population of oceanic sharks and rays had dropped by 71% over the previous half-century.[^cite_note-Einhorn-8]

Shark finning yields are estimated at 1.44 million metric tons (1.59 million short tons) for 2000, and 1.41 million metric tons (1.55 million short tons) for 2010. Based on an analysis of average shark weights, this translates into a total annual mortality estimate of about 100 million sharks in 2000, and about 97 million sharks in 2010, with a total range of possible values between 63 and 273 million sharks per year.[^cite_note-MarinePolicy201212-133][^cite_note-eLife2014-134] Sharks are a common seafood in many places, including [Japan](15573.md) and [Australia](4689264.md). In southern Australia, shark is commonly used in [fish and chips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_and_chips),[^cite_note-135] in which fillets are battered and [deep-fried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_frying) or crumbed and grilled. In fish and chip shops, shark is called [flake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flake_\(fish\)). In [India](14533.md), small sharks or baby sharks (called sora in [Tamil language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_language), [Telugu language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telugu_language)) are sold in local markets. Since the flesh is not developed, cooking the flesh breaks it into powder, which is then fried in oil and spices (called sora puttu/sora poratu). The soft bones can be easily chewed, they are considered a delicacy in coastal [Tamil Nadu](29918.md). [Icelanders](14531.md) ferment [Greenland sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland_shark) to produce a delicacy called [hákarl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%A1karl).[^cite_note-Herz2012-136] During a four-year period from 1996 to 2000, an estimated 26 to 73 million sharks were killed and traded annually in commercial markets.[^cite_note-Bakalar_10_12_2006-137]

Sharks are often killed for [shark fin soup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_fin_soup). Fishermen capture live sharks, fin them, and dump the finless animal back into the water. [Shark finning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_finning) involves removing the fin with a hot metal blade.[^cite_note-sharkfinsoupcnn-132] The resulting immobile shark soon dies from suffocation or predators.[^cite_note-138] Shark fin has become a major trade within black markets all over the world. Fins sell for about \$300/lb in 2009.[^cite_note-sharkconservation1-139] Poachers illegally fin millions each year. Few governments enforce laws that protect them.[^cite_note-eLife2014-134] In 2010 Hawaii became the first U.S. state to prohibit the possession, sale, trade or distribution of shark fins.[^cite_note-140] From 1996 to 2000, an estimated 38 million sharks had been killed per year for harvesting shark fins.[^cite_note-Bakalar_10_12_2006-137] It is estimated by [TRAFFIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_\(conservation_programme\)) that over 14,000 tonnes of shark fins were exported into Singapore between 2005–2007 and 2012–2014.[^cite_note-141]

Shark fin soup is a [status symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Status_symbol) in Asian countries and is erroneously considered healthy and full of nutrients. Scientific research has revealed, however, that high concentrations of [BMAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BMAA) are present in shark fins.[^cite_note-142] Because BMAA is a [neurotoxin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurotoxin), consumption of [shark fin soup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_fin_soup) and cartilage pills, therefore, may pose a health risk.[^cite_note-ScienceDaily-143] BMAA is under study for its pathological role in neurodegenerative diseases such as [ALS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ALS), [Alzheimer's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alzheimer's_disease), and [Parkinson's disease](22228064.md).

Sharks are also killed for [meat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_meat). European diners consume [dogfishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squalidae), [smoothhounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoothhound), [catsharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scyliorhinidae), makos, porbeagle and also skates and rays.[^cite_note-144] However, the [U.S.](3434750.md) [FDA](11632.md) lists sharks as one of four fish (with [swordfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swordfish), [king mackerel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_mackerel), and [tilefish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tilefish)) whose high [mercury content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_in_fish) is hazardous to children and pregnant women.

Sharks generally reach [sexual maturity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_maturity) only after many years and produce few offspring in comparison to other harvested fish. Harvesting sharks before they reproduce severely impacts future populations. Capture induced premature birth and abortion (collectively called capture-induced parturition) occurs frequently in sharks/rays when fished.[^cite_note-Adams-78] Capture-induced parturition is rarely considered in fisheries management despite being shown to occur in at least 12% of live bearing sharks and rays (88 species to date).[^cite_note-Adams-78]

The majority of shark fisheries have little monitoring or management. The rise in demand for shark products increases pressure on fisheries.[^cite_note-ParttGruberTaniuchi-44] Major declines in shark stocks have been recorded—some species have been depleted by over 90% over the past 20–30 years with population declines of 70% not unusual.[^cite_note-145] A study by the [International Union for Conservation of Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Union_for_Conservation_of_Nature) suggests that one quarter of all known species of sharks and rays are threatened by extinction and 25 species were classified as critically endangered.[^cite_note-xray-146][^cite_note-Bloomberg-147]

### Shark culling

Main article: [Shark culling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_culling)

In 2014, a [shark cull in Western Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Australian_shark_cull) killed dozens of sharks (mostly [tiger sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiger_sharks)) using [drum lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_line_\(shark_control\)),[^cite_note-148] until it was cancelled after public protests and a decision by the Western Australia EPA; from 2014 to 2017, there was an "imminent threat" policy in Western Australia in which sharks that "threatened" humans in the ocean were shot and killed.[^cite_note-149] This "imminent threat" policy was criticized by senator Rachel Siewart for killing endangered sharks.[^cite_note-150] The "imminent threat" policy was cancelled in March 2017.[^cite_note-151] In August 2018, the Western Australia government announced a plan to re-introduce drum lines (though, this time the drum lines are "SMART" drum lines).[^cite_note-152]

From 1962 to the present,[^cite_note-153] the government of [Queensland](59051.md) has targeted and killed sharks in large numbers by using [drum lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_line_\(shark_control\)), under a "shark control" program—this program has also inadvertently killed large numbers of other animals such as [dolphins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolphin); it has also killed endangered [hammerhead sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammerhead_shark).[^cite_note-154][^cite_note-155][^cite_note-sealifetrust-156][^cite_note-ntd-157] Queensland's drum line program has been called "outdated, cruel and ineffective".[^cite_note-ntd-157] From 2001 to 2018, a total of 10,480 sharks were killed on lethal drum lines in Queensland, including in the [Great Barrier Reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Barrier_Reef).[^cite_note-158] From 1962 to 2018, roughly 50,000 sharks were killed by Queensland authorities.[^cite_note-Decline-159]

The government of [New South Wales](21654.md) has a program that deliberately kills sharks using [nets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_net).[^cite_note-sealifetrust-156][^cite_note-160] The current net program in New South Wales has been described as being "extremely destructive" to marine life, including sharks.[^cite_note-161] Between 1950 and 2008, 352 [tiger sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiger_shark) and 577 [great white sharks](43619.md) were killed in the nets in New South Wales—also during this period, a total of 15,135 marine animals were killed in the nets, including dolphins, whales, turtles, dugongs, and critically endangered [grey nurse sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grey_nurse_shark).[^cite_note-162] There has been a very large decrease in the number of sharks in eastern Australia, and the shark-killing programs in Queensland and New South Wales are partly responsible for this decrease.[^cite_note-Decline-159]

[Kwazulu-Natal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwazulu-Natal), an area of [South Africa](17416221.md), has a shark-killing program using nets and drum lines—these nets and drum lines have killed turtles and dolphins, and have been criticized for killing wildlife.[^cite_note-sharkangels-163] During a 30-year period, more than 33,000 sharks have been killed in KwaZulu-Natal's shark-killing program—during the same 30-year period, 2,211 turtles, 8,448 rays, and 2,310 dolphins were killed in KwaZulu-Natal.[^cite_note-sharkangels-163] Authorities on the French island of [Réunion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%A9union) kill about 100 sharks per year.[^cite_note-164]

Killing sharks negatively affects the marine ecosystem.[^cite_note-pursuit-165][^cite_note-166] Jessica Morris of [Humane Society International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humane_Society_International) calls shark culling a "knee-jerk reaction" and says, "sharks are top order predators that play an important role in the functioning of marine ecosystems. We need them for healthy oceans."[^cite_note-167]

[George H. Burgess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_H._Burgess), the former[^cite_note-168] director of the [International Shark Attack File](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Shark_Attack_File), "describes \[shark] culling as a form of revenge, satisfying a public demand for blood and little else";[^cite_note-thestar-169] he also said shark culling is a "retro-type move reminiscent of what people would have done in the 1940s and 50s, back when we didn't have an ecological conscience and before we knew the consequences of our actions."[^cite_note-thestar-169] Jane Williamson, an associate professor in marine ecology at Macquarie University, says "There is no scientific support for the concept that culling sharks in a particular area will lead to a decrease in shark attacks and increase ocean safety."[^cite_note-170]

### Other threats

Other threats include habitat alteration, damage and loss from coastal development, pollution and the impact of fisheries on the seabed and prey species.[^cite_note-171] The 2007 documentary *[Sharkwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharkwater)* exposed how sharks are being hunted to extinction.[^cite_note-172]

## Conservation

Further information: [List of threatened sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_threatened_sharks) and [Shark sanctuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_sanctuary)

In 1991, [South Africa](17416221.md) was the first country in the world to declare great white sharks a legally protected species[^cite_note-173] (however, the [KwaZulu-Natal Sharks Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KwaZulu-Natal_Sharks_Board) is allowed to kill great white sharks in its "[shark control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_culling)" program in eastern South Africa).[^cite_note-sharkangels-163]

Intending to ban the practice of shark finning while at sea, the United States Congress passed the [Shark Finning Prohibition Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_Finning_Prohibition_Act) in 2000.[^cite_note-HR5461-174] Two years later the Act saw its first legal challenge in *[United States v. Approximately 64,695 Pounds of Shark Fins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_v._Approximately_64,695_Pounds_of_Shark_Fins)*. In 2008 a [Federal Appeals Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Appeals_Court) ruled that a [loophole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loophole) in the law allowed non-fishing vessels to *purchase* shark fins from [fishing vessels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing_vessels) while on the high seas.[^cite_note-Appeals_court_decision_979-175] Seeking to close the loophole, the [Shark Conservation Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_Conservation_Act) was passed by Congress in December 2010, and it was signed into law in January 2011.[^cite_note-HR81-176][^cite_note-177]

In 2003, the European Union introduced a general shark finning ban for all vessels of all nationalities in Union waters and for all vessels flying a flag of one of its member states.[^cite_note-178] This prohibition was amended in June 2013 to close remaining loopholes.[^cite_note-179]

In 2009, the [International Union for Conservation of Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Union_for_Conservation_of_Nature)'s *[IUCN Red List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IUCN_Red_List) of Endangered Species* named 64 species, one-third of all oceanic shark species, as being at risk of extinction due to fishing and shark finning.[^cite_note-IUCNRedList-180][^cite_note-GuardianSharks-181]

In 2010, the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species ([CITES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CITES)) rejected proposals from the [United States](3434750.md) and [Palau](23372.md) that would have required countries to strictly regulate trade in several species of [scalloped hammerhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalloped_hammerhead), [oceanic whitetip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_whitetip_shark) and [spiny dogfish sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiny_dogfish_shark). The majority, but not the required two-thirds of voting delegates, approved the proposal. [China](5405.md), by far the world's largest shark market, and [Japan](15573.md), which battles all attempts to extend the convention to marine species, led the opposition.[^cite_note-cites-182][^cite_note-183] In March 2013, three endangered commercially valuable sharks, the [hammerheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammerhead_shark), the oceanic whitetip and [porbeagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porbeagle) were added to Appendix 2 of [CITES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CITES), bringing shark fishing and commerce of these species under licensing and regulation.[^cite_note-184]

In 2010, Greenpeace International added the [school shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_shark), [shortfin mako shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortfin_mako_shark), [mackerel shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mackerel_shark), [tiger shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiger_shark) and [spiny dogfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiny_dogfish) to its seafood red list, a list of common [supermarket](51862.md) fish that are often sourced from unsustainable fisheries.[^cite_note-185] Advocacy group Shark Trust campaigns to limit shark fishing. Advocacy group [Seafood Watch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seafood_Watch) directs American consumers to not eat sharks.[^cite_note-186]

Under the auspices of the [Convention on the Conservation of Migratory Species of Wild Animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_on_the_Conservation_of_Migratory_Species_of_Wild_Animals) (CMS), also known as the Bonn Convention, the [Memorandum of Understanding on the Conservation of Migratory Sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memorandum_of_Understanding_on_the_Conservation_of_Migratory_Sharks) was concluded and came into effect in March 2010. It was the first global instrument concluded under CMS and aims at facilitating international coordination for the protection, conservation and management of migratory sharks, through multilateral, intergovernmental discussion and scientific research.

In July 2013, New York state, a major market and entry point for shark fins, banned the shark fin trade joining seven other states of the United States and the three Pacific U.S. territories in providing legal protection to sharks.[^cite_note-Humane-187]

In the [United States](3434750.md), and as of January 16, 2019, 12 states including ([Massachusetts](1645518.md), [Maryland](18858.md), [Delaware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware), [California](5407.md), [Illinois](14849.md), [Hawaii](13270.md), [Oregon](26811621.md), [Nevada](21216.md), [Rhode Island](25410.md), [Washington](13015878.md), [New York](8210131.md) and [Texas](29810.md)) along with 3 [U.S. territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territories_of_the_United_States) ([American Samoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Samoa), [Guam](11974.md) and the [Northern Mariana Islands](21402.md)) have passed laws against the sale or possession of shark fins.[^cite_note-188][^cite_note-189]

Several regions now have [shark sanctuaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_sanctuary) or have banned shark fishing—these regions include [American Samoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Samoa), the [Bahamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahamas), the [Cook Islands](7067.md), [French Polynesia](10737.md), [Guam](11974.md), the [Maldives](19117.md), the [Marshall Islands](19147.md), [Micronesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federated_States_of_Micronesia), the [Northern Mariana Islands](21402.md), and [Palau](23372.md).[^cite_note-190][^cite_note-191][^cite_note-192]

In April 2020 researchers reported to have traced the origins of [shark fins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fin) of endangered [hammerhead sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammerhead_shark) from a retail market in Hong Kong back to their source populations and therefore the approximate locations where the sharks were first caught using [DNA analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_analysis).[^cite_note-193][^cite_note-194]

In July 2020 scientists reported results of a survey of 371 reefs in 58 nations estimating the [conservation status of reef sharks globally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_conservation). No sharks have been observed on almost 20% of the surveyed reefs and shark depletion was strongly associated with both socio-economic conditions and conservation measures.[^cite_note-195][^cite_note-196] Sharks are considered to be a vital part of the ocean [ecosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem).

According to a 2021 study in *[Nature](43427.md)*,[^cite_note-197] [overfishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overfishing) has resulted in a 71% global decline in the number of oceanic sharks and [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batoidea) over the preceding 50 years. The oceanic whitetip, and both the scalloped hammerhead and [great hammerheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_hammerhead) are now classified as [critically endangered](7451605.md).[^cite_note-Briggs_2021-198] Sharks in [tropical waters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_climate) have declined more rapidly than those in [temperate zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate_zones) during the period studied.[^cite_note-Richardson_2021-199] A 2021 study published in *[Current Biology](15501806.md)* found that overfishing is currently driving over one-third of sharks and rays to [extinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinction).[^cite_note-200]
