In [Canada](5042916.md), [Indigenous peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas) (also known as Aboriginal Canadians)[^cite_note-indian-2] represent roughly 5% of the [overall population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_of_Canada). They comprise the [Inuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit),[^cite_note-ICCcharter-3] [Métis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A9tis#M%C3%A9tis_people_in_Canada),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEToddThorntonCollins200110-4] and [First Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Nations_in_Canada),[^cite_note-First_Nations_Culture_Areas_Index-5] of which there are over 600 recognized [First Nations governments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_First_Nations_peoples_in_Canada) or [bands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Band_government) with distinctive cultures, languages, music and arts.[^cite_note-Aboriginal_Identity_2011-6][^cite_note-three2-7]

[Old Crow Flats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Crow_Flats) and [Bluefish Caves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluefish_Caves) are some of the earliest known sites of human habitation in Canada.[^cite_note-8] The characteristics of Indigenous cultures in Canada prior to European colonization included permanent settlements,[^cite_note-9] agriculture,[^cite_note-10] civic and ceremonial architecture,[^cite_note-11] complex [societal hierarchies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hierarchy), and [trading networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade).[^cite_note-12] Métis nations of mixed ancestry originated in the mid-17th century when First Nations and Inuit married Europeans, primarily [French settlers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_colonization_of_the_Americas).[^cite_note-testt-13] First Nations and Métis peoples played a critical part in the development of European colonies in Canada, particularly for their role in assisting Europeans during the [North American fur trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_fur_trade).

Various [Aboriginal laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Aboriginal_law), [treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty#Canada), and legislation have been enacted between European immigrants and Indigenous groups across Canada. The impact of [settler colonialism in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Settler_colonialism_in_Canada) can be seen in its culture, history, politics, laws, and legislatures.[^cite_note-y517-14] Historically, this included assimilationist policies affecting Indigenous languages, traditions, religion and the degradation of Indigenous communities that has contemporarily been described by some, including academics and politicians, as a [cultural genocide, or genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_genocide_of_Indigenous_peoples).[^cite_note-15]

The modern Indigenous right to [self-government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-governance) provides for [Indigenous self-government in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_self-government_in_Canada) and the management of cultural, political, health and economic responsibilities within Indigenous communities. [National Indigenous Peoples Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Indigenous_Peoples_Day) recognizes the vast cultures and contributions of Indigenous peoples to the [history of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Canada).[^cite_note-history-16] First Nations, Inuit, and Métis peoples of all backgrounds have become [prominent figures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_Canadian_personalities) in Canada and have helped shape the [Canadian cultural identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Canada).[^cite_note-17]

## Terminology



Indigenous peoples in [Canada](5042916.md) and the [United States](3434750.md), % of population by area.

In [Section 35](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_35_of_the_Constitution_Act,_1982) of the *[Constitution Act, 1982](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_Act,_1982)*, "Aboriginal peoples of Canada" includes [Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Nations_in_Canada), [Inuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit), and [Métis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A9tis) peoples.[^cite_note-defe-18] "Aboriginal" as a [collective noun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_noun)[^cite_note-19] is a specific [term of art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jargon) used as a legal term encompassing all Indigenous peoples living in Canada.[^cite_note-term-20][^cite_note-Terminology-21] Although "Indian" is a term still commonly used in legal documents for First Nations, the descriptors "Indian" and "[Eskimo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eskimo)" have fallen into disuse in Canada; most consider them to be [pejorative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pejorative).[^cite_note-indian-2][^cite_note-22][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOlsonPappas1994213-23] *Indian* remains in place as the legal term used in the [Canadian Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Canada).[^cite_note-indian-2][^cite_note-25] *Aboriginal peoples* has begun to be considered outdated and is slowly being replaced by the term *Indigenous peoples*.[^cite_note-32] There is also an effort to recognize each Indigenous group as a distinct nation, much as there are distinct European, African, and Asian cultures in their respective places.[^cite_note-33]

*First Nations* (most often used in the plural) has come into general use since the 1970s, replacing *Indians* and *Indian bands* in everyday vocabulary.[^cite_note-term-20][^cite_note-Terminology-21] However, on [Indian reserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_reserve), *First Nations* is being supplanted by members of various nations referring to themselves by their group or ethnic identity. In conversation, this would be "I am [Haida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haida_people)", or "we are [Kwantlens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwantlen_First_Nation)", in recognition of their First Nations ethnicities.[^cite_note-34] Also coming into general use since the 1970s, First Peoples refers to all Indigenous groups, i.e. First Nations, Inuit, and Métis.[^cite_note-FN-35][^cite_note-36][^cite_note-indian-2]

Notwithstanding Canada's location within the [Americas](29833.md), the term *Native American* is hardly ever used in Canada, in order to avoid any confusion due to the ambiguous meaning of the word "American". Therefore, the term is typically used only in reference to the [Indigenous peoples within the boundaries of the present-day United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States).[^cite_note-37] *Native Canadians* was often used in Canada to differentiate this American term until the 1980s.[^cite_note-38]

In contrast to the more-specific *Aboriginal*, one of the issues with the term [native](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/native#English) is its general applicability: in certain contexts, it could be used in reference to non-Indigenous peoples in regards to an individual place of origin / birth.[^cite_note-\:1-39] For instance, people who were born or grew up in [Calgary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calgary) may call themselves "Calgary natives", as in they are *native* to that city. With this in mind, even the term *native American*, as another example, may very well indicate someone who is *native to America* rather than a person who is ethnically Indigenous to the boundaries of the present-day United States. In this sense, *native* may encompass a broad range of populations and is therefore not recommended,[^cite_note-\:1-39] although it is not generally considered offensive.

The *[Indian Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Act)* ([Revised Statutes of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revised_Statutes_of_Canada) (R.S.C.), 1985, c. I-5) sets the legal term *Indian*, designating that "a person who pursuant to this Act is registered as an Indian or is entitled to be registered as an Indian."[^cite_note-Branch-40] Section 5 of the act states that a registry shall be maintained "in which shall be recorded the name of every person who is entitled to be registered as an Indian under this Act."[^cite_note-Branch-40] No other term is legally recognized for the purpose of registration and the term *Indian* specifically excludes reference to Inuit as per section 4 of the act.[^cite_note-u408-41]



An Aboriginal community in [Northern Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ontario)

The term *[Eskimo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eskimo)* has [pejorative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pejorative) connotations in Canada and [Greenland](12118.md). Indigenous peoples in those areas have replaced the term *Eskimo* with *Inuit*,[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-eski-43] though the [Yupik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yupik_peoples) of [Alaska](624.md) and [Siberia](29639.md) do not consider themselves Inuit, and [ethnographers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnography) agree they are a distinct people.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOlsonPappas1994213-23][^cite_note-eski-43] They prefer the terminology Yupik, Yupiit, or Eskimo. The [Yupik languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yupik_languages) are linguistically distinct from the [Inuit languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit_languages), but are related to each other.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOlsonPappas1994213-23] [Linguistic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_family) of [Arctic people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumpolar_peoples) have no universal replacement term for *Eskimo*, inclusive of all Inuit and Yupik across the geographical area inhabited by them.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOlsonPappas1994213-23]

Besides these ethnic descriptors, Aboriginal peoples are often divided into legal categories based on their relationship with [the Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crown) (i.e. the state). [Section 91(24)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_Act,_1867#First_Nations,_Inuit_and_M%C3%A9tis) of the *[Constitution Act, 1867](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_Act,_1867)* gives the federal government (as opposed to the provinces) the sole responsibility for "Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians." The government inherited treaty obligations from the British colonial authorities in [Eastern Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Canada) and signed treaties itself with First Nations in [Western Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Canada) (the [Numbered Treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numbered_Treaties)). The *Indian Act*, passed by the federal Parliament in 1876, has long governed its interactions with all treaty and non-treaty peoples.[^cite_note-z557-44]

Members of First Nations bands who are subject to the *Indian Act* are compiled on a list called the [Indian Register](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Register), and such people are designated as status Indians. Many non-treaty First Nations and all Inuit and Métis are not subject to the *Indian Act*. However, two court cases have clarified that Inuit, Métis, and [non-status First Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-status_Indian) are all covered by the term *Indians* in the *Constitution Act, 1867*. The first was *[Reference Re Eskimos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_Re_Eskimos)* (1939), covering Inuit; the second was *[Daniels v. Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniels_v_Canada_\(Indian_Affairs_and_Northern_Development\))* (2013), which concerns Métis and non-status First Nations.[^cite_note-45]

## History

See also: [History of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Canada)

### Paleo-Indian period

See also: [Peopling of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peopling_of_the_Americas) and [Paleo-Indians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleo-Indians)

Further information: [Technological and industrial history of Canada § The Stone Age: Fire (14,000 BC – AD 1600)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_and_industrial_history_of_Canada#The_Stone_Age:_Fire_\(14,000_BC_%E2%80%93_AD_1600\))



Map of [early human migrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_human_migrations) based on the [Out of Africa theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recent_African_origin_of_modern_humans); figures are in thousands of years ago (kya)

[^cite_note-46]

According to [North American archaeological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_archaeological_periods_\(North_America\)) and genetic evidence, [migration to North and South America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peopling_of_the_Americas) made them the last continents in the world with [human habitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_human_migrations).[^cite_note-national-47] During the [Wisconsin glaciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisconsin_glaciation), 50,000–17,000 years ago, falling sea levels allowed people to move across the [Bering land bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beringia) that joined Siberia to northwest North America (Alaska).[^cite_note-Goebel-48] Alaska was [ice-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glacial_refugium) because of [low snowfall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rain_shadow), allowing a [small population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_population_size) to exist. The [Laurentide ice sheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurentide_ice_sheet) covered most of Canada, blocking [nomadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomad) inhabitants and confining them to Alaska (East Beringia) for thousands of years.[^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50]

[Indigenous genetic studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_history_of_the_Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas) suggest that the first inhabitants of the Americas share a single ancestral population, one that developed in isolation, conjectured to be Beringia.[^cite_note-SpencerWells2-51][^cite_note-52] The isolation of these peoples in Beringia might have lasted 10,000–20,000 years.[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-nda-54][^cite_note-discov-55] Around 16,500 years ago, the [glaciers began melting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Glacial_Maximum), allowing people to move south and east into Canada and beyond.[^cite_note-aaa-56][^cite_note-57][^cite_note-58]

The first inhabitants of North America arrived in Canada at least 14,000 years ago.[^cite_note-FaganDurrani2016-59] It is believed the inhabitants entered the Americas pursuing [Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene) mammals such as the [giant beaver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castoroides), [steppe wisent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steppe_bison) (bison), [muskox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muskox), [mastodons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mastodon), [woolly mammoths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woolly_mammoth) and ancient reindeer (early caribou).[^cite_note-Meltzer-60] One route hypothesized is that people walked south by way of an ice-free corridor on the east side of the [Rocky Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_Mountains), and then fanned out across North America before continuing on to South America.[^cite_note-albert-61] The other conjectured route is that they migrated, either on foot or using [primitive boats](3996.md#History), down the Pacific coast to the tip of South America, and then crossed the Rockies and [Andes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andes).[^cite_note-62] Evidence of the latter has been covered by a [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise) of hundreds of metres following the last ice age.[^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64]

The [Old Crow Flats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Crow_Flats) and basin was one of the areas in Canada untouched by glaciations during the [Pleistocene Ice ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternary_glaciation), thus it served as a pathway and refuge for ice age plants and animals.[^cite_note-mountain-65] The area holds evidence of early human habitation in Canada dating from about 12,000 years ago.[^cite_note-CordellLightfoot2008-66] Fossils from the area include some never accounted for in North America, such as [hyenas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyena) and large [camels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camel).[^cite_note-taiga.net-67] [Bluefish Caves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluefish_Caves) is an archaeological site in [Yukon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukon) from which a specimen of apparently human-worked mammoth bone was radiocarbon dated to 12,000 years ago.[^cite_note-CordellLightfoot2008-66]

Clovis sites dated at 13,500 years ago were discovered in western North America during the 1930s. [Clovis peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clovis_culture) were regarded as the first widespread [Paleo-Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleo-Indians) inhabitants of the [New World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_World) and ancestors to all [Indigenous peoples in the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas).[^cite_note-cloves-68]

Localized regional cultures developed from the time of the [Younger Dryas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Younger_Dryas) cold climate period from 12,900 to 11,500 years ago.[^cite_note-69] The [Folsom tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folsom_tradition) is characterized by the use of [Folsom points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folsom_point) as projectile tips at archaeological sites. These tools assisted activities at kill sites that marked the slaughter and butchering of bison.[^cite_note-70]

The land bridge existed until 13,000–11,000 years ago, long after the oldest proven human settlements in the New World began.[^cite_note-mapSurvey-71] Lower sea levels in the [Queen Charlotte sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Charlotte_Sound_\(Canada\)) and [Hecate Strait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hecate_Strait) produced great grass lands called *archipelago of [Haida Gwaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haida_Gwaii)*.[^cite_note-hadi-72] [Hunter-gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherer) of the area left distinctive [lithic technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithic_technology) tools and the remains of large butchered mammals, occupying the area from 13,000–[9,000 years ago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7th_millennium_BC).[^cite_note-hadi-72] In July 1992, the Government of Canada officially designated [X̱á\:ytem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X%CC%B1%C3%A1\:ytem) (near [Mission, British Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission,_British_Columbia)) as a [national historic site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Historic_Sites_of_Canada), one of the first Indigenous spiritual sites in Canada to be formally recognized in this manner.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJameson1997159-73]

The [Plano cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plano_cultures) was a group of hunter-gatherer communities that occupied the Great Plains area of North America between 10,000 and 8,000 years ago.[^cite_note-palo-74] The Paleo-Indians moved into new territory as it emerged from under the glaciers. Big game flourished in this new environment.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETaylor200210-75] The Plano culture is characterized by a range of projectile point tools collectively called [Plano points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plano_point), which were used to hunt [bison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bison). Their diets also included [pronghorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pronghorn), [elk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elk), [deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deer), [raccoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raccoon) and [coyote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coyote).[^cite_note-palo-74] At the beginning of the [Archaic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaic_period_\(North_America\)), they began to adopt a [sedentary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedentary_lifestyle) approach to subsistence.[^cite_note-palo-74] Sites in and around [Belmont, Nova Scotia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belmont,_Colchester,_Nova_Scotia), have evidence of Plano-Indians, indicating small seasonal hunting camps, perhaps re-visited over generations from around 11,000–10,000 years ago.[^cite_note-palo-74] Seasonal large and smaller game fish and fowl were food and raw material sources. Adaptation to the harsh environment included tailored clothing and skin-covered tents on wooden frames.[^cite_note-palo-74]

### Archaic period

See also: [Pre-Columbian era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_era)

The North American climate stabilized by 8000 [BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era) (10,000 years ago); climatic conditions were very similar to today's.[^cite_note-icaage-76] This led to widespread migration, [cultivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_cultivation) and later a dramatic rise in population all over the Americas.[^cite_note-icaage-76] Over the course of thousands of years, Indigenous peoples of the Americas domesticated, bred and cultivated a large array of plant species. These species now constitute 50–60% of all crops in cultivation worldwide.[^cite_note-77]

"Map of North America showing in red the pre-contact distribution of Na-Dene languages"

Distribution of [Na-Dene languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Na-Dene_languages) shown in red

The vastness and variety of Canada's climates, ecology, vegetation, [fauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fauna), and landform separations have defined ancient peoples implicitly into cultural or [linguistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics) divisions. Canada is surrounded north, east, and west with coastline and since the last ice age, Canada has consisted of distinct forest regions. Language contributes to the identity of a people by influencing social life ways and spiritual practices.[^cite_note-prepre-78] Indigenous religions developed from [anthropomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropomorphism) and [animism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animism) philosophies.[^cite_note-79]

The placement of artifacts and materials within an Archaic burial site indicated social differentiation based upon status.[^cite_note-icaage-76] There is a continuous record of occupation of [S'ólh Téméxw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St%C3%B3%EA%9E%89l%C5%8D) by Indigenous people dating from the early [Holocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene) period, 10,000–9,000 years ago.[^cite_note-thor-80] Archaeological sites at [Stave Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stave_Lake), [Coquitlam Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coquitlam_Lake), [Fort Langley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Langley) and region uncovered early period artifacts. These early inhabitants were highly mobile hunter-gatherers, consisting of about 20 to 50 members of an extended family.[^cite_note-thor-80] The [Na-Dene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Na-Dene_languages) people occupied much of the land area of northwest and central North America starting around 8,000 BCE.[^cite_note-81] They were the earliest ancestors of the [Athabaskan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athabaskan_languages)-speaking peoples, including the [Navajo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navajo) and [Apache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apache). They had villages with large multi-family dwellings, used seasonally during the summer, from which they hunted, fished and gathered food supplies for the winter.[^cite_note-82] The [Wendat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wyandot_people) peoples settled into [Southern Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Ontario) along the [Eramosa River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eramosa_River) around 8,000–7,000 BCE (10,000–9,000 years ago).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERay1996-83] They were concentrated between [Lake Simcoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Simcoe) and [Georgian Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_Bay). Wendat hunted caribou to survive on the glacier-covered land.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERay1996-83] Many different First Nations cultures relied upon the buffalo starting by 6,000–5,000 BCE (8,000–7,000 years ago).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERay1996-83] They hunted buffalo by herding migrating buffalo off cliffs. [Head-Smashed-In Buffalo Jump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head-Smashed-In_Buffalo_Jump), near [Fort Macleod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Macleod), Alberta, is a hunting grounds that was in use for about 5,000 years.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERay1996-83]

Photograph of a circular arrangement of rocks on open ground with a body of water in the background

[Thule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thule_people) site ([Copper Inuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_Inuit)) near the waters of [Cambridge Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Bay) ([Victoria Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_Island))

By 7,000–5000 BCE (9,000–7,000 years ago) the [west coast of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Columbia_Coast) saw various cultures who organized themselves around salmon fishing.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERay1996-83] The [Nuu-chah-nulth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuu-chah-nulth) of [Vancouver Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vancouver_Island) began [whaling](33557.md) with advanced long spears at about this time.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERay1996-83] The [Maritime Archaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_Archaic) is one group of North America's [Archaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaic_Period_\(Americas\)) culture of [sea-mammal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_mammal) hunters in the [subarctic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subarctic). They prospered from approximately 7,000 BCE–1,500 BCE (9,000–3,500 years ago) along the [Atlantic Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_Ocean) of North America.[^cite_note-84] Their settlements included [longhouses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longhouse) and boat-topped temporary or seasonal houses. They engaged in long-distance trade, using as currency white [chert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chert), a rock quarried from northern [Labrador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labrador) to [Maine](19977.md).[^cite_note-85] The [Pre-Columbian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_era) culture, whose members were called [Red Paint People](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Paint_People), is indigenous to the [New England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England) and [Atlantic Canada](71095.md) regions of North America. The culture flourished between 3,000 BCE – 1,000 BCE (5,000–3,000 years ago) and was named after their burial ceremonies, which used large quantities of red [ochre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ochre) to cover bodies and grave goods.[^cite_note-86]

The [Arctic small tool tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_small_tool_tradition) is a broad cultural entity that developed along the [Alaska Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Peninsula), around [Bristol Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Bay), and on the eastern shores of the Bering Strait around 2,500 BCE (4,500 years ago).[^cite_note-Fagan-87] These [Paleo-Arctic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleo-Arctic_tradition) peoples had a highly distinctive toolkit of small blades ([microblades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microblade_technology)) that were pointed at both ends and used as side- or end-barbs on [arrows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow) or spears made of other materials, such as bone or [antler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antler). [Scrapers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scraper_\(archaeology\)), engraving tools and [adze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adze) blades were also included in their toolkits.[^cite_note-Fagan-87] The Arctic small tool tradition branches off into two cultural variants, including the Pre-Dorset, and the [Independence traditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_II_culture), ancestors of the [Thule people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thule_people), and Inuit displaced these two groups by 1000 CE.[^cite_note-Fagan-87]<sup>: 179–81 </sup>

The [Old Copper complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Copper_complex) societies dating from 3,000 BCE – 500 BCE (5,000–2,500 years ago) are a manifestation of the [Woodland culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodland_period), and are pre-pottery in nature.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWinchell1881601–602-88] Evidence found in the northern [Great Lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes) regions indicates that they extracted copper from local glacial deposits and used it in its [natural form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_copper) to manufacture tools and implements.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWinchell1881601–602-88]

The Woodland cultural period dates from about 1,000 BCE – 1,000 CE, and has locales in [Ontario](22218.md), [Quebec](7954867.md), and [Maritime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Maritimes) regions.[^cite_note-89] The introduction of pottery distinguishes the Woodland culture from the earlier Archaic stage inhabitants. [Laurentian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurentides) people of southern Ontario manufactured the oldest pottery excavated to date in Canada.[^cite_note-prepre-78] They created pointed-bottom beakers decorated by a cord marking technique that involved impressing tooth implements into wet clay. Woodland technology included items such as beaver incisor knives, bangles, and chisels. The population practising sedentary agricultural life ways continued to increase on a diet of squash, corn, and bean crops.[^cite_note-prepre-78]

The Hopewell tradition is an Indigenous culture that flourished along American rivers from 300 BCE – 500 CE. At its greatest extent, the [Hopewell Exchange System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopewell_tradition) networked cultures and societies with the peoples on the Canadian shores of [Lake Ontario](17947.md). Canadian expression of the Hopewellian peoples encompasses the [Point Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_Peninsula_complex), [Saugeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saugeen_complex), and [Laurel complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurel_complex).[^cite_note-90][^cite_note-91][^cite_note-Price-92]

#### First Nations

Main article: [First Nations in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Nations_in_Canada)

Black and white photograph of Skwxwu7mesh Chief George from the village of Senakw with his daughter in traditional regalia.

Chief George from the village of [Senakw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senakw) with his daughter in traditional [regalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regalia), c. 1906

First Nations peoples had settled and established trade routes across what is now Canada by 500 BCE – 1,000 CE. Communities developed each with its own culture, customs, and character.[^cite_note-Joe-93] In the northwest are the [Athapaskan speaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athabaskan_languages), [Slavey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavey), [Tłı̨chǫ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%82%C4%B1%CC%A8ch%C7%AB), [Tutchone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Tutchone), and [Tlingit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tlingit). Along the Pacific coast are the [Tsimshian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsimshian); Haida; [Coast Salish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coast_Salish); [Kwakwakaʼwakw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwakwaka%CA%BCwakw); [Heiltsuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heiltsuk); [Nootka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuu-chah-nulth); [Nisga'a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nisga'a); [Senakw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senakw) and [Gitxsan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gitxsan). In the Plains are the [Niisitapi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackfoot_Confederacy); [Káínawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kainai_Nation); [Tsuutʼina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsuut%CA%BCina_Nation); and [Piikáni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piikani_Nation). In the Northern Woodlands are the [Cree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cree) and [Chipewyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chipewyan). Around the Great Lakes are the [Anishinaabe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anishinaabe); [Algonquin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algonquin_people); [Haudenosaunee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haudenosaunee) and Wendat. Along the Atlantic Coast are the [Wolastoqiyik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolastoqiyik), [Innu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innu), [Abenaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abenaki), and [Mi'kmaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mi'kmaq) and formerly the [Beothuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beothuk).

Many First Nations civilizations[^cite_note-94] established characteristics and hallmarks that included permanent urban settlements or cities,[^cite_note-95] agriculture, civic and [monumental architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mound_Builders), and [complex societal hierarchies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_society).[^cite_note-complex-96] These cultures had evolved and changed by the time of the first permanent European arrivals (c. late 15th–early 16th centuries), and have been brought forward through archaeological investigations.[^cite_note-97]

There are indications of contact made before [Christopher Columbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Columbus) between the first peoples and those from other continents. Indigenous people in Canada first interacted with Europeans around 1000 CE, but prolonged contact came after Europeans established permanent settlements in the 17th and 18th centuries.[^cite_note-Woodcock,_part_I-98] European written accounts generally recorded friendliness of the First Nations, who profited in trade with Europeans.[^cite_note-Woodcock,_part_I-98] Such trade generally strengthened the more organized political entities such as the [Iroquois Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iroquois).[^cite_note-99] Throughout the 16th century, European fleets made almost annual visits to the eastern shores of Canada to cultivate the fishing opportunities. A sideline industry emerged in the un-organized [traffic of furs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fur_trade) overseen by the [British Indian Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Indian_Department).[^cite_note-100]

Prominent First Nations people include Joe Capilano, who met with King of the United Kingdom, [Edward VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_VII), to speak of the need to settle [land claims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_claim) and [Ovide Mercredi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovide_Mercredi), a leader at both the [Meech Lake Accord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meech_Lake_Accord) constitutional reform discussions and [Oka Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oka_Crisis).[^cite_note-101][^cite_note-102]

#### Inuit

Main article: [Inuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit)

[Inuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit) are the descendants of what [anthropologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology) call the [Thule culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thule_people), which emerged from western Alaska around 1,000 CE and spread eastward across the [Arctic](36971.md), displacing the [Dorset culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorset_culture) (in [Inuktitut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuktitut), the *Tuniit*). Inuit historically referred to the Tuniit as "giants", who were taller and stronger than the Inuit.[^cite_note-103] Researchers hypothesize that the Dorset culture lacked dogs, larger weapons and other technologies used by the expanding Inuit society.[^cite_note-104] By 1300, Inuit had settled in west Greenland, and finally moved into east Greenland over the following century. The Inuit had trade routes with more southern cultures. Boundary disputes were common and led to aggressive actions.[^cite_note-warin-105]

"black and white image of an Inuit hunter seated in a kayak holding a harpoon"

[Inuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit) in a [kayak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kayak), c. 1908–1914

Warfare was common among Inuit groups with sufficient population density. Inuit, such as the [Nunamiut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nunamiut) ([Uummarmiut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uummarmiut)) who inhabited the [Mackenzie River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mackenzie_River) delta area, often engaged in common warfare. The Central Arctic Inuit lacked the population density to engage in warfare. In the 13th century, the Thule culture began arriving in Greenland from what is now Canada. Norse accounts are scant. Norse-made items from Inuit campsites in Greenland were obtained by either trade or plunder.[^cite_note-106] One account, [Ívar Bárðarson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%8Dvar_B%C3%A1r%C3%B0arson), speaks of "small people" with whom the Norsemen fought.[^cite_note-107] 14th-century accounts relate that a western settlement, one of the two Norse settlements, was taken over by the [Skræling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skr%C3%A6ling).[^cite_note-108]

After the disappearance of the Norse colonies in Greenland, the Inuit had no contact with Europeans for at least a century. By the mid-16th century, [Basque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basques) fishers were already working the Labrador coast and had established whaling stations on land, such as those excavated at [Red Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Bay,_Newfoundland_and_Labrador).[^cite_note-109] The Inuit appear not to have interfered with their operations, but they did raid the stations in winter for tools, and particularly worked iron, which they adapted to native needs.[^cite_note-110]

Notable among the Inuit are [Abraham Ulrikab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Ulrikab) and family who became a [zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoo) exhibit in [Hamburg](13467.md), Germany, and [Tanya Tagaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanya_Tagaq), a traditional [throat singer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit_throat_singing).[^cite_note-111] [Abe Okpik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abe_Okpik) was instrumental in helping Inuit obtain [surnames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surname) rather than [disc numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disc_number) and [Kiviaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiviaq) (David Ward) won the legal right to use his single-word [Inuktitut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuktitut) name.[^cite_note-112][^cite_note-113]

#### Métis

Main article: [Métis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A9tis)

Black and white photograph of a man with a short moustache and earrings, wearing a fur lined dress jacket, bow tie and fur hat

[Mixed-blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed-blood) [fur trader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_fur_trade), c. 1870

The [Métis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A9tis) are people descended from marriages between Europeans (mainly French)[^cite_note-114] and [Cree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cree), [Ojibwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ojibwe), [Algonquin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algonquin_people), [Saulteaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saulteaux), [Menominee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menominee), [Mi'kmaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mi'kmaq), [Maliseet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maliseet), and other First Nations.[^cite_note-testt-13] Their history dates to the mid-17th century.[^cite_note-First_Nations_Culture_Areas_Index-5]

When Europeans first arrived to Canada they relied on Indigenous peoples for fur trading skills and survival. To ensure alliances, relationships between European fur traders and Indigenous women were often consolidated through marriage.[^cite_note-115] The Métis homeland consists of the [Canadian provinces](75763.md) of [British Columbia](3392.md), [Alberta](717.md), [Saskatchewan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saskatchewan), [Manitoba](18926.md), and [Ontario](22218.md), as well as the [Northwest Territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Territories) (NWT).[^cite_note-116]

Amongst notable Métis people are singer and actor [Tom Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Jackson_\(actor\)),[^cite_note-117] [Commissioner of the Northwest Territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commissioner_of_the_Northwest_Territories) [Tony Whitford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Whitford), and [Louis Riel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Riel) who led two resistance movements: the [Red River Rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_River_Rebellion) of 1869–1870 and the [North-West Rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North-West_Rebellion) of 1885, which ended in his [trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trial_of_Louis_Riel) and subsequent execution.[^cite_note-LouisRielCE-118][^cite_note-119][^cite_note-120]

The languages inherently Métis are either [Métis French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A9tis_French) or a mixed language called [Michif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michif). Michif, Mechif or Métchif is a [phonetic spelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pronunciation_respelling) of Métif, a variant of Métis.[^cite_note-mist-121] The Métis today predominantly speak [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_English), with [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_French) a strong second language, as well as numerous [Indigenous tongues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_languages_of_the_Americas). A 19th-century community of the Métis people, the [Anglo-Métis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-M%C3%A9tis), were referred to as Countryborn. They were children of [Rupert's Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert's_Land) fur trade typically of [Orcadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orcadians), Scottish, or English paternal descent and Indigenous maternal descent.[^cite_note-122] Their first languages would have been Indigenous ([Cree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cree_language), [Saulteaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Ojibwa_language), [Assiniboine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assiniboine_language), etc.) and English. Their fathers often spoke [Gaelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Gaelic) or the [Orcadian dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orcadian_dialect), thus leading to the development of an English dialect referred to as "[Bungi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bungi_dialect)".[^cite_note-123]

S.35 of the *Constitution Act, 1982* mentions the Métis yet there has long been debate over legally defining the term Métis,[^cite_note-Foster2006gh-124] but on September 23, 2003, the [Supreme Court of Canada](123468.md) ruled that Métis are a distinct people with significant rights ([Powley ruling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R_v_Powley)).[^cite_note-pow-125]

Unlike First Nations people, there has been no distinction between status and non-status Métis;[^cite_note-Company2005-126] the Métis, their heritage and Indigenous ancestry have often been absorbed and assimilated into their surrounding populations.[^cite_note-127]

### Forced assimilation

Main articles: [Canadian genocide of Indigenous peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_genocide_of_Indigenous_peoples) and [Settler colonialism in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Settler_colonialism_in_Canada)

From the late 18th century, European Canadians (and the Canadian government) encouraged [assimilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_assimilation) of Indigenous culture into what was referred to as "[Canadian culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Canada)."[^cite_note-RoyalCom-sg3-128][^cite_note-129] These attempts reached a climax in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, with a series of initiatives that aimed at complete assimilation and subjugation of the Indigenous peoples. These policies, which were made possible by legislation such as the *[Gradual Civilization Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradual_Civilization_Act)*[^cite_note-gradcivact-130] and the *Indian Act*,[^cite_note-Indian_Act-131] focused on European ideals of Christianity, sedentary living, agriculture, and education.[^cite_note-h269-132]

#### Christianization

Indigenous children working at long desks

Study period at a [Roman Catholic](606848.md) Indian Residential School in [Fort Resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Resolution), [NWT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Territories)

[Missionary work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missionary) directed at the Indigenous people of Canada had been ongoing since the first missionaries arrived in the 1600s, generally from France, some of whom were martyred ([Jesuit saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_saints_of_the_Society_of_Jesus) called the [Canadian Martyrs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Martyrs)). [Christianization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianization) as government policy became more systematic with the *Indian Act* in 1876, which would bring new sanctions for those who did not [convert to Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_to_Christianity). For example, the new laws would prevent non-Christian Indigenous people from testifying or having their cases heard in court, and ban alcohol consumption.[^cite_note-Armitage-133] When the *Indian Act* was amended in 1884, traditional religious and social practices, such as the [Potlatch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch), would be banned, and further amendments in 1920 would prevent "[status Indians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Register)" (as defined in the *Act*) from wearing traditional dress or performing traditional dances in an attempt to stop all non-Christian practices.[^cite_note-Armitage-133]

#### Sedentary living, reserves, and "gradual civilization"

Another focus of the Canadian government was to make the Indigenous groups of Canada sedentary, as they thought that this would make them easier to assimilate. In the 19th century, the government began to support the creation of model farming villages, which were meant to encourage non-sedentary Indigenous groups to settle in an area and begin to cultivate agriculture.[^cite_note-Doesett-134] When most of these model farming villages failed,[^cite_note-Doesett-134] the government turned instead to the creation of [Indian reserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_reserve) with the *[Indian Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Act)* of 1876.[^cite_note-Indian_Act-131] With the creation of these reserves came many restricting laws, such as further bans on all intoxicants, restrictions on eligibility to vote in band elections, decreased hunting and fishing areas, and inability for status Indians to visit other groups on their reserves.[^cite_note-Indian_Act-131] Farming was still seen as an important practice for assimilation on reserves; however, by the late 19th century the government had instituted restrictive policies here too, such as the [Peasant Farm Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peasant_Farm_Policy), which restricted reserve farmers largely to the use of hand tools.[^cite_note-135] This was implemented largely to limit the competitiveness of First Nations farming.[^cite_note-136]

Through the *[Gradual Civilization Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradual_Civilization_Act)* in 1857, the government would encourage Indians (i.e., First Nations) to *[enfranchise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffrage)* – to *remove all legal distinctions between \[Indians] and Her Majesty's other Canadian Subjects*.[^cite_note-gradcivact-130] If an Indigenous chose to enfranchise, it would strip them and their family of [Aboriginal title](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aboriginal_title), with the idea that they would become "less savage" and "more civilized," thus become assimilated into Canadian society.[^cite_note-Miller-137] However, they were often still defined as *non-citizens* by Europeans, and those few who did enfranchise were often met with disappointment.[^cite_note-Miller-137]

#### Residential system

Main articles: [Canadian Indian residential school system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Indian_residential_school_system) and [Residential school denialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residential_school_denialism)



St. Paul's Indian Industrial School, Middlechurch, Manitoba, 1901

The final government strategy of assimilation, made possible by the *Indian Act* was the [Canadian residential school system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Indian_residential_school_system):

> Of all the initiatives that were undertaken in the first century of Confederation, none was more ambitious or central to the civilizing strategy of the Department, to its goal of assimilation than the residential school system… it was the residential school experience that would lead children most effectively out of their "savage" communities into "higher civilization" and "full citizenship."[^cite_note-138]

Beginning in 1874 and lasting until 1996, the Canadian government, in partnership with the dominant Christian Churches, ran 130 residential boarding schools across Canada for Indigenous children. Sometimes school attendance was forced.[^cite_note-139] While the schools provided some education, they were plagued by under-funding, disease, and abuse.[^cite_note-140]

According to some scholars, the Canadian government's laws and policies, including the residential school system, that encouraged or required Indigenous peoples to [assimilate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_imperialism) into a [Eurocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurocentrism) society, violated the [United Nations](31769.md) [Genocide Convention](143988.md) that Canada signed in 1949 and passed through Parliament in 1952. Therefore, these scholars believe that Canada could be tried in [international court](433437.md) for [genocide](12441.md).[^cite_note-Restoule,_Jean-Paul_2002-141] A legal case resulted in settlement of CA\$2 billion in 2006 and the 2008 establishment of the [Truth and Reconciliation Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth_and_Reconciliation_Commission_of_Canada), which confirmed the injurious effect on children of this system and turmoil created between Indigenous peoples and non-Indigenous peoples.[^cite_note-T\&RC-142] In 2008, Prime Minister [Stephen Harper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Harper) issued an apology on behalf of the Canadian government and its citizens for the residential school system.[^cite_note-143]

## Politics, law, and legislation

See also: [Monarchy of Canada and the Indigenous peoples of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy_of_Canada_and_the_Indigenous_peoples_of_Canada)

### Indigenous law vs. Aboriginal law

Further information: [Canadian Indigenous law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Indigenous_law)

The term *[Canadian Indigenous law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Indigenous_law)* refers to Indigenous peoples' own legal systems. This includes the laws and legal processes developed by Indigenous groups to govern their relationships, manage their natural resources, and manage conflicts.[^cite_note-Borrows-144] Indigenous law is developed from a variety of sources and institutions, which differ across legal traditions.[^cite_note-Guides-145] [Canadian Aboriginal law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Aboriginal_law) is the area of law related to the Canadian government's relationship with the Indigenous peoples. Section 91(24) of the *Constitution Act, 1867* gives the federal parliament exclusive power to legislate in matters related to Aboriginals, which includes groups governed by the *Indian Act*, different Numbered Treaties and outside of those acts.[^cite_note-LeuprechtRussell2011-146]

### Treaties

Photograph showing the two sides of a round silver medal, showing the profile of Queen Victoria on one side and the inscription "Victoria Regina", with the other side having a depiction of a man in European garb shaking hands with a man in historical First Nation clothing with the inscription "Indian Treaty 187"

The Indian Chiefs Medal, presented to commemorate [Treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numbered_Treaties) [3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_3), [4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_4), [5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_5), [6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_6) and [7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_7), bearing the image of [Queen Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Victoria)

The [Monarchy of Canada and the Indigenous peoples of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy_of_Canada_and_the_Indigenous_peoples_of_Canada) began [interactions in North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_the_European_colonization_of_North_America) during the European colonization period. The Royal Proclamation of 1763 recognized Indigenous title and the [Treaty of Niagara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Niagara) of 1764 bound the Crown and the Indigenous peoples of the Great Lakes basin together in a familial relationship, a relationship that exists to this day, exemplified by First Nations attendance at the coronation of [King Charles III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Charles_III).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECoteTidridge202443-147] Post-Confederation Canada adopted a paternalistic approach and imposed an approach as though the nation-to-nation relationship did not exist, administering relations solely under Canadian law.

After Canada's acquisition of [Rupert's Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert's_Land) and the [North-Western Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North-Western_Territory) in 1870, the eleven [Numbered Treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numbered_Treaties) were imposed on the First Nations from 1871 to 1921. These treaties are agreements with the Crown administered by [Canadian Aboriginal law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Aboriginal_law) and overseen by the [Minister of Crown–Indigenous Relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_Crown%E2%80%93Indigenous_Relations).[^cite_note-148]

Treaty rights would be recognized and incorporated into the 1982 Constitution. Many agreements signed before the [Confederation of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Confederation) are recognized in Canadian law, such as the [Peace and Friendship Treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_and_Friendship_Treaties), the [Robinson Treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robinson_Treaties), the [Douglas Treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Treaties), and many others, although many First Nations still have no treaty with the Crown recognizing their title, such as the Mikmaq, the Anishnaabe and several northern British Columbia nations.

For many years, Canada did not negotiate with First Nations to address [their aboriginal title](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_land_claims_in_Canada). Canadian court judgments and political pressure led to a change in ways following the [Canadian Centennial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Centennial) year. The first treaty implemented under the new framework was the *[James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Bay_and_Northern_Quebec_Agreement)* in 1970 between the Cree and Quebec. This was followed by the *Inuvialuit Final Agreement* in 1984 that led to the creation of the [Inuvialuit Settlement Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuvialuit_Settlement_Region).[^cite_note-149] The [Nunavut Land Claims Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nunavut_Land_Claims_Agreement) of 1993 lead to the creation of the [Inuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit)-majority territory of [Nunavut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nunavut) later that decade. The Canadian Crown continues to sign new treaties with Indigenous peoples, notably though the [British Columbia Treaty Process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Columbia_Treaty_Process).[^cite_note-150]

According to the *First Nations–Federal Crown Political Accord*, "cooperation will be a cornerstone for partnership between Canada and First Nations, wherein *Canada* is the short-form reference to [Her Majesty the Queen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Style_of_the_British_sovereign) in Right of Canada.[^cite_note-FN-35] The Supreme Court of Canada argued that treaties "served to reconcile pre-existing Indigenous sovereignty with assumed Crown sovereignty, and to define Aboriginal rights."[^cite_note-FN-35] First Nations interpreted agreements covered in [Treaty 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_8) to last "as long as the sun shines, grass grows and rivers flow."[^cite_note-151] However, the Canadian government has frequently breached the Crown's treaty obligations over the years, and tries to address these issues by negotiating [specific land claim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_specific_land_claims_in_Canada).[^cite_note-152]

### *Indian Act*

Main article: [Indian Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Act)

Colour photograph of former federal Indian affairs minister David Crombie speaking to reporters on the floor of the 1983 Progressive Conservative leadership convention

Former federal Indian affairs minister [David Crombie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Crombie) was responsible for Bill C-31.

The *Indian Act* is Canadian law that dates from 1876. The Act replaced pre-Confederation Canadian laws, and was intended to administer the Indigenous people, and define Canadian interactions. Successive Canadian governments used its powers to impose conditions on the First Nations, and guide their integration into Canada. Today still, the *Indian Act* indicates how reserves and bands can operate and defines who is recognized as an "Indian."[^cite_note-laws-153] There have been many updates to this law since then, allowing Canadian citizenship and voting rights among others.[^cite_note-154]

In 1985, the Canadian Parliament passed Bill C-31, *An Act to Amend the Indian Act*. Because of a constitutional requirement, the bill took effect on April 17, 1985.[^cite_note-bill-155]

- It ends discriminatory provisions of the *Indian Act*, especially those that discriminated against women.

  [^cite_note-bill-155]

- It changes the meaning of *status* and for the first time allows for limited reinstatement of Indians who were denied or lost status or band membership.

  [^cite_note-bill-155]

- It allows bands to define their own membership rules.

  [^cite_note-bill-155]

Those people accepted into band membership under band rules may not be status Indians. C-31 clarified that various sections of the *Indian Act* apply to band members. The sections under debate concern community life and land holdings. Sections pertaining to Indians (First Nations peoples) as individuals (in this case, wills and taxation of personal property) were not included.[^cite_note-bill-155]

### Royal Commission

Main article: [Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission_on_Aboriginal_Peoples)



Map with areas labelled where the [Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth_and_Reconciliation_Commission_of_Canada) took part in outreach and statement gathering events over the impact of [Canadian residential schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_residential_schools) with the Indigenous peoples of Canada

The [Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission_on_Aboriginal_Peoples) was a [royal commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_commission) undertaken by the Government of Canada in 1991 to address issues of the Indigenous peoples of Canada.[^cite_note-royal-156] It assessed past government policies toward Indigenous people, such as residential schools, and provided policy recommendations to the government.[^cite_note-Cairns200j0-157] The Commission issued its final report in November 1996. The five-volume, 4,000-page report covered a vast range of issues; its 440 recommendations called for sweeping changes to the interaction between Indigenous, non-Indigenous people and the governments in Canada.[^cite_note-royal-156] The report "set out a 20-year agenda for change."[^cite_note-McCaslinCentre2005fu-158]

### Health policy

Main article: [Indian Health Transfer Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Health_Transfer_Policy)

In 1995, the Government of Canada announced the Aboriginal Right to Self-Government Policy.[^cite_note-159] This policy recognizes that First Nations and Inuit have the constitutional right to shape their own forms of government to suit their particular historical, cultural, political and economic circumstances. The [Indian Health Transfer Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Health_Transfer_Policy) provided a framework for the assumption of control of health services by Indigenous peoples, and set forth a developmental approach to transfer centred on self-determination in health.[^cite_note-160][^cite_note-161] Through this process, the decision to enter transfer discussions with [Health Canada](216516.md) rests with each community. Once involved in transfer, communities can take control of health programme responsibilities at a pace determined by their individual circumstances and health management capabilities.[^cite_note-162] The [National Aboriginal Health Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Aboriginal_Health_Organization) (NAHO) incorporated in 2000, was an Indigenous -designed and-controlled not-for-profit body in Canada that worked to influence and advance the health and well-being of Indigenous Peoples.[^cite_note-WaldramHerring2006-163] Its funding was discontinued in 2012.[^cite_note-164]

### Political organization

Further information: [Band government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Band_government) and [History of Indigenous organizations in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Indigenous_organizations_in_Canada)

First Nations and Inuit organizations ranged in size from [band societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Band_society) of a few people to multi-nation confederacies like the Iroquois. First Nations leaders from across the country formed the [Assembly of First Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assembly_of_First_Nations), which began as the National Indian Brotherhood in 1968.[^cite_note-price-165] Métis and Inuit are represented nationally by the [Métis National Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A9tis_National_Council) and [Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit_Tapiriit_Kanatami) respectively.

Today's political organizations have resulted from interaction with European-style methods of government through the [Federal Interlocutor for Métis and Non-Status Indians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Interlocutor_for_M%C3%A9tis_and_Non-Status_Indians). Indigenous political organizations throughout Canada vary in political standing, viewpoints, and reasons for forming.[^cite_note-166] First Nations, Métis and Inuit negotiate with the Government of Canada through [Crown–Indigenous Relations and Northern Affairs Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown%E2%80%93Indigenous_Relations_and_Northern_Affairs_Canada) in all affairs concerning land, entitlement, and rights.[^cite_note-price-165] The First Nation groups that operate independently do not belong to these groups.[^cite_note-price-165]

## Culture

See also: [Mythologies of the Indigenous peoples of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythologies_of_the_Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas)

Black and white photo of a woman kneeling on the ground making a snowshoe. She is wearing a plaid shirt and white dress locking down at the snowshoe. Around her is four frames of snowshoe to be made leaning on a tippy,

Traditional [snowshoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowshoe) maker, c. 1900

Countless Indigenous words, inventions and games have become an everyday part of [Canadian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Canada) and use. The [canoe](99960.md), [snowshoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowshoe), the [toboggan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toboggan), [lacrosse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lacrosse), [tug of war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tug_of_war), [maple syrup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maple_syrup) and tobacco are just a few of the products, inventions and games.[^cite_note-167] Some of the words include the [barbecue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbecue), [caribou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reindeer), [chipmunk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chipmunk), [woodchuck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundhog), [hammock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammock), [skunk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skunk), and [moose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moose).[^cite_note-Edinburgh-168]

[Many places in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_place_names_in_Canada_of_Indigenous_origin), both natural features and human habitations, use Indigenous names. The word *Canada* itself derives from the [St. Lawrence Iroquoian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurentian_language) word meaning 'village' or 'settlement'.[^cite_note-169] The province of Saskatchewan derives its name from the [Saskatchewan River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saskatchewan_River), which in the Cree language is called *Kisiskatchewani Sipi*, meaning 'swift-flowing river'.[^cite_note-places-170] *[Ottawa](22219.md)*, the name of Canada's capital city, comes from the [Algonquin language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algonquin_language) term *adawe*, meaning 'to trade'.[^cite_note-places-170]

Modern youth groups, such as [Scouts Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scouts_Canada) and the [Girl Guides of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girl_Guides_of_Canada), include programs based largely on Indigenous lore, arts and crafts, character building and outdoor camp craft and living.[^cite_note-171]

Indigenous cultural areas depend upon their ancestors' primary [lifeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society#Types), or occupation, at the time of European contact. These culture areas correspond closely with [physical and ecological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Canada) [regions of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_regions_in_Canada).[^cite_note-three1-172] The [Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Pacific_Northwest_Coast) were centred around ocean and river fishing; in the interior of British Columbia, hunter-gatherer and river fishing. In both of these areas, the salmon was of chief importance. For the people of the plains, bison hunting was the primary activity. In the [subarctic forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiga), other species such as the moose were more important. For peoples near the [Great Lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes) and [St. Lawrence River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Lawrence_River), [shifting agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shifting_cultivation) was practised, including the raising of [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize), [beans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bean), and [squash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cucurbita).[^cite_note-three2-7][^cite_note-three1-172] While for Inuit, hunting was the primary source of food with [seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinniped) the primary component of their diet.[^cite_note-Searles-173] The caribou, fish, other [marine mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_mammal) and to a lesser extent plants, berries and seaweed are part of the [Inuit diet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit_cuisine). One of the most noticeable symbols of [Inuit culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit_culture), the [inuksuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuksuk) is the emblem of the [Vancouver](32706.md) [2010 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Winter_Olympics). *Inuksuit* are rock sculptures made by stacking stones; in the shape of a human figure, they are called *inunnguaq*.[^cite_note-vancouver-174]

Colour photograph of Tsuu T'ina children in traditional costume on horseback at a Stampede Parade in front of an audience

[Tsuu T'ina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsuut%CA%BCina_Nation) children at a parade

[Indian reserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_reserve), established in [Canadian law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Canada) by treaties such as [Treaty 7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_7), are lands of First Nations recognized by non-Indigenous governments.[^cite_note-175] Some reserves are within cities, such as the Opawikoscikan Reserve in [Prince Albert, Saskatchewan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Albert,_Saskatchewan), [Wendake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendake) in [Quebec City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_City) or [Enoch Cree Nation 135](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enoch_Cree_Nation_135) in the [Edmonton Metropolitan Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonton_Metropolitan_Region). There are more reserves in Canada than there are First Nations, which were ceded multiple reserves by treaty.[^cite_note-176] Indigenous people currently work in a variety of occupations and may live outside their ancestral homes. The traditional cultures of their ancestors, shaped by nature, still exert a strong influence on them, from spirituality to political attitudes.[^cite_note-three2-7][^cite_note-three1-172] [National Indigenous Peoples Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Indigenous_Peoples_Day) is a day of recognition of the cultures and contributions of the First Nations, Inuit and Métis peoples of Canada. The day was first celebrated in 1996, after it was proclaimed that year, by then [Governor General of Canada](38152.md), [Roméo LeBlanc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rom%C3%A9o_LeBlanc), to be celebrated on June 21 annually.[^cite_note-history-16] Most provincial jurisdictions do not recognize it as a [statutory holiday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_holidays_in_Canada).[^cite_note-history-16]

Further information: [Indigenous languages of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_languages_of_the_Americas) and [Languages of Canada § Indigenousl languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Canada#Indigenousl_languages)

See also: [Canadian Aboriginal syllabics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Aboriginal_syllabics)

There are thirteen [Indigenous language groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Canada#Indigenous_languages), eleven oral and two [sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_language), in Canada, made up of more than sixty-five distinct dialects.[^cite_note-177] Of these, only [Cree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cree_language), [Inuktitut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuktitut), and [Ojibwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ojibwe_language) have a large enough population of fluent speakers to be considered viable to survive in the long term.[^cite_note-online-178] Two of Canada's territories give official status to native languages. In Nunavut, [Inuktut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuktut), also known as the [Inuit language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit_languages), (Inuktitut and [Inuinnaqtun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuinnaqtun)) are official languages alongside the national languages of English and French.[^cite_note-179][^cite_note-180][^cite_note-181]

In the Northwest Territories, the *[Official Languages Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Territories#Languages)* declares that there are 11 different languages: [Chipewyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chipewyan_language), Cree, English, French, [Gwichʼin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwich%CA%BCin_language), Inuinnaqtun, Inuktitut, [Inuvialuktun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuvialuktun), North [Slavey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavey_language), South Slavey, and [Tłįchǫ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogrib_language).[^cite_note-182] Besides English and French, these languages are not vehicular in government; official status entitles citizens to receive services in them on request and to deal with the government in them.[^cite_note-online-178]

### Visual art

Further information: [Visual arts of the Indigenous peoples of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_arts_of_the_Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas)

A colour photograph of a young girl in a traditional shawl between two adults

A young [Métis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A9tis) girl wearing a traditional [shawl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shawl)

[Indigenous peoples were producing art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_arts_of_the_Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas) for thousands of years before the arrival of European [settler colonists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Settler_colonialism) and the eventual establishment of Canada as a [nation state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nation_state). Like the peoples who produced them, Indigenous art traditions spanned territories across North America. Indigenous art traditions are organized by art historians according to cultural, linguistic or regional groups: Northwest Coast, [Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Northwest_Plateau), [Plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plains_Indians), [Eastern Woodlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Eastern_Woodlands), Subarctic, and Arctic.[^cite_note-art-183]

Art traditions vary enormously amongst and within these diverse groups. Indigenous art with a focus on portability and the body is distinguished from European traditions and its focus on architecture. Indigenous visual art may be used in conjunction with other arts. Among Inuit the [masks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masks_among_Eskimo_peoples) and rattles of the *[angakkuq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angakkuq)* ([shaman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamanism)) are used ceremoniously in dance, storytelling and music.[^cite_note-art-183] Artworks preserved in museum collections date from the period after European contact and show evidence of the creative adoption and adaptation of European trade goods such as metal and glass beads.[^cite_note-184] The distinct Métis cultures that have arisen from inter-cultural relationships with Europeans contribute culturally hybrid art forms.[^cite_note-Hempstead2010-185] During the 19th and the first half of the 20th century the Canadian government pursued an active policy of [forced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forced_assimilation) and cultural assimilation toward Indigenous peoples. The *Indian Act* banned manifestations of the [Sun Dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Dance), the Potlatch, and works of art depicting them.[^cite_note-IA1880-186]

Female dancer in costume performing in front of an audience

Dancer at Drum Dance Festival, [Gjoa Haven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gjoa_Haven), [Nunavut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nunavut)

It was not until the 1950s and 1960s that Indigenous artists such as [Mungo Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mungo_Martin), [Bill Reid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Reid) and [Norval Morrisseau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norval_Morrisseau) began to publicly renew and re-invent Indigenous art traditions. Currently, there are Indigenous artists practising in all media in Canada and two Indigenous artists, Edward Poitras and [Rebecca Belmore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebecca_Belmore), have represented Canada at the [Venice Biennale](301876.md) in 1995 and 2005 respectively.[^cite_note-art-183]

### Music

Main article: [Indigenous music of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_music_of_Canada)

Indigenous peoples in Canada encompass diverse ethnic groups with their [individual musical traditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_music_of_Canada). Music is usually social (public) or ceremonial (private). Public, social music may be [dance music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance_music) accompanied by [rattles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rattle_\(percussion_instrument\)) and drums. Private, ceremonial music includes vocal songs with accompaniment on percussion, used to mark occasions like Midewivin ceremonies and [Sun Dances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Dance).

Traditionally, Indigenous peoples used the materials at hand to make their instruments for centuries before Europeans immigrated to Canada.[^cite_note-second-187] First Nations people made [gourds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gourd) and animal [horns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_\(anatomy\)) into rattles, which were elaborately carved and brightly painted.[^cite_note-PDF-188] In woodland areas, they made horns of [birch bark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birch_bark) and [drumsticks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percussion_mallet) of carved antlers and wood. Traditional percussion instruments such as drums were generally made of carved wood and animal [hides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hide_\(skin\)). These [musical instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_instrument) provide the background for songs, and songs the background for dances. Traditional First Nations people consider song and dance to be sacred. For years after Europeans came to Canada, First Nations people were forbidden to practice their ceremonies.[^cite_note-IA1880-186][^cite_note-second-187]

## Demography

Further information: [Population of Canada § Indigenous peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_of_Canada#Indigenous_peoples), and [Population history of the Indigenous peoples of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_history_of_the_Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas)

See also: [List of First Nations peoples in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_First_Nations_peoples_in_Canada), [List of Indian reserves in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Indian_reserves_in_Canada), and [List of Canadian census divisions with Indigenous majority populations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Canadian_census_divisions_with_Indigenous_majority_populations)



Percent reporting indigenous identity by census division as of the [2021 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Canadian_census).

There are three ([First Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Nations_in_Canada),[^cite_note-First_Nations_Culture_Areas_Index-5] [Inuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit)[^cite_note-ICCcharter-3] and [Métis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A9tis#M%C3%A9tis_people_in_Canada)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEToddThorntonCollins200110-4]) distinctive groups of Indigenous peoples that are recognized in the Canadian *Constitution Act, 1982*, [sections 25](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_25_of_the_Canadian_Charter_of_Rights_and_Freedoms) and 35.[^cite_note-defe-18] Under the *[Employment Equity Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employment_equity_\(Canada\))*, Indigenous people are a designated group along with women, visible minorities, and persons with disabilities;[^cite_note-189] as such, they are neither a [visible minority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_minority) under the *Act* or in the view of [Statistics Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics_Canada).[^cite_note-190]

The [2021 Census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Canadian_census) data reveals that there are over 1.8 million Indigenous people in Canada, comprising 5.0% of the overall population and a slight increase from 4.9% in 2016.[^cite_note-t481-191]

The growth of the Indigenous population has slowed compared to previous years. The population grew by 18.9% between 2011 to 2016, while the growth from 2016 to 2021 was only 9.4%. For the first time, the Census recorded more than 1 million First Nations people living in Canada. The Indigenous population continues to grow at a faster rate than the non-Indigenous population but at a reduced speed. The Indigenous population is projected to reach between 2.5 million and 3.2 million in the next 20 years.[^cite_note-t481-191]

The First Nations population overall increased by 9.7% from 2016 to 2021. However, Status First Nations saw a slower growth of 4.1%, compared to those without Registered Indian status, which grew by 27.2%. The Métis population rose by 6.3%, and the Inuit population grew by 8.5%. More than half of First Nations people (55. 5%) lived in Western Canada as of 2021. Ontario had the highest number of First Nations people, with 251,030 (about 23.9%) of the total First Nations population. Approximately 11.1% of First Nations people lived in Quebec, with 7.6% in Atlantic Canada and 1.9% in the territories.[^cite_note-t481-191]

The 2021 Census showed nearly three in four Canadians lived in urban areas, with 801,045 Indigenous people living in large urban centers. This is an increase of 12.5% from 2016, signifying that Indigenous people were more likely to reside in these areas compared to before.[^cite_note-t481-191]

Moreover, the Indigenous population is generally younger than the non-Indigenous population. In 2021, the average age of Indigenous people was 33.6 years, compared to 41.8 years for non-Indigenous people. The Inuit population was the youngest, averaging 28.9 years, followed by First Nations at 32.5 years and Métis at 35.9 years. In total, there were 459,215 Indigenous children aged 14 years and younger, making up 25.4% of the Indigenous population, while only 16.0% of the non-Indigenous population fell into this age category.[^cite_note-t481-191]

In the 20th century, the Indigenous population of Canada increased tenfold.[^cite_note-statcan_aboriginal_demographics-192] Between 1900 and 1950 the population grew by 29%. After the 1960s the [infant mortality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant_mortality) level on reserves dropped dramatically.[^cite_note-193][^cite_note-babies-194] Since the 1980s, the number of First Nations babies more than doubled and in 2009 almost half of the First Nations population was under the age of 25.[^cite_note-statcan_aboriginal_demographics-192][^cite_note-babies-194]

Indigenous people assert that their [sovereign rights](28791.md) are valid, pointing to the [Royal Proclamation of 1763](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Proclamation_of_1763), which is mentioned in the Canadian *Constitution Act, 1982*, Section 25, the *[British North America Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_North_America_Acts)* and the [1969 Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_Convention_on_the_Law_of_Treaties) (to which Canada is a signatory) in support of this claim.[^cite_note-195][^cite_note-196]

### Geographical distribution

[Ethnographers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnography) commonly classify Indigenous peoples of the Americas in the United States and Canada into ten geographical regions, [cultural areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_the_Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas), with shared cultural traits.[^cite_note-201] The Canadian regions are:

- [Arctic cultural area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit) ([Eskaleut languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eskaleut_languages))

- [Subarctic culture area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Subarctic) ([Na-Dene languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Na-Dene_languages) and [Algic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algic_languages))

- [Eastern Woodlands (Northeast) cultural area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Eastern_Woodlands) ([Algic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algic_languages) and [Iroquoian languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iroquoian_languages))

- [Plains cultural area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plains_Indians) ([Siouan–Catawban languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siouan_languages))

- [Northwest Plateau cultural area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Northwest_Plateau) ([Interior Salish languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interior_Salish_languages))

- [Northwest Coast cultural area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Pacific_Northwest_Coast) ([Penutian languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penutian_languages), [Tsimshianic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsimshianic_languages) and [Wakashan languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wakashan_languages))
