The **wild boar** (***Sus scrofa***), also known as the **wild swine**,[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] **common wild pig**,[^cite_note-oliver1993-5] **Eurasian wild pig**,[^cite_note-mammaldiversity.org-6] or simply **wild pig**,[^cite_note-7] is a [suid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suidae) native to much of [Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasia) and [North Africa](21714.md) and has been introduced to [the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Americas) and [Oceania](22621.md). The species is now one of the widest-ranging mammals in the world as well as the most widespread [suiform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suina).[^cite_note-oliver1993-5] It has been assessed as [least concern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_concern) on the [IUCN Red List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IUCN_Red_List) due to its wide range, high numbers, and adaptability to a diversity of habitats.[^cite_note-iucn-1] It has become an [invasive species](67554.md) in part of its introduced range. Wild boars probably originated in [Southeast Asia](28741.md) during the [Early Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Pleistocene)[^cite_note-chen2007-8] and outcompeted other suid species as they spread throughout the [Old World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_World).[^cite_note-kurten1968-9] However, extensive interspecific introgression among suids has complicated reconstruction of the evolutionary and geographic origins of wild boars.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11] A 2026 genomic study accounting for this complex history challenged the traditional view, placing the origin of modern wild boars in tropical continental Asia rather than the Southeast Asian islands. The study found that wild boars expanded from South Asia during the Middle Pleistocene and subsequently dispersed across Eurasia and into Southeast Asian islands.[^cite_note-12]

As of 2005, up to 16 [subspecies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspecies) are recognized, which are divided into four regional groupings based on skull height and [lacrimal bone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lacrimal_bone) length.[^cite_note-msw3-2] The species lives in matriarchal societies consisting of interrelated females and their young (both male and female). Fully grown males are usually solitary outside the [breeding season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breeding_season).[^cite_note-marsan75-13] The [wolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf) is the wild boar's main predator in most of its natural range except in the [Far East](11672.md) and the [Lesser Sunda Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser_Sunda_Islands), where it is replaced by the [tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiger) and [Komodo dragon](17360.md) respectively.[^cite_note-baskin2003-14][^cite_note-auffenberg1981-15] The wild boar has a long history of association with [humans](682482.md), having been the ancestor of most [domestic pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestic_pig) breeds and a [big-game animal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big-game_hunting) for millennia. Boars have also re-[hybridized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_\(biology\)) in recent decades with [feral pigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feral_pig); these [boar–pig hybrids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boar%E2%80%93pig_hybrid) have become a serious pest wild animal in the [Americas](29833.md) and [Australia](4689264.md).

## Terminology

As true wild boars became extinct in [Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain) before the development of [Modern English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_English), the same terms are often used for both true wild boar and pigs, especially large or semi-wild ones. The [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) [*boar*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/boar#English) stems from the [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English) *[bār](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/bar#Old_English)*, which is thought to be derived from the [West Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Germanic_languages) *[bair](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Reconstruction\:Proto-West%20Germanic/bair#Proto-West_Germanic)*, of unknown origin.[^cite_note-16] *Boar* is sometimes used specifically to refer to males, and may also be used to refer to male domesticated pigs, especially breeding males that have not been castrated.[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18]

*Sow*, the traditional name for a female, again comes from Old English and Germanic; it stems from [Proto-Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_language), and is related to the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) [sus](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/sus#Latin) and [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) [*hus*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E1%BD%97%CF%82#Ancient_Greek), and more closely to the [New High German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_High_German) *[Sau](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Sau#German)*. The young may be called *piglets* or *boarlets*.[^cite_note-19]

The animals' specific name [*scrofa*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/scrofa#Latin) is [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) for 'sow'.[^cite_note-20]

## Taxonomy and evolution



Skull of *[Sus strozzii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sus_strozzi)* ([Museo di Storia Naturale di Firenze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museo_di_Storia_Naturale_di_Firenze)), a [Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene) suid that was outcompeted by *S. scrofa*

[MtDNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MtDNA) studies indicate that the wild boar originated from islands in Southeast Asia such as [Indonesia](14579.md) and the [Philippines](23440.md), and subsequently spread onto mainland Eurasia and North Africa.[^cite_note-chen2007-8] The earliest fossil finds of the species come from both Europe and Asia, and date back to the [Early Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Pleistocene).[^cite_note-ruvinsky-22] By the late [Villafranchian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villafranchian), *S. scrofa* largely displaced the related *[S. strozzii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sus_strozzi)*, a large, possibly swamp-adapted suid ancestral to the modern *[S. verrucosus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Javan_warty_pig)* throughout the Eurasian mainland, restricting it to insular Asia.[^cite_note-kurten1968-9] Its closest wild relative is the [bearded pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bornean_bearded_pig) of [Malacca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malacca) and surrounding islands.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4]

### Subspecies



Wild boar (left) and [domestic pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestic_pig) (right) skulls: Note the greatly shortened facial region of the latter.

[^cite_note-23]

As of 2005, 16 subspecies are recognised, which are divided into four regional groupings:[^cite_note-msw3-2]

- **Western:** Includes *S. s. scrofa*, *S. s. meridionalis*, *S. s. algira*, *S. s. attila*, *S. s. lybicus*, *S. s. majori* and *S. s. nigripes*. These subspecies are typically high-skulled (though *lybicus* and some *scrofa* are low-skulled), with thick underwool and (excepting *scrofa* and *attila*) poorly developed [manes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mane_\(horse\)).

  [^cite_note-groves1993-24]

- **Indian:** Includes *S. s. davidi* and *S. s. cristatus*. These subspecies have sparse or absent underwool, with long manes and prominent bands on the snout and mouth. While *S. s. cristatus* is high-skulled, *S. s. davidi* is low-skulled.

  [^cite_note-groves1993-24]

- **Eastern:** Includes *S. s. sibiricus*, *S. s. ussuricus*, *S. s. leucomystax*, *S. s. riukiuanus*, *S. s. taivanus* and *S. s. moupinensis*. These subspecies are characterised by a whitish streak extending from the corners of the mouth to the lower jaw. With the exception of *S. s. ussuricus*, most are high-skulled. The underwool is thick, except in *S. s. moupinensis*, and the mane is largely absent.

  [^cite_note-groves1993-24]

- **Indonesian:** Represented solely by *S. s. vittatus*, it is characterised by its sparse body hair, lack of underwool, fairly long mane, a broad reddish band extending from the muzzle to the sides of the neck.

  [^cite_note-groves1993-24]

  &#x20;It is the most [basal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basal_\(phylogenetics\)) of the four groups, having the smallest relative brain size, more primitive dentition and unspecialised cranial structure.

  [^cite_note-hemmer1990-25]

### Domestication



Male wild boar-domestic pig hybrid

With the exception of domestic pigs in [Timor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timor) and [Papua New Guinea](22943.md) (which appear to be of [Sulawesi warty pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celebes_warty_pig) stock), the wild boar is the ancestor of most [pig breeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_pig_breeds).[^cite_note-hemmer1990-25][^cite_note-46] Archaeological evidence suggests that pigs were [domesticated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domesticated_animal) from wild boar as early as 13,000–12,700 BCE in the [Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_East) in the [Tigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris) Basin,[^cite_note-47] being managed in the wild in a way similar to the way they are managed by some modern New Guineans.[^cite_note-48] Remains of pigs have been dated to earlier than 11,400 BCE in [Cyprus](5593.md). Those animals must have been introduced from the mainland, which suggests domestication in the adjacent mainland by then.[^cite_note-49] There was also a separate domestication in China, which took place about 8,000 years ago.[^cite_note-giuffra-50][^cite_note-51]

[DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA) evidence from sub-fossil remains of teeth and jawbones of Neolithic pigs shows that the first domestic pigs in Europe had been brought from the Near East. This stimulated the domestication of local European wild boars, resulting in a third domestication event with the Near Eastern genes dying out in European pig stock. Modern domesticated pigs have involved complex exchanges, with European domesticated lines being exported in turn to the ancient Near East.[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53] Historical records indicate that Asian pigs were introduced into Europe during the 18th and early 19th centuries.[^cite_note-giuffra-50] Domestic pigs tend to have much more developed hindquarters than their wild boar ancestors, to the point where 70% of their body weight is concentrated in the posterior, which is the opposite of wild boar, where most of the muscles are concentrated on the head and shoulders.[^cite_note-scheggi87-54]

### Synonymous species

The Heude's pig (*Sus bucculentus*), also known as the Indochinese warty pig or Vietnam warty pig, was an alleged pig species found in [Laos](17752.md) and [Vietnam](202354.md). It was virtually unknown and was feared [extinct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinct), until the discovery of a skull from a recently killed individual in the [Annamite Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annamite_Range), Laos, in 1995.[^cite_note-55] Subsequent studies indicated that *Sus bucculentus* was not a valid taxon.[^cite_note-56][^cite_note-57][^cite_note-58] As of 2022 the Mammal Diversity Database included it in *Sus scrofa*.[^cite_note-mammaldiversity.org-6]

## Description



The skull of a wild boar



Dentition, as illustrated by Charles Knight

The wild boar is a bulky, massively built suid with short and relatively thin legs. The trunk is short and robust, while the hindquarters are comparatively underdeveloped. The region behind the shoulder blades rises into a hump and the neck is short and thick to the point of being nearly immobile. The animal's head is very large, taking up to one-third of the body's entire length.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] The structure of the head is well suited for digging. The head acts as a plough, while the powerful neck muscles allow the animal to upturn considerable amounts of soil:[^cite_note-marsan70-59] it is capable of digging 8–10 cm (3–4 in) into frozen ground and can upturn rocks weighing 40–50 kg (88–110 lb).[^cite_note-baskin2003-14] The eyes are small and deep-set and the ears long and broad. The species has well developed [canine teeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canine_teeth), which protrude from the mouths of adult males. The medial hooves are larger and more elongated than the lateral ones and are capable of quick movements.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] The animal can run at a maximum speed of 40 km/h (25 mph) and jump at a height of 140–150 cm (55–59 in).[^cite_note-baskin2003-14]

[Sexual dimorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_dimorphism) is very pronounced in the species, with males being typically 5–10% larger and 20–30% heavier than females. Males also sport a mane running down the back, which is particularly apparent during autumn and winter.[^cite_note-marsan26-60] The canine teeth are also much more prominent in males and grow throughout life. The upper canines are relatively short and grow sideways early in life, though they gradually curve upwards. The lower canines are much sharper and longer, with the exposed parts measuring 10–12 cm (3.9–4.7 in) in length. In the breeding period, males develop a coating of [subcutaneous tissue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subcutaneous_tissue), which may be 2–3 cm (0.79–1.18 in) thick, extending from the shoulder blades to the rump, thus protecting vital organs during fights. Males sport a roughly chicken egg–sized sac of unclear function near the opening of the penis that collects urine and emits a sharp odour.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4]



Skeleton, as illustrated by [Richard Lydekker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Lydekker).



A European wild boar piglet, painted by [Hans Hoffman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Hoffmann_\(painter\)) in 1578. Note the stripes, a characteristic feature of piglets.

Adult size and weight is largely determined by environmental factors; boars living in arid areas with little productivity tend to attain smaller sizes than their counterparts inhabiting areas with abundant food and water. In most of [Europe](9239.md), males average 75–100 kg (165–220 lb) in weight, 75–80 cm (30–31 in) in shoulder height and 150 cm (59 in) in body length, whereas females average 60–80 kg (130–180 lb) in weight, 70 cm (28 in) in shoulder height and 140 cm (55 in) in body length. In Europe's [Mediterranean regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Europe), males may reach average weights as low as 50 kg (110 lb) and females 45 kg (99 lb), with shoulder heights of 63–65 cm (25–26 in). In the more productive areas of [Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe), males average 110–130 kg (240–290 lb) in weight, 95 cm (37 in) in shoulder height and 160 cm (63 in) in body length, while females weigh 95 kg (209 lb), reach 85–90 cm (33–35 in) in shoulder height, and reach 145 cm (57 in) in body length. In [Western](33800.md) and [Central Europe](5188.md), the largest males weigh 200 kg (440 lb) and females 120 kg (260 lb). In Northeastern Asia, large males can reach [brown bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_bear)-like sizes, weighing 270 kg (600 lb) and measuring 110–118 cm (43–46 in) in shoulder height. Some adult males in [Primorsky Krai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primorsky_Krai) and [Manchuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchuria) have been recorded to weigh 300–350 kg (660–770 lb) and measure 125 cm (49 in) in shoulder height. Adults of this size are generally immune from wolf predation.[^cite_note-marsan28-61] Such giants are rare in modern times, as past overhunting has prevented animals from attaining their full growth.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4]

The winter coat consists of long, coarse bristles underlaid with short brown downy fur. The length of these bristles varies along the body, with the shortest being around the face and limbs and the longest running along the back. These back bristles form the aforementioned mane prominent in males and stand erect when the animal is agitated. Colour is highly variable; specimens around [Lake Balkhash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Balkhash) are very lightly coloured, and can even be white, while some boars from [Belarus](3457.md) and Ussuriland can be black. Some subspecies sport a light-coloured patch running backward from the corners of the mouth. Coat colour also varies with age, with piglets having light brown or rusty-brown fur with pale bands extending from the flanks and back.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4]

The wild boar produces a number of different sounds which are divided into three categories:

- **Contact calls**: Grunting noises which differ in intensity according to the situation.

  [^cite_note-cabanau29-62]

  &#x20;Adult males are usually silent, while females frequently grunt and piglets whine.

  [^cite_note-heptner1988-4]

  &#x20;When feeding, boars express their contentment through purring. Studies have shown that piglets imitate the sounds of their mother, thus different litters may have unique vocalisations.

  [^cite_note-cabanau29-62]

- **Alarm calls**: Warning cries emitted in response to threats.

  [^cite_note-cabanau29-62]

  &#x20;When frightened, boars make loud huffing *ukh! ukh!* sounds or emit screeches transcribed as *gu-gu-gu*.

  [^cite_note-heptner1988-4]

- **Combat calls**: High-pitched, piercing cries.

  [^cite_note-cabanau29-62]

Its sense of [smell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olfaction) is very well developed to the point that the animal is used for drug detection in Germany.[^cite_note-cabanau28-63] Its [hearing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearing) is also acute, though its [eyesight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyesight) is comparatively weak,[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] lacking [color vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_vision)[^cite_note-cabanau28-63] and being unable to recognise a standing human 10–15 metres (33–49 ft) away.[^cite_note-baskin2003-14]

Pigs are one of four known mammalian taxa which possess mutations in the [nicotinic acetylcholine receptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicotinic_acetylcholine_receptor) that protect against snake venom. [Mongooses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongoose), [honey badgers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honey_badger), [hedgehogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedgehog), and pigs all have modifications to the receptor pocket which prevents the [snake venom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snake_venom) [α-neurotoxin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snake_venom#Neurotoxins) from binding. These represent four separate, independent mutations.[^cite_note-64]

## Social behaviour and life cycle

Boars are typically social animals, living in [female-dominated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matriarchy) [sounders](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/sounder#Etymology_4) consisting of barren sows and mothers with young led by an old matriarch. Male boars leave their sounder at the age of 8–15 months, while females either remain with their mothers or establish new territories nearby. Subadult males may live in loosely knit groups, while adult and elderly males tend to be solitary outside the breeding season.[^cite_note-marsan75-13][^cite_note-66]



Central European wild boar (*S. s. scrofa*) piglets suckling

The breeding period in most areas lasts from November to January, though most mating only lasts a month and a half. Prior to mating, the males develop their subcutaneous armour in preparation for confronting rivals. The [testicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testicles) double in size and the glands secrete a foamy yellowish liquid. Once ready to reproduce, males travel long distances in search of a sounder of sows, eating little on the way. Once a sounder has been located, the male drives off all young animals and persistently chases the sows. At this point, the male fiercely fights potential rivals.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] A single male can mate with 5–10 sows.[^cite_note-baskin2003-14] By the end of the rut, males are often badly mauled and have lost 20% of their body weight,[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] with bite-induced injuries to the penis being common.[^cite_note-67] The [gestation period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestation_period) varies according to the age of the expecting mother. For first-time breeders, it lasts 114–130 days, while it lasts 133–140 days in older sows. Farrowing occurs between March and May, with litter sizes depending on the age and nutrition of the mother. The average litter consists of 4–6 piglets, with the maximum being 10–12.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4][^cite_note-69] The piglets are [whelped](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whelping) in a nest constructed from twigs, grasses and leaves. Should the mother die prematurely, the piglets are adopted by the other sows in the sounder.[^cite_note-marsan83-70]



Piglet standing in grass. All wild boar piglets are dark brown with pale longitudinal stripes. After three to four months, these stripes disappear completely.

Newborn piglets weigh around 600–1,000 grams, lacking underfur and bearing a single milk incisor and canine on each half of the jaw.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] There is intense competition between the piglets over the most milk-rich nipples, as the best-fed young grow faster and have stronger constitutions.[^cite_note-marsan83-70] The piglets do not leave the lair for their first week of life. Should the mother be absent, the piglets lie closely pressed to each other. By two weeks of age, the piglets begin accompanying their mother on her journeys. Should danger be detected, the piglets take cover or stand immobile, relying on their camouflage to keep them hidden. The neonatal coat fades after three months, with adult colouration being attained at eight months. Although the [lactation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactation) period lasts 2.5–3.5 months, the piglets begin displaying adult feeding behaviours at the age of 2–3 weeks. The [permanent dentition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permanent_teeth) is fully formed by 1–2 years. With the exception of the canines in males, the teeth stop growing during the middle of the fourth year. The canines in old males continue to grow throughout their lives, curving strongly as they age. Sows attain [sexual maturity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_maturity) at the age of one year, with males attaining it a year later. However, [estrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estrus) usually first occurs after two years in sows, while males begin participating in the rut after 4–5 years, as they are not permitted to mate by the older males.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] The maximum lifespan in the wild is 10–14 years, though few specimens survive past 4–5 years.[^cite_note-marsan87-71] Boars in captivity have lived for 20 years.[^cite_note-baskin2003-14]

## Behaviour and ecology

### Habitat and sheltering



An individual from higher ridges of Himalayas at 2,900 m (9,600 ft) in [Pangolakha Wildlife Sanctuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pangolakha_Wildlife_Sanctuary), Sikkim, India



Wild boars frequently wallow in mud, possibly to regulate temperature or remove parasites

The wild boar inhabits a diverse array of [habitats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitat) from boreal [taigas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiga) to [deserts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert).[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] In mountainous regions, it can even occupy [alpine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_climate) zones, occurring up to 1,900 m (6,200 ft) in the [Carpathians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpathians), 2,600 m (8,500 ft) in the [Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasus) and up to 3,600–4,000 m (11,800–13,100 ft) in the mountains in [Central Asia](6742.md) and [Kazakhstan](16642.md).[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] In order to survive in a given area, wild boars require a habitat fulfilling three conditions: heavily brushed areas providing shelter from predators, water for drinking and bathing purposes and an absence of regular snowfall.[^cite_note-marsan55-72]

The main habitats favored by boars in [Europe](9239.md) are [deciduous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate_deciduous_forest) and [mixed forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate_broadleaf_and_mixed_forest), with the most favorable areas consisting of forest composed of [oak](39696.md) and [beech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beech) enclosing [marshes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsh) and [meadows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meadow). In the [Białowieża Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bia%C5%82owie%C5%BCa_Forest), the animal's primary habitat consists of well-developed [broad-leaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broad-leaved_tree) and mixed forests, along with marshy mixed forests, with [coniferous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinophyta) forests and undergrowths being of secondary importance. Forests made up entirely of oak groves and beeches are used only during the fruit-bearing season. This is in contrast to the Caucasian and [Transcaucasian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcaucasia) mountain areas, where boars will occupy such fruit-bearing forests year-round. In the mountainous areas of the [Russian Far East](173135.md), the species inhabits [nutpine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pine) groves, hilly mixed forests where [Mongolian oak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongolian_oak) and [Korean pine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_pine) are present, swampy mixed taiga and coastal oak forests. In [Transbaikalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transbaikal), boars are restricted to river valleys with nut pine and shrubs. Boars are regularly encountered in [pistachio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pistachio) groves in winter in some areas of [Tajikistan](30108.md) and [Turkmenistan](198149.md), while in spring they migrate to open deserts; boar have also colonized deserts in several areas they have been introduced to.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4][^cite_note-marsan55-72][^cite_note-73]

On the islands of [Komodo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komodo_\(island\)) and [Rinca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rinca), the boar mostly inhabits savanna or open monsoon forests, avoiding heavily forested areas unless pursued by humans.[^cite_note-auffenberg1981-15] Wild boar are known to be competent swimmers, capable of covering long distances. In 2013, one boar was reported to have completed the 11-kilometre (7 mi) swim from [France](5843419.md) to [Alderney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alderney) in the [Channel Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_Islands). Due to concerns about disease, it was shot and incinerated.[^cite_note-74]

Wild boar rest in shelters, which contain insulating material like [spruce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spruce) branches and dry [hay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hay). These resting places are occupied by whole families (though males lie separately) and are often located in the vicinity of streams, in swamp forests and in tall grass or shrub thickets. Boars never defecate in their shelters and will cover themselves with soil and pine needles when irritated by insects.[^cite_note-baskin2003-14]

### Diet



Male Indian wild boar feeding on a [chital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chital) (*Axis axis*) carcass

The wild boar is a highly versatile [omnivore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnivore), whose diversity in choice of food is comparable to that of [humans](682482.md).[^cite_note-marsan70-59] Its foods can be divided into four categories:

- [Rhizomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhizome), [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root), [tubers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuber) and [bulbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulb), all of which it digs up throughout the year.

  [^cite_note-heptner1988-4]

- [Nuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nut_\(fruit\)), [berries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berries) and [seeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seed), which it digs up from under the snow when necessary and consumes when ripened.

  [^cite_note-heptner1988-4]

- [Leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaves), [bark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bark_\(botany\)), twigs and [shoots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoot_\(botany\)), along with [garbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garbage).

  [^cite_note-heptner1988-4]

- In warm periods, it eats [earthworms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthworm), [insects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insect), [mollusks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mollusk), [fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish), [rodents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodent), [insectivores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insectivora), [bird eggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_egg), [lizards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lizard), [snakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snake), [frogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frog) and [carrion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrion).

  [^cite_note-heptner1988-4]

A 50 kg (110 lb) boar needs around 4,000–4,500 [calories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calorie) of food per day, though this required amount increases during winter and pregnancy,[^cite_note-marsan70-59] with the majority of its diet consisting of food items dug from the ground, like underground plant material and burrowing animals.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] [Acorns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acorn) and [beechnuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beech) are invariably its most important food items in [temperate zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate_climate),[^cite_note-75] as they are rich in the [carbohydrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbohydrate) necessary for the buildup of fat reserves needed to survive lean periods.[^cite_note-marsan70-59] In Western Europe, underground plant material favoured by boars includes [bracken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pteridium_aquilinum), [willow herb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epilobium), bulbs, meadow herb roots and bulbs and the bulbs of cultivated crops. Such food is favoured in early spring and summer, but may also be eaten in autumn and winter during beechnut and acorn crop failures. Should regular wild foods become scarce, boars will eat tree bark and [fungi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungi), as well as visit cultivated [potato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato) and [artichoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artichoke) fields.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] Boar soil disturbance and foraging have been shown to [facilitate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_facilitation) [invasive](67554.md) plants.[^cite_note-76][^cite_note-77] Boars of the *vittatus* subspecies in [Ujung Kulon National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ujung_Kulon_National_Park) in Java differ from most other populations by their primarily frugivorous diet, which consists of 50 different fruit species, especially [figs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ficus), thus making them important seed dispersers.[^cite_note-oliver1993-5] The wild boar can consume numerous genera of [poisonous plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_poisonous_plants) without ill effect, including *[Aconitum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aconitum)*, *[Anemone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anemone)*, *[Calla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calla)*, *[Caltha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caltha)*, *[Ferula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferula)* and *[Pteridium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pteridium)*.[^cite_note-baskin2003-14]

Boars may occasionally prey on small [vertebrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertebrate) like newborn [deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deer) fawns, [leporids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leporidae) and [galliform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galliformes) chicks, as well as small [calves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle), [lambs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheep) and other [livestock](25160767.md).[^cite_note-marsan70-59] Boars inhabiting the [Volga Delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volga_Delta) and near some lakes and rivers of Kazakhstan have been recorded to feed extensively on fish like [carp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carp) and [Caspian roach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vobla). Boars in the former area also feed on [cormorant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cormorant) and [heron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heron) chicks, [bivalved molluscs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juliidae), trapped [muskrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muskrat) and mice.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] There is at least one record of a wild boar killing and eating a [bonnet macaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonnet_macaque) in southern India's [Bandipur National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandipur_National_Park), though this may have been a case of [intraguild predation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intraguild_predation), brought on by [interspecific competition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interspecific_competition) for human handouts.[^cite_note-78] There is also at least one recorded case of a group of wild boar attacking, killing, and eating an adult, healthy female axis deer (*[Axis axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_axis)*) as a pack.[^cite_note-79]

Stable isotope analysis of fossil wild boar tooth enamel from the late Middle Pleistocene found in Thailand indicate that it fed on a versatile mixed vegetation.[^cite_note-80]

### Predators



[Tigers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiger) killing a wild boar in [Kanha Tiger Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanha_Tiger_Reserve)

Piglets are vulnerable to attack from medium-sized felids like [Eurasian lynx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_lynx) (*Lynx lynx*), [jungle cats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungle_cat) (*Felis chaus*), and [snow leopards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snow_leopard) (*Panthera uncia*), as well as other carnivorans like [brown bears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_bear) (*Ursus arctos*) and [yellow-throated martens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow-throated_marten) (*Martes flavigula*).[^cite_note-heptner1988-4]

The [wolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf) (*Canis lupus*) is the main predator of wild boar throughout most of its range. A single wolf can kill around 50 to 80 boars of differing ages in one year.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] In Italy[^cite_note-marsan96-81] and Belarus' [Belovezhskaya Pushcha National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belovezhskaya_Pushcha_National_Park), boars are the wolf's primary prey, despite an abundance of alternative, less powerful ungulates.[^cite_note-marsan96-81] Wolves are particularly threatening during the winter, when deep snow impedes the boars' movements. In the Baltic regions, heavy snowfall can allow wolves to eliminate boars from an area almost completely. Wolves primarily target piglets and subadults and only rarely attack adult sows. Adult males are usually avoided entirely.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] [Dholes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhole) (*Cuon alpinus*) may also prey on boars, to the point of keeping their numbers down in northwestern Bhutan, despite there being many more [cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle) in the area.[^cite_note-82]



[Banded pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banded_pig) (*S. s. vittatus*) being eaten by [Komodo dragons](17360.md)

[Leopards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopard) (*Panthera pardus*) are predators of wild boar in the Caucasus (particularly Transcaucasia), the Russian Far East, India, China[^cite_note-sovietleopard-83] and Iran. In most areas, boars constitute only a small part of the leopard's diet. However, in Iran's Sarigol National Park, boars are the second most frequently targeted prey species after [mouflon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mouflon) (*Ovis gmelini*), though adult individuals are generally avoided, as they are above the leopard's preferred weight range of 10–40 kg (22–88 lb).[^cite_note-84] This dependence on wild boar is largely due in part to the local leopard subspecies' large size.[^cite_note-85]

Boars of all ages were once the primary prey of the [tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiger) (*Panthera tigris*) in Transcaucasia, Kazakhstan, Middle Asia and the Far East up until the late 19th century. In modern times, tiger numbers are too low to have a limiting effect on boar populations. A single tiger can systematically destroy an entire sounder by preying on its members one by one, before moving on to another sounder. Tigers have been noted to chase boars for longer distances than with other prey. In two rare cases, boars were reported to gore a small tiger and a tigress to death in self-defense.[^cite_note-soviettiger-86] A "large male tiger" died of wounds inflicted by an old wild boar it had killed in "a battle royal" between the two animals.[^cite_note-Prynn-1980-87]<sup>: 500 </sup>

In the [Amur region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amur_River), wild boars are one of the two most important prey species for [Siberian tigers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siberian_tiger), alongside the [Manchurian wapiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchurian_wapiti) (*Cervus canadensis xanthopygus*), with the two species collectively comprising roughly 80% of the felid's prey.[^cite_note-j1-88] In [Sikhote Alin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhote_Alin), a tiger can kill 30–34 boars a year.[^cite_note-baskin2003-14] Studies of tigers in India indicate that boars are usually secondary in preference to various [cervids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cervidae) and [bovids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bovidae), though when boars are targeted, healthy adults are caught more frequently than young and sick specimens.[^cite_note-89]

On the islands of [Komodo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komodo_\(island\)), [Rinca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rinca) and [Flores](11426.md), the boar's main predator is the [Komodo dragon](17360.md) (*Varanus komodoensis*).[^cite_note-auffenberg1981-15]

## Distribution and habitat

### Reconstructed range

The species originally occurred in [North Africa](21714.md) and much of [Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasia); from the [British Isles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Isles) to [Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea) and the [Sunda Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunda_Islands). The northern limit of its range extended from southern [Scandinavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scandinavia) to southern [Siberia](29639.md) and [Japan](15573.md). Within this range, it was only absent in extremely dry deserts and [alpine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_climate) zones. It was once found in North Africa along the [Nile](21244.md) valley up to [Khartoum](17320.md) and north of the [Sahara](325363.md). The species occurs on a few [Ionian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionian_Islands) and [Aegean Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_Islands), sometimes swimming between islands.[^cite_note-masseti2012-90] The reconstructed northern boundary of the animal's Asian range ran from [Lake Ladoga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Ladoga) (at 60°N) through the area of [Novgorod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novgorod) and [Moscow](19004.md) into the southern [Urals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ural_\(region\)), where it reached 52°N. From there, the boundary passed [Ishim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishim,_Tyumen_Oblast) and farther east the [Irtysh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irtysh) at 56°N. In the eastern [Baraba steppe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baraba_steppe) (near [Novosibirsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novosibirsk)) the boundary turned steep south, encircled the [Altai Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altai_Mountains) and went again eastward including the [Tannu-Ola Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tannu-Ola_Mountains) and [Lake Baikal](56865.md). From here, the boundary went slightly north of the [Amur River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amur_River) eastward to its lower reaches at the [Sea of Okhotsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_of_Okhotsk). On [Sakhalin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakhalin), there are only [fossil](10958.md) reports of wild boar. The southern boundaries in Europe and Asia were almost invariably identical to the seashores of these continents. It is absent in the dry regions of [Mongolia](19271.md) from 44 to 46°N southward, in China westward of [Sichuan](65185.md) and in India north of the [Himalayas](37966.md). It is absent in the higher elevations of the [Pamir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pamir_Mountains) and the [Tian Shan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tian_Shan), though they do occur in the [Tarim Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarim_Basin) and on the lower slopes of the Tian Shan.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4]

### Present range

In recent centuries, the range of wild boar has changed dramatically, largely due to hunting by humans and more recently because of captive wild boar escaping into the wild. Prior to the 20th century, boar populations had declined in numerous areas, with British populations probably becoming extinct during the 13th century.[^cite_note-britishwildboar.org.uk-91] In the warm period after the ice age, wild boar lived in the southern parts of [Sweden](5058739.md) and [Norway](21241.md) and north of Lake Ladoga in [Karelia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karelia).[^cite_note-92] It was previously thought that the species did not live in [Finland](10577.md) during prehistory because no prehistoric wild boar bones had been found within the borders of the country.[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94] It was not until 2013, when a wild boar bone was found in [Askola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Askola), that the species was found to have lived in Finland more than 8,000 years ago. It is believed, however, that man prevented its establishment by hunting.[^cite_note-95][^cite_note-96] In [Denmark](76972.md), the last boar was shot at the beginning of the 19th century, and by 1900 they were absent in Tunisia and Sudan and large areas of Germany, Austria and Italy. In Russia, they were extirpated in wide areas by the 1930s.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] The last boar in [Egypt](8087628.md) reportedly died on 20 December 1912 in the [Giza Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giza_Zoo), with wild populations having disappeared by 1894–1902. [Prince Kamal el Dine Hussein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Kamal_el_Dine_Hussein) attempted to repopulate [Wadi El Natrun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadi_El_Natrun) with boars of Hungarian stock, but they were quickly exterminated by poachers.[^cite_note-osborn1980-97]

A revival of boar populations began in the middle of the 20th century. By 1950, wild boar had once again reached their original northern boundary in many parts of their Asiatic range. By 1960, they reached [Leningrad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leningrad) and Moscow and by 1975, they were to be found in [Arkhangelsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arkhangelsk) and [Astrakhan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrakhan). In the 1970s they again occurred in Denmark and Sweden, where captive animals escaped and now survive in the wild. Boars have since spread into Norway and by the 2020s have established breeding populations in the southeast of the country.[^cite_note-98] In England, wild boar populations re-established themselves in the 1990s, after escaping from specialist farms that had imported European stock.[^cite_note-britishwildboar.org.uk-91]

### Status in Great Britain



Mixed sounder of wild boar and domestic pigs at Culzie, [Scotland](26994.md)

By the 11th century, wild boars were apparently already becoming rare in Britain. A 1087 forestry law enacted by [William the Conqueror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_the_Conqueror) punished through blinding the unlawful killing of a boar. [Charles I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_of_England) attempted to reintroduce the species into the [New Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Forest), but this population was exterminated in the 17th century during the [English Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Civil_War). Between their medieval extinction and the 1980s, when wild boar farming began, only a handful of captive wild boar, imported from the continent, were present in Britain. Occasional escapes of wild boar from wildlife parks have occurred as early as the 1970s, but since the early 1990s significant populations have re-established themselves after escapes from farms, the number of which has increased as the demand for meat from the species has grown. A 1998 [MAFF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Agriculture,_Fisheries_and_Food_\(United_Kingdom\)) (now [DEFRA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_for_Environment,_Food_and_Rural_Affairs)) study on wild boar living wild in Britain confirmed the presence of two populations of wild boar living in Britain; one in [Kent](16766.md)/[East Sussex](67923.md) and another in [Dorset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorset).[^cite_note-britishwildboar.org.uk-91]

Another DEFRA report, in February 2008,[^cite_note-99] confirmed the existence of these two sites as 'established breeding areas' and identified a third in [Gloucestershire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloucestershire)/[Herefordshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herefordshire); in the [Forest of Dean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_of_Dean)/[Ross on Wye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross_on_Wye) area. A 'new breeding population' was also identified in [Devon](8166.md). There is another significant population in [Dumfries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumfries) and [Galloway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galloway). Populations estimates were as follows:

- The largest population, in Kent/East Sussex, was then estimated at 200 animals in the core distribution area.

- The smallest, in west Dorset, was estimated to be fewer than 50 animals.

- Since winter 2005–2006 significant escapes/releases have also resulted in animals colonizing areas around the fringes of [Dartmoor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmoor), in [Devon](8166.md). These are considered as an additional single 'new breeding population' and currently estimated to be up to 100 animals.

Population estimates for the Forest of Dean are disputed as, at the time that the DEFRA population estimate was 100, a photo of a boar sounder in the forest near Staunton with over 33 animals visible was published and at about the same time over 30 boar were seen in a field near the original escape location of Weston under Penyard many kilometres or miles away. In early 2010 the Forestry Commission embarked on a cull,[^cite_note-100] with the aim of reducing the boar population from an estimated 150 animals to 100. By August it was stated that efforts were being made to reduce the population from 200 to 90, but that only 25 had been killed.[^cite_note-101] The failure to meet cull targets was confirmed in February 2011.[^cite_note-102]

Wild boars have crossed the [River Wye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Wye) into [Monmouthshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monmouthshire), Wales. Iolo Williams, the BBC Wales wildlife expert, attempted to film Welsh boar in late 2012.[^cite_note-103] Many other sightings, across the UK, have also been reported.[^cite_note-104] The effects of wild boar on the U.K.'s woodlands were discussed with Ralph Harmer of the [Forestry Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forestry_Commission) on the BBC Radio's *[Farming Today](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farming_Today)* radio programme in 2011. The programme prompted activist writer [George Monbiot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Monbiot) to propose a thorough population study, followed by the introduction of permit-controlled [culling](2980973.md).[^cite_note-guardian_blog_zoophobic_legacy-105]

In Scotland, wild boar are professionally referred to as 'feral pigs' as the genetics of the established feral populations may come from a mix of both wild boar and domestic pigs.[^cite_note-106] They are now known to be present in [Dumfries and Galloway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumfries_and_Galloway) and a number of sites in the [Highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Highlands), mainly centred around the [Loch Ness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loch_Ness) area.[^cite_note-107] They can be killed there legally without a license and are culled by land managers as wild populations appear occasionally.[^cite_note-108] As of 2024, an agency that advises the Scottish government estimates that Scotland is home to a few thousand wild boars. Locals around Loch Ness that were interviewed by *[The New York Times](30680.md)* believed that the boars were becoming an increasingly worse problem, and farmers noted that they had killed and eaten several lambs.[^cite_note-109]

### Introduction to North America



"[Razorbacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feral_pig)" confronting an [American alligator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_alligator) in [Florida](18933066.md)

Wild boars are an [invasive species](67554.md) in the Americas, having been introduced by European explorers and settlers in the 16th century to serve as a source of food. Wild boars now cause problems including out-competing native species for food, destroying the nests of ground-nesting species, killing fawns and young domestic livestock, destroying agricultural crops, eating tree seeds and seedlings, destroying native vegetation and wetlands through wallowing, damaging water quality, coming into violent conflict with humans and pets and carrying pig and human diseases including [brucellosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brucellosis), [trichinosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichinosis) and [pseudorabies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudorabies). In some jurisdictions, it is illegal to import, breed, release, possess, sell, distribute, trade, transport, hunt, or trap Eurasian boars. Hunting and trapping is done systematically, to increase the chance of eradication and to remove the incentive to illegally release boars, which have mostly been spread deliberately by sport hunters.[^cite_note-110]

#### History

While domestic pigs, both captive and feral (popularly termed "razorbacks"), have been in North America since the earliest days of [European colonization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_colonization_of_the_Americas), pure wild boars were not introduced into the New World until the 19th century. The suids were released into the wild by wealthy landowners as big game animals. The initial introductions took place in fenced enclosures, though several escapes occurred, with the escapees sometimes intermixing with already established feral pig populations.

The first of these introductions occurred in [New Hampshire](21134.md) in 1890. Thirteen wild boars from Germany were purchased by [Austin Corbin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austin_Corbin) from [Carl Hagenbeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Hagenbeck) and released into [a 9,500-hectare (23,000-acre) game preserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corbin_Park) in [Sullivan County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sullivan_County,_New_Hampshire). Several of these boars escaped, though they were quickly hunted down by locals. Two further introductions were made from the original stocking, with several escapes taking place due to breaches in the game preserve's fencing. These escapees have ranged widely, with some specimens having been observed crossing into [Vermont](32578.md).[^cite_note-mayer2009-111]

In 1902, 15–20 wild boar from Germany were released into a 3,200-hectare (7,900-acre) estate in [Hamilton County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton_County,_New_York), New York. Several specimens escaped six years later, dispersing into the [William C. Whitney Wilderness Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_C._Whitney_Wilderness_Area), with their descendants surviving for at least 20 years.[^cite_note-mayer2009-111]

The most extensive boar introduction in the US took place in western [North Carolina](21650.md) in 1912, when 13 boars of undetermined European origin were released into two fenced enclosures in a game preserve in [Hooper Bald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooper_Bald), Graham County. Most of the specimens remained in the preserve for the next decade, until a large-scale hunt caused the remaining animals to break through their confines and escape. Some of the boars migrated to [Tennessee](30395.md), where they intermixed with both free-ranging and feral pigs in the area. In 1924, a dozen Hooper Bald wild pigs were shipped to [California](5407.md) and released in a property between [Carmel Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carmel_River_\(California\)) and the [Los Padres National Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Padres_National_Forest). These hybrid boar were later used as breeding stock on various private and public lands throughout the state, as well as in other states like [Florida](18933066.md), [Georgia](48830.md), [South Carolina](27956.md), [West Virginia](32905.md) and [Mississippi](16949861.md).[^cite_note-mayer2009-111]

Several wild boars from [Leon Springs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Springs,_Texas) and the [San Antonio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Antonio_Zoo_and_Aquarium), [Saint Louis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Louis_Zoo) and [San Diego Zoos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Diego_Zoo) were released in the Powder Horn Ranch in [Calhoun County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calhoun_County,_Texas), Texas, in 1939. These specimens escaped and established themselves in surrounding ranchlands and coastal areas, with some crossing the [Espiritu Santo Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Espiritu_Santo_Bay) and colonizing [Matagorda Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matagorda_Island). Descendants of the Powder Horn Ranch boars were later released onto [San José Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Jos%C3%A9_Island_\(Texas\)) and the coast of [Chalmette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalmette,_Louisiana), Louisiana.[^cite_note-mayer2009-111]

Wild boar of unknown origin were stocked in a ranch in the [Edwards Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwards_Plateau) in the 1940s, only to escape during a storm and hybridize with local feral pig populations, later spreading into neighboring counties.[^cite_note-mayer2009-111]

Starting in the mid-1980s, several boars purchased from the San Diego Zoo and [Tierpark Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tierpark_Berlin) were released into the United States. A decade later, more specimens from farms in [Canada](5042916.md) and Białowieża Forest were let loose. In recent years, wild pig populations have been reported in 44 states within the US, most of which are likely wild boar–feral hog hybrids. Pure wild boar populations may still be present, but are extremely localized.[^cite_note-mayer2009-111]

### Introduction and lack of control in South America



Distribution of wild boar and "javaporco" in Brazil as of 2022. In red: [States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federative_units_of_Brazil) with registered sightings.

In [South America](26769.md), the European boar is believed to have been introduced for the first time in [Argentina](18951905.md) and [Uruguay](31843.md) around the 20th century for breeding purposes.[^cite_note-ref01-112] In [Brazil](3383.md), the creation of wild boar and hybrids started on a large scale in the mid-1990s. With the invasion of wild boar that crossed the border and entered [Rio Grande do Sul](256268.md) around 1989, and the escape and intentional release by several Brazilian breeders in the late 1990s – in response to a [IBAMA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBAMA) decision against the import and breeding of wild boar in 1998 – numerous [feral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feral) species formed a growing population, which progressively advances in Brazilian territory.[^cite_note-ref02-113][^cite_note-ref03-114]

#### Pest control in Brazil

As a form of control for the wild boar population, hunting and killing are allowed for [Collectors, Shooters and Hunters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectors,_Shooters_and_Hunters) (CACs)[^cite_note-cacjvl-115] duly registered by the environmental control agency, [IBAMA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBAMA), which, on the other hand, seeks to encourage the preservation of similar species of native [peccaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peccary), such as the *"[queixada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-lipped_peccary)"* and the *"[caititu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collared_peccary)"*.[^cite_note-pltstn-116][^cite_note-glbrbp-117][^cite_note-glbrl-118]

#### Effect on other habitats

Wild boars negatively impact other habitats through the destruction of the environment, or homes of wildlife. When wild boars invade new areas, they adapt to the new area by trampling and rooting, as well as displacing many saplings/nutrients. This causes a decrease in growing of many plants and trees. Water is also affected negatively by wild boars. When wild boars are active in streams, or small pools of water, it causes increased turbidity (excessive silt and particle suspension).[^cite_note-119] In some cases, the fecal coliform concentration increases to dangerous levels because of wild boars. Aquatic wildlife is affected, more prominently fish, and amphibians. Wild boars have caused a great decrease in over 300 animal or plant species, 250 being endangered or threatened.[^cite_note-120]

The boars cause many habitats to become less diverse because of their feeding behaviors and predation. Wild boars will dig up eggs of species and eat them, as well as killing other wildlife for food. When these boars compete with other species for resources, they usually come out successful.[^cite_note-121] A study published in the Journal of Experimental Marine Biology and Ecology was conducted on the results of Feral Swine control. Only two years after the control started, the amount of turtle nests jumped from 57 to 143, and the turtle nest predation percent dropped from 74 to 15.[^cite_note-122] They kill and eat deer, lizards, birds, snakes, and more. These boars are called "opportunist omnivores", which means they eat almost anything. This means they can survive almost anywhere. A big surplus of food and the ability to adapt to any new place causes lots of breeding. All of these factors make it difficult to get rid of wild boars.[^cite_note-123]

## Diseases and parasites



Lesions consistent with [bovine tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycobacterium_bovis) on the lower jaw and lung of a wild boar

Wild boars are known to host at least 20 different parasitic worm species, with maximum infections occurring in summer. Young animals are vulnerable to [helminths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helminth) like *[Metastrongylus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metastrongylus)*, which are consumed by boars through earthworms and cause death by parasitising the lungs. Wild boar also carry parasites known to infect humans, including *[Gastrodiscoides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastrodiscoides)*, *[Trichinella spiralis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichinella_spiralis)*, *[Taenia solium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taenia_solium)*, *[Balantidium coli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balantidium_coli)* and *[Toxoplasma gondii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxoplasma_gondii)*.[^cite_note-toxo-124] Wild boar in southern regions are frequently infested with [ticks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tick) (*[Dermacentor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dermacentor)*, *[Rhipicephalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhipicephalus)*, and *[Hyalomma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyalomma)*) and [hog lice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haematopinus_suis). The species also suffers from blood-sucking [flies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diptera), which it escapes by bathing frequently or hiding in dense shrubs.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4]

[Swine plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_swine_fever) spreads very quickly in wild boar, with [epizootics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epizootic) being recorded in Germany, Poland, Hungary, Belarus, the Caucasus, the Far East, Kazakhstan and other regions. [Foot-and-mouth disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot-and-mouth_disease) can also take on epidemic proportions in boar populations. The species occasionally, but rarely contracts *[Pasteurellosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasteurellosis)*, [hemorrhagic sepsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemorrhagic_septicemia), [tularemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tularemia), and [anthrax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthrax). Wild boar may on occasion contract swine [erysipelas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erysipelas) through rodents or hog lice and ticks.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4]

The wild boar features prominently in the cultures of [Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages) people, many of which saw the animal as embodying warrior virtues.[^cite_note-128] Cultures throughout Europe and Asia Minor saw the killing of a boar as proof of one's valor and strength. [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic) [hunter gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter_gatherers) depicted reliefs of ferocious wild boars on their temple pillars at [Göbekli Tepe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6bekli_Tepe) some 11,600 years ago.[^cite_note-129][^cite_note-130] Virtually all heroes in [Greek mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mythology) fight or kill a boar at one point. The [demigod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demigod) [Herakles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herakles)' [third labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labours_of_Hercules) involves the capture of the [Erymanthian boar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erymanthian_boar), [Theseus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theseus) slays the wild sow [Phaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crommyonian_Sow), and a disguised [Odysseus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odysseus) is recognised by his handmaiden [Eurycleia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurycleia) by the scars inflicted on him by a boar during a hunt in his youth.[^cite_note-mallory1997-131] To the mythical [Hyperboreans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperborea), the boar represented spiritual authority.[^cite_note-cabanau63-125] Several Greek myths use the boar as a symbol of darkness, death and winter.[^cite_note-132] One example is the story of the youthful [Adonis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adonis), who is killed by a boar and is permitted by [Zeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus) to depart from [Hades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_underworld) only during the spring and summer period. This theme also occurs in [Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_mythology) and [Egyptian mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_mythology), where the animal is explicitly linked to the month of October, therefore autumn. This association likely arose from aspects of the boar's actual nature. Its dark colour was linked to the night, while its solitary habits, proclivity to consume crops and nocturnal nature were associated with evil.[^cite_note-scheggi14-133] The [foundation myth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origin_myth) of [Ephesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephesus) has the city being built over the site where Prince Androklos of [Athens](1216.md) killed a boar.[^cite_note-scheggi16-134] Boars were frequently depicted on Greek funerary monuments alongside [lions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lion), representing gallant losers who have finally met their match, as opposed to victorious hunters as lions are. The theme of the doomed, yet valorous boar warrior also occurred in [Hittite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hittites) culture, where it was traditional to sacrifice a boar alongside a dog and a prisoner of war after a military defeat.[^cite_note-mallory1997-131]



The head of wild boar is prominent in the [crest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crest_\(heraldry\)) of the [Scottish](26994.md) [Clan Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clan_Campbell).&#x20;



3rd century sandstone Varaha sculpture from [Mathura](283812.md), depicting the Hindu boar god [Varaha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varaha) rescuing the earth, depicted as a goddess dangling from his tusks.

&#x20;In [East Asian Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asian_Buddhism), the goddess [Mārīcī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marici_\(Buddhism\)) is often depicted riding a boar.[^cite_note-135]

The boar as a warrior also appears in [Germanic cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_peoples), with its image having been frequently engraved on shields and swords. They also feature on [Germanic boar helmets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_boar_helmets), such as the [Benty Grange helmet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benty_Grange_helmet), where it was believed to offer protection to the wearer and has been theorised to have been used in spiritual transformations into swine, similar to [berserkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berserkers). The boar features heavily in religious practice in [Germanic paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_paganism) where it is closely associated with [Freyr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freyr) and has also been suggested to have been a totemic animal to the Swedes, especially to the [Yngling royal dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yngling) who claimed descent from the god.[^cite_note-Kovářová-136]

According to [Tacitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacitus), the [Baltic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_people) [Aesti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesti) featured boars on their helmets and may have also worn boar masks. The boar and pig were held in particularly high esteem by the [Celts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celts), who considered them to be their most important sacred animal. Some [Celtic deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_deities) linked to boars include [Moccus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moccus) and [Veteris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veteris). It has been suggested that some early myths surrounding the Welsh hero [Culhwch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culhwch) involved the character being the son of a boar god.[^cite_note-mallory1997-131] Nevertheless, the importance of the boar as a culinary item among Celtic tribes may have been exaggerated in popular culture by the *[Asterix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asterix)* series, as wild boar bones are rare among Celtic archaeological sites and the few that do occur show no signs of butchery, having probably been used in sacrificial rituals.[^cite_note-green2002-137]

The boar also appears in [Vedic mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_mythology) and [Hindu mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_mythology). A story present in the [Brahmanas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmanas) has the god [Indra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra) slaying an avaricious boar, who has stolen the treasure of the [asuras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asura_\(Hinduism\)), then giving its carcass to the god [Vishnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu), who offered it as a sacrifice to the gods. In the story's retelling in the [Charaka Samhita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charaka_Samhita), the boar is described as a form of [Prajapati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prajapati) and is credited with having raised the Earth from the primeval waters. In the [Ramayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramayana) and the [Puranas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puranas), the same boar is portrayed as [Varaha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varaha), an [avatar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avatar) of Vishnu.[^cite_note-138]



[Herakles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herakles) brings [Eurystheus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurystheus) the [Erymanthian boar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erymanthian_boar), as depicted on a black-figure amphora (c. 550 BC) from [Vulci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulci).

In [Japanese culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_culture), the boar is widely seen as a fearsome and reckless animal, to the point that several words and expressions in [Japanese](15606.md) referring to recklessness include references to boars. The boar is the last animal of the [Oriental zodiac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_zodiac), with people born during the [year of the Pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig_\(zodiac\)) being said to embody the boar-like traits of determination and impetuosity. Among Japanese hunters, the boar's courage and defiance is a source of admiration and it is not uncommon for hunters and [mountain people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_people) to name their sons after the animal *inoshishi* (猪). Boars are also seen as symbols of fertility and prosperity; in some regions, it is thought that boars are drawn to fields owned by families including pregnant women, and hunters with pregnant wives are thought to have greater chances of success when boar hunting. The animal's link to prosperity was illustrated by its inclusion on the [¥](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C2%A5)10 note during the [Meiji period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_period) and it was once believed that a man could become wealthy by keeping a clump of boar hair in his wallet.[^cite_note-knight2003-139]

In the folklore of the [Mongol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongolian_people) [Altai Uriankhai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altai_Uriankhai) tribe, the wild boar was associated with the watery underworld, as it was thought that the spirits of the dead entered the animal's head, to be ultimately transported to the water.[^cite_note-pegg2001-140] Prior to the conversion to Islam, the [Kyrgyz people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrgyz_people) believed that they were descended from boars and thus did not eat pork. In [Buryat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buryats) mythology, the forefathers of the Buryats descended from heaven and were nourished by a boar.[^cite_note-holmberg1927-141] In [China](5405.md), the boar is the emblem of the [Miao people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miao_people).[^cite_note-cabanau63-125]

The boar ([sanglier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boars_in_heraldry)) is frequently displayed in [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_heraldry), [Scottish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_heraldry) and [Welsh heraldry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_heraldry). As with the [lion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lion_\(heraldry\)), the boar is often shown as armed and [langued](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langued). As with the [bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bear_in_heraldry), Scottish and Welsh heraldry displays the boar's head with the neck cropped, unlike the English version, which retains the neck.[^cite_note-142] The [white boar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_boar) served as the [badge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldic_badge) of [King Richard III of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_III_of_England), who distributed it among his northern retainers during his tenure as [Duke of Gloucester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_of_Gloucester).[^cite_note-143]

### As a game animal and food source

Main article: [Boar hunting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boar_hunting)



Wild boar haunches and [trophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trophy_hunting), [Umbria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umbria), Italy



A wild boar dish served in [Helsinki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsinki), Finland

Humans have been hunting boar for millennia, the earliest artistic depictions of such activities dating back to the [Upper Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Paleolithic).[^cite_note-mallory1997-131] At some archaeological sites in China dating back to the [Holocene Climatic Optimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene_climatic_optimum) (HCO), boar composed up to 73% of all medium and large mammal remains, indicating heavy reliance on these suids as a food source.[^cite_note-144] The animal was seen as a source of food among the [Ancient Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece), as well as a sporting challenge and source of epic narratives. The [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) inherited this tradition, with one of its first practitioners being [Scipio Aemilianus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scipio_Aemilianus). Boar hunting became particularly popular among the young nobility during the 3rd century BC as preparation for manhood and battle. A typical Roman boar hunting tactic involved surrounding a given area with large nets, then flushing the boar with dogs and immobilizing it with smaller nets. The animal would then be dispatched with a *venabulum*, a short [spear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spear) with a [crossguard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossguard) at the base of the blade. More than their Greek predecessors, the Romans extensively took inspiration from boar hunting in their art and sculpture. With the ascension of [Constantine the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_the_Great), boar hunting took on Christian allegorical themes, with the animal being portrayed as a "black beast" analogous to [the dragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_George_and_the_Dragon) of [Saint George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_George).[^cite_note-scheggi9-145]

Boar hunting continued after the [fall of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire), though the Germanic tribes considered the [red deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_deer) to be a more noble and worthy quarry. The post-Roman nobility hunted boar as their predecessors did, but primarily as training for battle rather than sport. It was not uncommon for medieval hunters to deliberately hunt boars during the breeding season when the animals were more aggressive. During the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance), when [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation) and the introduction of [firearms](11966.md) reduced boar numbers, boar hunting became the sole prerogative of the nobility, one of many charges brought up against the rich during the [German Peasants' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Peasants'_War) and the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution).[^cite_note-scheggi9-145]

During the mid-20th century, 7,000–8,000 boars were caught in the Caucasus, 6,000–7,000 in Kazakhstan and about 5,000 in Central Asia during the [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet) period, primarily through the use of dogs and beats.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] In Nepal, farmers and poachers eliminate boars by baiting balls of wheat flour containing explosives with kerosene oil, with the animals' chewing motions triggering the devices.[^cite_note-146]

Wild boar can thrive in captivity, though piglets grow slowly and poorly without their mothers. Products derived from wild boar include meat, hide and bristles.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] *[Apicius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apicius)* devotes a whole chapter to the cooking of boar meat, providing 10 recipes involving roasting, boiling and what sauces to use. The Romans usually served boar meat with [garum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garum).[^cite_note-scheggi30-147] [Boar's head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boar's_Head_Feast) was the centrepiece of most medieval [Christmas](6237.md) celebrations among the nobility.[^cite_note-148] Although growing in popularity as a captive-bred source of food, the wild boar takes longer to mature than most domestic pigs and it is usually smaller and produces less meat. Nevertheless, wild boar meat is leaner and healthier than [pork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pork),[^cite_note-harris2009-149] being of higher [nutritional value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutritional_value) and having a much higher concentration of essential [amino acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid).[^cite_note-150] Most meat-dressing organizations agree that a boar carcass should yield 50 kg (110 lb) of meat on average. Large specimens can yield 15–20 kg (33–44 lb) of fat, with some giants yielding 30 kg (66 lb) or more. A boar hide can measure 3 [m<sup>2</sup>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_metre) (4,700 sq in) and can yield 350–1,000 grams (12–35 oz) of bristle and 400 grams (14 oz) of underwool.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4]

### Crop and garbage raiding



An adult sow and young that have broken open a litter bag in Berlin seeking food

Boars can be damaging to [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture) in situations where their natural habitat is sparse. Populations living on the outskirts of towns or farms can dig up [potatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato) and damage [melons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melon), [watermelons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermelon) and [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize). However, they generally only encroach upon farms when natural food is scarce. In the [Belovezh forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bia%C5%82owie%C5%BCa_Forest) for example, 34–47% of the local boar population will enter fields in years of moderate availability of natural foods. While the role of boars in damaging crops is often exaggerated,[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] cases are known of boar depredations causing [famines](51329.md), as was the case in [Hachinohe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hachinohe,_Aomori), Japan in 1749, where 3,000 people died of what became known as the "wild boar famine". Still, within Japanese culture, the boar's status as vermin is expressed through its title as "king of pests" and the popular saying (addressed to young men in rural areas) "When you get married, choose a place with no wild boar."[^cite_note-knight2003-139][^cite_note-151]

In Central Europe, farmers typically repel boars through distraction or fright, while in Kazakhstan it is usual to employ guard dogs in plantations. However, research shows that when compared with other mitigation tactics, hunting is the only strategy to significantly reduce crop damage by boars.[^cite_note-152] Although large boar populations can play an important role in limiting forest growth, they are also useful in keeping pest populations such as [June bugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_chafer) under control.[^cite_note-heptner1988-4] The growth of urban areas and the corresponding decline in natural boar habitats has led to some sounders entering human habitations in search of food. As in natural conditions, sounders in peri-urban areas are matriarchal, though males tend to be much less represented and adults of both sexes can be up to 35% heavier than their forest-dwelling counterparts. As of 2010, at least 44 cities in 15 countries have experienced problems of some kind relating to the presence of habituated wild boar.[^cite_note-cahill2012-153]

A 2023 study found that allowing wild pigs to forage on edible garbage in large regional landfills results in those animals getting physically large/heavier, having larger litters of piglets, and causing more wild pig-vehicle collisions in the vicinity of the landfill. The effects of letting these pigs scavenge in these landfills can present unique challenges to population management, control, public safety, and disease transmission. Wild pigs foraging on edible food waste in landfills has also been identified as a vector that facilitates the spread of [African swine fever virus](5094165.md).[^cite_note-154]

### Attacks on humans

Actual attacks on humans are rare, but can be serious, resulting in penetrating injuries to the lower part of the body. They generally occur during the boars' [rutting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rut_\(mammalian_reproduction\)) season from November to January, in agricultural areas bordering forests or on paths leading through forests. The animal typically attacks by charging and pointing its tusks towards the intended victim, with most injuries occurring on the [thigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thigh) region. Once the initial attack is over, the boar steps back, takes position and attacks again if the victim is still moving, only ending once the victim is completely incapacitated.[^cite_note-manipady2006-155][^cite_note-gunduz2007-156]

Boar attacks on humans have been documented throughout history. The Romans and Ancient Greeks wrote of these attacks ([Odysseus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odysseus) was wounded by a boar and [Adonis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adonis) was killed by one). A 2012 study compiling recorded attacks from 1825 to 2012 found accounts of 665 human victims of both wild boars and feral pigs: the highest number of those attacks (24%) occurred in the United States, though the next-highest (19%), in India, was also the greatest percentage of attacks occurring in the animal's native range. Most of the attacks occurred in rural areas during the winter months in non-hunting contexts and were committed by solitary males.[^cite_note-mayer2013-157]

### Management

Managing wild boar is a pressing task in both native and invasive contexts as they can be disruptive to other systems when not addressed. Wild boar find their success through adaptation of daily patterns to circumvent threats. They avoid human contact through nocturnal lifestyles, despite the fact that they are not evolutionarily predisposed, and alter their diets substantially based on what is available.[^cite_note-Johann-2020-158] These "adaptive generalists", can survive in a variety of landscapes, making the prediction of their movement patterns and any potential close contact areas crucial to limiting damage.[^cite_note-Clontz-2021-159] All of these qualities make them equally difficult to manage or limit.

Within Central Europe, the native habitat of the wild boar, there has been a push to re-evaluate interactions between wild boar and humans, with the priority of fostering positive engagement. Negative media and public perception of wild boar as "crop raiders" have made those living alongside them less willing to accept the economic damages of their behaviors, as wild boar are seen as pests. This media tone impacts management policy, with every 10 negative articles increasing wild boar policy activity by 6.7%.[^cite_note-Miller-2018-160] Contrary to this portrayal, wild boar, when managed well within their natural environments, can be a crucial part of forest ecosystems.

Defining the limits of proper management is difficult, but the exclusion of wild boar from rare environments is generally agreed upon, as when not properly managed, they can damage agricultural ventures and harm vulnerable plant life.[^cite_note-Gray-2020-161] These damages are estimated at \$800 million yearly in environmental and financial costs for the United States alone.[^cite_note-Gray-2020-161] The breadth of this damage is due to prior inattention and lack of management tactics for extended lengths of time.[^cite_note-Miller-2018-160] Managing wild boar is a complex task, as it involves coordinating a combination of crop harvest techniques, fencing, toxic bait, corrals, and hunting. The most common tactic employed by private land owners in the United States is recreational hunting; however, this is generally not as effective on its own.[^cite_note-Beasley-2018-162] Management strategies are most successful when they take into account reproduction, dispersion, and the differences between ideal resources for males and females.[^cite_note-Clontz-2021-159]

Wild boars are causing soil disturbance that, among other problems, globally results in annual carbon dioxide emissions equivalent to that of \~1.1 million passenger vehicles (4.9 Mt, 0.01% of all GHG emissions as of 2022), implying that as of 2021, hunted boar meat – unlike other meat products – has beneficial effects on the environment[^cite_note-163][^cite_note-164] even though the effect would diminish if boars are introduced for meat production, so consistently retaining small populations of boars may be preferable.
