# Global surface temperature

This article is about the global average temperature at the Earth's surface. For records of extreme weather, see [List of weather records](4506267.md).



The blue line represents global surface temperature reconstructed over the last 2,000 years using [proxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxy_\(climate\)) data from [tree rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_rings), [corals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral), and [ice cores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_core).

[^cite_note-\:0-1]

&#x20;The red line shows direct surface temperature measurements since 1880.

[^cite_note-nasa_temperatures2-2]

**Global surface temperature** (**GST**) is the average temperature of Earth's surface at a given time. It is a combination of [sea surface temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_surface_temperature) and the [near-surface air temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near-surface_air_temperature) over land, weighted by their respective areas. Temperature data comes mainly from [weather stations](206018.md) and [satellites](27683.md). To estimate data in the distant past, [proxy data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxy_\(climate\)) can be used for example from [tree rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_rings), [corals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral), and [ice cores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_core).[^cite_note-\:0-1] Observing the rising GST over time is one of the many lines of evidence supporting the [scientific consensus on climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_consensus_on_climate_change), which is that [human activities are causing climate change](5042951.md). Alternative terms for the same concept are **global mean surface temperature** (**GMST**) or **global average surface temperature**.

Series of reliable temperature measurements in some regions began in the 1850—1880 time frame (this is called the **instrumental temperature record**). The longest-running temperature record is the [Central England temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_England_temperature) data series, which starts in 1659. The longest-running quasi-global records start in 1850.[^cite_note-\:3-3] For temperature measurements in the [upper atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_atmosphere) a variety of methods can be used. This includes radiosondes launched using weather balloons, a variety of satellites, and aircraft.[^cite_note-4] Satellites can monitor temperatures in the upper atmosphere but are not commonly used to measure temperature change at the surface. Ocean temperatures at different depths are measured to add to global surface temperature datasets. This data is also used to calculate the [ocean heat content](13165796.md).

GMST is often further aggregated by year or month. Through 1940, the average annual GMST increased, but was relatively stable between 1940 and 1975. Since 1975, it has increased by roughly 0.15 °C to 0.20 °C per decade, to at least 1.1 °C (1.9 °F) above 1880 levels.[^cite_note-5] The current annual GMST is about 15 °C (59 °F),[^cite_note-NASAtemp_20230904-6] though monthly temperatures can vary almost 2 °C (4 °F) above or below this figure.[^cite_note-Copernicus_20230615-7]

The global average and combined land and ocean surface temperature show a warming of 1.09 °C (range: 0.95 to 1.20 °C) from 1850–1900 to 2011–2020, based on multiple independently produced datasets.[^cite_note-\:03-8]<sup>: 5 </sup> The trend is faster since the 1970s than in any other 50-year period over at least the last 2000 years.[^cite_note-\:03-8]<sup>: 8 </sup> Within that upward trend, some variability in temperatures happens because of [natural internal variability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_variability_and_change) (for example due to [El Niño–Southern Oscillation](317311.md)).

The global temperature record shows the changes of the temperature of the atmosphere and the oceans through various spans of time. There are numerous estimates of temperatures since the end of the [Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene) [glaciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaciation), particularly during the current [Holocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene) epoch. Some temperature information is available through geologic evidence, going back millions of years. More recently, information from [ice cores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_core) covers the period from 800,000 years ago until now. [Tree Rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_Rings) and measurements from ice cores can give evidence about the global temperature from 1,000–2,000 years before the present until now.[^cite_note-\:1-9]

## Definition



Projected surface temperature changes relative to 1850–1900, based on [CMIP6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupled_Model_Intercomparison_Project#CMIP_Phase_6) multi-model mean changes

The [IPCC Sixth Assessment Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPCC_Sixth_Assessment_Report) defines *global mean surface temperature* (GMST) as the "estimated global average of [near-surface air temperatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near-surface_air_temperature) over land and sea ice, and [sea surface temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_surface_temperature) (SST) over ice-free ocean regions, with changes normally expressed as departures from a value over a specified reference period".[^cite_note-IPCC_glossary-10]<sup>: 2231 </sup>

Put simply, the global surface temperature (GST) is calculated by averaging the temperature at the surface layer of the ocean ([sea surface temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_surface_temperature)) and over land ([surface air temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_air_temperature)).

In comparison, the *global mean surface air temperature* (GSAT) is the "global average of near-surface air temperatures over land, oceans and [sea ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_ice). Changes in GSAT are often used as a measure of global temperature change in climate models."[^cite_note-IPCC_glossary-10]<sup>: 2231 </sup>

*Global temperature* can have different definitions. There is a small difference between air and surface temperatures.[^cite_note-IPCC_SR15_SPM-11]<sup>: 12 </sup>

## Temperature data from 1850 to the present time

### Total warming and trends

See also: [Climate change § Global temperature rise](5042951.md#Global_temperature_rise)

NASA animation portraying global surface temperature changes since 1880. The colour blue denotes cooler temperatures and red denotes warmer temperatures. As reference value the mean temperature from 1951 to 1980 is used.

Changes in global temperatures over the past century provide evidence for the effects of increasing [greenhouse gases](21350772.md). When the [climate system](5565588.md) reacts to such changes, [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_\(general_concept\)) follows. Measurement of the GST is one of the many lines of evidence supporting the [scientific consensus on climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_consensus_on_climate_change), which is that [humans are causing warming of Earth's climate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming).

The global average and combined land and [ocean surface temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_surface_temperature), show a warming of 1.09 °C (range: 0.95 to 1.20 °C) from 1850–1900 to 2011–2020, based on multiple independently produced datasets.[^cite_note-\:03-8]<sup>: 5 </sup> The trend is faster since the 1970s than in any other 50-year period over at least the last 2000 years.[^cite_note-\:03-8]<sup>: 8 </sup>

Most of the observed warming occurred in two periods: around 1900 to around 1940 and around 1970 onwards;[^cite_note-12] the cooling/plateau from 1940 to 1970 has been mostly attributed to [sulfate aerosol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfate#Environmental_effects).[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14]<sup>: 207 </sup> Some of the temperature variations over this time period may also be due to ocean circulation patterns.[^cite_note-15]

Land air temperatures are rising faster than sea surface temperatures. Land temperatures have warmed by 1.59 °C (range: 1.34 to 1.83 °C) from 1850–1900 to 2011–2020, while sea surface temperatures have warmed by 0.88 °C (range: 0.68 to 1.01 °C) over the same period.[^cite_note-\:03-8]<sup>: 5 </sup>

For 1980 to 2020, the linear warming trend for combined land and sea temperatures has been 0.18 °C to 0.20 °C per decade, depending on the data set used.[^cite_note-\:13-16]<sup>: Table 2.4 </sup>

&#x20;It is unlikely that any uncorrected effects from urbanisation, or changes in [land use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_use) or [land cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_cover), have raised global land temperature changes by more than 10%.[^cite_note-17]<sup>: 189 </sup> However, larger urbanisation signals have been found locally in some rapidly urbanising regions, such as eastern China.[^cite_note-\:13-16]<sup>: Section 2.3.1.1.3 </sup>

Global warming affects all parts of Earth's [climate system](5565588.md).[^cite_note-Effects_of_climate_change_Kennedy-2019-19] Global surface temperatures have risen by 1.1 °C (2.0 °F). Scientists say they will rise further in the future.[^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21] The changes in climate are not uniform across the Earth. In particular, most land areas have warmed faster than most ocean areas. The [Arctic](36971.md) is warming faster than most other regions.[^cite_note-Effects_of_climate_change_Lindsey-2022-22] Night-time temperatures have increased faster than daytime temperatures.[^cite_note-23] The impact on nature and people depends on how much more the Earth warms.[^cite_note-Effects_of_climate_change_Schneider-2007a-24]<sup>: 787 </sup>

Scientists use several methods to predict the effects of human-caused climate change. One is to investigate past natural changes in climate.[^cite_note-25] To assess changes in Earth's [past climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoclimate) scientists have studied [tree rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_rings), [ice cores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_core), [corals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral), and ocean and lake [sediments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sediment).[^cite_note-Effects_of_climate_change_noaa_proxy_climate_data-26] These show that recent temperatures have surpassed anything in the last 2,000 years.[^cite_note-Effects_of_climate_change_phys.org-27] By the end of the 21st century, temperatures may increase to a level last seen in the [mid-Pliocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-Pliocene_Warm_Period). This was around 3 million years ago.[^cite_note-28]<sup>: 322 </sup> At that time, mean global temperatures were about 2–4 °C (3.6–7.2 °F) warmer than pre-industrial temperatures. The global mean sea level was up to 25 metres (82 ft) higher than it is today.[^cite_note-Effects_of_climate_change_Oppenheimer-2019-29]<sup>: 323 </sup> The modern observed rise in temperature and CO<sub>2</sub> concentrations has been rapid. Even abrupt geophysical events in Earth's history do not approach current rates.[^cite_note-30]<sup>: 54 </sup>



[Global average temperature datasets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_temperature_record) from various scientific organizations show substantial agreement concerning the progress and extent of global warming: 1880– pairwise correlations of the four longer-term datasets are at least 99.29%.

### Methods

The instrumental temperature record is a record of [temperatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature) within Earth's [climate](5565588.md) based on direct [measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature_measurement) of [air temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_temperature) and [ocean temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_temperature). Instrumental temperature records do not use indirect reconstructions using [climate proxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxy_\(climate\)) data such as from [tree rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_rings) and [marine sediments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_sediment).[^cite_note-NCDC_proxy-31]

#### Early temperature records



Exterior of a [Stevenson screen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stevenson_screen) used for temperature measurements on land stations.



Interior of a Stevenson screen

The longest-running temperature record is the [Central England temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_England_temperature) data series, a single-location data set that starts in 1659.[^cite_note-MetOfficeCET_20190731-32]

On 15 December 2025, the [Copernicus Programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicus_Programme)'s Earth System Science Data released the GloSAT reference analysis, a gridded data set of air temperature change across global land and ocean extending back to the 1780s, using marine air temperature observations rather than the sea surface temperature measurements.[^cite_note-ESSD_20251215-33]

Before GloSAT's release, the period for which reasonably reliable instrumental records of near-surface land temperature exist with quasi-global coverage was generally considered to begin around 1850.[^cite_note-\:3-3] Earlier records exist, but with sparser coverage, largely confined to the [Northern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Hemisphere), and less standardized instrumentation.

The temperature data for the record come from measurements from land stations, ships, and [buoys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buoy). On land, temperatures are measured either using electronics sensors, or mercury or alcohol [thermometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermometer) which are read manually, with the instruments being sheltered from direct sunlight using a shelter such as a [Stevenson screen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stevenson_screen). The sea record consists of ships taking sea temperature measurements, mostly from hull-mounted sensors, engine inlets or buckets, and more recently includes measurements from moored and drifting [buoys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buoy). The land and marine records can be compared.

Areas that are densely populated tend to have a high density of measurement points. In contrast, temperature observations are more spread out in sparsely populated areas such as polar regions and deserts, as well as in many regions of Africa and South America.[^cite_note-34] In the past, [thermometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermometer) were read manually to record temperatures. Nowadays, measurements are usually connected with electronic sensors which transmit data automatically. Surface temperature data is usually presented as anomalies rather than as absolute values.

Land and sea measurement and instrument calibration is the responsibility of [national meteorological services](33584.md#Current_member_states). Standardization of methods is organized through the [World Meteorological Organization](33584.md) (and formerly through its predecessor, the [International Meteorological Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Meteorological_Organization)).[^cite_note-GOSGuide-35]

Most meteorological observations are taken for use in weather forecasts. Centers such as [European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Centre_for_Medium-Range_Weather_Forecasts) show instantaneous map of their coverage; or the [Hadley Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Met_Office_Hadley_Centre) show the coverage for the average of the year 2000. Coverage for earlier in the 20th and 19th centuries would be significantly less. While temperature changes vary both in size and direction from one location to another, the numbers from different locations are combined to produce an estimate of a global average change.

#### Satellite and balloon temperature records (1950s–present)

Main article: [Satellite temperature measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_temperature_measurement)

[Weather balloon](154877.md) [radiosonde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiosonde) measurements of atmospheric temperature at various altitudes begin to show an approximation of global coverage in the 1950s. Since December 1978, [microwave sounding units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave_sounding_unit) on satellites have produced data which can be used to [infer temperatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MSU_temperature_measurements) in the [troposphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troposphere).

Several groups have analyzed the satellite data to calculate temperature trends in the troposphere. Both the [University of Alabama in Huntsville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UAH_satellite_temperature_dataset) (UAH) and the private, NASA funded, corporation [Remote Sensing Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_Sensing_Systems) (RSS) find an upward trend. For the lower troposphere, UAH found a global average trend between 1978 and 2019 of 0.130 degrees Celsius per decade.[^cite_note-36] RSS found a trend of 0.148 degrees Celsius per decade, to January 2011.[^cite_note-37]

In 2004 scientists found trends of +0.19  degrees Celsius per decade when applied to the RSS dataset.[^cite_note-38] Others found 0.20  degrees Celsius per decade up between 1978 and 2005, since which the dataset has not been updated.[^cite_note-39]

The most recent climate model simulations give a range of results for changes in global-average temperature. Some models show more warming in the troposphere than at the surface, while a slightly smaller number of simulations show the opposite behaviour. There is no fundamental inconsistency among these model results and observations at the global scale.[^cite_note-40]

The satellite records used to show much smaller warming trends for the troposphere which were considered to disagree with model prediction; however, following revisions to the satellite records, the trends are now similar.

#### Absolute temperatures v. anomalies

Main article: [Temperature anomaly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature_anomaly)

Records of global average surface temperature are usually presented as [anomalies](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/anomaly) rather than as absolute temperatures. A temperature anomaly is measured against a *reference value* (also called *baseline period* or *long-term average*).[^cite_note-44] Usually it is a period of 30 years. For example, a commonly used baseline period is 1951–1980. Therefore, if the average temperature for that time period was 15 °C, and the currently measured temperature is 17 °C, then the temperature anomaly is +2 °C.

Temperature anomalies are useful for deriving average surface temperatures because they tend to be highly correlated over large distances (of the order of 1000 km).[^cite_note-45] In other words, anomalies are representative of temperature changes over large areas and distances. By comparison, absolute temperatures vary markedly over even short distances. A dataset based on anomalies will also be less sensitive to changes in the observing network (such as a new station opening in a particularly hot or cold location) than one based on absolute values will be.

The Earth's average surface absolute temperature for the 1961–1990 period has been derived by spatial interpolation of average observed near-surface air temperatures from over the land, oceans and sea ice regions, with a best estimate of 14 °C (57.2 °F).[^cite_note-jonesreview-46] The estimate is uncertain, but probably lies within 0.5 °C of the true value.[^cite_note-jonesreview-46] Given the difference in uncertainties between this absolute value and any annual anomaly, it's not valid to add them together to imply a precise absolute value for a specific year.[^cite_note-47]

#### Siting of temperature measurement stations

The U.S. [National Weather Service](286989.md) Cooperative Observer Program has established minimum standards regarding the instrumentation, siting, and reporting of surface temperature stations.[^cite_note-48] The observing systems available are able to detect year-to-year temperature variations such as those caused by El Niño or volcanic eruptions.[^cite_note-CCSP-49]

Another study concluded in 2006, that existing empirical techniques for validating the local and regional consistency of temperature data are adequate to identify and remove biases from station records, and that such corrections allow information about long-term trends to be preserved.[^cite_note-50] A study in 2013 also found that urban bias can be accounted for, and when all available station data is divided into rural and urban, that both temperature sets are broadly consistent.[^cite_note-51]

## Factors influencing global temperature

Further information: [Causes of climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causes_of_climate_change) and [Climate variability and change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_variability_and_change)



Colored bars show how El Niño years (red, regional warming) and La Niña years (blue, regional cooling) relate to overall global warming. The [El Niño–Southern Oscillation](317311.md) has been linked to variability in longer-term global average temperature increase, with El Niño years usually corresponding to annual global temperature increases.

Factors that influence global temperature include:

- [Greenhouse gases](21350772.md) trap outgoing radiation warming the atmosphere which in turn warms the land ([greenhouse effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_effect)).

- [El Niño–Southern Oscillation](317311.md) (ENSO): [El Niño](317311.md) generally tends to increase global temperatures. [La Niña](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Ni%C3%B1a), on the other hand, usually causes years which are cooler than the short-term average.

  [^cite_note-NOAA-64]

  &#x20;El Niño is the warm phase of the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and La Niña the cold phase. In the absence of other short-term influences such as volcanic eruptions, strong El Niño years are typically 0.1 °C to 0.2 °C warmer than the years immediately preceding and following them, and strong La Niña years 0.1 °C to 0.2 °C cooler. The signal is most prominent in the year in which the El Niño or La Niña phase ends, with global average temperatures typically rising by 0.1 °C to 0.2 °C during strong El Niño years, and falling by a similar margin during strong La Niña events, in the absence of other short-term influences such as volcanic eruptions.

  [^cite_note-65]

- Aerosols and volcanic eruptions: [Aerosols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerosol) diffuse incoming radiation generally cooling the planet. On a long-term basis, aerosols are primarily of anthropogenic origin, but major [volcanic eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Types_of_volcanic_eruptions) can produce quantities of aerosols which exceed those from anthropogenic sources over periods of time up to a few years. Volcanic eruptions which are sufficiently large to inject significant quantities of [sulfur dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur_dioxide) into the [stratosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratosphere) can have a significant global cooling effect for one to three years after the eruption. This effect is most prominent for tropical volcanoes as the resultant aerosols can spread over both hemispheres. The largest eruptions of the last 100 years, such as the [Mount Pinatubo eruption in 1991](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1991_eruption_of_Mount_Pinatubo) and [Mount Agung eruption in 1963–1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Agung#1963%E2%80%9364_eruption), have been followed by years with global mean temperatures 0.1 °C to 0.2 °C below long-term trends at the time.

- [Land use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_use) change like [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation) can increase greenhouse gases through burning [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass). [Albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albedo) can also be changed.

- Incoming [solar radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_irradiance) varies very slightly, with the main variation controlled by the approximately 11-year [solar magnetic activity cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_cycle).

## Robustness of evidence

There is a [scientific consensus that climate is changing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_opinion_on_climate_change) and that greenhouse gases emitted by human activities are the primary driver.[^cite_note-joint_statement_on_climate_change-66] The scientific consensus is reflected, for example, by the [Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change](15030.md) (IPCC), an international body which summarizes existing science, and the [U.S. Global Change Research Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Global_Change_Research_Program).[^cite_note-joint_statement_on_climate_change-66]

### Internal climate variability and global warming

Further information: [Causes of climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causes_of_climate_change) and [Climate variability and change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_variability_and_change)

One of the issues that has been raised in the media is the view that global warming "stopped in 1998".[^cite_note-74][^cite_note-scott_variability_p1-75] This view ignores the presence of internal climate variability.[^cite_note-scott_variability_p1-75][^cite_note-met_office_internal_variability-76] Internal climate variability is a result of complex interactions between components of the climate system, such as the [coupling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupling_\(physics\)) between the [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth) and ocean.[^cite_note-77] An example of internal climate variability is the [El Niño–Southern Oscillation](317311.md) (ENSO).[^cite_note-scott_variability_p1-75][^cite_note-met_office_internal_variability-76] The [El Niño in 1998](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997%E2%80%9398_El_Ni%C3%B1o_event) was particularly strong, possibly one of the strongest of the 20th century, and 1998 was at the time the world's warmest year on record by a substantial margin.

Cooling over the 2007 to 2012 period, for instance, was likely driven by internal modes of climate variability such as [La Niña](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Ni%C3%B1a).[^cite_note-scott_variability_p3-78] The area of cooler-than-average sea surface temperatures that defines La Niña conditions can push global temperatures downward, if the phenomenon is strong enough.[^cite_note-scott_variability_p3-78] The slowdown in global warming rates over the 1998 to 2012 period is also less pronounced in current generations of observational datasets than in those available at the time in 2012. The temporary slowing of warming rates ended after 2012, with every year from 2015 onwards warmer than any year prior to 2015, but it is expected that warming rates will continue to fluctuate on decadal timescales through the 21st century.[^cite_note-79]<sup>: Box 3.1 </sup>

### Trends and predictions

Further information: [Climate change § Future warming and the carbon budget](5042951.md#Future_warming_and_the_carbon_budget), and [Climate change scenario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_scenario)

Each of the seven years in 2015–2021 was clearly warmer than any pre-2014 year, and this trend is expected to be true for some time to come (that is, the 2016 record will be broken before 2026 etc.).[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84] A decadal forecast by the World Meteorological Organisation issued in 2021 stated a probability of 40% of having a year above 1.5 C in the 2021–2025 period.[^cite_note-85]

Global warming is very likely to reach 1.0 °C to 1.8 °C by the late 21st century under the [very low GHG emissions scenario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shared_Socioeconomic_Pathways#SSP1:_Sustainability_\(Taking_the_Green_Road\)). In an [intermediate scenario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shared_Socioeconomic_Pathways#SSP3:_Regional_rivalry_\(A_Rocky_Road\)) global warming would reach 2.1 °C to 3.5 °C, and 3.3 °C to 5.7 °C under the [very high GHG emissions scenario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shared_Socioeconomic_Pathways#SSP5:_Fossil-Fueled_Development_\(Taking_the_Highway\)).[^cite_note-\:03-8]<sup>: SPM-17 </sup> These projections are based on [climate models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_model) in combination with observations.[^cite_note-86]<sup>: TS-30 </sup>

Research in the mid 2020s suggests that an acceleration in global warming may contribute to an oscillation of the nominal average global temperature anomaly beyond the [Paris Agreement](30242372.md) target of 1.5 °C (2.7 °F) before 2030."[^cite_note-Hansen-87] In addition, by scrubbing [noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise_\(spectral_phenomenon\)) associated with [natural climatic variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_variability_and_change), other research suggests that the warming trend rose from 0.19–0.20 °C (0.34–0.36 °F) per decade (for 1970–2015) to 0.35 °C (0.63 °F) (for 2015–2025). The researchers concluded with 98% confidence that this accelerating warming would result in a breach of the 1.5 °C (2.7 °F) goal by 2030."[^cite_note-Foster-88]

### Regional temperature changes

See also: [Effects of climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_climate_change) and [Climate variability and change § Variability between regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_variability_and_change#Variability_between_regions)

The changes in climate are not expected to be uniform across the Earth. In particular, land areas change more quickly than oceans, and northern high latitudes change more quickly than the tropics. There are three major ways in which global warming will make changes to regional climate: melting ice, changing the [hydrological cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrological_cycle) (of evaporation and precipitation) and changing [currents in the oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_current).

## Temperature estimates from prior to 1850



A December 2025 article published in the [Copernicus Programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicus_Programme)'s *Earth System Science Data* presented a GloSAT global surface air temperature dataset based on marine air temperature observations rather than the sea surface temperature measurements typically used. GloSAT extended back to the 1780s, earlier than 1850 covered by previous datasets.

[^cite_note-ESSD_Copernicus_20251215-89]

The global temperature record shows the fluctuations of the temperature of the atmosphere and the oceans through various spans of time. There are numerous estimates of temperatures since the end of the [Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene) [glaciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaciation), particularly during the current [Holocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene) epoch. Some temperature information is available through geologic evidence, going back millions of years. More recently, information from [ice cores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_core) covers the period from 800,000 years ago until now. A study of the [paleoclimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoclimatology) covers the time period from 12,000 years ago. [Tree Rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_Rings) and measurements from ice cores can give evidence about the global temperature from 1,000–2,000 years ago. The most detailed information exists since 1850, when [methodical thermometer-based records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_temperature_record) began. Modifications on the [Stevenson-type screen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stevenson_screen) were made for uniform instrument measurements around 1880.[^cite_note-\:1-9]

### Tree rings and ice cores (from 1,000–2,000 years before present)

Further information: [Temperature record of the last 2,000 years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature_record_of_the_last_2,000_years)

[Proxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxy_\(climate\)) measurements can be used to reconstruct the temperature record before the historical period. Quantities such as [tree ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_ring) widths, [coral growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral#Climate_research), [isotope variations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_core#Isotopic_analysis) in [ice cores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_core), ocean and lake sediments, [cave deposits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speleothem#Climate_proxies), [fossils](10958.md), [ice cores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_core), [borehole temperatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxy_\(climate\)#Boreholes), and [glacier](12463.md) length records are correlated with climatic fluctuations. From these, proxy temperature reconstructions of the last 2000 years have been performed for the northern hemisphere, and over shorter time scales for the southern hemisphere and tropics.[^cite_note-90][^cite_note-91][^cite_note-National_Research_Council_2006-92]

&#x20;Geographic coverage by these proxies is necessarily sparse, and various proxies are more sensitive to faster fluctuations. For example, tree rings, ice cores, and corals generally show variation on an annual time scale, but borehole reconstructions rely on [rates of thermal diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_diffusivity), and small scale fluctuations are washed out. Even the best proxy records contain far fewer observations than the worst periods of the observational record, and the spatial and temporal resolution of the resulting reconstructions is correspondingly coarse. Connecting the measured proxies to the variable of interest, such as temperature or rainfall, is highly non-trivial. Data sets from multiple complementary proxies covering overlapping time periods and areas are reconciled to produce the final reconstructions.[^cite_note-National_Research_Council_2006-92][^cite_note-93]



Temperature record of the last 2,000 years (the so-called [Medieval Warm Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Warm_Period) and [Little Ice Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age) were not planet-wide phenomena)

Proxy reconstructions extending back 2,000 years have been performed, but reconstructions for the last 1,000 years are supported by more and higher quality independent data sets. These reconstructions indicate:[^cite_note-National_Research_Council_2006-92]

- global mean surface temperatures over the last 25 years have been higher than any comparable period since AD 1600, and probably since AD 900

- there was a [Little Ice Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age) centered on AD 1700

- there was a [Medieval Warm Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Warm_Period) centered on AD 1000, but this was not a global phenomenon.

  [^cite_note-94]

#### Indirect historical proxies

As well as natural, numerical proxies (tree-ring widths, for example) there exist records from the human historical period that can be used to infer climate variations, including: reports of [frost fairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frost_fair) on the [Thames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thames); records of good and bad harvests; dates of spring blossom or lambing; extraordinary falls of rain and snow; and unusual floods or droughts.[^cite_note-95] Such records can be used to infer historical temperatures, but generally in a more qualitative manner than natural proxies.

Recent evidence suggests that a sudden and short-lived [climatic shift between 2200 and 2100 BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4.2-kiloyear_event) occurred in the region between [Tibet](31516.md) and [Iceland](14531.md), with some evidence suggesting a global change. The result was a cooling and reduction in precipitation. This is believed to be a primary cause of the collapse of the [Old Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Kingdom) of [Egypt](8087628.md).[^cite_note-96]

### Paleoclimate (from 12,000 years before present)



Plot showing the variations, and relative stability, of climate during the last 12000 years.

Main article: [Paleoclimatology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoclimatology)

Many estimates of past temperatures have been made over [Earth's history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Earth). The field of paleoclimatology includes ancient temperature records. As the present article is oriented toward recent temperatures, there is a focus here on events since the retreat of the [Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene) [glaciers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaciers). The 10,000 years of the [Holocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene) epoch covers most of this period, since the end of the Northern Hemisphere's [Younger Dryas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Younger_Dryas) millennium-long cooling. The [Holocene Climatic Optimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene_Climatic_Optimum) was generally warmer than the 20th century, but numerous regional variations have been noted since the start of the Younger Dryas.

Even longer term records exist for few sites: the recent Antarctic [EPICA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Project_for_Ice_Coring_in_Antarctica) core reaches 800 kyr; many others reach more than 100,000 years. The EPICA core covers eight glacial/interglacial cycles. The [NGRIP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Greenland_Ice_Core_Project) core from Greenland stretches back more than 100 kyr, with 5 kyr in the [Eemian interglacial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eemian_interglacial). Whilst the large-scale signals from the cores are clear, there are problems interpreting the detail, and connecting the isotopic variation to the temperature signal.[^cite_note-97]

#### Ice core locations



Ice core data location

[^cite_note-98]

The World Paleoclimatology Data Center (WDC) maintains the ice core data files of glaciers and ice caps in polar and low latitude mountains all over the world.

#### Ice core records from Greenland

As a paleothermometry, the ice core in central Greenland showed consistent records on the surface-temperature changes.[^cite_note-\:12-99] According to the records, changes in global climate are rapid and widespread. Warming phase only needs simple steps, however, the cooling process requires more prerequisites and bases.[^cite_note-100] Also, Greenland has the clearest record of abrupt climate changes in the ice core, and there are no other records that can show the same time interval with equally high time resolution.[^cite_note-\:12-99]

When scientists explored the trapped gas in the ice core bubbles, they found that the methane concentration in Greenland ice core is significantly higher than that in Antarctic samples of similar age, the records of changes of concentration difference between Greenland and Antarctic reveal variation of latitudinal distribution of methane sources.[^cite_note-101] Increase in methane concentration shown by Greenland ice core records implies that the global wetland area has changed greatly over past years.[^cite_note-102] Methane records derived from Greenland ice cores have provided valuable information about past atmospheric methane concentrations and their relationship to abrupt climate changes, making them an important proxy for studying past climate variability.[^cite_note-103]

#### Ice core records from Antarctica

The Antarctic ice sheet originated in the late Eocene, the drilling has restored a record of 800,000 years in [Dome Concordia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dome_C), and it is the longest available ice core in Antarctica. In recent years, more and more new studies have provided older but discrete records.[^cite_note-104] Due to the uniqueness of the Antarctic ice sheet, the Antarctic ice core not only records the global temperature changes, but also contains huge quantities of information about the global biogeochemical cycles, climate dynamics and abrupt changes in global climate.[^cite_note-105]

By comparing with current climate records, the ice core records in Antarctica further confirm that [polar amplification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_amplification).[^cite_note-106]Although numerous ice cores have been recovered from Antarctica, large areas of the continent remain unsampled at high spatial resolution. As a result, researchers continue to seek additional drilling sites to improve the geographic coverage and temporal resolution of Antarctic paleoclimate records.[^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108]

#### Ice core records from low-latitude regions

The ice core records from low-latitude regions are not as common as records from polar regions, however, these records still provide much useful information for scientists. Ice cores in low-latitude regions are usually from high altitude areas. The Guliya record is the longest record from low-latitude, high altitude regions, which spans over 700,000 years.[^cite_note-\:02-109] According to these records, scientists found the evidence which can prove the [Last Glacial Maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Glacial_Maximum) (LGM) was colder in the tropics and subtropics than previously believed.[^cite_note-110] Also, the records from low-latitude regions helped scientists confirm that the 20th century was the warmest period in the last 1000 years.[^cite_note-\:02-109]

On longer time scales, marine sediment cores indicate that the cycles of glacials and interglacials occurred within a long-term cooling trend that began with the onset of large-scale Antarctic glaciation during the Cenozoic Era. The intensification of Northern Hemisphere glaciation approximately 2.7–3 million years ago marked a further expansion of Earth's ice sheets and the establishment of pronounced glacial–interglacial cycles. Such long-term climatic changes have occurred repeatedly throughout Earth's history and have been linked in part to changes in the configuration of continents and oceans resulting from plate tectonics and continental drift.[^cite_note-113][^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115]
