**Troy weight** is a system of [units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_unit) of [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) whose origin is uncertain.[^cite_note-HallockWade1906-1] By far the most common troy unit is the [troy ounce](#Troy_ounce) (oz t), the standard mass unit for [precious metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precious_metals) in industry and in trade; it equals 31.1034768 [grams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram).[^cite_note-Perth_Mint-2] The troy weight units are the [grain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain_\(unit\)), the [pennyweight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennyweight) (24 grains), the troy ounce (20 pennyweights), and the troy pound (12 troy ounces). The troy grain is equal to the [grain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain_\(unit\)) unit of the [avoirdupois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avoirdupois) and [apothecaries'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apothecaries%27_system) systems, but the troy ounce is heavier than the [avoirdupois ounce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ounce#International_avoirdupois_ounce), and the troy pound is lighter than the [avoirdupois pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_\(mass\)#Avoirdupois_pound).&#x20;

## Etymology

Troy weight is generally supposed to take its name from the French market town of [Troyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troyes) where English merchants traded at least as early as the early [9th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/9th_century).[^cite_note-OED3troy2-3][^cite_note-4] The name *troy* is first attested in 1390, describing the weight of a platter, in an account of the travels in Europe of the [Earl of Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_IV_of_England#Relationship_with_Richard_II).[^cite_note-OED3troy2-3][^cite_note-5]&#x20;

[Charles Moore Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Moore_Watson) (1844–1916) proposes an alternative etymology: *The Assize of Weights and Measures* (also known as *[Tractatus de Ponderibus et Mensuris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weights_and_Measures_Acts_\(UK\)#Acts_of_Parliament)*), one of the [statutes of uncertain date](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statutes_of_uncertain_date) from the reign of either [Henry III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_III_of_England) or [Edward I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_I_of_England), thus before 1307, specifies "*troni ponderacionem*"—which the Public Record Commissioners translate as "troy weight". The word *tron* refers to markets. Wright's *The English Dialect Dictionary* lists the word *troi* as meaning a [balance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weighing_scale#Mechanical_balances), related to the alternate form 'tron' which also means market or the place of weighing. From this, Watson suggests that 'troy' derives from the manner of weighing by balance precious goods such as bullion or drugs; in contrast to the word 'avoirdupois' used to describe bulk goods such as corn or coal, sometimes weighed in ancient times by a kind of [steelyard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steelyard_balance) called the [auncel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auncel).[^cite_note-CMW1910-6]&#x20;

Troy weight referred to the [Tower system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_units_of_measurement#Troy_and_Tower); the earliest reference to the modern troy weights is in 1414.[^cite_note-CMW1910-6][^cite_note-7]&#x20;

## History

The origin of the troy weight system is unknown, although the name probably comes from the [Champagne fairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champagne_fairs) at [Troyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troyes), in northeastern France.[^cite_note-8] English troy weights were nearly identical to the troy weight system of [Bremen](9420388.md). (The Bremen troy ounce had a mass of 480.8 British Imperial grains.)[^cite_note-1977zupko-9]&#x20;

Many aspects of the troy weight system were indirectly derived from the [Roman monetary system](299259.md). Before they used coins, [early Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) used bronze bars of varying weights as currency. An *[aes grave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aes_grave)* ("heavy bronze") weighed one [pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_\(mass\)). One twelfth of an *aes grave* was called an *[uncia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Roman_units_of_measurement#Weight)*, or in English, an "ounce". Before the adoption of the metric system, many systems of troy weights were in use in various parts of Europe, among them Holland troy, Paris troy, etc.[^cite_note-Kelly-10] Their values varied from one another by up to several percentage points. Troy weights were first used in England in the 15th century and were made official for gold and silver in 1527.[^cite_note-HallockWade1906-1] The British Imperial system of weights and measures (also known as [imperial units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_units)) was established in 1824, prior to which the troy weight system was a subset of pre-imperial [English units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_units_of_measurement).&#x20;

The troy ounce in modern use is essentially the same as the British Imperial troy ounce (1824–1971), adopted as an official weight standard for United States coinage by act of [Congress](31756.md) on May 19, 1828.[^cite_note-11] The British Imperial troy ounce (known more commonly simply as the imperial troy ounce) was based on, and virtually identical to, the pre-1824 British troy ounce and the pre-1707 English troy ounce. (1824 was the year the British Imperial system of weights and measures was adopted; 1707 was the year of the [Act of Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_Union_1707) which created the [Kingdom of Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Britain).) Troy ounces have been used in England since the early 15th century, and the English troy ounce was officially adopted for coinage in 1527. Before that time, various sorts of troy ounces were in use on the continent.[^cite_note-1977zupko-9]&#x20;

The troy ounce and grain were also part of the [apothecaries' system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apothecaries%27_system). This was long used in medicine, but has been largely replaced by the [metric system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_system) (milligrams).[^cite_note-dictionary.com-12] The only troy weight in widespread use is the British Imperial troy ounce and its American counterpart. Both are based on a grain of 0.06479891 gram (exact, by definition), with 480 grains to a troy ounce (compared with 437+1⁄2 grains for an ounce avoirdupois). The British Empire abolished the 12-ounce troy pound in the 19th century. It has been retained, though rarely used, in the American system. Larger amounts of precious metals are conventionally counted in hundreds or thousands of troy ounces, or in kilograms.&#x20;

Troy ounces have been and are still often used in precious metal markets in countries that otherwise use [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) (SI).[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14] However, the [People's Bank of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People%27s_Bank_of_China) – which had been using troy measurements in minting [Gold Pandas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Gold_Panda#Revised_weights) since 1982 – from 2016 specifies Chinese bullion coins in an integer numbers of grams.[^cite_note-15]&#x20;

## Units of measurement



Chart comparing the mass (in grams) of tower, Troy, merchant, avoirdupois and London pounds. Each colored block represents one of that system's ounces (gold=Troy, blue= avoirdupois, purple=tower)

### Troy pound (lb t)&#x20;



The standard British troy pound manufactured in 1758; it bears the abbreviation [℔](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E2%84%94) ("pound") and the letter "T" for troy.

The troy pound (lb t) consists of twelve troy ounces[^cite_note-Perth_Mint-2] and thus is 5760 grains (373.24172 grams). (An avoirdupois pound is approximately 21.53% heavier, at 7000 grains (453.59237 grams), and consists of sixteen avoirdupois ounces.)&#x20;

### Troy ounce (oz t)&#x20;

A troy ounce is 480 [grains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain_\(unit\)).[^cite_note-Perth_Mint-2] Since the implementation of the [international yard and pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_yard_and_pound) agreement of 1 July 1959, the grain measure is precisely 64.79891 [milligrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milligram).[^cite_note-NIST-16] [^cite_note-17]<sup>: C-19 </sup>[^cite_note-nbs447-18] Thus one troy ounce = 480 grains × 0.06479891 grams/grain = 31.10347680 grams. Since the ounce avoirdupois is 437.5 grains, a troy ounce is exactly 480⁄437.5 = 192⁄175 or about 1.09714 ounces avoirdupois or about 9.7% more. The troy ounce for trading precious metals is considered to be sufficiently approximated by 31.10 g in EU directive 80/181/EEC.[^cite_note-80/181/EER-19] The troy ounce is the only troy unit allowed in trade in the United Kingdom, and only for precious metals, since the implementation of the [Weights and Measures Act of 1985](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weights_and_Measures_Acts_\(UK\)#Metric_units_of_measure).[^cite_note-20]&#x20;

The Dutch troy system is based on a *mark* of 8 Dutch ounces, the ounce of 20 *engels* (pennyweights), the *engel* of 32 *azen*. The *mark* was rated as 3,798 troy grains or 246.084 grams. The divisions are identical to the tower system.[^cite_note-Kelly-10]&#x20;

### Pennyweight (dwt)

The [pennyweight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennyweight) symbol is *dwt*. One pennyweight is 24 grains, and 20 pennyweights make one troy ounce.[^cite_note-Perth_Mint-2] Because there are 12 troy ounces in the old troy pound, there are 240 pennyweights to the pound (mass) – just as there are 240 pennies in the [original pound-sterling](270673.md#Anglo-Saxon,_c_800_CE). However, prior to 1526, the English [pound sterling](270673.md) was based on the [tower pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_pound), which is 15⁄16 of a troy pound. The *d* in *dwt* stands for *[denarius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denarius)*, the ancient Roman coin that equates loosely to a penny. The symbol *d* for penny can be recognized in the form of British pre-decimal pennies, in which pounds, shillings, and pence are indicated using the symbols *£*, *s*, and *d*, respectively.&#x20;

### Troy grain

Main article: [Grain (mass)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain_\(mass\))

There is no specific 'troy grain'. All [Imperial systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_unit) use the same measure of mass called a grain (historically of [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley)), each weighing 1⁄7000 of an avoirdupois pound (and thus a little under 65 milligrams).[^cite_note-21]&#x20;

### Mint masses

Mint masses, also known as *moneyers' masses*, were legalized by Act of Parliament dated 17 July 1649 entitled *An Act touching the monies and coins of England*. A grain is 20 mites, a mite is 24 droits, a droit is 20 perits, a perit is 24 blanks.[^cite_note-Britain)1891-22][^cite_note-Miege-23]&#x20;
