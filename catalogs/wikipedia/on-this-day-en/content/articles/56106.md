A **wildfire**, **forest fire**, **brushfire** or a **bushfire** is an unplanned and uncontrolled [fire](11145.md) in an area of [combustible](9808214.md) [vegetation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation).[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-CIFFC_Glossary2-2] Some natural forest ecosystems [depend on wildfire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_ecology).[^cite_note-Fire_Tree_Mortality2222-3] Modern [forest management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_management) often engages in prescribed burns to mitigate fire risk and promote natural forest cycles. Controlled burns can turn into wildfires if they escape containment lines.

Wildfires can be classified by cause of ignition, physical properties, combustible material present, and the effect of weather on the fire.[^cite_note-UToronto2-4] Wildfire severity results from a combination of factors such as available fuels, physical setting, and weather.[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6][^cite_note-FireBehavior-7][^cite_note-8] Climatic cycles with wet periods that create substantial fuels, followed by [drought](37335.md) and heat, often precede severe wildfires.[^cite_note-9] These cycles have been intensified by [climate change](5042951.md),[^cite_note-\:0-10]<sup>: 247 </sup> and can be exacerbated by curtailment of mitigation measures (such as budget or equipment funding), or sheer enormity of the event.&#x20;

&#x20;Wildfires are a common [disaster](58921.md) in many parts of the world, for example in Russia ([Siberia](29639.md)), the United States (for example in [California](5407.md), [Washington](13015878.md), [Oregon](26811621.md), [Texas](29810.md), [Florida](18933066.md)), [Canada](5042916.md) and [Australia](4689264.md).[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14][^cite_note-dangelo2022-15] Wildfires can severely impact humans and their settlements. Effects include for example the direct health impacts of smoke and fire, as well as destruction of property (especially in [wildland–urban interfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildland%E2%80%93urban_interface)), and economic losses. There is also the potential for contamination of water and soil.



Annual number of fires



Annual area burnt by wildfires

At a global level, human practices have made the impacts of wildfire worse, with a doubling in land area burned by wildfires compared to natural levels.[^cite_note-\:0-10]<sup>: 247 </sup> Humans have impacted wildfire through [climate change](5042951.md) (e.g. more intense [heat waves](293270.md) and [droughts](37335.md)), [land-use change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land-use_change), and [wildfire suppression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildfire_suppression).[^cite_note-\:0-10]<sup>: 247 </sup> The carbon released from wildfires can add to [carbon dioxide concentrations in the atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide_in_Earth's_atmosphere) and thus contribute to the [greenhouse effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_effect). This creates a [climate change feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_feedbacks).[^cite_note-IPCC-2021-16]<sup>: 20 </sup>

Naturally occurring wildfires can have beneficial effects on those [ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem) that have evolved with fire.[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18][^cite_note-\:22-19] In fact, many plant species depend on the effects of fire for growth and reproduction.[^cite_note-NOVA222-20]

## Ignition

The ignition of a fire takes place through either natural causes or human activity (deliberate or not). Over 85% of wildfires in the United States are started by human activity.[^cite_note-21]



Lightning-sparked wildfires are frequent occurrences during the dry summer season in [Nevada](21216.md).

### Natural causes

Natural occurrences that can ignite wildfires without the involvement of humans include [lightning](61344.md), [volcanic eruptions](8921015.md), [sparks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spark_\(fire\)) from rock falls, and [spontaneous combustions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_combustion).[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-Scott2000-23]

### Human activity

Sources of human-caused fire may include [arson](184695.md), accidental ignition, or the uncontrolled use of fire in land-clearing and agriculture such as [slash-and-burn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash-and-burn) farming.[^cite_note-24] In the [tropics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropics), farmers often practice the slash-and-burn method of clearing fields during the [dry season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_season).

In [middle latitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_latitudes), the most common human causes of wildfires are equipment generating sparks (chainsaws, grinders, mowers, etc.), [overhead power lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility-caused_wildfires), and arson.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26][^cite_note-27][^cite_note-Krock-28][^cite_note-29]

Arson may account for over 20% of human caused fires,[^cite_note-30] although human activities, including campfires, power line failures, and equipment use, are responsible for approximately 85% of wildfires.[^cite_note-31] The combination of these ignition sources with dry conditions leads to more frequent and severe fires. However, in the [2019–20 Australian bushfire season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019%E2%80%9320_Australian_bushfire_season) "an independent study found online [bots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_bot) and [trolls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troll_\(slang\)) exaggerating the role of arson in the fires."[^cite_note-32] In the [2023 Canadian wildfires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Canadian_wildfires) false claims of [arson](184695.md) gained traction on social media; however, arson is generally not the main cause of wildfires in Canada.[^cite_note-33][^cite_note-wild-theories-34] In California, generally 6–10% of wildfires annually are arson.[^cite_note-35]

[Coal seam fires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_seam_fire) burn in the thousands around the world, such as those in [Burning Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burning_Mountain), New South Wales; [Centralia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centralia,_Pennsylvania), Pennsylvania; and several [coal-sustained fires in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal-seam_fire#China). They can also flare up unexpectedly and ignite nearby flammable material.[^cite_note-36]

The spread of wildfires varies based on the flammable material present, its vertical arrangement and moisture content, and weather conditions.[^cite_note-Graham,_et_al.,_iv-37] Fuel arrangement and density is governed in part by [topography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topography), as land shape determines factors such as available sunlight and water for plant growth. Overall, fire types can be generally characterized by their fuels as follows:

- **Ground** fires are fed by subterranean roots, duff on the [forest floor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_floor), and other buried [organic matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_matter). Ground fires typically burn by smoldering, and can burn slowly for days to months, such as [peat fires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peat#Peat_Fires) in [Kalimantan](165470.md) and Eastern [Sumatra](28679.md), [Indonesia](14579.md), which resulted from a [riceland creation project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mega_Rice_Project_\(Kalimantan\)) that unintentionally drained and dried the peat.

  [^cite_note-38]

  [^cite_note-39]

  [^cite_note-40]

- **Crawling** or **surface** fires are fueled by low-lying vegetative matter on the forest floor such as leaf and timber litter, debris, grass, and low-lying shrubbery.

  [^cite_note-41]

  &#x20;This kind of fire often burns at a relatively lower temperature than crown fires (less than 400 °C or 750 °F) and may spread at slow rate, though steep slopes and wind can accelerate the rate of spread.

  [^cite_note-42]

  &#x20;This fuel type is especially susceptible to ignition due to *spotting* (see [below](#Spotting)).

- **Ladder** fires consume material between low-level vegetation and tree canopies, such as small trees, downed logs, and [vines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vine). [Kudzu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kudzu), [Old World climbing fern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_World_climbing_fern), and other [invasive plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasive_plant) that scale trees may also encourage ladder fires.

  [^cite_note-FireInitiative-43]

- **Crown, canopy**, or **aerial** fires burn suspended material at the canopy level, such as tall trees, vines, and mosses. The ignition of a crown fire, termed *crowning*, is dependent on the density of the suspended material, canopy height, canopy continuity, sufficient surface and ladder fires, vegetation moisture content, and weather conditions during the blaze.

  [^cite_note-44]

  &#x20;Stand-replacing fires lit by humans can spread into the [Amazon rain forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_rain_forest), damaging ecosystems not particularly suited for heat or arid conditions.

  [^cite_note-45]

## Physical properties

See also: [Combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion), [Fire control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_control), [Heat wave](293270.md), and [Firestorm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firestorm)



A dirt road acted as a fire barrier in South Africa. The effects of the barrier can clearly be seen on the unburnt (left) and burnt (right) sides of the road.

Wildfires occur when all the necessary elements of a fire triangle come together in a susceptible area: an ignition source is brought into contact with a combustible material such as [vegetation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation) that is subjected to enough heat and has an adequate supply of oxygen from the ambient air. A high moisture content usually prevents ignition and slows propagation, because higher temperatures are needed to evaporate any water in the material and heat the material to its [fire point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_point).[^cite_note-FireBehavior-7][^cite_note-NIFC-46]

Dense forests usually provide more shade, resulting in lower ambient temperatures and greater [humidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humidity), and are therefore less susceptible to wildfires.[^cite_note-Graham,_et_al.,_12-47] Less dense material such as grasses and leaves are easier to ignite because they contain less water than denser material such as branches and trunks.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-48] Plants continuously lose water by [evapotranspiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evapotranspiration), but water loss is usually balanced by water absorbed from the soil, humidity, or rain.[^cite_note-49] When this balance is not maintained, often as a consequence of [droughts](37335.md), plants dry out and are therefore more flammable.[^cite_note-Structure-50][^cite_note-51]

A wildfire *front* is the portion sustaining continuous flaming combustion, where unburned material meets active flames, or the [smoldering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smolder) transition between unburned and burned material.[^cite_note-52] As the front approaches, the fire heats both the surrounding air and woody material through [convection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convection) and [thermal radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_radiation). First, wood is dried as water is vaporized at a temperature of 100 °C (212 °F). Next, the [pyrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrolysis) of wood at 230 °C (450 °F) releases flammable gases. Finally, wood can smolder at 380 °C (720 °F) or, when heated sufficiently, ignite at 590 °C (1,000 °F).[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54] Even before the flames of a wildfire arrive at a particular location, [heat transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_transfer) from the wildfire front warms the air to 800 °C (1,500 °F), which pre-heats and dries flammable materials, causing materials to ignite faster and allowing the fire to spread faster.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-48][^cite_note-FireScars-55] High-temperature and long-duration surface wildfires may encourage [flashover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flashover) or *torching*: the drying of [tree canopies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canopy_\(ecology\)) and their subsequent ignition from below.[^cite_note-56]

&#x20;Wildfires have a rapid *forward rate of spread* (FROS) when burning through dense uninterrupted fuels.[^cite_note-57] They can move as fast as 10.8 kilometres per hour (6.7 mph) in forests and 22 kilometres per hour (14 mph) in grasslands.[^cite_note-58] Wildfires can advance tangentially to the main front to form a *flanking* front, or burn in the opposite direction of the main front by *backing*.[^cite_note-59] They may also spread by *jumping* or *spotting* as winds and vertical [convection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convection) columns carry *firebrands* (hot wood embers) and other burning materials through the air over roads, rivers, and other barriers that may otherwise act as [firebreaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firebreak).[^cite_note-underfire-60][^cite_note-61] Torching and fires in tree canopies encourage spotting, and dry ground fuels around a wildfire are especially vulnerable to ignition from firebrands.[^cite_note-62] Spotting can create spot fires as hot embers and firebrands ignite fuels downwind from the fire. In [Australian bushfires](20827796.md), spot fires are known to occur as far as 20 kilometres (12 mi) from the fire front.[^cite_note-Kilmore-East-63]

Especially large wildfires may affect air currents in their immediate vicinities by the [stack effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_effect): air rises as it is heated, and large wildfires create powerful [updrafts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Updraft) that will [draw in](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advection) new, cooler air from surrounding areas in [thermal columns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_column).[^cite_note-64] Great vertical differences in temperature and humidity encourage [pyrocumulus clouds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrocumulus_cloud), strong winds, and [fire whirls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_whirl) with the force of tornadoes at speeds of more than 80 kilometres per hour (50 mph).[^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67] Rapid rates of spread, prolific crowning or spotting, the presence of fire whirls, and strong convection columns signify extreme conditions.[^cite_note-68]

### Intensity variations during day and night



A wildfire in Venezuela during a [drought](37335.md)

Intensity also increases during daytime hours. Burn rates of smoldering logs are up to five times greater during the day due to lower humidity, increased temperatures, and increased wind speeds.[^cite_note-69] Sunlight warms the ground during the day which creates air currents that travel uphill. At night the land cools, creating air currents that travel downhill. Wildfires are fanned by these winds and often follow the air currents over hills and through valleys.[^cite_note-70] Fires in Europe occur frequently during the hours of 12\:00 p.m. and 2\:00 p.m.[^cite_note-71] Wildfire suppression operations in the United States revolve around a 24-hour *fire day* that begins at 10\:00 a.m. due to the predictable increase in intensity resulting from the daytime warmth.[^cite_note-72] Based on [climate](5042951.md)-driven weakening of day-night weather constraints, a study published in *[Science Advances](45385909.md)* estimated that from 1975 to 2024, annual potential burning hours for wildfires in North America rose 36%.[^cite_note-SciAdvances_20260417-73]

### Increasing risks due to climate change

Climate change promotes the type of weather that makes wildfires more likely. In some areas, an increase of wildfires has been attributed directly to climate change.[^cite_note-\:0-10]<sup>: 247 </sup> Evidence from Earth's past also shows more fire in warmer periods.[^cite_note-Jones-2022-78] Climate change increases [potential evapotranspiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_evapotranspiration). This can cause vegetation and soils to dry out when potential evaporation exceeds precipitation and available moisture from the given ecosystem. The [vapor pressure deficit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vapour-pressure_deficit) also contributes to increasing wildfire risk and has been worsening in the warming climate.[^cite_note-79] When a fire starts in an area with very dry vegetation, it can spread rapidly. Higher temperatures can also lengthen the fire season. This is the time of year in which severe wildfires are most likely, particularly in regions where snow is disappearing.[^cite_note-Dunne-2020-80]

Weather conditions are raising the risks of wildfires. But the total area burnt by wildfires has decreased. This is mostly because [savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savanna) has been converted to [cropland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croplands), so there are fewer trees to burn.[^cite_note-Dunne-2020-80]

Even though [Boreal forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiga) don't account for a large fraction of global burned area, they do contribute substantially to the global carbon emissions from wildfire.[^cite_note-81] These forests could face additional risk under climate change due if the amount of [lightning](61344.md) increases as expected.[^cite_note-82]

[Climate variability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_variability) including [heat waves](293270.md), [droughts](37335.md), and [El Niño](317311.md), and regional weather patterns, such as high-pressure ridges, can increase the risk and alter the behavior of wildfires dramatically.[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84][^cite_note-85] Years of high precipitation can produce rapid vegetation growth, which when followed by warmer periods can encourage more widespread fires and longer fire seasons.[^cite_note-86] High temperatures dry out the fuel loads and make them more flammable, increasing tree mortality and posing significant risks to global forest health.[^cite_note-Hartmann-87][^cite_note-Brando-88][^cite_note-89] Since the mid-1980s, in the Western US, earlier snowmelt and associated warming have also been associated with an increase in length and severity of the wildfire season, or the most fire-prone time of the year.[^cite_note-90] A 2019 study indicates that the increase in [fire risk in California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_fires) may be partially attributable to [human-induced climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man-made_climate_change).[^cite_note-91]

In the summer of 1974–1975 (southern hemisphere), [Australia](4689264.md) suffered its worst recorded wildfire, when 15% of Australia's land mass suffered "extensive fire damage".[^cite_note-abs1995-92] Fires that summer burned up an estimated 117 million [hectares](24919513.md) (290 million [acres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acre); 1,170,000 [square kilometres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_kilometre); 450,000 [square miles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_mile)).[^cite_note-AIDR-93][^cite_note-94] In Australia, the annual number of hot days (above 35 °C or 95 °F) and very hot days (above 40 °C or 104 °F) has increased significantly in many areas of the country since 1950. The country has always had bushfires but in 2019, the extent and ferocity of these fires increased dramatically.[^cite_note-95] For the first time catastrophic bushfire conditions were declared for Greater Sydney. New South Wales and Queensland declared a state of emergency but fires were also burning in South Australia and Western Australia.[^cite_note-96]

In [2019, extreme heat and dryness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_in_climate_change) caused massive [wildfires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildfires_in_2019) in [Siberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Siberia_wildfires), [Alaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deshka_Landing_Fire), [Canary Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Canary_Islands_wildfires), [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019%E2%80%9320_Australian_bushfire_season), and in the [Amazon rainforest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Amazon_rainforest_wildfires). The fires in the latter were caused mainly by [illegal logging](19732090.md). The smoke from the fires expanded over a huge territory including major cities, dramatically reducing air quality.[^cite_note-97]

&#x20;As of August 2020, the [wildfires in that year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildfires_in_2020) were 13% worse than in 2019 due primarily to [climate change](5042951.md), [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation) and agricultural burning. The [Amazon rainforest](48139.md)'s existence is threatened by fires.[^cite_note-watching-earth-burn-98][^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100][^cite_note-101] Record-breaking [wildfires in 2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildfires_in_2021) occurred in [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Turkey_wildfires), [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Greece_wildfires) and [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Russia_wildfires), thought to be linked to climate change.[^cite_note-102]

### Carbon dioxide and other emissions from fires

The carbon released from wildfires can add to greenhouse gas concentrations. [Climate models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_model) do not yet fully reflect this [feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_feedbacks).[^cite_note-IPCC-2021-16]<sup>: 20 </sup>

&#x20;Wildfires release large amounts of carbon dioxide, black and brown carbon particles, and ozone precursors such as [volatile organic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volatile_organic_compound) and [nitrogen oxides (NOx)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NOx) into the atmosphere.[^cite_note-103][^cite_note-104] These emissions affect radiation, clouds, and climate on regional and even global scales.[^cite_note-dangelo2022-15] Wildfires also emit substantial amounts of semi-volatile organic species that can partition from the gas phase to form [secondary organic aerosol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_organic_aerosol) (SOA) over hours to days after emission. In addition, the formation of the other pollutants as the air is transported can lead to harmful exposures for populations in regions far away from the wildfires.[^cite_note-105][^cite_note-dangelo2022-15] While direct emissions of harmful pollutants can affect first responders and residents, wildfire smoke can also be transported over long distances and impact air quality across local, regional, and global scales.[^cite_note-106]



Wildfire near [Yosemite National Park](48664.md), United States, in 2013. The [Rim Fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rim_Fire) burned more than 250,000 acres (1,000 km

2

) of forest.

The health effects of wildfire smoke, such as worsening [cardiovascular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulatory_system) and respiratory conditions, extend beyond immediate exposure, contributing to nearly 16,000 annual deaths, a number expected to rise to 30,000 by 2050. The economic impact is also significant, with projected costs reaching \$240 billion annually by 2050, surpassing other climate-related damages.[^cite_note-107]

Over the past century, wildfires have accounted for 20–25% of global carbon emissions, the remainder from human activities.[^cite_note-108] Global carbon emissions from wildfires through August 2020 equaled the average annual emissions of the [European Union](9317.md).[^cite_note-global-carbon-fire-2019-109] In 2020, the carbon released by California's wildfires was significantly larger than the state's other carbon emissions.[^cite_note-110]

Forest fires in Indonesia in 1997 were estimated to have released between 0.81 and 2.57 gigatonnes (0.89 and 2.83 billion [short tons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_ton)) of CO<sub>2</sub> into the atmosphere, which is between 13–40% of the annual global carbon dioxide emissions from burning fossil fuels.[^cite_note-111][^cite_note-112]

In June and July 2019, fires in the Arctic emitted more than 140 megatons of carbon dioxide, according to an analysis by CAMS. To put that into perspective this amounts to the same amount of carbon emitted by 36 million cars in a year. The recent wildfires and their massive CO<sub>2</sub> emissions mean that it will be important to take them into consideration when implementing measures for reaching greenhouse gas reduction targets accorded with the [Paris climate agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_climate_agreement).[^cite_note-113] Due to the complex oxidative chemistry occurring during the transport of wildfire smoke in the atmosphere,[^cite_note-114] the toxicity of emissions was indicated to increase over time.[^cite_note-115][^cite_note-116]

Atmospheric models suggest that these concentrations of sooty particles could increase absorption of incoming [solar radiation](27979.md) during winter months by as much as 15%.[^cite_note-117] The Amazon is estimated to hold around 90 billion tons of carbon. As of 2019, the earth's atmosphere has 415 parts per million of carbon, and the destruction of the Amazon would add about 38 parts per million.[^cite_note-118]

Some research has shown wildfire smoke can have a cooling effect.[^cite_note-119][^cite_note-120][^cite_note-121]

Research in 2007 stated that black carbon in snow changed temperature three times more than atmospheric carbon dioxide. As much as 94 percent of Arctic warming may be caused by dark carbon on snow that initiates melting. The dark carbon comes from fossil fuels burning, wood and other biofuels, and forest fires. Melting can occur even at low concentrations of dark carbon (below five parts per billion).[^cite_note-Biello_2007_m566-122]

## Prevention and mitigation

See also: [Fire protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_protection)

A short video on managing and protecting the natural habitat between a town and the hillside, from the risk of fire.

Wildfire prevention refers to the preemptive methods aimed at reducing the risk of fires as well as lessening its severity and spread.[^cite_note-123] Prevention techniques aim to manage air quality, maintain ecological balances, protect resources,[^cite_note-van_Wagtendonk,_14-124] and to affect future fires.[^cite_note-125] Prevention policies must consider the role that humans play in wildfires, since, for example, 95% of forest fires in [Europe](9239.md) are related to human involvement.[^cite_note-126]

Wildfire prevention programs around the world may employ techniques such as *wildland fire use* (WFU) and *prescribed or [controlled burns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_burn)*.[^cite_note-127][^cite_note-128] *Wildland fire use* refers to any fire of natural causes that is monitored but allowed to burn. *Controlled burns* are fires ignited by government agencies under less dangerous weather conditions.[^cite_note-129] Other objectives can include maintenance of healthy forests, rangelands, and wetlands, and support of ecosystem diversity.[^cite_note-US_F\&W_Fire_Mgt-130]

A small fire on the slope of a hill. The hill features small, green shrubbery and some trees. A person in light-colored clothing in seen in the background, some distance from the flames.

A prescribed burn in a *[Pinus nigra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinus_nigra)* stand in Portugal

Strategies for wildfire prevention, detection, control and suppression have varied over the years.[^cite_note-131] One common and inexpensive technique to reduce the risk of uncontrolled wildfires is [controlled burning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_burn): intentionally igniting smaller less-intense fires to minimize the amount of flammable material available for a potential wildfire.[^cite_note-IS-132][^cite_note-133] Vegetation may be burned periodically to limit the accumulation of plants and other debris that may serve as fuel, while also maintaining high species diversity.[^cite_note-\:3-134][^cite_note-\:4-135] While other people claim that controlled burns and a policy of allowing some wildfires to burn are the cheapest method and an ecologically appropriate policy for many forests, they tend not to take into account the economic value of resources that are consumed by the fire, especially merchantable timber.[^cite_note-\:5-136] Some studies conclude that while fuels may also be removed by logging, such thinning treatments may not be effective at reducing fire severity under extreme weather conditions.[^cite_note-\:6-137] Regulatory barriers can in some cases delay wildfire prevention measures.[^cite_note-d893-138]

Building codes in fire-prone areas typically require that structures be built of flame-resistant materials and a [defensible space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defensible_space_\(fire_control\)) be maintained by clearing flammable materials within a prescribed distance from the structure.[^cite_note-\:8-139][^cite_note-\:9-140] Communities in the [Philippines](23440.md) also maintain [fire lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_line) 5 to 10 meters (16 to 33 ft) wide between the forest and their village, and patrol these lines during summer months or seasons of dry weather.[^cite_note-141] Continued residential development in fire-prone areas and rebuilding structures destroyed by fires has been met with criticism.[^cite_note-Trial2-142] The ecological benefits of fire are often overridden by the economic and safety benefits of protecting structures and human life.[^cite_note-NOAA-143]

### Goat grazing programs

As climate change drives more frequent and more intense wildfires, more effort is being given to mitigation of fire potential by active measures such as managing fire fuels (ground cover, weeds, small shrubs, [coyote brush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coyote_brush), etc). In Northern California, for example, [goat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goat) herds have been used in many communities to reduce the amount of fire fuels on the outskirts of some communities. It is estimated that 60 to 80,000 goats were thus employed by 2024.[^cite_note-144]

## Detection

See also: [Remote sensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_sensing)

A four-legged tower with a small at the top, next to two one-story buildings. The tower is four stories tall. Trees are at either side, and in the foreground, there are rocks, some vegetation, and a rough trail.

Dry Mountain Fire Lookout in the [Ochoco National Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ochoco_National_Forest), [Oregon](26811621.md), US circa 1930

The demand for timely, high-quality fire information has increased in recent years. Fast and effective detection is a key factor in wildfire fighting.[^cite_note-145] Early detection efforts were focused on early response, accurate results in both daytime and nighttime, and the ability to prioritize fire danger.[^cite_note-Integration-146] [Fire lookout towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_lookout_tower) were used in the United States in the early 20th century and fires were reported using telephones, [carrier pigeons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrier_pigeon), and [heliographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliograph).[^cite_note-147] Aerial and land photography using [instant cameras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant_camera) were used in the 1950s until [infrared scanning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_photography) was developed for fire detection in the 1960s. However, information analysis and delivery was often delayed by limitations in communication technology. Early satellite-derived fire analyses were hand-drawn on maps at a remote site and sent via overnight mail to the [fire manager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incident_commander). During the [Yellowstone fires of 1988](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellowstone_fires_of_1988), a data station was established in [West Yellowstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Yellowstone), permitting the delivery of satellite-based fire information in approximately four hours.[^cite_note-Integration-146]

Public hotlines, [fire lookouts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_lookout) in towers, and ground and aerial patrols can be used as a means of early detection of forest fires. However, accurate human observation may be limited by [operator fatigue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asthenopia), time of day, time of year, and geographic location. Electronic systems have gained popularity in recent years as a possible resolution to human operator error. These systems may be semi- or fully automated and employ systems based on the risk area and degree of human presence, as suggested by [GIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_information_system) data analyses. An integrated approach of multiple systems can be used to merge satellite data, aerial imagery, and personnel position via [Global Positioning System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Positioning_System) (GPS) into a collective whole for near-realtime use by wireless [Incident Command Centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incident_Command_System).[^cite_note-148]

### Local sensor networks

A small, high risk area that features thick vegetation, a strong human presence, or is close to a critical urban area can be monitored using a local [sensor network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensor_network). Detection systems may include [wireless sensor networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_sensor_network) that act as automated weather systems: detecting temperature, humidity, and smoke.[^cite_note-149][^cite_note-150][^cite_note-151][^cite_note-152] These may be battery-powered, solar-powered, or *tree-rechargeable*: able to recharge their battery systems using the small electrical currents in plant material.[^cite_note-153] Larger, medium-risk areas can be monitored by scanning towers that incorporate fixed cameras and sensors to detect smoke or additional factors such as the infrared signature of carbon dioxide produced by fires. Additional capabilities such as [night vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Night_vision), brightness detection, and color change detection may also be incorporated into [sensor arrays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensor_array).[^cite_note-154][^cite_note-155][^cite_note-156]

The [Department of Natural Resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Natural_Resources) signed a contract with [PanoAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PanoAI?action=edit\&redlink=1) for the installation of 360 degree 'rapid detection' cameras around the Pacific northwest, which are mounted on cell towers and are capable of continuous monitoring of a 24-kilometre (15 mi) radius.[^cite_note-157] Additionally, [Sensaio Tech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensaio_Tech?action=edit\&redlink=1), based in Brazil and Toronto, has released a sensor device that continuously monitors 14 different variables common in forests, ranging from soil temperature to salinity. This information is connected live back to clients through dashboard visualizations, while mobile notifications are provided regarding dangerous levels.[^cite_note-158]

### Satellite and aerial monitoring



The [2020 Córdoba wildfires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_C%C3%B3rdoba_wildfires) imaged by [NASA](18426568.md)'s [FIRMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_Information_for_Resource_Management_System)

[Satellite](27683.md) and aerial monitoring through the use of planes, helicopter, or UAVs can provide a wider view and may be sufficient to monitor very large, low risk areas. These more sophisticated systems employ [GPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Positioning_System) and aircraft-mounted infrared or high-resolution visible cameras to identify and target wildfires.[^cite_note-159][^cite_note-160] Satellite-mounted sensors such as [Envisat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envisat)'s [Advanced Along Track Scanning Radiometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AATSR) and [European Remote-Sensing Satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Remote-Sensing_Satellite)'s Along-Track Scanning Radiometer can measure infrared radiation emitted by fires, identifying hot spots greater than 39 °C (102 °F).[^cite_note-161][^cite_note-162] The [National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration](37876.md)'s Hazard Mapping System combines remote-sensing data from satellite sources such as [Geostationary Operational Environmental Satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostationary_Operational_Environmental_Satellite) (GOES), [Moderate-Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moderate-Resolution_Imaging_Spectroradiometer) (MODIS), and [Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Very_High_Resolution_Radiometer) (AVHRR) for detection of fire and smoke plume locations.[^cite_note-163][^cite_note-Swarm-164] However, satellite detection is prone to offset errors, anywhere from 2 to 3 kilometers (1 to 2 mi) for MODIS and AVHRR data and up to 12 kilometers (7.5 mi) for GOES data.[^cite_note-165] Satellites in geostationary orbits may become disabled, and satellites in polar orbits are often limited by their short window of observation time. Cloud cover and image resolution may also limit the effectiveness of satellite imagery.[^cite_note-166] [Global Forest Watch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Forest_Watch)[^cite_note-167] provides detailed daily updates on fire alerts.[^cite_note-168]

In 2015 a new fire detection tool is in operation at the [U.S. Department of Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Agriculture) (USDA) [Forest Service](42652.md) (USFS) which uses data from the [Suomi National Polar-orbiting Partnership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suomi_NPP) (NPP) satellite to detect smaller fires in more detail than previous space-based products. The high-resolution data is used with a computer model to predict how a fire will change direction based on weather and land conditions.[^cite_note-169]

In 2014, an international campaign was organized in South Africa's Kruger National Park to validate fire detection products including the new VIIRS active fire data. In advance of that campaign, the Meraka Institute of the Council for Scientific and Industrial Research in Pretoria, South Africa, an early adopter of the VIIRS 375 m fire product, put it to use during several large wildfires in Kruger.[^cite_note-170]

Since 2021 NASA has provided active fire locations in [near real-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_real-time) via the [Fire Information for Resource Management System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_Information_for_Resource_Management_System) (FIRMS).

The increased prevalence of wildfires has led to proposals deploy technologies based on [artificial intelligence](1164.md) for early detection, prevention, and prediction of wildfires.[^cite_note-171][^cite_note-172][^cite_note-173]

## Suppression

Main article: [Wildfire suppression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildfire_suppression)



A Russian firefighter extinguishing a wildfire



A [JSDF](2785204.md) helicopter dropping water at [a wildfire](83048717.md)

Wildfire suppression depends on the technologies available in the area in which the wildfire occurs. In less developed nations the techniques used can be as simple as throwing sand or beating the fire with sticks or palm fronds.[^cite_note-174] In more advanced nations, the suppression methods vary due to increased technological capacity. [Silver iodide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_iodide) can be used to encourage snow fall,[^cite_note-175] while [fire retardants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_retardant) and water can be dropped onto fires by [unmanned aerial vehicles](58900.md), [planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airtanker), and [helicopters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helitack).[^cite_note-176][^cite_note-177] Complete fire suppression is no longer an expectation, but the majority of wildfires are often extinguished before they grow out of control. While more than 99% of the 10,000 new wildfires each year are contained, escaped wildfires under extreme weather conditions are difficult to suppress without a change in the weather. Wildfires in Canada and the US burn an average of 54,500 square kilometers (13,000,000 acres) per year.[^cite_note-178][^cite_note-179]

Above all, fighting wildfires can become deadly. A wildfire's burning front may also change direction unexpectedly and jump across fire breaks. Intense heat and smoke can lead to disorientation and loss of appreciation of the direction of the fire, which can make fires particularly dangerous. For example, during the 1949 [Mann Gulch fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mann_Gulch_fire) in [Montana](19978.md), United States, thirteen [smokejumpers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smokejumper) died when they lost their communication links, became disoriented, and were overtaken by the fire.[^cite_note-180] In the Australian [February 2009 Victorian bushfires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_2009_Victorian_bushfires), at least 173 people died and over 2,029 homes and 3,500 structures were lost when they became engulfed by wildfire.[^cite_note-homeslost-181]

### Costs of wildfire suppression

The suppression of wild fires takes up a large amount of a country's [gross domestic product](12594.md) which directly affects the country's economy.[^cite_note-ellison-182] While costs vary wildly from year to year, depending on the severity of each fire season, in the United States, local, state, federal and tribal agencies collectively spend tens of billions of dollars annually to suppress wildfires. In the United States, it was reported that approximately \$6 billion was spent between 2004–2008 to suppress wildfires in the country.[^cite_note-ellison-182] In California, the [U.S. Forest Service](42652.md) spends about \$200 million per year to suppress 98% of wildfires and up to \$1 billion to suppress the other 2% of fires that escape initial attack and become large.[^cite_note-183]

### Wildland firefighting safety



Wildland firefighter working a brush fire in [Hopkinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopkinton,_New_Hampshire), New Hampshire, US

Wildland fire fighters face several life-threatening hazards including [heat stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_stress), [fatigue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatigue_\(safety\)), [smoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoke) and [dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), as well as the risk of other injuries such as [burns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burn), [cuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wound) and [scrapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrasion_\(medical\)), [animal bites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_bites), and even [rhabdomyolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhabdomyolysis).[^cite_note-niosh-blog-184][^cite_note-Hot_Tips_from_NIOSH-185] Between 2000 and 2016, more than 350 wildland firefighters died on-duty.[^cite_note-NIOSH_Wildland_Firefighting_default_page-186]

Especially in hot weather conditions, fires present the risk of heat stress, which can entail feeling heat, fatigue, weakness, vertigo, headache, or nausea. Heat stress can progress into heat strain, which entails physiological changes such as increased heart rate and core body temperature. This can lead to heat-related illnesses, such as heat rash, cramps, exhaustion or [heat stroke](24999087.md). Various factors can contribute to the risks posed by heat stress, including strenuous work, personal risk factors such as age and [fitness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_fitness), dehydration, sleep deprivation, and burdensome [personal protective equipment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_protective_equipment). Rest, cool water, and occasional breaks are crucial to mitigating the effects of heat stress.[^cite_note-niosh-blog-184]

Smoke, ash, and debris can also pose serious respiratory hazards for wildland firefighters. The smoke and dust from wildfires can contain gases such as [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide), [sulfur dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur_dioxide) and [formaldehyde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formaldehyde), as well as [particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_particulate_matter) such as [ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_ash) and [silica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silica). To reduce smoke exposure, wildfire fighting crews should, whenever possible, rotate firefighters through areas of heavy smoke, avoid downwind firefighting, use equipment rather than people in holding areas, and minimize mop-up. Camps and command posts should also be located upwind of wildfires. Protective clothing and equipment can also help minimize exposure to smoke and ash.[^cite_note-niosh-blog-184]

Firefighters are also at risk of cardiac events including strokes and heart attacks. Firefighters should maintain good physical fitness. Fitness programs, medical screening and examination programs which include stress tests can minimize the risks of firefighting cardiac problems.[^cite_note-niosh-blog-184] Other injury hazards wildland firefighters face include slips, trips, falls, burns, scrapes, and cuts from tools and equipment, being struck by trees, vehicles, or other objects, plant hazards such as thorns and poison ivy, snake and animal bites, vehicle crashes, electrocution from power lines or lightning storms, and unstable building structures.[^cite_note-niosh-blog-184]

Beyond immediate events, wildland firefighters are one of three high-risk groups for occupational air pollution exposure, along with agricultural and construction workers. For firefighters, the risks of spending long periods of time outdoors exposed to high levels of air pollution are compounded by exposure to heat, continued physical exertion, and an increased minute ventilation/metabolic rate. Workers are exposed to higher concentrations of pollutants for longer times with a higher per-minute ventilation and metabolic rate. These factors put firefighters at increased risk for long-term negative health effects from air pollution, compared to the health effects documented in the general population. Long-term effects can include increased risks for cardiovascular disease, lung cancer, COPD, pneumonia, asthma and poor mental health.[^cite_note-MacMurdo-187]

### Fire retardants

Main article: [Fire retardant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_retardant)

Fire retardants are used to slow wildfires by inhibiting combustion. They are aqueous solutions of ammonium phosphates and ammonium sulfates, as well as thickening agents.[^cite_note-188] The decision to apply retardant depends on the magnitude, location and intensity of the wildfire. In certain instances, fire retardant may also be applied as a precautionary fire defense measure.[^cite_note-coloradoan.com-189]

Typical fire retardants contain the same agents as fertilizers. Fire retardants may also affect water quality through leaching, [eutrophication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutrophication), or misapplication. Fire retardant's effects on drinking water remain inconclusive.[^cite_note-minesnewsroom.com-190] Dilution factors, including water body size, rainfall, and water flow rates lessen the concentration and potency of fire retardant.[^cite_note-coloradoan.com-189] Wildfire debris (ash and sediment) clog rivers and reservoirs increasing the risk for floods and erosion that ultimately slow and/or damage water treatment systems.[^cite_note-minesnewsroom.com-190][^cite_note-191] There is continued concern of fire retardant effects on land, water, wildlife habitats, and watershed quality, additional research is needed. However, on the positive side, fire retardant (specifically its nitrogen and phosphorus components) has been shown to have a fertilizing effect on nutrient-deprived soils and thus creates a temporary increase in vegetation.[^cite_note-coloradoan.com-189]

### Modeling

Fire Propagation Model



[2003 Canberra bushfires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_Canberra_bushfires), visible from [Parliament House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_House,_Canberra)

This section is an excerpt from [Wildfire modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildfire_modeling).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Wildfire_modeling\&action=edit)]

[Wildfire modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildfire_modeling) is concerned with [numerical simulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_simulation) of wildfires to comprehend and predict fire behavior.[^cite_note-192][^cite_note-193] Wildfire modeling aims to aid wildfire suppression, increase the safety of firefighters and the public, and minimize damage. Wildfire modeling can also aid in protecting [ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem), [watersheds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin), and [air quality](10934212.md).

Using [computational science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_science), wildfire modeling involves the statistical analysis of past fire events to predict spotting risks and front behavior. Various wildfire propagation models have been proposed in the past, including simple ellipses and egg- and fan-shaped models. Early attempts to determine wildfire behavior assumed terrain and vegetation uniformity. However, the exact behavior of a wildfire's front is dependent on a variety of factors, including wind speed and slope steepness. Some modern growth models utilize a combination of past ellipsoidal descriptions and [Huygens' Principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huygens'_Principle) to simulate fire growth as a continuously expanding polygon.[^cite_note-194][^cite_note-195] [Extreme value theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_value_theory) may also be used to predict the size of large wildfires. However, large fires that exceed suppression capabilities are often regarded as statistical outliers in standard analyses, even though fire policies are more influenced by large wildfires than by small fires.[^cite_note-Wildfire_modeling_Alvarado,_et_al.,_66-68-196]

## Categorization

In the US, the National Wildfire Coordinating Group utilizes a classification system that describes the complexity and threat of a wildfire.

The fire is evaluated for several criteria. Risk assessment includes threat to infrastructure, values, and social/economic concerns; probability of spread based on climate and environment; and growth and barriers for spread, fuel availability, and behavior conditions. The organization of the fire is evaluated, including external influences, ownership or jurisdiction issues, complexity of incident strategy due to weather, accessibility, or terrain, and potential duration.&#x20;

Based on these evaluations, the fire is assigned a type number. Type 5 wildfires have low risk and complexity, while Type 1 wildfires have high or overwhelming risk and complexity.[^cite_note-NWCG-197]

## Impacts on the natural environment

### On forests

Forests face many disturbances that can adversely affect their health and vitality. Fire is a significant disturbance in forests and a contributor to forest loss and degradation. On average, 261 million ha of land was affected by fire annually in 2007–2019; almost half (49%) of this area was forested. About 123 million ha of forest was affected by fire in 2019, of which 79% was in the subtropical climatic domain, 8% was in the boreal domain, 8% was in the tropical domain and 6% was in the temperate domain.[^cite_note-198]

### On the atmosphere

See also: [Air pollution](10934212.md), [Carbon cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_cycle), [Atmospheric chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_chemistry), [Haze](998156.md), [1997 Southeast Asian haze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Southeast_Asian_haze), and [2005 Malaysian haze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_Malaysian_haze)

Most of Earth's weather and air pollution resides in the [troposphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troposphere), the part of the atmosphere that extends from the surface of the planet to a height of about 10 kilometers (6 mi). The vertical lift of a severe thunderstorm or [pyrocumulonimbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrocumulonimbus) can be enhanced in the area of a large wildfire, which can propel smoke, [soot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soot) ([black carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_carbon)), and other [particulate matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_particulate_matter) as high as the lower [stratosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratosphere).[^cite_note-199] Previously, prevailing scientific theory held that most particles in the stratosphere came from [volcanoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcano), but smoke and other wildfire emissions have been detected from the lower stratosphere.[^cite_note-200] Pyrocumulus clouds can reach 6,100 meters (20,000 ft) over wildfires.[^cite_note-201] Satellite observation of smoke plumes from wildfires revealed that the plumes could be traced intact for distances exceeding 1,600 kilometers (1,000 mi).[^cite_note-202] Computer-aided models such as [CALPUFF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CALPUFF) may help predict the size and direction of wildfire-generated smoke plumes by using [atmospheric dispersion modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_dispersion_modeling).[^cite_note-203]

Wildfires can affect local atmospheric pollution,[^cite_note-204] and release carbon in the form of carbon dioxide.[^cite_note-205] [Wildfire emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildland_fire_emissions) contain fine particulate matter which can cause cardiovascular and respiratory problems.[^cite_note-206] Increased fire byproducts in the troposphere can increase ozone concentrations beyond safe levels.[^cite_note-207]

### On ecosystems

Main article: [Fire ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_ecology) See also: [Disturbance (ecology)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disturbance_\(ecology\)) and [Forestry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forestry)

Wildfires are common in climates that are sufficiently moist to allow the growth of vegetation but feature extended dry, hot periods.[^cite_note-NOVA222-20] Such places include the vegetated areas of Australia and [Southeast Asia](28741.md), the [veld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veld) in southern Africa, the [fynbos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fynbos) in the Western Cape of [South Africa](17416221.md), the forested areas of the United States and Canada, and the [Mediterranean Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Basin).

Some ecosystems are adapted to low-severity fires, where trees can survive but underbrush is cleared. Human suppression of lightning-caused fires in areas like Canada and the United States has created a buildup of fuel compared to more frequent fires before the 20th century. This has resulted in fewer but higher-severity fires which can kill mature trees.[^cite_note-208][^cite_note-209]

High-severity wildfire creates [complex early seral forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_early_seral_forest) habitat (also called "snag forest habitat"), which often has higher species richness and diversity than unburned old forest.[^cite_note-\:1-210] Plant and animal species in most types of North American forests evolved with fire, and many of these species depend on wildfires, and particularly high-severity fires, to reproduce and grow. Fire helps to return nutrients from plant matter back to the soil. The heat from fire is necessary to the germination of certain types of seeds, and the snags (dead trees) and early successional forests created by high-severity fire create habitat conditions that are beneficial to wildlife.[^cite_note-\:1-210] Early successional forests created by high-severity fire support some of the highest levels of native biodiversity found in temperate conifer forests.[^cite_note-\:2-211][^cite_note-212] Post-fire logging has no ecological benefits and many negative impacts; the same is often true for post-fire seeding.[^cite_note-\:5-136] The exclusion of wildfires can contribute to vegetation regime shifts, such as [woody plant encroachment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woody_plant_encroachment).[^cite_note-213][^cite_note-214]

Although some ecosystems rely on naturally occurring fires to regulate growth, some ecosystems suffer from too much fire, such as the [chaparral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaparral) in [southern California](62520.md) and lower-elevation deserts in the American Southwest. The increased fire frequency in these ordinarily fire-dependent areas has upset natural cycles, damaged native plant communities, and encouraged the growth of non-native weeds.[^cite_note-215][^cite_note-216][^cite_note-217][^cite_note-218] [Invasive species](67554.md), such as *[Lygodium microphyllum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lygodium_microphyllum)* and *[Bromus tectorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bromus_tectorum)*, can grow rapidly in areas that were damaged by fires. Because they are highly flammable, they can increase the future risk of fire, creating a [positive feedback loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_feedback_loop) that increases fire frequency and further alters native vegetation communities.[^cite_note-FireInitiative-43][^cite_note-van_Wagtendonk,_14-124]

In the [Amazon rainforest](48139.md), drought, logging, cattle ranching practices, and [slash-and-burn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash-and-burn) agriculture damage fire-resistant forests and promote the growth of flammable brush, creating a cycle that encourages more burning.[^cite_note-219] Fires in the rainforest threaten its collection of diverse species and produce large amounts of CO<sub>2</sub>.[^cite_note-220] Also, fires in the rainforest, along with drought and human involvement, could damage or destroy more than half of the Amazon rainforest by 2030.[^cite_note-221] Wildfires generate ash, reduce the availability of organic nutrients, and cause an increase in water runoff, eroding other nutrients and creating [flash flood](244391.md) conditions.[^cite_note-Graham,_et_al.,_iv-37][^cite_note-222] A 2003 wildfire in the [North Yorkshire Moors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Yorkshire_Moors) burned off 2.5 square kilometers (600 acres) of [heather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ericaceae) and the underlying [peat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peat) layers. Afterwards, wind erosion stripped the ash and the exposed soil, revealing archaeological remains dating to 10,000 BC.[^cite_note-223] Wildfires can also have an effect on climate change, increasing the amount of carbon released into the atmosphere and inhibiting vegetation growth, which affects overall carbon uptake by plants.[^cite_note-224] Burn severity can be measured by satellites to calculate the Normalized Burn Ratio.[^cite_note-225]

### On agriculture

Forest fires have emerged as an increasingly severe threat to agriculture, with 2024 and 2025 witnessing record-breaking fire seasons across multiple continents. In Canada, 3.24 million hectares had already burned by June 2025. This is only rivalled by the record-breaking 18 million hectares that burned in 2023, with smoke plumes affecting air quality and agricultural productivity thousands of kilometres away.[^cite_note-226]

### On waterways

Debris and chemical runoff into waterways after wildfires can make drinking water sources unsafe.[^cite_note-227] Though it is challenging to quantify the impacts of wildfires on surface water quality, research suggests that the concentration of many pollutants increases post-fire. The impacts occur during active burning and up to years later.[^cite_note-228] Increases in nutrients and total suspended sediments can happen within a year while heavy metal concentrations may peak 1–2 years after a wildfire.[^cite_note-229]

Benzene is one of many chemicals that have been found in drinking water systems after wildfires. Benzene can permeate certain plastic pipes and thus require long times to be removed from the water distribution infrastructure. Researchers estimated that, in worst case scenarios, more than 286 days of constant flushing of a contaminated HDPE service line were needed to reduce benzene below safe drinking water limits.[^cite_note-230][^cite_note-231] Temperature increases caused by fires, including wildfires, can cause plastic water pipes to generate toxic chemicals[^cite_note-232] such as [benzene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzene).[^cite_note-233]

### On plant and animals

This section is an excerpt from [Fire adaptations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_adaptations).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fire_adaptations\&action=edit)]

[Fire adaptations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_adaptations) are traits of plants and animals that help them survive wildfire or to use resources created by wildfire. These traits can help plants and animals increase their survival rates during a fire and/or reproduce offspring after a fire. Both plants and animals have multiple strategies for surviving and reproducing after fire. Plants in wildfire-prone [ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystems) often survive through adaptations to their local [fire regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_regime). Such adaptations include physical protection against heat, increased growth after a fire event, and flammable materials that encourage fire and may eliminate [competition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Competition).

For example, plants of the genus *[Eucalyptus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucalyptus)* contain flammable oils that encourage fire and hard [sclerophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sclerophyll) leaves to resist heat and drought, ensuring their dominance over less fire-tolerant species.[^cite_note-234][^cite_note-235] Dense bark, shedding lower branches, and high water content in external structures may also protect trees from rising temperatures.[^cite_note-Fire_adaptations_NOVA222-236] Fire-resistant seeds and reserve [shoots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoot_\(botany\)) that sprout after a fire encourage species preservation, as embodied by [pioneer species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pioneer_species). Smoke, charred wood, and heat can stimulate the germination of seeds in a process called *[serotiny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serotiny)*.[^cite_note-237] Exposure to smoke from burning plants promotes [germination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germination) in other types of plants by inducing the production of the orange [butenolide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butenolide).[^cite_note-Fire_adaptations_flematti-238]

Fires have also allowed for surviving species to create adaptations post-fire. This allows for better survival post-fire and furthers establishment.[^cite_note-Fire_adaptations_arees-v53-239] Adaptations as an example for boreal forests can include thick bark and post-fire flowering that is directly linked to fires.[^cite_note-Fire_adaptations_arees-v53-239] Other examples of species outside of plants and trees allow for further adaptation post-fire to survive and regenerate their population. This allows for better dispersal and new dispersal patterns to create a more stable environment.[^cite_note-240] Vegetation, as an example, showed that it had greater [gene flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_flow) than when unburnt and undisturbed.

## Impacts on humans

Wildfire risk is the chance that a wildfire will start in or reach a particular area and the potential loss of human values if it does. Risk is dependent on variable factors such as human activities, weather patterns, availability of wildfire fuels, and the availability or lack of resources to suppress a fire.[^cite_note-241][^cite_note-242] Wildfires have continually been a threat to human populations. However, human-induced geographic and climatic changes are exposing populations more frequently to wildfires and increasing wildfire risk. It is speculated that the increase in wildfires arises from a century of wildfire suppression coupled with the rapid expansion of human developments into fire-prone wildlands.[^cite_note-243] Wildfires are naturally occurring events that aid in promoting forest health. Global warming and climate changes are causing an increase in temperatures and more droughts nationwide which contributes to an increase in wildfire risk.[^cite_note-244][^cite_note-245]



The 2009 Station Fire burns in the foothills of the [San Gabriel Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Gabriel_Mountains) above the [Jet Propulsion Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_Propulsion_Laboratory), near [Pasadena, California](92408.md)

### Airborne hazards

See also: [Heavy metals § Toxicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_metals#Toxicity), [Health effects of wood smoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_effects_of_wood_smoke), and [Joss paper § Health impact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joss_paper#Health_impact)

The most noticeable adverse effect of wildfires is the destruction of property. However, hazardous chemicals released also significantly impact human health.[^cite_note-246]

Wildfire smoke is composed primarily of carbon dioxide and water vapor. Other common components present in lower concentrations are carbon monoxide, formaldehyde, [acrolein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acrolein), polyaromatic hydrocarbons, and benzene.[^cite_note-oehha.ca.gov-247] Small airborne particulates (in solid form or liquid droplets) are also present in smoke and ash debris. 80–90% of wildfire smoke, by mass, is within the fine particle size class of 2.5 micrometers in diameter or smaller.[^cite_note-248]

Carbon dioxide in smoke poses a low health risk due to its low toxicity. Rather, carbon monoxide and fine [particulate matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particulate_matter), particularly 2.5 μm in diameter and smaller, have been identified as the major health threats.[^cite_note-oehha.ca.gov-247] High levels of [heavy metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_metals), including [lead](17747.md), [arsenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsenic), [cadmium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadmium), and copper were found in the ash debris following the 2007 Californian wildfires. A [national clean-up campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_clean-up_campaign?action=edit\&redlink=1) was organised in fear of the health effects from exposure.[^cite_note-249] In the devastating California [Camp Fire (2018)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_Fire_\(2018\)) that killed 85 people, lead levels increased by around 50 times in the hours following the fire at a site nearby ([Chico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chico,_California)). [Zinc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc) concentration also increased significantly in Modesto, 240 kilometres (150 mi) away. Heavy metals such as [manganese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese) and calcium were found in numerous California fires as well.[^cite_note-250] Other chemicals are considered to be significant hazards but are found in concentrations that are too low to cause detectable health effects.

The degree of wildfire smoke exposure to an individual is dependent on the length, severity, duration, and proximity of the fire. People are exposed directly to smoke via the respiratory tract through inhalation of air pollutants. Indirectly, communities are exposed to wildfire debris that can contaminate soil and water supplies.

The [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency](58666.md) (EPA) developed the [air quality index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_quality_index) (AQI), a public resource that provides national air quality standard concentrations for common air pollutants. The public can use it to determine their exposure to hazardous air pollutants based on visibility range.[^cite_note-251]

### Health effects

See also: [Particulate matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particulate_matter), [Smoke § Health effects_of wood smoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoke#Health_effects_of_wood_smoke), and [Household air pollution § Health impacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Household_air_pollution#Health_impacts) Further information: [Manganese poisoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese_poisoning), [Lead poisoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_poisoning), and [Chronic cough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronic_cough)



Animation of diaphragmatic breathing with the diaphragm shown in green

Wildfire smoke contains [particulate matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particulate_matter) that may have adverse effects upon the human respiratory system. Evidence of the health effects should be relayed to the public so that exposure may be limited. The evidence can also be used to influence policy to promote positive health outcomes.[^cite_note-\:11-252]

Inhalation of smoke from a wildfire can be a health hazard.[^cite_note-253][^cite_note-254][^cite_note-255] Wildfire smoke is composed of combustion products i.e. [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide), [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide), [water vapor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor), [particulate matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particulate_matter), organic chemicals, [nitrogen oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_oxide) and other compounds. The principal health concern is the inhalation of particulate matter and carbon monoxide.[^cite_note-256]

Particulate matter (PM) is a type of air pollution made up of particles of dust and liquid droplets. They are characterized into three categories based on particle diameter: coarse PM, fine PM, and ultrafine PM. Coarse particles are between 2.5 micrometers and 10 micrometers, fine particles measure 0.1 to 2.5 micrometers, and ultrafine particle are less than 0.1 micrometer. lmpact on the body upon inhalation varies by size. Coarse PM is filtered by the upper airways and can accumulate and cause pulmonary inflammation. This can result in eye and sinus irritation as well as sore throat and coughing.[^cite_note-\:10-257][^cite_note-\:12-258] Coarse PM is often composed of heavier and more toxic materials that lead to short-term effects with stronger impact.[^cite_note-\:12-258]

Smaller PM moves further into the respiratory system creating issues deep into the lungs and the bloodstream.[^cite_note-\:10-257][^cite_note-\:12-258] In asthma patients, PM<sub>2.5</sub> causes inflammation but also increases oxidative stress in the epithelial cells. These particulates also cause apoptosis and autophagy in lung epithelial cells. Both processes damage the cells and impact cell function. This damage impacts those with respiratory conditions such as asthma where the lung tissues and function are already compromised.[^cite_note-\:12-258] Particulates less than 0.1 micrometer are called [ultrafine particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrafine_particle) (UFP). It is a major component of wildfire smoke.[^cite_note-pmid32952154-259] UFP can enter the bloodstream like PM<sub>2.5–0.1</sub> however studies show that it works into the blood much quicker. The inflammation and epithelial damage done by UFP has also shown to be much more severe.[^cite_note-\:12-258] PM<sub>2.5</sub> is of the largest concern in regards to wildfire.[^cite_note-\:11-252] This is particularly hazardous to the very young, elderly and those with chronic conditions such as asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cystic fibrosis and cardiovascular conditions. The illnesses most commonly associated with exposure to fine PM from wildfire smoke are bronchitis, exacerbation of asthma or COPD, and pneumonia. Symptoms of these complications include wheezing and shortness of breath and cardiovascular symptoms include chest pain, rapid heart rate and fatigue.[^cite_note-\:10-257]

#### Asthma exacerbation

Several epidemiological studies have demonstrated a close association between [air pollution](10934212.md) and respiratory allergic diseases such as bronchial [asthma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asthma).[^cite_note-\:11-252]

An observational study of smoke exposure related to the 2007 San Diego wildfires revealed an increase both in healthcare utilization and respiratory diagnoses, especially [asthma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asthma) among the group sampled.[^cite_note-\:13-260] Projected climate scenarios of wildfire occurrences predict significant increases in respiratory conditions among young children.[^cite_note-\:13-260] PM triggers a series of biological processes including inflammatory immune response, [oxidative stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidative_stress), which are associated with harmful changes in allergic respiratory diseases.[^cite_note-261]

Although some studies demonstrated no significant acute changes in lung function among people with [asthma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asthma) related to PM from wildfires, a possible explanation for these counterintuitive findings is the increased use of [quick-relief medications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asthma), such as inhalers, in response to elevated levels of smoke among those already diagnosed with asthma.[^cite_note-\:14-262]

There is consistent evidence between wildfire smoke and the exacerbation of asthma.[^cite_note-\:14-262]

Asthma is one of the most common chronic disease among children in the United States, affecting an estimated 6.2 million children.[^cite_note-263] Research on asthma risk focuses specifically on the risk of air pollution during the gestational period. Several pathophysiology processes are involved in this. Considerable airway development occurs during the 2nd and 3rd trimesters and continues until 3 years of age.[^cite_note-264] It is hypothesized that exposure to these toxins during this period could have consequential effects, as the epithelium of the lungs during this time could have increased permeability to toxins. Exposure to air pollution during parental and pre-natal stage could induce epigenetic changes which are responsible for the development of asthma.[^cite_note-265] Studies have found significant association between PM<sub>2.5</sub>, NO<sub>2</sub> and development of asthma during childhood despite heterogeneity among studies.[^cite_note-266] Furthermore, maternal exposure to chronic stressors is most likely present in distressed communities, and as this can be correlated with childhood asthma, it may further explain links between early childhood exposure to air pollution, neighborhood poverty, and childhood risk.[^cite_note-267]

#### Carbon monoxide danger

Main article: [Carbon monoxide poisoning](488743.md)

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a colorless, odorless gas that can be found at the highest concentration at close proximity to a smoldering fire. Thus, it is a serious threat to the health of wildfire firefighters. CO in smoke can be inhaled into the lungs where it is absorbed into the bloodstream and reduces oxygen delivery to the body's vital organs. At high concentrations, it can cause headaches, weakness, dizziness, confusion, nausea, disorientation, visual impairment, coma, and even death. Even at lower concentrations, such as those found at wildfires, individuals with cardiovascular disease may experience chest pain and cardiac arrhythmia.[^cite_note-oehha.ca.gov-247] A recent study tracking the number and cause of wildfire firefighter deaths from 1990 to 2006 found that 21.9% of the deaths occurred from heart attacks.[^cite_note-268]

Another important and somewhat less obvious health effect of wildfires is psychiatric diseases and disorders. Both adults and children from various countries who were directly and indirectly affected by wildfires were found to demonstrate different mental conditions linked to their experience with the wildfires. These include [post-traumatic stress disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-traumatic_stress_disorder_among_athletes) (PTSD), [depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depression_\(mood\)), anxiety, and [phobias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobia).[^cite_note-269][^cite_note-270][^cite_note-271][^cite_note-272][^cite_note-273]

The Western US has seen an increase in both the frequency and intensity of wildfires over the last several decades. This has been attributed to the arid climate of there and the effects of global warming. An estimated 46 million people were exposed to wildfire smoke from 2004 to 2009 in the Western US. Evidence has demonstrated that wildfire smoke can increase levels of airborne particulate.[^cite_note-\:11-252]

The EPA has defined acceptable concentrations of PM in the air, through the National Ambient Air Quality Standards and monitoring of ambient air quality has been mandated.[^cite_note-\:15-274] Due to these monitoring programs and the incidence of several large wildfires near populated areas, epidemiological studies have been conducted and demonstrate an association between human health effects and an increase in fine particulate matter due to wildfire smoke.

An increase in PM smoke emitted from the Hayman fire in Colorado in June 2002, was associated with an increase in respiratory symptoms in patients with COPD.[^cite_note-275] Looking at the wildfires in Southern California in 2003, investigators have shown an increase in hospital admissions due to asthma symptoms while being exposed to peak concentrations of PM in smoke.[^cite_note-276] Another epidemiological study found a 7.2% (95% confidence interval: 0.25%, 15%) increase in risk of respiratory related hospital admissions during smoke wave days with high wildfire-specific particulate matter 2.5 compared to matched non-smoke-wave days.[^cite_note-\:11-252]

Children participating in the Children's Health Study were also found to have an increase in eye and respiratory symptoms, medication use and physician visits.[^cite_note-277] Mothers who were pregnant during the fires gave birth to babies with a slightly reduced average birth weight compared to those who were not exposed. Suggesting that pregnant women may also be at greater risk to adverse effects from wildfire.[^cite_note-278] Worldwide, it is estimated that 339,000 people die due to the effects of wildfire smoke each year.[^cite_note-279]

&#x20;Besides the size of PM, their chemical composition should also be considered. Antecedent studies have demonstrated that the chemical composition of PM<sub>2.5</sub> from wildfire smoke can yield different estimates of human health outcomes as compared to other sources of smoke such as solid fuels.[^cite_note-\:11-252]

### Post-fire risks



Charred shrubland in suburban [Sydney](27862.md) ([2019–20 Australian bushfires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019%E2%80%9320_Australian_bushfire_season))

After a wildfire, hazards remain. Residents returning to their homes may be at risk from falling fire-weakened trees. Humans and pets may also be harmed by falling into [ash pits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ash_pit). The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) also reports that wildfires cause significant damage to electric systems, especially in dry regions.[^cite_note-280]

Chemically contaminated drinking water, at levels of hazardous waste concern, is a growing problem. In particular, hazardous waste scale chemical contamination of buried water systems was first identified in the U.S. in 2017,[^cite_note-281] and has since been increasingly documented in Hawaii, Colorado, and Oregon after wildfires.[^cite_note-282] In 2021, Canadian authorities adapted their post-fire public safety investigation approaches in British Columbia to screen for this risk, but have not found it as of 2023. Another challenge is that private drinking wells and the plumbing within a building can also become chemically contaminated and unsafe.[^cite_note-283] Households experience a wide-variety of significant economic and health impacts related to this contaminated water.[^cite_note-284] Evidence-based guidance on how to inspect and test wildfire impacted wells [^cite_note-285] and building water systems was developed for the first time in 2020.[^cite_note-286] In Paradise, California, for example,[^cite_note-287] the 2018 Camp Fire caused more than \$150 million worth of damage. This required almost a year of time to decontaminate and repair the municipal drinking water system from wildfire damage.

The source of this contamination was first proposed after the 2018 Camp Fire in California as originating from thermally degraded plastics in water systems, smoke and vapors entering depressurized plumbing, and contaminated water in buildings being sucked into the municipal water system. In 2020, it was first shown that thermal degradation of plastic drinking water materials was one potential contamination source.[^cite_note-288] In 2023, the second theory was confirmed where contamination could be sucked into pipes that lost water pressure.[^cite_note-289]

Other post-fire risks, can increase if other [extreme weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_weather) follows. For example, wildfires make soil less able to absorb precipitation, so heavy rainfall can result in more severe [flooding](50482.md) and damages like [mud slides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mud_slide).[^cite_note-290][^cite_note-291]

### At-risk groups

#### Firefighters

Main article: [Firefighting](855608.md)

Firefighters are at greatest risk for acute and chronic health effects resulting from wildfire smoke exposure. Some of the most common health conditions that firefighters acquire from prolonged smoke inhalation include cardiovascular and respiratory diseases.[^cite_note-292] For example, wildland firefighters can become [hypoxic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypoxia_\(medicine\)) as a result of oxygen deprivation.[^cite_note-293] Due to firefighters' occupational duties, they are frequently exposed to [hazardous chemicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_hazard) at close proximity for longer periods of time. A case study on the exposure of wildfire smoke among wildland firefighters shows that firefighters are exposed to significant levels of carbon monoxide and respiratory irritants above [OSHA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_Safety_and_Health_Administration)-permissible exposure limits (PEL) and ACGIH threshold limit values (TLV). 5–10% are overexposed.[^cite_note-294]

Between 2001 and 2012, over 200 [fatalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_fatality) occurred among wildland firefighters. In addition to heat and chemical hazards, firefighters are also at risk for electrocution from power lines; injuries from equipment; [slips, trips, and falls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_injury); injuries from vehicle rollovers; [heat-related illness](17557798.md); [insect bites and stings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insect_bites_and_stings); [stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_stress); and [rhabdomyolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhabdomyolysis).[^cite_note-295] Wildfires that reach urban environments create additional toxic fumes and carcinogenic particles from burning metals, plastics, electronics, paints, and other common materials.[^cite_note-s261-296]

#### Residents



Smoke from the [2020 California wildfires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_California_wildfires) settles over [San Francisco](49728.md)

Residents in communities surrounding wildfires are exposed to lower concentrations of chemicals, but they are at a greater risk for indirect exposure through water or [soil contamination](4229946.md). Exposure to residents is greatly dependent on individual susceptibility. Vulnerable persons such as children (ages 0–4), the elderly (ages 65 and older), smokers, and pregnant women are at an increased risk due to their already compromised body systems, even when the exposures are present at low chemical concentrations and for relatively short exposure periods.[^cite_note-297] They are also at risk for future wildfires and may move away to areas they consider less risky.[^cite_note-298]

Wildfires affect large numbers of people in Western Canada and the United States. In California alone, more than 350,000 people live in towns and cities in "very high fire hazard severity zones".[^cite_note-rgj-299] The current state of California's wildfires can be tracked through the tool: <https://www.fire.ca.gov/incidents> [Archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20250130064355/https://www.fire.ca.gov/incidents) 30 January 2025 at the [Wayback Machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayback_Machine).

Direct risks to building residents in fire-prone areas can be moderated through design choices such as choosing fire-resistant vegetation, maintaining landscaping to avoid debris accumulation and to create firebreaks, and by selecting fire-retardant roofing materials.[^cite_note-300] Potential compounding issues with poor air quality and heat during warmer months may be addressed with MERV 11 or higher outdoor air filtration in building ventilation systems, mechanical cooling, and a provision of a refuge area with additional air cleaning and cooling, if needed.[^cite_note-301]

## History

Further information: [Fossil record of fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_record_of_fire)



*[Elk Bath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elk_Bath)*, an award-winning photograph of elk avoiding a wildfire in [Montana](19978.md)

[Paleobotany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleobotany) provides the first evidence of wildfires, in fossils of the giant fungi *[Prototaxites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prototaxites)* preserved as [charcoal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charcoal) in [South Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Wales) and [Poland](22936.md), dating to the [Silurian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silurian) period (about [430](https://geoltime.github.io/?Ma=430) million years ago).[^cite_note-302] Smoldering surface fires started to occur sometime before the Early [Devonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devonian) period [405](https://geoltime.github.io/?Ma=405) million years ago. Low atmospheric oxygen during the Middle and Late Devonian was accompanied by a decrease in charcoal abundance.[^cite_note-Glasspool-303][^cite_note-304] Additional charcoal evidence suggests that fires continued through the [Carboniferous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboniferous) period. Later, the overall increase of atmospheric oxygen from 13% in the Late Devonian to 30–31% by the [Late Permian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Permian) was accompanied by a more widespread distribution of wildfires.[^cite_note-Scott2006-305] Later, a decrease in wildfire-related charcoal deposits from the late Permian to the [Triassic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triassic) periods is explained by a decrease in oxygen levels.[^cite_note-Pausas_594-306]

Wildfires during the Paleozoic and Mesozoic periods followed patterns similar to fires that occur in modern times. Surface fires driven by dry seasons are evident in Devonian and Carboniferous [progymnosperm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progymnosperm) forests. *[Lepidodendron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepidodendron)* forests dating to the Carboniferous period have charred peaks, evidence of crown fires. In Jurassic [gymnosperm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnosperm) forests, there is evidence of high frequency, light surface fires.[^cite_note-Pausas_594-306] The increase of fire activity in the late [Tertiary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_\(period\))[^cite_note-cenozoic-division-307] is possibly due to the increase of [C<sub>4</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C4_carbon_fixation)-type grasses. As these grasses shifted to more [mesic habitats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesic_habitat), their high flammability increased fire frequency, promoting grasslands over woodlands.[^cite_note-308] However, fire-prone habitats may have contributed to the prominence of trees such as those of the genera *[Eucalyptus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucalyptus)*, *Pinus* and *[Sequoia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequoia_\(genus\))*, which have thick bark to withstand fires and employ [pyriscence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serotiny#Fire-mediated_serotiny).[^cite_note-309][^cite_note-310]

### Human involvement

See also: [Control of fire by early humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_of_fire_by_early_humans), [Deforestation § Historical causes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation#Historical_causes), [Environmental history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history), [History of firefighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_firefighting), and [Native American use of fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_use_of_fire)



Aerial view of deliberate wildfires on the [Khun Tan Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khun_Tan_Range), [Thailand](30128.md). These fires are lit by local farmers every year to promote the growth of a certain mushroom.

The human use of fire for agricultural and hunting purposes during the [Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleolithic) and [Mesolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesolithic) ages altered pre-existing landscapes and fire regimes. Woodlands were gradually replaced by smaller vegetation that facilitated travel, hunting, seed-gathering and planting.[^cite_note-311] In recorded human history, minor allusions to wildfires were mentioned in the [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible) and by classical writers such as [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer). However, while ancient Hebrew, Greek, and Roman writers were aware of fires, they were not very interested in the uncultivated lands where wildfires occurred.[^cite_note-RackhamFire-312][^cite_note-RackhamHist-313] Wildfires were used in battles throughout human history as [early thermal weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_thermal_weapons). From the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), accounts were written of [occupational burning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_burning) as well as customs and laws that governed the use of fire. In Germany, regular burning was documented in 1290 in the [Odenwald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odenwald) and in 1344 in the [Black Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Forest).[^cite_note-Goldammer-314] In the 14th century [Sardinia](29376.md), firebreaks were used for wildfire protection. In Spain during the 1550s, [sheep husbandry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheep_husbandry) was discouraged in certain provinces by [Philip II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Spain) due to the harmful effects of fires used in [transhumance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transhumance).[^cite_note-RackhamFire-312][^cite_note-RackhamHist-313] As early as the 17th century, Native Americans were observed [using fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_use_of_fire) for many purposes including cultivation, [signaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoke_signal), and warfare. Scottish botanist [David Douglas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Douglas_\(botanist\)) noted the native use of fire for tobacco cultivation, to encourage deer into smaller areas for hunting purposes, and to improve foraging for honey and grasshoppers. Charcoal found in sedimentary deposits off the Pacific coast of Central America suggests that more burning occurred in the 50 years before the [Spanish colonization of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_colonization_of_the_Americas) than after the colonization.[^cite_note-315] In the post-World War II [Baltic region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_region), socio-economic changes led more stringent air quality standards and bans on fires that eliminated traditional burning practices.[^cite_note-Goldammer-314] In the mid-19th century, explorers from [HMS *Beagle*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Beagle) observed [Aboriginal Australians](2912594.md) using fire for ground clearing, hunting, and regeneration of plant food in a method later named [fire-stick farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire-stick_farming).[^cite_note-316] Such careful use of fire has been employed for centuries in lands protected by [Kakadu National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kakadu_National_Park) to encourage biodiversity.[^cite_note-317]

Wildfires typically occur during periods of increased temperature and [drought](37335.md). An increase in fire-related [debris flow](4415356.md) in [alluvial fans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alluvial_fan) of northeastern [Yellowstone National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellowstone_National_Park) was linked to the period between AD 1050 and 1200, coinciding with the [Medieval Warm Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Warm_Period).[^cite_note-318] However, human influence caused an increase in fire frequency. [Dendrochronological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendrochronological) fire scar data and charcoal layer data in [Finland](10577.md) suggests that, while many fires occurred during severe drought conditions, an increase in the number of fires during 850 BC and 1660 AD can be attributed to human influence.[^cite_note-319] Charcoal evidence from the Americas suggested a general decrease in wildfires between 1 AD and 1750 compared to previous years. However, a period of increased fire frequency between 1750 and 1870 was suggested by charcoal data from North America and Asia, attributed to human population growth and influences such as land clearing practices. This period was followed by an overall decrease in burning in the 20th century, linked to the expansion of agriculture, increased livestock grazing, and fire prevention efforts.[^cite_note-320] A meta-analysis found that 17 times more land burned annually in California before 1800 compared to recent decades (1,800,000 hectares/year compared to 102,000 hectares/year).[^cite_note-321]

According to a paper published in the journal *[Science](193513.md)*, the number of natural and human-caused fires decreased by 24.3% between 1998 and 2015. Researchers explain this as a transition from [nomadism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomadism) to settled lifestyle and intensification of [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture) that lead to a drop in the use of fire for land clearing.[^cite_note-322][^cite_note-323]

Increases of certain tree species (i.e. [conifers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conifers)) over others (i.e. [deciduous trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deciduous_tree)) can increase wildfire risk, especially if these trees are also planted in [monocultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoculture).[^cite_note-324][^cite_note-325] Some [invasive species](67554.md), moved in by humans (i.e., for the [pulp and paper industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulp_and_paper_industry)) have in some cases also increased the intensity of wildfires. Examples include species such as [Eucalyptus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucalyptus) in California[^cite_note-326][^cite_note-327] and [gamba grass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamba_grass) in Australia.

## Society and culture

Wildfires have a place in many cultures. "To spread like wildfire" is a common idiom in English, meaning something that "quickly affects or becomes known by more and more people".[^cite_note-spread_like_wildfire_definition_in_the_Cambridge_English_Dictionary-328]

Wildfire activity has been attributed as a major factor in the development of [Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece). In modern Greece, as in many other regions, it is the most common [disaster caused by a natural hazard](58921.md) and figures prominently in the social and economic lives of its people.[^cite_note-329]

In 1937, U.S. President [Franklin D. Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) initiated a nationwide fire prevention campaign, highlighting the role of human carelessness in forest fires. Later posters of the program featured [Uncle Sam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncle_Sam), characters from the Disney movie *[Bambi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bambi)*, and the official mascot of the [U.S. Forest Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Forest_Service), [Smokey Bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smokey_Bear).[^cite_note-330] The Smokey Bear fire prevention campaign has yielded one of the most popular characters in the United States; for many years there was a living Smokey Bear mascot, and it has been commemorated on postage stamps.[^cite_note-331]

There are also significant indirect or second-order societal impacts from wildfire, such as demands on utilities to prevent power transmission equipment from becoming ignition sources, and the cancellation or nonrenewal of homeowners insurance for residents living in wildfire-prone areas.[^cite_note-332]
