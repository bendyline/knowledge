**[Environmental issues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_issues)** in the **United States** include climate change, energy, species conservation, invasive species, [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation_in_the_United_States), mining, nuclear accidents, pesticides, pollution, waste and overpopulation. Despite taking hundreds of measures, environmental issues are increasing rapidly instead of decreasing. The United States is among the most significant emitters of greenhouse gases in the world. In terms of both total and per capita emissions, it is among the largest contributors.[^cite_note-2] The [climate policy of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_policy_of_the_United_States) has a major influence on the world.[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4]

## Movements and ideas

Main articles: [Conservation movement § United States](45441.md#United_States), and [Conservation in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_in_the_United_States)

### 20th century

Both conservationism and environmentalism appeared in political debate in forests about the [Progressive Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_Era) in the early 20th century. There were three main positions. The **laissez-faire** position held that owners of private property—including lumber and mining companies—should be allowed to do anything they wished with their property.[^cite_note-5]

The **Conservationists**, led by President [Theodore Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Roosevelt) and his close ally [Gifford Pinchot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gifford_Pinchot), said that the laissez-faire approach was too wasteful and inefficient. In any case, they noted, most of the natural resources in the western states were already owned by the federal government. The best course of action, they argued, was a long-term plan devised by national experts to maximize the long-term economic benefits of natural resources.

**Environmentalism** was the third position, led by [John Muir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Muir) (1838–1914). Muir's passion for nature made him the most influential American environmentalist. Muir preached that nature was sacred and that humans are intruders who should look but not develop. He founded the [Sierra Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Club) and remains an icon of the environmentalist movement. He was primarily responsible for defining the environmentalist position in the debate between Conservation and environmentalism.

Environmentalism preached that nature was almost sacred, and that man was an intruder. It allowed for limited tourism (such as hiking), but opposed automobiles in national parks. It strenuously opposed timber cutting on most public lands and vehemently denounced the dams Roosevelt supported for water supplies, electricity and flood control. Especially controversial was the [Hetch Hetchy dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O'Shaughnessy_Dam_\(California\)) in [Yosemite National Park](48664.md), which Roosevelt approved and which supplies the water supply of San Francisco.

[Climate change](5042951.md) has led to the United States warming up by 2.6 °F (1.4 °C) since 1970. In 2023, the global average near-surface temperature reached 1.45 °C above pre-industrial levels, making it the warmest year on record.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-Climate_change_in_the_United_States_ClimateCentral_20220420-9] The [climate of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_the_United_States) is shifting in ways that are widespread and varied between [regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regions_of_the_United_States).[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-Climate_change_in_the_United_States_Heidari_2020_e2020EF001657-11] From 2010 to 2019, the United States experienced its hottest decade on record.[^cite_note-12] [Extreme weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_weather) events, [invasive species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasive_species_in_the_United_States), [floods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lists_of_floods_in_the_United_States) and [droughts](18442086.md) are increasing.[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15] [Climate change's impacts on tropical cyclones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_cyclones_and_climate_change) and [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise) also affect regions of the country.

Cumulatively since 1850, the [U.S. has emitted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas_emissions_by_the_United_States) a larger share than any country of the greenhouse gases causing current climate change, with some 20% of the global total of carbon dioxide alone.[^cite_note-16] Current [US emissions per person](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_greenhouse_gas_emissions_per_person) are among the largest in the world.[^cite_note-Climate_change_in_the_United_States_watch-17] Various [state and federal climate change policies](25447994.md) have been introduced, and the US has ratified the [Paris Agreement](30242372.md) despite [temporarily withdrawing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_withdrawal_from_the_Paris_Agreement). In 2021, the country set a target of halving its annual greenhouse gas emissions by 2030,[^cite_note-Climate_change_in_the_United_States_CAT-18] however oil and gas companies still get [tax breaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_breaks).[^cite_note-19]

Climate change is having considerable impacts on the environment and society of the United States. This includes implications for [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_and_agriculture_in_the_United_States), the [economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_impacts_of_climate_change) (especially the [affordability and availability of insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_and_insurance_in_the_United_States)), [human health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_climate_change_on_human_health), and [indigenous peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_and_indigenous_peoples), and it is seen as [a national security threat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_security).[^cite_note-20] US States that emit more [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) per person and introduce policies to oppose [climate action](2119179.md) are generally experiencing greater impacts.[^cite_note-Climate_change_in_the_United_States_nature.com-21][^cite_note-Climate_change_in_the_United_States_ecowatch.com-22] 2020 was a historic year for billion-dollar weather and climate disasters in U.S.[^cite_note-Climate_change_in_the_United_States_NCEI_thru202009-23] In 2024, the United States experienced 27 separate weather and climate disasters, each causing over \$1 billion in damages, in total, it caused \$27 billion in damages. This set a record for the most billion dollars disasters in a single year.[^cite_note-24]

Although historically a [non-partisan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonpartisanism) issue, climate change has become [controversial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming_controversy) and [politically divisive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politicization_of_science) in the country in recent decades. [Oil companies](994887.md) have known since the 1970s that burning [oil and gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuels) could cause global warming but nevertheless [funded deniers for years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ExxonMobil_climate_change_controversy).[^cite_note-Climate_change_in_the_United_States_NYT-20151105-25][^cite_note-Climate_change_in_the_United_States_TG-20150708-26] Despite the support of a clear [scientific consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_consensus_on_climate_change), as recently as 2021 one-third of Americans [deny that human-caused climate change exists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_denial)[^cite_note-27] although the majority are concerned or alarmed about the issue.[^cite_note-Climate_change_in_the_United_States_\:5-28]

### Energy

Main article: [Energy in the United States](7635170.md)

Since about 82.6% of all types of energy used in the United States are derived from [fossil fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel) consumption it is closely linked to [greenhouse gas emissions](9528025.md). The energy policy of the United States is determined by federal, state and local public entities, which address issues of energy production, distribution, and consumption, such as building codes and gas mileage advancements. The production and transport of fossil fuels are also tied to significant environmental issues.

## Species conservation

Main article: [Conservation in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_in_the_United_States)

Many plant and animal species became extinct in North America soon after first human arrival, including the [North American megafauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene_megafauna#North_America); others have become nearly extinct since European settlement, among them the [American bison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_bison) and [California condor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_condor).[^cite_note-31]

The last of the [passenger pigeons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passenger_pigeon) died in 1914 after being the most common bird in North America. They were killed as both a source of food and because they were a threat to farming. Saving the [bald eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bald_eagle), the national bird of the U.S., from extinction was a notable conservation success.

As of 13 December 2016, the [International Union for the Conservation of Nature's Red List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IUCN_Red_List) shows the United States has 1,514 species on its threatened list (critically endangered, endangered and vulnerable categories).

### Invasive species

This section is an excerpt from [Invasive species in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasive_species_in_the_United_States).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Invasive_species_in_the_United_States\&action=edit)]



The [brown tree snake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_tree_snake) (*Boiga irregularis*), an invasive species in the United States

[Invasive species](67554.md) pose a crucial threat to many native [habitats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitat) and [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species) in the [United States](3434750.md) and impose high costs on agriculture, forestry, and recreation. An invasive species refers to an organism that is not native to a specific region and poses significant economic and environmental threats to its new habitat.[^cite_note-32] The term "invasive species" can also refer to [feral species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feral) or introduced diseases. Some introduced species, such as the [dandelion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taraxacum_officinale), do not cause significant economic or ecological damage and are not widely considered as invasive. Economic damages from invasive species and control costs are estimated at \$120 billion per year.[^cite_note-33]

The main geomorphological impacts of invasive plants include bioconstruction and bioprotection.[^cite_note-34]

Degrees of ecological impacts by introduced faunas that can [potentially compensate for extinct native animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene_rewilding) (especially [megafauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megafauna)) and treatments of these animals [have long been subjects to discussions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free-roaming_horse_management_in_North_America#Introduced_vs._reintroduced_species) most notably [mustangs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustang) and [burros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donkey), while contributions by other species[^cite_note-35] to the native ecosystems are also debatable.[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-Invasive_species_in_the_United_States_Ming-37]

### Deforestation

This section is an excerpt from [Deforestation in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation_in_the_United_States).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Deforestation_in_the_United_States\&action=edit)]

In the [United States](3434750.md), [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation) was an ongoing process until recently. Between 2010 and 2020, the US forests increased 0.03% annually, according to FAO ([Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_and_Agriculture_Organization)).[^cite_note-FAO-2020-a-38]

Native Americans cleared millions of acres of forest for many reasons, including hunting, farming, berry production, and building materials.[^cite_note-39] Prior to the arrival of [European-Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European-Americans), about one half of the United States land area was forest, about 1,023,000,000 acres (4,140,000 km<sup>2</sup>) estimated in 1630. [Forest cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_cover) in the Eastern United States reached its lowest point in roughly 1872 with about 48 percent compared to the amount of forest cover in 1620. The majority of deforestation took place prior to 1910 with the Forest Service reporting the minimum forestation as 721,000,000 acres (2,920,000 km<sup>2</sup>) around 1920.[^cite_note-40] The forest resources of the United States remained relatively constant through the 20th century.[^cite_note-Deforestation_in_the_United_States_ForestResources-41] The Forest Service reported total forestation as 766,000,000 acres (3,100,000 km<sup>2</sup>) in 2012.[^cite_note-Deforestation_in_the_United_States_US-FS-ForestFacts2012-42][^cite_note-Deforestation_in_the_United_States_US-FS-NatRepSustainable-2010-43][^cite_note-Deforestation_in_the_United_States_ForestResources-41] A 2017 study estimated 3 percent loss of forest between 1992 and 2001.[^cite_note-44]

The 2005 (FAO) *Global Forest Resources Assessment* ranked the United States as seventh highest country losing its [old-growth forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old-growth_forest), a vast majority of which were removed prior to the 20th century.[^cite_note-Deforestation_in_the_United_States_ForestResources-41]

In 2025, new research by Earth System Science Data showed that over the past four centuries vast stretches of forestland in the United States giving way to agriculture, urban development, and industry. Since the 1600s, the country has a net loss of approximately 258 million acres of forest.[^cite_note-45]

## Mining

Main article: [Mining in the United States](23756501.md)

This section is an excerpt from [Mining in the United States](23756501.md).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Mining_in_the_United_States\&action=edit)]

[Mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining) in the [United States](3434750.md) has been active since the beginning of colonial times, but became a major industry in the 19th century with a number of new mineral discoveries causing a series of mining rushes. In 2015, the value of coal, metals, and industrial minerals mined in the United States was US\$109.6 billion. 158,000 workers were directly employed by the [mining industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining_industry).[^cite_note-Mining_in_the_United_States_auto-46]

The mining industry has a number of impacts on communities, individuals and the environment. Mine safety incidents have been important parts of American [occupational safety and health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_safety_and_health) history. Mining has a [number of environmental impacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_mining). In the United States, issues like [mountaintop removal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountaintop_removal_mining), and [acid mine drainage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_mine_drainage) have widespread impacts on all parts of the environment. As of January 2020, the EPA lists 142 mines in the [Superfund program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superfund).[^cite_note-Mining_in_the_United_States_\:1mius-47]

In 2019, the country was the 4th world producer of [gold](12240.md);[^cite_note-48] 5th largest world producer of [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper);[^cite_note-49] 5th worldwide producer of [platinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platinum);[^cite_note-50] 10th worldwide producer of [silver](27119.md);[^cite_note-51] 2nd largest world producer of [rhenium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhenium);[^cite_note-52] 2nd largest world producer of [sulfur](27127.md);[^cite_note-53] 3rd largest world producer of [phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate);[^cite_note-54] 3rd largest world producer of [molybdenum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molybdenum);[^cite_note-55] 4th largest world producer of [lead](17747.md);[^cite_note-56] 4th largest world producer of [zinc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc);[^cite_note-57] 5th worldwide producer of [vanadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanadium);[^cite_note-58] 9th largest world producer of [iron ore](145352.md);[^cite_note-59] 9th largest world producer of [potash](56509.md);[^cite_note-60] 12th largest world producer of [cobalt](24580536.md);[^cite_note-61] 13th largest world producer of [titanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanium);[^cite_note-62] world's largest producer of [gypsum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gypsum);[^cite_note-63] 2nd largest world producer of [kyanite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyanite);[^cite_note-64] 2nd largest world producer of [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone);[^cite_note-65] in addition to being the 2nd largest world producer of [salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Table_salt).[^cite_note-66] It was the world's 10th largest producer of [uranium](31743.md) in 2018.[^cite_note-67]

### Abandoned fossil fuel wells

This section is an excerpt from [Orphaned wells in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orphaned_wells_in_the_United_States).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Orphaned_wells_in_the_United_States\&action=edit)]



Abandoned gas well located in [Lower Rio Grande Valley National Wildlife Refuge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Rio_Grande_Valley_National_Wildlife_Refuge)

Though different jurisdictions have varying criteria for what exactly qualifies as an [orphaned or abandoned oil well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orphan_wells), generally speaking, an [oil well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_well) is considered abandoned when it has been permanently taken out of production. Similarly, orphaned wells may have different legal definitions across different jurisdictions, but can be thought of as wells whose legal owner it is not possible to determine.[^cite_note-Orphaned_wells_in_the_United_States_\:0-68]

Once a well is abandoned, it can be a source of toxic emissions and pollution contaminating groundwater and releasing [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane_emissions), making [orphan wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orphan_wells) a significant contributor to national [greenhouse gas emissions](9528025.md).[^cite_note-Orphaned_wells_in_the_United_States_\:5-69] For this reason, several state and federal programs have been initiated to plug wells; however, many of these programs are under capacity.[^cite_note-Orphaned_wells_in_the_United_States_\:5-69] In states like [Texas](29810.md) and [New Mexico](21649.md), these programs do not have enough funding or staff to fully evaluate and implement mitigation programs.[^cite_note-Orphaned_wells_in_the_United_States_\:5-69]

According to the [Government Accountability Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Accountability_Office), the 2.1 million unplugged abandoned wells in the United States could cost as much as \$300 billion.[^cite_note-Orphaned_wells_in_the_United_States_\:5-69] A joint [Grist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grist_\(magazine\)) and [The Texas Observer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Texas_Observer) investigation in 2021 highlighted how government estimates of abandoned wells in Texas and New Mexico were likely underestimated and that market forces might have reduced prices so much creating [peak oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_oil) conditions that would lead to more abandonment.[^cite_note-Orphaned_wells_in_the_United_States_\:5-69] Advocates of programs like the [Green New Deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_New_Deal) and broader [climate change mitigation](2119179.md) policy in the United States have advocated for funding plugging programs that would address [stranded assets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stranded_asset) and provide a [Just Transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_Transition) for skilled oil and gas workers.[^cite_note-70]

## Nuclear

Main article: [Nuclear power in the United States](4131402.md)



The locations across the U.S. where [nuclear waste](37257.md) is stored

The most notable accident involving [nuclear power in the United States](4131402.md) was the [Three Mile Island accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Mile_Island_accident) in 1979. [Davis–Besse Nuclear Power Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davis%E2%80%93Besse_Nuclear_Power_Station) has been the source of two of the top five most dangerous nuclear incidents in the United States since 1979.[^cite_note-71]

[Nuclear safety in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_safety_in_the_United_States) is governed by federal regulations and continues to be studied by the [Nuclear Regulatory Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_Regulatory_Commission) (NRC). The safety of nuclear plants and materials controlled by the U.S. government for research and weapons production, as well as those powering naval vessels, is not governed by the NRC.

The [anti-nuclear movement in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-nuclear_movement_in_the_United_States) consists of more than eighty anti-nuclear groups which have acted to oppose nuclear power and/or [nuclear weapons](21785.md) in the USA. The movement has delayed construction or halted commitments to build some new nuclear plants,[^cite_note-protest-72][^cite_note-73] and has pressured the [Nuclear Regulatory Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_Regulatory_Commission) to enforce and strengthen the safety regulations for nuclear power plants.[^cite_note-74] [Anti-nuclear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-nuclear) campaigns that captured national public attention in the 1970s and 1980s involved the [Calvert Cliffs Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvert_Cliffs_Nuclear_Power_Plant), [Seabrook Station Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seabrook_Station_Nuclear_Power_Plant), [Diablo Canyon Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diablo_Canyon_Power_Plant), [Shoreham Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoreham_Nuclear_Power_Plant), and [Three Mile Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Mile_Island_accident).[^cite_note-protest-72]

## Pesticides

Main article: [Pesticide use in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_use_in_the_United_States)

[Pesticide](48340.md) use in the United States is predominately by the [agricultural sector](242778.md), which in 2012 comprised 89% of conventional pesticide usage in the United States.[^cite_note-75]



1976\. Sign reads Warning Keep Out Pesticide Storage Area. Oregon and Washington cooperative western spruce budworm control project.

The [Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Insecticide,_Fungicide,_and_Rodenticide_Act) (FIFRA) was first passed in 1947, giving the United States Department of Agriculture responsibility for regulating pesticides. In 1972, FIFRA underwent a major revision and transferred responsibility for pesticide regulation to the Environmental Protection Agency and shifted emphasis to protection of the environment and public health.

## Pollution

Main article: [Pollution in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollution_in_the_United_States)

This section is an excerpt from [Pollution in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollution_in_the_United_States).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pollution_in_the_United_States\&action=edit)]



Pollution from the [Exxon Valdez oil spill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exxon_Valdez_oil_spill)

As with many countries, [pollution in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollution_in_the_United_States) is a concern for [environmental organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_organization), government agencies, and individuals.

Billions of pounds of toxic chemicals are released into the air, land, and waterways in the U.S. each year. In 2019, approximately 21,000 facilities reported releasing 2.16 billion pounds of these chemicals onto land, 580 million pounds into the air, and 201 million pounds into water sources. Exposure to these pollutants can lead to various health problems, from short-term symptoms like headaches and temporary nervous system effects (e.g., "metal fume fever") to serious long-term risks such as cancer and early death.[^cite_note-76]

Pollution from U.S. manufacturing has declined massively since 1990 (despite an increase in production). A 2018 study in the *American Economic Review* found that environmental regulation is the primary driver of the reduction in pollution.[^cite_note-77]

### Air pollution

Main article: [Air pollution in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_pollution_in_the_United_States)

This section is an excerpt from [Air pollution in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_pollution_in_the_United_States).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Air_pollution_in_the_United_States\&action=edit)]



Looking down from the [Hollywood Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hollywood_Hills), with [Griffith Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griffith_Observatory) on the hill in the foreground, air pollution is visible in downtown [Los Angeles](18110.md) on a late afternoon.

[Air pollution](10934212.md) is the introduction of [chemicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical), [particulate matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_particulate_matter), or [biological materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biotic_material) into the [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_atmosphere) that cause harm or discomfort to humans or other living organisms, or damage [ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem). Health problems attributed to air pollution include premature death, [cancer](105219.md), organ failure, infections, behavioral changes, and other diseases. These health effects are not equally distributed across the U.S. population; there are demographic disparities by race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and education.[^cite_note-78] Air pollution can derive from natural sources, such as wildfires and volcanoes, or from anthropogenic sources. Anthropogenic air pollution has affected the [United States](3434750.md) since the beginning of the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution).[^cite_note-79]

According to a 2024 report: "39% of people living in America—131.2 million people—still live in places with failing grades for unhealthy levels of ozone or particle pollution." Analyzing data from 2020 to 2022, the American Lung Association found the number of people living in counties with a failing grade for ozone declined, this year by 2.4 million people.[^cite_note-Air_pollution_in_the_United_States_\:16-80]

A 2016 study reported that levels of [nitrogen oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_oxides), which contribute to [smog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smog) and [acid rain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_rain), had plummeted over the previous decade,[^cite_note-81] due to better regulations, economic shifts, and technological innovations. The [National Aeronautics and Space Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Aeronautics_and_Space_Administration) (NASA) reported a 32% decrease of [nitrogen dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_dioxide) in [New York City](645042.md) and a 42% decrease in Atlanta between the periods of 2005–2007 and 2009–2011.[^cite_note-82]

During June 2023, due to early season [wildfires in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fires_in_Canada), cities like New York and Washington D.C. suffered from dangerous levels of air pollution. It was the worst regional air quality in decades for the Northeast.[^cite_note-Air_pollution_in_the_United_States_state.gov-83]

### Water pollution

Main article: [Water pollution in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution_in_the_United_States)

This section is an excerpt from [Water pollution in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution_in_the_United_States).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Water_pollution_in_the_United_States\&action=edit)]



Topsoil runoff from farm, central Iowa (2011)

[Water pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution) [in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution_in_the_United_States) is a growing problem that became critical in the 19th century with the development of [mechanized agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanised_agriculture), mining, and [manufacturing industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing)—although laws and regulations introduced in the late 20th century have improved water quality in many water bodies.[^cite_note-Water_pollution_in_the_United_States_EPA-25_years-84] Extensive industrialization and rapid urban growth exacerbated water pollution combined with a lack of regulation has allowed for discharges of [sewage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewage), [toxic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxic) [chemicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemicals), [nutrients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutrient_pollution), and other pollutants into surface water. This has led to the need for more improvement in water quality as it is still threatened and not fully safe.[^cite_note-Water_pollution_in_the_United_States_\:2-85][^cite_note-Water_pollution_in_the_United_States_Burian_et_al-86]

In the early 20th century, communities began to install [drinking water treatment systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_purification), but control of the principal pollution sources—domestic sewage, [industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_wastewater_treatment), and [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_pollution)—was not effectively regulated in the US until the 1970s.[^cite_note-Water_pollution_in_the_United_States_\:2-85] These pollution sources can affect both [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater) and [surface water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_water). Multiple pollution incidents such as the [Kingston Fossil Plant coal fly ash slurry spill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingston_Fossil_Plant_coal_fly_ash_slurry_spill) (2008) and the [*Deepwater Horizon* oil spill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deepwater_Horizon_oil_spill) (2010) have left lasting impacts on [water quality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_quality#United_States), ecosystems, and public health in the United States.[^cite_note-Water_pollution_in_the_United_States_natgeo19-87][^cite_note-Water_pollution_in_the_United_States_Britannica-Deepwater-88] The [United States Geological Survey](23814944.md) (USGS) reported in 2023 that at least 45% of drinking water in the United States contains [per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per-_and_polyfluoroalkyl_substances) (PFAS), commonly referred to as "forever chemicals."[^cite_note-Water_pollution_in_the_United_States_USGS_2023-89][^cite_note-Water_pollution_in_the_United_States_Christensen-90][^cite_note-91] The [Environmental Protection Agency](58666.md) (EPA) has been able to identify around 70,000 water bodies that do not meet revised water quality standards due to PFAS.[^cite_note-Water_pollution_in_the_United_States_\:2-85]

Many solutions to water pollution in the United States can be implemented to curtail water pollution: [municipal wastewater treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wastewater_treatment), [agricultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_wastewater_treatment) and [industrial wastewater treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_wastewater_treatment), [erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erosion_control) and [sediment control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sediment_control), and the control of [urban runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_runoff). The continued implementation of pollution prevention, control, and treatment measures are used to pursue the goal of maintaining water quality within levels specified in federal and state regulations; however, many water bodies across the country continue to violate water quality standards in the 21st century.[^cite_note-Water_pollution_in_the_United_States_EPA_healthy_waterways-92]

#### Policy

This section is an excerpt from [Solid waste policy of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_waste_policy_of_the_United_States).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Solid_waste_policy_of_the_United_States\&action=edit)]

[Solid waste policy in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_waste_policy_of_the_United_States) is aimed at developing and implementing proper mechanisms to effectively manage solid waste. For solid waste policy to be effective, inputs should come from stakeholders, including citizens, businesses, community-based organizations, non-governmental organizations, government agencies, universities, and other research organizations. These inputs form the basis of policy frameworks that influence solid waste management decisions.[^cite_note-Solid_waste_policy_of_the_United_States_michigan-96] In the [United States](3434750.md), the [Environmental Protection Agency](58666.md) (EPA) regulates household, industrial, manufacturing, and commercial solid and hazardous wastes under the 1976 [Resource Conservation and Recovery Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resource_Conservation_and_Recovery_Act) (RCRA).[^cite_note-Solid_waste_policy_of_the_United_States_us_epa_waste-97] Effective solid waste management is a cooperative effort involving federal, state, regional, and local entities.[^cite_note-Solid_waste_policy_of_the_United_States_texas_waste-98] Thus, the RCRA's Solid Waste program section D encourages the environmental departments of each state to develop comprehensive plans to manage nonhazardous industrial and municipal solid waste.[^cite_note-Solid_waste_policy_of_the_United_States_us_epa_waste-97] Each state will have different methods on how to educate and control the flow of waste

##### Electronic waste

This section is an excerpt from [Electronic waste in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_waste_in_the_United_States).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Electronic_waste_in_the_United_States\&action=edit)]



Cathode ray tube monitors being packed for shipping at a recycling event in [Ann Arbor, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ann_Arbor,_Michigan)

[Electronic waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_waste) or e-waste in the United States refers to electronic products that have reached the end of their operable lives, and the United States is beginning to address its waste problems with regulations at a state and federal level. Used electronics are the quickest-growing source of waste and can have serious health impacts.[^cite_note-Electronic_waste_in_the_United_States_\:103-99] The [United States](3434750.md) is the world leader in producing the most e-waste, followed closely by China; both countries domestically recycle and export e-waste.[^cite_note-Electronic_waste_in_the_United_States_\:1-100] Only recently has the United States begun to make an effort to start regulating where e-waste goes and how it is disposed of. There is also an economic factor that has an effect on where and how e-waste is disposed of. Electronics are the primary users of precious and special metals, retrieving those metals from electronics can be viewed as important as raw metals may become more scarce[^cite_note-101]

The United States does not have an official federal e-waste regulation system, yet certain states have implemented state regulatory systems. *The National Strategy for Electronic Stewardship* was co-founded by the [Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EPA), the [Council on Environmental Quality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_on_Environmental_Quality) (CEQ), and the [General Services Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Services_Administration) (GSA), and was introduced in 2011 to focus on federal action to establish electronic stewardship in the United States.[^cite_note-Electronic_waste_in_the_United_States_\:23-102] E-waste management is critical due to the toxic chemicals present in electronic devices. According to the United States EPA, toxic substances such as [lead](17747.md), [mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_\(element\)), [arsenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsenic), and [cadmium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadmium) are often released into the environment and endanger whole communities; these toxic contaminants can have detrimental effects on the health of ecosystems and living organisms.[^cite_note-Electronic_waste_in_the_United_States_EPA.1-103] United States e-waste management includes [recycling and reuse programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_recycling), domestic landfill dumping, and international shipments of domestically produced e-waste. The EPA estimates that in 2009, the United States disposed of 2.37 million tons of e-waste, 25% of which was recycled domestically.[^cite_note-Electronic_waste_in_the_United_States_EPA.1-103]

Lack of awareness for e-waste issues is also a problem in the U.S., especially among young people. In a 2020 survey of people between the ages of 18 and 38, 60% did not know what the term "e-waste" is, and 57% did not consider electronic waste to be "a significant contributor to toxic waste."[^cite_note-104] With electronic recycling options readily available in most states, the issue seems to be awareness, not availability. In 2018, an association of European electronic recyclers based in Brussels called the WEEE Forum, created International E-Waste Day on October 13, with the support of 19 e-waste companies globally, in order to raise awareness about how large of an issue e-waste has become.[^cite_note-105]

#### Hazardous waste

Main article: [Hazardous waste in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazardous_waste_in_the_United_States)

This section is an excerpt from [Hazardous waste in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazardous_waste_in_the_United_States).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Hazardous_waste_in_the_United_States\&action=edit)]



A household hazardous waste collection center in [Seattle](11388236.md), [Washington](13015878.md), U.S.

Under [United States environmental policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_policy_of_the_United_States), [hazardous waste](38031.md) is a waste (usually a solid waste) that has the potential to:

- cause, or significantly contribute to an increase in [mortality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death) or an increase in serious irreversible, or incapacitating reversible illness; or

- pose a substantial present or potential hazard to human health or the environment when improperly treated, stored, transported, or disposed of, or otherwise managed.

Under the 1976 [Resource Conservation and Recovery Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resource_Conservation_and_Recovery_Act) (RCRA), a facility that treats, stores or disposes of hazardous waste must obtain a permit for doing so. Generators of and transporters of hazardous waste must meet specific requirements for handling, managing, and tracking waste. Through RCRA, [Congress](31756.md) directed EPA to issue regulations for the management of hazardous waste. EPA developed strict requirements for all aspects of hazardous waste management including the treatment, storage, and disposal of hazardous waste. In addition to these federal requirements, states may develop more stringent requirements or requirements that are broader in scope than the federal regulations.

EPA authorizes states to implement the RCRA hazardous waste program. Authorized states must maintain standards that are equivalent to and at least as stringent as the federal program. Implementation of the authorized program usually includes activities such as permitting, corrective action, inspections, monitoring and enforcement.

## Population

Main article: [Population of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_of_the_United_States)

The total U.S. population crossed the 100 million mark around 1915, the 200 million mark in 1967, and the 300 million mark in 2006 (estimated on Tuesday, October 17).[^cite_note-106][^cite_note-MSNBC_15298443-107] The U.S. population more than tripled during the 20th century – a growth rate of about 1.3 percent a year – from about 76 million in 1900 to 281 million in 2000.&#x20;

Population growth is fastest among minorities, and according to the [United States Census Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau)'s estimation for 2005, 45% of American children under age 5 are minorities.[^cite_note-108] In 2007, the nation's minority population reached 102.5 million.[^cite_note-109] A year before, the minority population totaled 100.7 million. [Hispanic and Latino Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic_and_Latino_Americans) accounted for almost half (1.4 million) of the national [population growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_growth) of 2.9 million between July 1, 2005, and July 1, 2006.[^cite_note-110]

Based on a [Population Clock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_Clock) maintained by the [U.S. Census Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau), the current U.S. population, as of July 2021 is about 332 million.[^cite_note-111] A 2004 U.S. Census Bureau report predicted an increase of one-third by the year 2050.[^cite_note-112] A subsequent 2008 report projects a population of 439 million, which is a 44% increase from 2008.

## Conservation and environmental movement

Main articles: [Conservation movement § United States](45441.md#United_States), and [Environmental movement in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_movement_in_the_United_States)

Today, the organized [environmental movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_movement) is represented by a wide range of organizations sometimes called [non-governmental organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-governmental_organizations) or NGOs. These organizations exist on local national and international scales. Environmental NGOs vary widely in political views and in the extent to which they seek to influence government. The environmental movement today consists of both large national groups and many smaller local groups with local concerns. Some resemble the old U.S. conservation movement – whose modern expression is the Nature Conservancy, Audubon Society and National Geographic Society – American organizations with a worldwide influence.
