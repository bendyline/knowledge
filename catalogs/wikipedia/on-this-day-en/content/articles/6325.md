A **church**, **church building**, or **church house** is a building used for [Christian worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_worship) [services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_service) and other [Christian religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_religion) activities. The earliest identified Christian church is a [house church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_church) founded between 233 [AD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AD) and 256 AD.[^cite_note-Snyder_2003_128-1]

Sometimes, the word *church* is used erroneously to refer to the [buildings of other religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place_of_worship), such as [mosques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosques) and [synagogues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synagogues).[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3] *Church* is also used to describe a [body or an assembly of Christian believers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_\(congregation\)), while "the Church" may be used to refer to the worldwide Christian religious community as a whole.[^cite_note-4]

In traditional [Christian architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_architecture), the [plan view](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plan_view) of a church often forms a [Christian cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_cross) with the centre [aisle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aisle) and seating representing the vertical beam and the [bema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_architecture#Characteristics_of_the_early_Christian_church_building) and [altar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altar) forming the horizontal. The area of the church containing the altar is the [chancel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancel). Churches are usually topped with a [steeple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steeple), which metaphorically directs the minds of the faithful upward to God. Steeples often house a [belfry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belfry) that contains [church bells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_bell), which call Christians to [Christian prayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_prayer) and [worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_worship).[^cite_note-Nelson2024-5] Modern churches have a variety of [architectural styles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architectural_style) and layouts. Some buildings designed for other purposes have been converted to churches, while many original church buildings have been put to other uses. From the 11th to the 14th century, there had been a wave of church construction in [Western Europe](33800.md).

Many churches worldwide are of considerable historical, national, cultural, and architectural significance, with several included in the list of [UNESCO World Heritage Sites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_by_religion).[^cite_note-Tsivolas_2014_3–4-6]

## Etymology



*Cyrican* is an [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English) word for churches and church property

The word *church* is derived from [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English) *cirice*, 'place of assemblage set aside for Christian worship', from the [Common Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Germanic_language) word *kirika*. This was probably borrowed via [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_language) from [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) *kyriakon doma*, 'the Lord's (house)', from *[kyrios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrios)*, 'ruler, lord'. *Kyrios* in turn comes from the [Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_language) root \**ḱewh₁-*, meaning 'to spread out, to swell' (euphemistically: 'to prevail, to be strong').[^cite_note-7]

The various forms of the cognates to *church* in various languages reflect the word's linguistic roots in Greek and Proto-Indo-European origins. For instance, in early Germanic languages such as Old High German, the word evolved into *kirihha*, highlighting its spread through the Christianization of Germanic peoples. This etymological journey illustrates how the concept of a place of Christian worship was linguistically adapted as Christianity expanded across Europe. Additionally, the use of the word in early Christian communities emphasized the association of the building with its dedication to God.[^cite_note-OEtymDChurch-8]

The Greek *kyriakon*, 'of the Lord', has been used of houses of Christian worship since c. AD 300, especially in the East, although it was less common in this sense than *[ekklesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesia_\(ancient_Greece\))* or *[basilike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica)*.[^cite_note-OEtymDChurch-8]

## History

### Antiquity



South facade of the [Church of Saint Simeon Stylites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Saint_Simeon_Stylites) in Aleppo, Syria, is considered to be one of the oldest surviving church buildings in the world.

Further information: [List of oldest church buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_oldest_church_buildings)

The earliest archeologically identified Christian church is a [house church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_church) (*domus ecclesiae*), the [Dura-Europos church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dura-Europos_church), founded between 233 AD and 256 AD.[^cite_note-Snyder_2003_128-1]

In the second half of the third century AD, the first purpose-built halls for Christian worship (*aula ecclesiae*) began to be constructed. Although many of these were destroyed early in the next century during the [Diocletianic Persecution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocletianic_Persecution). Even larger and more elaborate churches began to appear during the reign of Emperor [Constantine the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_the_Great).[^cite_note-9]

### Medieval times

From the 11th through the 14th centuries, a wave of cathedral building and the construction of smaller [parish churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parish_church) occurred across [Western Europe](33800.md). Besides serving as a [place of worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place_of_worship), the cathedral or parish church was frequently employed as a general gathering place by the communities in which they were located, hosting such events as [guild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guild) meetings, [banquets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banquet), [mystery plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystery_plays), and [fairs](504805.md). Church grounds and buildings were also used for the threshing and storage of grain.[^cite_note-10]

#### Romanesque architecture

Between 1000 and 1200, the [Romanesque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanesque_architecture) style became popular across [Europe](9239.md). The Romanesque style is defined by large and bulky edifices typically composed of simple, compact, sparsely decorated geometric structures. Frequent features of the Romanesque church include [circular arches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arch), round or [octagonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octagon) towers, and [cushion capitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_\(architecture\)) on pillars. In the early Romanesque era, [coffering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffer) on the ceiling was fashionable, while later in the same era, [groined vaults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groin_vault) gained popularity. Interiors widened, and the motifs of sculptures took on more epic traits and themes.[^cite_note-11] Romanesque architects adopted many Roman or early Christian architectural ideas, such as a cruciform ground plan, as that of [Angoulême Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angoul%C3%AAme_Cathedral), and the [basilica system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica) of a nave with a central vessel and side aisles.[^cite_note-dkp-12]

#### Gothic architecture



The [Cathedral of Ani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Ani), one of the founders of the Gothic style of architecture



The [Frauenkirche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich_Frauenkirche) in Munich is a largely [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_architecture), medieval church.

The [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_architecture) style emerged around 1140 in [Île-de-France](1716427.md) and subsequently spread throughout Europe.[^cite_note-13] Gothic churches lost the compact qualities of the Romanesque era, and decorations often contained [symbolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbol) and [allegorical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegorical) features. The first [pointed arches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointed_arch), [rib vaults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rib_vault), and [buttresses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttress) began to appear, all possessing geometric properties that reduced the need for large, rigid walls to ensure structural stability. This also permitted the size of windows to increase, producing brighter and lighter interiors. [Nave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nave) ceilings rose, and pillars and steeples heightened.[^cite_note-wd-14][^cite_note-dkp-12] Many architects used these developments to push the limits of structural possibility – an inclination that resulted in the collapse of several towers whose designs had unwittingly exceeded the boundaries of soundness. In Germany, the Netherlands and Spain, it became popular to build [hall churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hall_church), a style in which every [vault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vault_\(architecture\)) would be built to the same height.

Gothic cathedrals were lavishly designed, as in the Romanesque era, and many share Romanesque traits. However, several also exhibit unprecedented degrees of detail and complexity in decoration. [Notre-Dame de Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notre-Dame_de_Paris) and [Reims Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reims_Cathedral) in France, as well as the church of [San Francesco d'Assisi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francesco_d'Assisi,_Palermo) in [Palermo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palermo), [Salisbury Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salisbury_Cathedral) and the [wool churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wool_church) in England, and [Santhome Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santhome_Church) in [Chennai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chennai), [India](14533.md), show the elaborate stylings characteristic of Gothic cathedrals.

Some of the most well-known gothic churches remained unfinished for centuries after the style fell out of popularity. One such example is the construction of [Cologne Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne_Cathedral), which began in 1248, was halted in 1473, and didn't resume until 1842.[^cite_note-15]

### Renaissance

In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the changes in ethics and society due to the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance) and the [Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformation) also influenced the building of churches. The common style was much like the Gothic style but simplified. The [basilica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica) was not the most popular type of church anymore, but instead, [hall churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hall_church) were built. Typical features are columns and classical [capitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_\(architecture\)).[^cite_note-16]

In [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant) churches, where the proclamation of God's Word is of particular importance, the visitor's line of sight is directed towards the [pulpit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulpit).

### Baroque architecture



Central [nave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nave) of the [Church of St. Peter and St. Paul, Vilnius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_St._Peter_and_St._Paul,_Vilnius), Lithuania, an example of a [Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroque_architecture) church interior

The [Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroque_architecture) style was first used in Italy around 1575. From there, it spread to the rest of Europe and the European colonies. The building industry increased heavily during the [Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroque) era. Buildings, even churches, were used to indicate wealth, authority, and influence. The use of forms known from the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance) was extremely exaggerated. [Domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dome) and [capitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_\(architecture\)) were decorated with moulding, and the former [stucco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stucco) sculptures were replaced by [fresco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresco) paintings on the ceilings. For the first time, churches were seen as one connected work of art, and consistent artistic concepts were developed. Instead of long buildings, more central-plan buildings were created. The sprawling decoration with floral ornamentation and mythological motives lasted until about 1720, in the [Rococo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rococo) era.[^cite_note-17]

The Protestant [parishes](23623.md) preferred lateral churches, in which all the visitors could be as close as possible to the [pulpit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulpit) and the [altar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altar).[^cite_note-18]

## Architecture

Main article: [Church architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_architecture)



The view of the spire of [Norwich Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwich_Cathedral) from the [cloisters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloister), in Norfolk, England

A common trait of the architecture of many churches is the shape of a [cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_cross)[^cite_note-19] (a long central rectangle, with side rectangles and a rectangle in front for the [altar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altar) space or sanctuary). These churches also often have a [dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dome) or other large [vaulted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vault_\(architecture\)) space in the interior to represent or draw attention to the heavens. Other common shapes for churches include a circle, to represent eternity, or an [octagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octagon) or similar star shape, to represent the church's bringing light to the world. Another common feature is the [spire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spire), a tall tower at the "west" end of the church or over the [crossing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossing_\(architecture\)).

Another common feature of many Christian churches is the [eastwards orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_of_churches) of the front altar.[^cite_note-20] Often, the altar will not be oriented due east but toward the sunrise. This tradition originated in [Byzantium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantium) in the fourth century and became prevalent in the West in the eighth and ninth centuries. The old Roman custom of having the altar at the west end and the entrance at the east was sometimes followed as late as the eleventh century, even in areas of northern Europe under Frankish rule, as seen in [Petershausen (Constance)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petershausen_\(Constance\)), [Bamberg Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bamberg_Cathedral), [Augsburg Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augsburg_Cathedral), [Regensburg Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regensburg_Cathedral), and [Hildesheim Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hildesheim_Cathedral).[^cite_note-21]

The architecture of [evangelical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelicalism) places of worship, such as those of the Baptist and Methodist traditions, are often characterized by their sobriety.[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23] The [Latin cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_cross) is a well known [Christian symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_symbol) that can usually be seen on the building of an evangelical church and that identifies the place's belonging.[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25] There is usually a [baptistery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptistery) at the front of the church (called the [chancel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancel) in historic traditions) or in a separate room.[^cite_note-26][^cite_note-27]

## Types

### Basilica

Main article: [Basilica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica)

Further information: [Minster (church)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minster_\(church\))

The [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) word *basilica* was initially used to describe a [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) public building usually located in the [forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forum_\(Roman\)) of a Roman town.[^cite_note-OXFORD1-28][^cite_note-29] After the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) became [officially Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edict_of_Thessalonica), the term came by extension to refer to a large and influential church that has been given special ceremonial rights by the [Pope](23056.md).[^cite_note-30] The word thus retains two senses today, one architectural and the other ecclesiastical.

### Cathedral

Main articles: [Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral), [Duomo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duomo), and [Architecture of cathedrals and great churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_cathedrals_and_great_churches)



[Florence Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Cathedral)

A cathedral is a church, usually [Catholic](606848.md), [Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican), [Oriental Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Orthodox) or [Eastern Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox), housing the seat of a bishop. The word cathedral takes its name from *[cathedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedra)*, or Bishop's Throne (In [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language): *ecclesia cathedralis*). The term is sometimes (improperly) used to refer to any church of great size.

A church with a cathedral function is not necessarily a large building. It might be as small as [Christ Church Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_Church_Cathedral,_Oxford) in [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford), England, [Porvoo Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porvoo_Cathedral) in [Porvoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porvoo), Finland, [Sacred Heart Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_Heart_Cathedral_\(Raleigh,_North_Carolina\)) in [Raleigh, United States](57845.md), or [Chur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chur) Cathedral in Switzerland. However, frequently, the cathedral, along with some of the abbey churches, was the [largest building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_buildings_in_the_world) in any region.

Cathedrals tend to display a higher level of [contemporary architectural style](3970948.md) and the work of accomplished craftsmen, and occupy a status both ecclesiastical and social that an ordinary parish church rarely has. Such churches are generally among the finest buildings locally and a source of national and regional pride,[^cite_note-31] and many are among the world's most renowned works of architecture.[^cite_note-32]

### Chapel

Main article: [Chapel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapel)

Further information: [Chapel of ease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapel_of_ease) and [Sistine Chapel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sistine_Chapel)



[Cappella Palatina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cappella_Palatina), [Palermo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palermo), [Sicily](27619.md)

Either, a discrete space with an altar inside a larger cathedral, conventual, parish, or other church; or, a free standing small church building or room not connected to a larger church, to serve a particular [hospital](18994221.md), [school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School), [university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University), prison, private household, [palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace), [castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle), or other institution. Often proprietary churches and small conventual churches are referred to by this term.[^cite_note-33] A number of Roman Catholic and Evangelical-Lutheran churches have [side chapels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Side_chapel) that are used for prayer, and/or are dedicated for specific purposes, e.g. the normative place that baptisms are held in the church.[^cite_note-34]

### Collegiate church

Main article: [Collegiate church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collegiate_church)

A collegiate church is a church where the [daily office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daily_office) of worship is maintained by a [college](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_\(canon_law\)) of [canons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_\(priest\)), which may be presided over by a [dean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_\(religion\)) or [provost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provost_\(religion\)). Collegiate churches were often supported by extensive lands held by the church, or by [tithe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tithe) income from [appropriated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impropriation) [benefices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benefices). They commonly provide distinct spaces for congregational worship and for the choir offices of their clerical community.

### Conventual church

Further information: [Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey), [Priory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priory), and [Katholikon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katholikon)

A conventual church (in Eastern Orthodoxy *[katholikon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katholikon)*) is the main church in a Christian [monastery](45856.md) or [convent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convent), known variously as an [abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey), a [priory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priory), a [friary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friary), or a [preceptory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preceptor#Christian_military_orders).

### Parish church

Main article: [Parish Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parish_Church)

Further information: [Parish](23623.md) and [Clergy house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clergy_house)



St Margarete Parish Church, [Berndorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berndorf,_Lower_Austria), [Austria](26964606.md)

A parish church is a church built to meet the needs of people localised in a geographical area called a [parish](23623.md). The vast majority of Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, and Lutheran church buildings fall into this category. A parish church may also be a basilica, a cathedral, a conventual or collegiate church, or a place of pilgrimage. The vast majority of parish churches do not however enjoy such privileges.

In addition to a parish church, each parish may maintain auxiliary organizations and their facilities such as a [rectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectory), [parish hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_hall), [parochial school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parochial_school), or [convent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convent), frequently located on the same campus or adjacent to the church.

### Pilgrimage church

Main article: [Pilgrimage church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilgrimage_church)

A pilgrimage church is a church to which [pilgrimages](23763.md) are regularly made, or a church along a pilgrimage route, often located at the tomb of a [saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint), or holding icons or [relics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relic) to which miraculous properties are ascribed, the site of [Marian apparitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marian_apparition), etc.

### Proprietary church

Main article: [Proprietary church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proprietary_church)

During the Middle Ages, a proprietary church was a church, abbey, or cloister built on the private grounds of a feudal lord, over which he retained proprietary interests.

### Churchhouse and Meetinghouse



The meetinghouse of the Modesto Congeragation of the [Old German Baptist Brethren Church, New Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_German_Baptist_Brethren_Church,_New_Conference) in [Modesto, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modesto,_California)

Main article: [Meeting house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meeting_house)

The term "meetinghouse" (also called a "churchhouse") has been employed by a number of [Old Order Anabaptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Order_Anabaptist) and [Conservative Anabaptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Anabaptist) fellowships, as well as in the [Quaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quakerism) tradition. The term "meetinghouse", as well as "chapel" was employed to refer to houses of worship in the [Methodist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodist) tradition, though at present, the term "church" is normative.[^cite_note-35]

### House church

Main article: [House church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_church)



A house church in Shunyi, Beijing

In some countries of the world which apply [sharia](28840.md) or [communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communism), government authorizations for worship are complex for Christians.[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38] Because of [persecution of Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_Christians), Evangelical [house churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_churches) have thus developed.[^cite_note-39] For example, there is the [Evangelical house churches in China](357470.md) movement.[^cite_note-40] The meetings thus take place in private houses, in secret and in "illegality".[^cite_note-41]

### Megachurch

A church in which more than 2,000 people gather each Sunday is known as a [megachurch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megachurch). The term gigachurch is sometimes used to refer to especially large megachurches, such as [Lakewood Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakewood_Church) (United States) or [Yoido Full Gospel Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoido_Full_Gospel_Church) (South Korea).[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44]

### Alternative buildings

Further information: [Road church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road_church) and [Simultaneum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simultaneum)

Old and disused church buildings can be seen as an interesting proposition for developers as the architecture and location often provide for attractive homes[^cite_note-45] or city centre entertainment venues.[^cite_note-46] On the other hand, many newer churches have decided to host meetings in public buildings such as schools,[^cite_note-47] universities,[^cite_note-48] cinemas[^cite_note-49] or theatres.[^cite_note-50]

There is another trend to convert old buildings for [worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worship) rather than face the construction costs and planning difficulties of a new build. Unusual venues in the UK include a former tram power station,[^cite_note-51] a former [bus garage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus_garage),[^cite_note-52] a former cinema and [bingo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bingo_\(UK\)) hall,[^cite_note-53] a former [Territorial Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_Army_\(United_Kingdom\)) drill hall,[^cite_note-54] and a former [synagogue](48559.md).[^cite_note-55] [HMS *Tees*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Tees_\(1817\)) served as a floating church for mariners at [Liverpool](18081.md) from 1827 until she sank in 1872.[^cite_note-LM100672-56] A windmill has also been converted into a church at [Reigate Heath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reigate_Heath_Windmill).

## Management and maintenance

A [parochial church council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parochial_church_council) in the [Church of England](5955.md) is responsible for the [maintenance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building_maintenance) of the churches and [church halls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_hall) under its jurisdiction.[^cite_note-57]

&#x20;The [Catholic Church](606848.md) directs [priests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_priests) in parishes to ensure that

> The house of prayer in which the Most Holy Eucharist is celebrated and reserved, where the faithful gather and where the presence of the Son of God, our Savior, offered for us on the altar of sacrifice bestows strength and blessings on the faithful, \[is kept] spotless and suitable for prayer and sacred functions.[^cite_note-58]

Several partnerships have been established involving church management and private [real estate companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_estate_company) to redevelop church properties into mixed uses. While they have garnered criticism, these partnerships allow congregations to increase revenue while preserving the property.[^cite_note-59]

## Geographical distribution



[Church of the Holy Sepulchre](7810.md) is considered the most important church in all of [Christendom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christendom).

[^cite_note-60]

With the exception of [Saudi Arabia](349303.md) and the [Maldives](19117.md), all [sovereign states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign_states) and [dependent territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependent_territories) worldwide have church buildings.[^cite_note-Kurian_2015_187–188-61] [Afghanistan](737.md) has the fewest churches globally, featuring only one official church: the [Our Lady of Divine Providence Chapel in Kabul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_Lady_of_Divine_Providence_Chapel,_Kabul).[^cite_note-62] [Somalia](27358.md) follows closely, having once housed the [Mogadishu Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mogadishu_Cathedral),[^cite_note-63] along with the Saint [Anthony of Padua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_of_Padua) Church in [Somaliland](51425.md).[^cite_note-64] Other countries with a limited number of churches include [Bhutan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhutan) and [Western Sahara](33220.md).[^cite_note-65]

In contrast, some estimates suggest that the [United States](3434750.md) has the highest number of churches in the world, with around 380,000,[^cite_note-66] followed by [Brazil](3383.md) and [Italy](14532.md).[^cite_note-67] According to the Future for Religious Heritage, there are over 500,000 churches across [Europe](9239.md).[^cite_note-68] Several cities are commonly known as the "[City of Churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Churches)" due to their abundance of churches. These cities include [Adelaide](1148.md),[^cite_note-69] [Ani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ani),[^cite_note-70] [Ayacucho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayacucho),[^cite_note-71] [Kraków](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Krak%C3%B3w),[^cite_note-diecezja-72] [Moscow](19004.md),[^cite_note-73] [Montreal](7954681.md),[^cite_note-74] [Naples](55880.md),[^cite_note-75] [Ohrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohrid),[^cite_note-76] [Prague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_in_Prague), [Puebla](413436.md), [Querétaro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quer%C3%A9taro_\(city\)), [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churches_of_Rome),[^cite_note-77] [Salzburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salzburg), and [Vilnius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_churches_in_Vilnius).[^cite_note-78] Notably, Rome and [New York City](645042.md) are home to the highest number of churches of any city in the world.[^cite_note-79]



[St. Peter's Basilica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Peter's_Basilica) in [Vatican City](32408.md), the [largest church in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_church_buildings)

[^cite_note-80]

Although building churches is prohibited in Saudi Arabia, which has around 1.5 million Christians,[^cite_note-81] the country contains the remnants of a historic church known as the [Jubail Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jubail_Church), which dates back to the fourth century and was affiliated with the [Church of the East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_East).[^cite_note-\:1-82] Discovered in 1986, the site was excavated by the Saudi Antiquities Department in 1987. As of 2008, the findings from this excavation had not been published, reflecting sensitivities regarding artifacts from non-Islamic religions.[^cite_note-\:1-82] In the Maldives, which has approximately 1,400 Christians,[^cite_note-83] building churches is prohibited. However, only foreign Christian workers are allowed to practice their religion privately.[^cite_note-84] Despite the prohibition on church construction, both countries have secret [home churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_churches).[^cite_note-Kurian_2015_187–188-61]

[Christianity](5211.md) is the [world's largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_religious_groups) and most widespread religion,[^cite_note-85] with over 2.3 billion followers.[^cite_note-86] Churches are found across all seven [continents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continents) ([Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Asia), [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Africa), [North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_North_America), [South America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_South_America), [Antarctica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Antarctica), [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Europe), and [Oceania](22621.md)). Antarctica is home to [eight churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Antarctic_churches),[^cite_note-87] with two additional churches located south of the [Antarctic Convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_Convergence).[^cite_note-88]

Many churches worldwide are of considerable historical, national, [cultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_culture), and architectural significance, with several recognized as [UNESCO World Heritage Sites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_by_religion).[^cite_note-Tsivolas_2014_3–4-6] According to the *[Catholic Encyclopedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia)* the [Cenacle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cenacle) (the site of the [Last Supper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Supper)) in [Jerusalem](16043.md) was the "first Christian church".[^cite_note-89] The [Dura-Europos church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dura-Europos_church) in Syria is the oldest surviving church building in the world.[^cite_note-90] Several authors have cited the [Etchmiadzin Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etchmiadzin_Cathedral) ([Armenia](10918072.md)'s mother church) as the oldest [cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral) in the world.[^cite_note-91][^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93]
