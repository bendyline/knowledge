In [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics) and [finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance), **market manipulation** occurs when someone intentionally alters the supply or demand of a security to influence its price. This can involve spreading misleading information, executing misleading trades, or manipulating quotes and prices.[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3]

Market manipulation is prohibited in most countries, in particular, it is prohibited in the [United States](3434750.md) under Section 9(a)(2)[^cite_note-4] of the [Securities Exchange Act of 1934](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Securities_Exchange_Act_of_1934), in the [European Union](9317.md) under Article 12 of the *Market Abuse Regulation*,[^cite_note-5] in [Australia](4689264.md) under Section 1041A of the [Corporations Act 2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporations_Act_2001), and in [Israel](9282173.md) under Section 54(a) of the securities act of 1968. In the US, market manipulation is also prohibited for [wholesale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wholesale) electricity markets under Section 222 of the [Federal Power Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Power_Act)[^cite_note-6] and wholesale [natural gas](22131.md) markets under Section 4A of the [Natural Gas Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_Gas_Act).[^cite_note-7]

In India it is illegal under the [SEBI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Securities_and_Exchange_Board_of_India) (Prohibition of Fraudulent and Unfair Trade Practices relating to Securities Market) Regulations, 2003.[^cite_note-8]

## Examples

### Pools

Main article: [Pool operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pool_operation)

Agreements, often written, among a group of [traders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchant) to delegate authority to a single manager to trade in a specific [stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock) for a work period of time and then to share in the resulting profits or losses.[^cite_note-9] In Australia section 1041B prohibits pooling.[^cite_note-10]

### Runs

When a group of traders create activity or rumours in order to drive the price of a [security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security) up. An example is the [Guinness share-trading fraud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness_share-trading_fraud) of the 1980s. In the US, this activity is usually referred to as *painting the tape*.[^cite_note-11]

### Ramping (the market)

Actions designed to artificially raise the [market price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_price) of listed securities and give the impression of voluminous trading in order to make a quick profit.[^cite_note-12]

### Bear raid

In a [bear raid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bear_raid) there is an attempt to push the price of a stock down by heavy selling or [short selling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_selling).[^cite_note-13]

### Quote stuffing

[Quote stuffing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quote_stuffing) is made possible by high-frequency [trading programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trading_program?action=edit\&redlink=1) that can execute market actions with incredible speed. However, high-frequency trading in and of itself is not illegal. The tactic involves using specialized, high-bandwidth hardware to quickly enter and withdraw large quantities of orders in an attempt to flood the market, thereby gaining an advantage over slower market participants.[^cite_note-14]

### Cross-market manipulation

Cross-market manipulation occurs when a trader trades in one market for the purpose of manipulating the price of an asset in another market, capitalizing off the price-moving effects thus generated, instead of with the bona fide intent of profiting off the initial trade itself.[^cite_note-15]

### Cornering the market

Main article: [Cornering the market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornering_the_market)

In cornering the market, the manipulators buy a sufficiently large amount of an asset, often a [commodity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodity), so they can control the price creating in effect a [monopoly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopoly). For example, the brothers [Nelson Bunker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nelson_Bunker_Hunt) and [William Herbert Hunt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Herbert_Hunt) attempted to corner the world [silver](27119.md) markets in the late 1970s and early 1980s, at one stage holding the rights to more than half of the world's deliverable silver.[^cite_note-TEXAS-16] During the Hunts' accumulation of the precious metal, silver prices rose from \$11 an ounce in September 1979 to nearly \$50 an ounce in January 1980.[^cite_note-NYT-17] Silver prices ultimately collapsed to below \$11 an ounce two months later,[^cite_note-NYT-17] much of the fall occurring on a single day now known as [Silver Thursday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_Thursday), due to changes made to exchange rules regarding the purchase of commodities on margin.[^cite_note-TimeBubble-18]

### Pinging and spoofing

Pinging involves making small orders and then cancelling them with a computerized program in an effort to induce others in the marketplace to react to the "pings" and reveal their trading intentions to the party pinging.[^cite_note-19] Similarly, with spoofing, a computerized program submits or cancelling multiple bids or offers to manipulate the price of a security.

### Electronic front running

Similar to traditional [front running](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Front_running), electronic front running uses knowledge of future orders to execute trades ahead of a future price. However, electronic front running utilizes [financial technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_technology) to evaluate price changes and financial transactions for parties to execute advantageous trades.[^cite_note-20]
