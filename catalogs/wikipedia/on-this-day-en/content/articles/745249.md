**Washington Square Park** is a 9.75-acre (3.95 ha) [public park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_park) in the [Greenwich Village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenwich_Village) neighborhood of [Lower Manhattan](455646.md), New York City. It is an icon as well as a meeting place and center for cultural activity.[^cite_note-Parks-1] The park is operated by the [New York City Department of Parks and Recreation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City_Department_of_Parks_and_Recreation) (NYC Parks).

The park is an open space, dominated by the [Washington Square Arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Square_Arch) at the northern gateway to the park, with a tradition of celebrating nonconformity. The park's fountain area has long been one of the city's popular spots, and many of the local buildings have at one time served as homes and studios for artists. Many buildings have been built by [New York University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_University), while others have been converted from their former uses into academic and residential buildings.

## Location and features



[*Statue of Giuseppe Garibaldi*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue_of_Giuseppe_Garibaldi_\(New_York_City\))

Located at the foot of [Fifth Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_Avenue_\(Manhattan\)), the park is bordered by [Washington Square North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Square_North) (known as [Waverly Place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waverly_Place) east and west of the park), Washington Square East (known as [University Place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_Place_\(Manhattan\)) north of the park), Washington Square South (known as [West 4th Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_4th_Street_\(Manhattan\)) east and west of the park), and Washington Square West (known as [MacDougal Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacDougal_Street) north and south of the park).

While the park contains many [flower beds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower_bed) and trees, little of the park is used for plantings due to the paving. The two prominent features are the Washington Square Arch and the Tisch Fountain. It includes children's play areas, trees and gardens, paths to stroll on, a [chess](5134.md) and *[Scrabble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrabble)* playing area, park benches, picnic tables, commemorative statuary and two dog runs.[^cite_note-Parks-1]

Those commemorated by statues and monuments include [George Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington); Italian patriot and soldier [Giuseppe Garibaldi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Garibaldi), commander of the insurrectionist forces in Italy's struggle for unification; and [Alexander Lyman Holley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bust_of_Alexander_Lyman_Holley), a talented engineer who helped start the American steel industry after the invention of the [Bessemer process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessemer_process) for mass-producing steel.[^cite_note-Parks-1]

The [New York City Police Department](255413.md) operates security cameras in the park.[^cite_note-2] The park has a curfew from midnight to 6 a.m.[^cite_note-3]

## History

### Early usage&#x20;



[Hangman's Elm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangman's_Elm)

The land was once divided by a narrow marshy valley through which [Minetta Creek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minetta_Creek) (or Brook) ran.[^cite_note-Assessment-4] In the early 17th century, a Native American village known as [Sapohanikan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sapohanikan)[^cite_note-5] or "Tobacco Field" was nearby. By the mid-17th century, the land on each side of the Minetta was used as farm land by the Dutch. The Dutch gave the land, then outside the city limits (Wall Street) to Angolan residents of the colony, intending for their plots and settlement to serve as a buffer zone to Native Americans outside the settlement. In 1643, a group of "half-freed" slaves and elders such as Domingo Anthony, Manuel Trumpeter and Catalina Anthony, received land grants to build and maintain farms in the areas containing and surrounding Washington Square Park.[^cite_note-6] The families who received the land were no longer slaves, but had to give a portion of the profits they received from the land to the [Dutch West India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_West_India_Company) and pay annual land fees. Their children would be born as slaves, rather than free. The area became the core of an early African American community in New York, then called the [Land of the Blacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_of_the_Blacks_\(Manhattan\)) and later "Little Africa".[^cite_note-7] Among those who owned parcels in what is now Washington Square Park was Paulo D'Angola and his wife [Reytory Angola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reytory_Angola).[^cite_note-8]

It remained farmland until April 1797, when the [Common Council of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City_Council) purchased the fields to the east of the Minetta (which were not yet within city limits) for a new [potter's field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potter's_field), or public burial ground. The city bought all but the western thirty feet of the square from land owner Elbert Herring, and drained the swamp, utilizing it as a graveyard for victims of communicable diseases, unknown or unclaimed person, and those too poor to provide for their own burial.[^cite_note-\:1-9] When New York City (which did not include this area yet) went through [yellow fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_fever) epidemics in the early 19th century, most of those who died from yellow fever were buried here, away from town, as a hygienic measure.[^cite_note-parkwebsite-10] "From 1789 to 1823, in two-thirds of the present park site, from 10,000 to 22,000 bodies were hastily buried in this potters field."[^cite_note-\:1-9] The cemetery was closed in 1825. To this day, the remains of more than 20,000 bodies rest under Washington Square.[^cite_note-Assessment-4] Excavations have found tombstones under the park dating as far back as 1799.[^cite_note-11]

A legend in many tourist guides says that the large elm at the northwest corner of the park, [Hangman's Elm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangman's_Elm), was the old hanging tree.[^cite_note-parkwebsite-10] However, research indicates the tree was on the side of the former Minetta Creek that was the back garden of a private house.[^cite_note-Assessment-4] Records of only one public hanging at the potter's field exist. Two eyewitnesses to the recorded hanging differed on the location of the gallows. One said it had been put up at a spot where the fountain was prior to 2007 park redesign. Others placed the gallows closer to where the arch is now.&#x20;

### Creation of Washington Square



*Washington Square* in the [New York Public Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Public_Library) collection

In 1826, Alderman Abraham Valentine "introduced a resolution to re-appropriate an old potter's field into a military parade ground,"[^cite_note-12] and the city bought the land west of Minetta Creek, the square was laid out and leveled, and it was turned into the Washington Military Parade Ground. Military parade grounds were public spaces specified by the city where volunteer militia companies responsible for the nation's defense would train.

The streets surrounding the square became one of the city's most desirable residential areas in the 1830s. The protected row of Greek Revival style houses on the north side of the park remains from that time.[^cite_note-13]

In 1849 and 1850, the parade ground was reworked into the first park on the site. More paths were added and a new fence was built around it. In 1871, it came under the control of the newly formed [New York City Department of Parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City_Department_of_Parks), and it was redesigned again, with curving rather than straight secondary paths.[^cite_note-Assessment-4]

### Construction of the arch and addition of statues

Main article: [Washington Square Arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Square_Arch)



[Washington Square Arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Square_Arch)

&#x20;In 1889, to celebrate the centennial of [George Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington)'s [inauguration as president of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_inauguration_of_George_Washington), a large plaster and wood [Memorial Arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memorial_Arch) was erected over Fifth Avenue just north of the park. [Freemasons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freemasonry) from [St. John's Lodge No. 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._John's_Lodge_\(New_York\)) lead a procession through the arch with the [George Washington Inaugural Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington_Inaugural_Bible) for the Centennial Parade of Washington's Inauguration.[^cite_note-14] The temporary plaster and wood arch was so popular that in 1892, a permanent [Tuckahoe marble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuckahoe_marble) arch, designed by the New York architect [Stanford White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_White), was erected,[^cite_note-15] standing 77 feet (23 m) and modeled after the [Arc de Triomphe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_de_Triomphe), built in Paris in 1806. During the excavations for the eastern part of the arch, human remains, a coffin, and a gravestone dated to 1803 were uncovered 10 feet (3.0 m) below ground level.[^cite_note-Assessment-4]



Close-up of Washington Square Arch

The first fountain next to the arch was completed in 1852 and then replaced in 1872. In 1851 it was described as having "a very large circular basin, with a central jet and several side jets." A story on the completion of the fountain appeared in the first edition of the *New-York Daily Times*, which would eventually become the *[New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Times)*.[^cite_note-16] The [statue of Giuseppe Garibaldi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue_of_Giuseppe_Garibaldi_\(New_York_City\)) was unveiled in 1888.[^cite_note-Assessment-4] In 1918, two statues of George Washington were added to the north side.

### Early 20th-century renovation



*Washington Arch* by [Childe Hassam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Childe_Hassam), c. 1893

[Robert Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Moses) became the New York City Parks Commissioner in 1934. He embarked on a crusade to fully redesign Washington Square Park, and local activists began an opposing fight that lasted three decades.

In 1934, Robert Moses had the fountain renovated to also serve as a wading pool. In 1952, Moses finalized plans to extend 5th Avenue through the park.[^cite_note-17] He intended to eventually push it through the neighborhood south of the park, as part of an urban renewal project. Area residents, including [Eleanor Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleanor_Roosevelt), opposed the plans. The urbanist [Jane Jacobs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Jacobs) became an activist and is credited with stopping the Moses plan and closing Washington Square Park to all auto traffic, but Jacobs, in her book *[The Death and Life of Great American Cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Death_and_Life_of_Great_American_Cities)*, praised another local advocate in the fight against park traffic, [Shirley Hayes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shirley_Hayes?action=edit\&redlink=1): "Hayes and the Washington Square Park Committee advocated eliminating the existing road, that is, closing the park to all automobile traffic – but at the same time, not widening the perimeter roads either. In short, they proposed closing off a roadbed without compensating for it."[^cite_note-18]

Hayes, former chairman of the Washington Square Park Committee and member of the Greenwich Village Community Planning Board, a local resident and mother of four sons, started a public outcry for the park in 1952 when a large, modern apartment building was constructed on the northwest corner of Washington Square North and Fifth Avenue. When then-Manhattan [borough president](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_president) [Hulan E. Jack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulan_E._Jack) suggested an elevated pedestrian walkway over a four-lane road through the park, Ms. Hayes initiated "Save the Square!", a seven-year battle to keep automobiles out of the quiet area.[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21] Though several different proposals were given for a roadway in the park, Hayes and her followers rejected them all. Seeking to "best serve the needs of children and adults of this family community," Hayes in turn presented her own proposal: 1.75 acres (700m<sup>2</sup>) of roadway would be converted to parkland, a paved area would be created for emergency access only, and all other vehicles would be permanently banned from the park. This plan received widespread support, including that of then-Congressman [John Lindsay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lindsay), as well as Washington Square Park West resident Eleanor Roosevelt. After a public hearing in 1958, a "ribbon tying" ceremony was held to mark the inception of a trial period in which the park would be free of vehicular traffic. In August 1959, the efforts of Ms. Hayes and her allies paid off; from that time forward Washington Square Park has been completely closed to traffic. A plaque commemorating her tireless crusade can be seen in the park today. Hayes's papers are archived at the [New-York Historical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New-York_Historical_Society).[^cite_note-22]

### Mid- and late 20th century



The central fountain with the [Philip Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Johnson)-designed [Bobst Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobst_Library) (on right)

Following the end of [World War II](32927.md), [folksingers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_music) had been congregating on warm Sunday afternoons at the fountain in the center of the park. Tension and conflicts began to develop between the bohemian element and the remaining working-class residents of the neighborhood. The city government began showing an increasing hostility to the use of public facilities by the public and, in 1947, began requiring permits before public performances could be given in any city park.

In the spring of 1961, the new parks commissioner [Newbold Morris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newbold_Morris) refused a permit to the folksingers for their Sunday afternoon gatherings, because "the folksingers have been bringing too many undesirable \[[beatnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatnik)] elements into the park."[^cite_note-23] On April 9, 1961, folk music pioneer [Izzy Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izzy_Young), owner of the Folklore Center—who had been trying to get permits for the folksingers—and about 500 musicians and supporters gathered in the park and sang songs without a permit, then held a procession from the park through the arch at Fifth Avenue, and marched to the Judson Memorial Church on the other side of the park; the [New York City Police Department](255413.md) was sent into the park, attacked civilians with [billy clubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Club_\(weapon\)), and arrested 10 people.[^cite_note-24] The incident made the front pages of newspapers as far away as Washington, DC.[^cite_note-25] The *[New York Mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Mirror)* initially reported it as a "Beatnik Riot", but retracted the headline in the next edition, although tensions remained for a while.

The bus-turnaround loop at the northern end of the park, surrounding Washington Square Arch, was modified in 1961. Part of the old loop was closed and replaced with turf, and a set of stanchions were installed around the remaining parts of the loop.[^cite_note-26] In 1962, Manhattan borough president [Edward R. Dudley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Richard_Dudley) announced plans to close off two vehicular roadways in Washington Square Park that were used by emergency vehicles, as well as the bus turnaround loop. This move would add 1.33 acres (0.54 ha) of parkland.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28] As part of a redevelopment of the park, a skating rink and [bandshell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandshell) would have been reconstructed.[^cite_note-29] Later that year, a committee was organized to appoint a landscape architect for an \$800,000 renovation of Washington Square Park.[^cite_note-30]

The city studied a plan to add a fence around Washington Square Park in 1973.[^cite_note-31]

Four pedestrians were killed in a car crash at the park in 1992,[^cite_note-p278487462-32] prompting NYU to suggest closing the section of University Place along the eastern side of the park.[^cite_note-33] The proposal was highly controversial among local residents.[^cite_note-p278630368-34]

### Early 21st-century renovation



Visitors wading in the fountain

NYC Parks first considered renovating the park in 2004, though the proposal was controversial.[^cite_note-\:0-35] In 2005, the iconic fountain in the center of the park was officially named the Tisch Fountain following a \$2.5 million donation. This change was met with some backlash from the community, especially considering that the Parks Department orchestrated the naming without consulting the City Council.[^cite_note-36]

In December 2007, NYC Parks began construction on a project to redesign and refurbish Washington Square Park,[^cite_note-NYT_2007_q855-37] which at the time was projected to cost \$16 million.[^cite_note-\:0-35][^cite_note-38] Changes to the park included moving the fountain off center to improve its visual alignment with the arch when viewed from above, replacing the perimeter fence with a taller fence, and flattening and shrinking the central plaza, the park's politically contested gathering space. The plan also called for felling dozens of mature trees and installing ornamental water plumes in the fountain.[^cite_note-nyt20140509-39]

A spectrum of opponents had charged the mayor variously with privatizing the park and with social engineering park use, as part of a broader web of speculation schemes threatening nearby South Village and East Village communities and architecture. Five lawsuits were filed to challenge NYC Parks' renovation plans. In July 2006, [New York County Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Supreme_Court) Justice Emily Jane Goodman enjoined any renovation work on the fountain or fountain plaza area, pending further review of the plans by the local community board, the [New York City Landmarks Preservation Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City_Landmarks_Preservation_Commission), and the Art Commission, stating that NYC Parks had intentionally misrepresented the project in a scheme to secure its approval. The ruling was reversed on appeal.[^cite_note-40] Another lawsuit challenging the art commission's approval of the plan was dismissed. Two more lawsuits questioning the environmental review of the renovation project were heard in 2007 by the New York County Supreme Court, then dismissed.

Upon the completion of phase one of the park's renovation on May 22, 2009, the Coalition for a Better Washington Square Park, a private organization, began raising money to "hire off-duty cops and maintenance workers to patrol the park" by the summer of 2010.[^cite_note-41] On June 2, 2011, the eastern half of the park was reopened to the public, leaving only the park's southwest corner under construction. In mid-August 2012, the new granite benches heated up to 125 °F (52 °C) in the sun, seemingly vindicating community members who had charged that the renovations were primarily to discourage public use of the park.[^cite_note-42] The entire renovation was completed in 2014 for \$30.6 million.[^cite_note-nyt20140509-39]

In July 2020, the northwest lawn was reopened after a yearlong restoration which included new grass and sod for the over 39,000 square feet of green space. Funding for the \$170,000 project was provided by [Ray Dalio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Dalio)'s Dalio Philanthropies.[^cite_note-wash_Brin-43] In 2026, [Manhattan Community Board 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Community_Board_2) proposed that the city government consider adding permanent gates at the park's entrances. At the time, the [New York City Police Department](255413.md) put up temporary barricades at night to enforce a midnight curfew, but people still entered the park after-hours.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45]

## Cultural importance

Washington Square has long been a hub for politics and culture in New York City. The first neighborhood organization established in New York City was created in support of the park. For over 100 years, the Washington Square Association has been helping to support and better the park and the surrounding neighborhood.[^cite_note-washingtonsquarenyc.org-46]

### New York University

New York University, which has many of its "main campus" (of sorts, considering its urban location) buildings surrounding the park, once held its main graduation ceremonies in the park, before changing their locations to [Radio City Music Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_City_Music_Hall) in Manhattan and [Yankee Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yankee_Stadium) in [the Bronx](3338.md), mostly to accommodate the large numbers of students graduating from the university each year; nevertheless, even long after Washington Square lost the privilege of hosting said ceremonies, every year, the newest undergraduate/graduate students graduating from the university have (unofficially) celebrated the occasion within the park, both before and after the ceremonies.[^cite_note-47]

### Performers and entertainers

The presence of street performers has been one of the defining characteristics of Washington Square Park.[^cite_note-perf-48] For many years, people visiting the park have mingled with the buskers, performers, musicians, and poets.[^cite_note-49] Because of a change in policy on a 2010 rule that involved artists, the new ruling that was to come in on May 8, 2013, would involve entertainers. This could mean that performers could be fined \$250 for the first offense and up to \$1,000 for further violations. The 2010 rule on which the 2013 ruling was based stated that artists could not sell within 50 feet of a monument or five feet from any bench or fence.[^cite_note-perf-48]

Some holiday traditions in the park date back to 1924. Each December, the park is home to an annual tree-lighting ceremony as well as a Christmas Eve sing-along with carolers and festive music. The Washington Square Music Festival, which began in 1953, has brought chamber music concerts to park-goers every summer.[^cite_note-washingtonsquarenyc.org-46]

### Protests and demonstrations

Along with nearby [Union Square Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Square,_Manhattan), the park has long been the beginning and/or end point of many political demonstrations and other large protests.[^cite_note-50]

In 1834, [New York University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_University) decided to use prison labor to dress the stone for a new building, across from the park, as prison labor from [Sing Sing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sing_Sing) was cheaper than hiring local stonemasons. This, the stonecutters of the city said, was taking the bread out of their mouths. They held a rally in Washington Square Park, and then held the first labor march in the city. That turned into a riot, and the [27th New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/27th_New_York?action=edit\&redlink=1) regiment was called out to quell the stonecutters. The regiment camped in Washington Square for four days and nights until the excitement subsided. New York University continued its use of prison labor.[^cite_note-51]

On Labor Day September 2, 1912, approximately 20,000 workers (including 5,000 women) marched to the park to commemorate the [Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangle_Shirtwaist_Factory_fire), which had killed 146 workers the year before.[^cite_note-52] Many of the women wore fitted tucked-front blouses like those manufactured by the Triangle Shirtwaist Company.[^cite_note-53] This clothing style became the working woman's uniform and a symbol of female independence, reflecting the alliance of labor and suffrage movements. Over 25,000 people marched up Fifth Avenue from the park demanding women's suffrage in 1915.[^cite_note-54]

### Notable people



[Chess](5134.md) players in the southwest corner of the park

In 1888, [Robert Louis Stevenson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Louis_Stevenson), visiting the U.S. to seek medical help for his battle with [consumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumption_\(disease\)), talked to [Mark Twain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Twain) in the park.[^cite_note-55]

In the years before and after [World War I](4764461.md), the park was a center for many American artists, writers, and activists, including the photographer [André Kertész](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9_Kert%C3%A9sz), who photographed the square during winter. Later, the park was a gathering area for the [Beat Generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beat_Generation), folk, and [hippie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippie) movements in the 1950s and 1960s; in 1958, musician [Buddy Holly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddy_Holly), a nearby resident of [Greenwich Village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenwich_Village), spent time in the park both listening to people play and helping guitarists with musical chords.[^cite_note-56] Singers [Bob Dylan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Dylan) and [Joan Baez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Baez) lived in the neighbouring eponymous hotel and her lyrics in "[Diamonds & Rust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamonds_&_Rust_\(song\))" state "Now you're smiling out the window of that crummy hotel over Washington Square."

On September 27, 2007, [Democratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Democratic_Party) Presidential candidate [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) held a rally at Washington Square; 20,000 people registered for the event, and the crowds overflowed past security gates set up as a cordon. *[The New York Times](30680.md)* described the rally "as one of the largest campaign events of the year."[^cite_note-57]

### On television and film
