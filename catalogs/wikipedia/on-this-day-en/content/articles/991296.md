The **culture of Ireland** includes the [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_art), [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Ireland), [dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_dance), [folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Mythology), [religious practices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Ireland), [theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_theatre), [cinema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_Ireland), [traditional clothing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_clothing), [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language), [literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_literature), [cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_cuisine), and [sport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_in_Ireland) associated with [Ireland](147575.md) and the [Irish](775859.md). For most of its recorded history, Irish culture was primarily [Gaelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaels) (see [Gaelic Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_Ireland)). Strong [family values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_values), [wit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wit), and an appreciation for [tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tradition) are commonly associated with Irish culture.

Irish culture has been greatly influenced by [Christianity](5211.md), most notably by the [Roman Catholic Church](606848.md), and [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland) plays a role in the lives of many Irish people.[^cite_note-2] Today, there are often notable cultural differences between those of [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholicism_in_Ireland), [Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Ireland), and [Presbyterian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian_Church_in_Ireland) background. References to God can be found in spoken Irish, notably exemplified by the Irish equivalent of “Hello” — “Dia dhuit,” ([literally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation): "God be with you") and the traditional reply, "Dia is Mhuire dhuit" ([literally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation): "God and [Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_Mary) be with you").[^cite_note-3]

Irish culture has [Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celts), [Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Denmark), [Norwegian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Norway), [Swedish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Sweden), [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_France), and [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Spain)[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6] influences. It also has [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain) influences, primarily due to over eight centuries of [British rule in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_rule_in_Ireland), which suppressed numerous aspects of Irish culture.[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9] The [Vikings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vikings) first [invaded Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Ireland_\(795%E2%80%931169\)) in the 8th century, from [Denmark](76972.md), [Norway](21241.md) and [Sweden](5058739.md) in modern-day [Scandinavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scandinavia). They had a significant influence on Ireland’s [material culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Material_culture) at the time.[^cite_note-10] The [Normans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambro-Normans) [invaded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Norman_invasion_of_Ireland) Ireland in the 12th century, bringing British and French influences. Additionally, [Irish Travellers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Travellers) ([Shelta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelta): *Mincéirí*) have had some influence on the broader cultural tapestry of Ireland, introducing [nomadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomad) traditions and other cultural practices. In recent decades, Ireland has also to some degree been influenced by migration from [Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe).[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13]

Due to large-scale emigration from Ireland, Irish culture has a wide reach in the world, and festivals such as [Saint Patrick's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Patrick's_Day) ([Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language): *Lá Fhéile Pádraig*) and [Halloween](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halloween) (which finds its roots in the Gaelic festival [Samhain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhain)) are celebrated across much of the globe.[^cite_note-14] Irish culture has to some extent been inherited and modified by the [Irish diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_diaspora), which in turn has influenced the home country. Moreover, the culture of Ireland is to some degree influenced by its native folklore and legends, such as those detailed in *[Lebor Gabála Érenn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebor_Gab%C3%A1la_%C3%89renn)*.[^cite_note-15]

## Farming and rural tradition

As [archaeological](18951655.md) evidence from sites such as the [Céide Fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C3%A9ide_Fields) in County Mayo and [Lough Gur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lough_Gur) in County Limerick demonstrates, the farm in Ireland is an activity that goes back to the [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistoric_Ireland), about 6,000 years ago. Before this, the first settlers of the island of Ireland after the last Ice Age were a new wave of cavemen and the [Mesolithic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Mesolithic).[^cite_note-16] In historic times, texts such as the [Táin Bó Cúailinge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%A1in_B%C3%B3_C%C3%BAailinge) show a society in which cows represented a primary source of wealth and status. Little of this had changed by the time of the [Norman invasion of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_invasion_of_Ireland) in the 12th century. [Giraldus Cambrensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giraldus_Cambrensis) portrayed a Gaelic society in which cattle farming and [transhumance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transhumance) was the norm.

### Townlands, villages, parishes and counties

The Normans replaced traditional clan land management (under Brehon Law) with the [manorial system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manorialism) of [land tenure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_tenure) and social organisation. This led to the imposition of the village, parish and [county](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counties_of_Ireland) over the native system of [townlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Townlands). In general, a parish was a civil and religious unit with a manor, a village and a church at its centre. Each parish incorporated one or more existing townlands into its boundaries. With the gradual extension of English [feudalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feudalism) over the island, the Irish county structure came into existence and was completed in 1605.[^cite_note-17] These structures are still of vital importance in the daily life of Irish communities. Apart from the religious significance of the parish, most rural postal addresses consist of house and townland names. The village and parish are key focal points around which sporting rivalries and other forms of local identity are built and most people feel a strong sense of loyalty to their native county, a loyalty which also often has its clearest expression on the sports field.

### Land ownership and "land hunger"



Traditional Irish cottage in [County Antrim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Antrim)

With the Tudor [Elizabethan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabethan) English [conquest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_conquest_of_Ireland) in the 16th-17th centuries, the [Cromwellian conquest of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cromwellian_conquest_of_Ireland), and the organized [plantations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantations_of_Ireland) of English Tudor, and later Scottish, colonists, the Scottish confined to what's now mostly Northern Ireland, the patterns of land ownership in Ireland were altered greatly. The old order of transhumance and open range cattle breeding died out to be replaced by a structure of great landed estates, small tenant farmers with more or less precarious hold on their leases, and a mass of landless labourers. This situation continued up to the end of the 19th century, when the agitation of the [Land League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_National_Land_League) began to bring about land reform. In this process of reform, the former tenants and labourers became land owners, with the great estates being broken up into small- and medium-sized farms and smallholdings. The process continued well into the 20th century with the work of the [Irish Land Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Land_Commission). This contrasted with Britain, where many of the big estates were left intact. One consequence of this is the widely recognised cultural phenomenon of "land hunger" amongst the new class of Irish farmer. In general, this means that farming families will do almost anything to retain land ownership within the family unit, with the greatest ambition possible being the acquisition of additional land. Another is that [hillwalkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillwalking) in Ireland today are more constrained than their counterparts in Britain, as it is more difficult to agree rights of way with so many small farmers involved on a given route, rather than with just one landowner.

### Irish Travellers

[Irish Travellers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Travellers) ([Shelta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelta): *Mincéirí*) are known for their historically nomadic lifestyle; residing in ornamented barrel top wagons, they would traverse predominantly rural areas of the island. Their propensity for rural living was influenced by a variety of factors including cultural traditions, a desire for privacy and autonomy, work opportunities and their fondness of the natural world. Travellers would often find work in rural areas, predominantly in farming, horse trading and tinsmithing. While many Mincéirí in contemporary Ireland are now settled, including in urban areas, they often maintain rural traditions such as horseback riding, and attend traditional fairs and festivals in the countryside.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19]

## Holidays and festivals

Main articles: [Gaelic calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_calendar) and [Public holidays in the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_holidays_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland)



Buí Bolg at the [Saint Patrick's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Patrick's_Day) parade in [Dublin](8504.md)

[Christmas](6237.md) in Ireland has several local traditions. [Nativity scenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nativity_Scene), known in Ireland as "cribs", are common in some Irish homes, towns, shopping centres and schools at Christmastime. On 26 December ([Saint Stephen's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Stephen's_Day)), there is a custom of "[Wrenboys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrenboys)"[^cite_note-20] who call door to door with an arrangement of assorted material (which changes in different localities) to represent a dead [wren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wren) "caught in the furze", as their rhyme goes.

The national holiday in the Republic of Ireland and Northern Ireland is [Saint Patrick's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Patrick's_Day), that falls on the date 17 March and is marked by parades and festivals in cities and towns across the island of Ireland, and by the Irish diaspora around the world. The festival is in remembrance of [Saint Patrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Patrick), the most significant of Ireland's three patron saints. Pious legend credits Patrick with the banishing of the snakes (representing pagan deities/demons) from the island, and the legend also credits Patrick with teaching the Irish about the concept of the [Trinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity) by showing people the [shamrock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamrock), a 3-leaved clover, using it to highlight the Christian [belief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief) of 'three divine persons in the one God'.

In [Northern Ireland](21265.md) [The Twelfth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Twelfth) of July, or Orangemen's Day, commemorates [William III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_III_of_England)'s victory at the [Battle of the Boyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Boyne). A [public holiday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_holidays_in_the_United_Kingdom#Dates_in_England,_Northern_Ireland,_and_Wales), it is celebrated by Irish Protestants, in particular [Ulster Protestants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Protestants), the vast majority of whom live in Northern Ireland. It is notable for the numerous [parades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_walk) organised by the [Orange Order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_Order) which take place throughout Northern Ireland. These parades are colourful affairs with [Orange Banners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banners_in_Northern_Ireland) and sashes on display and include music in the form of traditional songs such as "[The Sash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sash)" and "[Derry's Walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry's_Walls)" performed by a mixture of pipe, flute, accordion, and brass marching bands. The Twelfth remains controversial as many in Northern Ireland's large and majority-[nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_nationalism) Catholic community see the holiday, celebrating a victory over Catholics that ensured the continued establishment of a [Protestant Ascendancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Ascendancy), as triumphalist, supremacist, and an assertion of British and Ulster Protestant dominance.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23][^cite_note-irishcentral-24][^cite_note-Roe-25][^cite_note-Wilson-26]

The 1st of February, known as [St. Brigid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigid_of_Ireland)'s Day (after St. Brigid, one of the patron saints of Ireland) or [Imbolc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imbolc), also does not have its origins in Christianity, being instead another religious observance superimposed at the beginning of spring. St. Brigid’s Day is the only official public holiday named after a woman in Ireland.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28]

Other pre-Christian festivals, whose names survive as Irish month names, are [Bealtaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bealtaine) (May), [Lúnasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lughnasadh) (August) and [Samhain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhain) (November). The last is still widely observed as [Halloween](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halloween) which is celebrated all over the world, including in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_culture_in_the_United_States) followed by [All Saints' Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Saints'_Day), another Christian holiday associated with a traditional one. Important Christian holidays include [Christmas](6237.md), [Easter](9325.md), [St. Patrick's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Patrick's_Day), [St. Brigid's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Brigid's_Day) and various [Marian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marian_devotion) observances, particularly within the month of May.

## Religion

Main article: [Religion in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Ireland)



[Celtic cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_cross) in the [Irish National Heritage Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_National_Heritage_Park)



11th century [cumdach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumdach) of the [Stowe Missal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stowe_Missal)

Christianity was brought to Ireland during or prior to the 5th century[^cite_note-29] and its early history among the Irish is in particular associated with [Saint Patrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Patrick), who is generally considered Ireland's leading [patron saint](68055.md).[^cite_note-30] The Celtic festival of *[Samhain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhain)*, not to be confused with [Halloween](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halloween), originated in Ireland and a reconstructed version is celebrated by some across the globe.[^cite_note-rogers_s-31]

Ireland is a place where religion and religious practice have long been held in high esteem. The majority of people on the island are [Roman Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholics);[^cite_note-32] however, there are significant [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism) and rather smaller [Orthodox](10186.md) minorities. Protestants are mostly concentrated in [Northern Ireland](21265.md), where they long made up a [plurality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_Northern_Ireland) of the population.[^cite_note-NI_census_21-33] The three main Protestant denominations on the island are the Anglican [Church of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Ireland), the [Presbyterian Church in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian_Church_in_Ireland) and the [Methodist Church in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodist_Church_in_Ireland). These are also joined by numerous smaller denominations including [Baptists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptists), several American gospel groups, and the [Salvation Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvation_Army). [Orthodox Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy) also has a notable presence in Ireland, where it has been the fastest growing religion since 1991, largely due to immigration from Eastern Europe.[^cite_note-34] Other minority denominations of Christianity include [Jehovah's Witnesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jehovah's_Witnesses) and [The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints (LDS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Church_of_Jesus_Christ_of_Latter-day_Saints). In addition to the Christian denominations there are centres for [Buddhists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhists), [Hindus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindus), [Baháʼís](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_Faith), [Pagans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-paganism) and for people of the [Islamic](6037917.md) and [Jewish](25955086.md) faiths.

In some Irish homes, especially in rural areas, the [Sacred Heart lamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_Heart_lamp) and [Infant of Prague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant_of_Prague) statue are common features. [Marian devotion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marian_devotion) is also reflected in roadside or village shrines and grottoes dedicated to the Virgin Mary. Baptisms, funerals and weddings are usually conducted by a priest of the local parish. Many larger towns have convents or friaries, priories or monasteries, and members of religious orders historically ran the local secondary schools and hospitals. A common tradition among Irish Catholics is to make the [sign of the cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_of_the_cross) when passing a church or graveyard. Prayers for the intercession of saints are common, with [Anthony of Padua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_of_Padua), [St. Jude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jude_Thaddeus_the_Apostle), [Therese of Lisieux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therese_of_Lisieux), [Padre Pio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padre_Pio) and [St. Brigid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Brigid_of_Ireland) being popular saints in Ireland.

In the 2021 census, of those in the [Republic of Ireland](14560.md) that stated their religious identity, 81.6% identified as [Christian](18337522.md); 68.8% as Roman Catholic, 4.2% as Protestant, 2.1% as Orthodox, 0.7% as other Christians, while 1.6% identified as [Muslim](19541.md), 0.7% as [Hindu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindus), 14.8% as having no religion and 7.1% not stating their religious identity.[^cite_note-NI_census_21-33] Amongst the Republic of Ireland's Roman Catholics, weekly church attendance has declined sharply over the past few decades, from 87% in 1981, to 60% in 1998, to 30% in 2021.[^cite_note-35] Still, this remains one of the higher attendance rates in Europe.[^cite_note-36] The decline is said to be linked to reports of [Catholic Church sexual abuse cases in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_sexual_abuse_cases_in_Ireland).

## Mythology and folklore

Main articles: [Irish mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_mythology) and [Irish folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_folklore)



The 'Land of the Ever Young', an illustration of the [Celtic Otherworld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Otherworld) by Arthur Rackham

Highly respected in Ireland historically were the stories of heroes such as [Fionn mac Cumhaill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fionn_mac_Cumhaill) and his followers, the [Fianna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fianna), from the [Fenian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fenian_cycle), and [Cuchulain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuchulain) from the [Ulster Cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Cycle), along with some of the High Kings. Legend has it that Fionn mac Cumhaill built the [Giant's Causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant's_Causeway) as a line of stepping-stones to Scotland, not to get his feet wet while seeking to fight an ugly Scottish giant. He also is said to have once scooped up part of Ireland to fling it at a rival, but it missed and landed in the [Irish Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Sea) – the clump becoming the [Isle of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_of_Man), a pebble [Rockall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockall), and the void left behind filling as [Lough Neagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lough_Neagh). The Irish king [Brian Boru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Boru) who ended the domination of the so-called [High Kingship of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Kingship_of_Ireland) by the [Uí Néill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U%C3%AD_N%C3%A9ill), is part of the historical cycle. The Irish princess [Iseult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iseult) is the adulterous lover of [Tristan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tristan) in the [Arthurian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthurian) romance and tragedy [Tristan and Iseult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tristan_and_Iseult). The many legends of ancient Ireland were captured by many collators, including [Lady Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Gregory) in two volumes with forewords by [W. B. Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._B._Yeats). These stories depict a status for Celtic women in ancient times somewhat different from many other cultures of the period.

According to the tales, the [leprechaun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leprechaun) is a mischievous fairy type creature in emerald green clothing who when not playing tricks spend all their time busily making shoes; he is said to have a [pot of gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainbow#Culture_and_mythology) hidden at the end of the [rainbow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainbow) and if ever captured by a human has the magical power to grant three wishes in exchange for release.[^cite_note-37] There are also tales of the [pooka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pooka) and [banshees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banshees), among others.

A plaster cast of a traditional Samhain turnip lantern

[Halloween](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halloween) is a traditional and much celebrated holiday in Ireland on the night of 31 October.[^cite_note-AHP-38] Supposedly the first evidence of the word halloween is found in a 16th-century Scottish document as a shortening for *All-Hallows-Eve*,[^cite_note-OED-39] and according to some historians it has its roots in the [gaelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaels) festival [Samhain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhain), where the Gaels believed the border between this world and the [otherworld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otherworld) became thin, and the dead would revisit the mortal world.[^cite_note-40] In Ireland, traditional Halloween customs include; [Guising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guising) – children disguised in [costume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halloween_costume) going from door to door requesting food or coins – which became practice by the late 19th century,[^cite_note-FRA-41][^cite_note-42] [turnips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnips) hollowed-out and carved with faces to make lanterns,[^cite_note-FRA-41] holding parties where games such as [apple bobbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_bobbing) are played.[^cite_note-43] Other practices in Ireland include lighting [bonfires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonfires), and having [firework](59493.md#Halloween) displays.[^cite_note-44] Mass transatlantic Irish and Scottish immigration in the 19th century popularised Halloween in the United States and Canada.[^cite_note-45]

## Literature and the arts

Main articles: [Irish art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_art), [Irish literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_literature), [Irish poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_poetry), [Irish prose fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_prose_fiction), [Irish theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_theatre), [Irish mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_mythology), [Modern literature in Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_literature_in_Irish), [Music of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Ireland), and [Irish dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_dance)



Folk music and theatre performers [Armagh Rhymers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Armagh_Rhymers) at Aonach Mhacha in [County Armagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Armagh) (2023)

The island of Ireland has made a large contribution to world literature in various branches, in both the Irish and English languages. The island's most widely known literary works are in English, including works by [James Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce), [Bram Stoker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bram_Stoker), [Jonathan Swift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Swift), [Oscar Wilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Wilde) and Ireland's four winners of the [Nobel Prize for Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Literature); [William Butler Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Butler_Yeats), [George Bernard Shaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Bernard_Shaw), [Samuel Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett) and [Seamus Heaney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seamus_Heaney). Three of these four Nobel prize winners were born in Dublin, making it the home of more Nobel literary laureates than any other city globally.[^cite_note-46] The Irish language has the third oldest literature in Europe (after [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) and [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin)), the most significant body of written literature (both ancient and recent) of any [Celtic language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_languages), as well as a strong oral tradition of legends and poetry. Poetry in Irish represents the oldest [vernacular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernacular_literature) poetry in Europe, with the earliest examples dating from the 6th century.

The early history of Irish visual art is generally considered to begin with early carvings found at sites such as [Newgrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newgrange) and is traced through [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age) artefacts, particularly ornamental gold objects, and the [Celtic brooches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_brooch) and [illuminated manuscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illuminated_manuscripts) of the ["Insular"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insular_art) early medieval period. During the course of the 19th and 20th centuries, a strong indigenous tradition of painting emerged, including such figures as [John Butler Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Butler_Yeats), [William Orpen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Orpen), [Jack Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Yeats) and [Louis le Brocquy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_le_Brocquy).



Handcrafted bowl and pitchers by Nicholas Mosse Pottery, founded in 1976



[Irish traditional music sessions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_traditional_music_session) often take place in public houses

The Irish tradition of [folk music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_music) and dance is also widely known, and both were redefined in the 1950s. In the middle years of the 20th century, as Irish society was attempting to modernise, traditional [Irish music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_music_of_Ireland) fell out of favour to some extent, especially in urban areas. Young people at this time tended to look to Britain and, particularly, the United States as models of progress and [jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz) and [rock and roll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_and_roll) became extremely popular. During the 1960s, and inspired by the American folk music movement, there was a revival of interest in the Irish tradition. This revival was inspired by groups like [The Dubliners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dubliners), the [Clancy Brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clancy_Brothers) and [Sweeney's Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweeney's_Men) and individuals like [Seán Ó Riada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Se%C3%A1n_%C3%93_Riada). The annual [Fleadh Cheoil na hÉireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleadh_Cheoil_na_h%C3%89ireann) is the largest festival of Irish music in Ireland. Groups and musicians like [Horslips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horslips), [Van Morrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Morrison) and even [Thin Lizzy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin_Lizzy) incorporated elements of traditional music into a rock idiom to form a unique new sound. During the 1970s and 1980s, the distinction between traditional and rock and pop musicians became blurred, with many individuals regularly crossing over between these styles of playing as a matter of course. This trend can be seen more recently in the work of bands like [U2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U2), [Snow Patrol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snow_Patrol), [The Cranberries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cranberries), [The Undertones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Undertones) and [The Corrs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Corrs), and individual artists such as [Enya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enya).

## Languages

Main article: [Languages of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Ireland)



An Irish-language information sign in the [Donegal Gaeltacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaeltacht#Donegal_Gaeltacht)

Irish and English are the most widely spoken languages in Ireland. English is the most widely spoken language on the island overall, and Irish is spoken as a first language only by a small minority, primarily, though not exclusively, in the government-defined [Gaeltacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaeltacht) regions in the Republic. A larger minority have Irish as a second language, with 40.6% of people in the [Republic of Ireland](14560.md) claiming some ability to speak the language in the 2011 census.[^cite_note-47] Article 8 of the [Constitution of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Ireland) states that Irish is the *national* and *first official* language of the Republic of Ireland.[^cite_note-constitution-48] English in turn is recognised as the State's *second official language*.[^cite_note-constitution-48] [Hiberno-English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiberno-English), the dialect of English spoken in most of the Republic of Ireland, has been greatly influenced by Irish.[^cite_note-49]

In [Northern Ireland](21265.md), English is a *[de facto](43594.md)* official language, with [Ulster English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_English) being common. In 2022, the Irish language received official language status in Northern Ireland as a result of the [Identity and Language (Northern Ireland) Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_and_Language_\(Northern_Ireland\)_Act_2022), with [Ulster Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Scots_dialect) receiving minority language status.[^cite_note-50] According to the 2021 census, 12.4% of the population could speak some amount of Irish, and 10.4% were able to speak some amount of Ulster Scots.[^cite_note-51] In addition, the [dialect and accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-Ulster_English) of the people of Northern Ireland is noticeably different from that of the majority in the Republic of Ireland, being influenced by Ulster Scots and Northern Ireland's proximity to Scotland.

Several other languages are spoken on the island, including [Shelta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelta), a mixture of Irish, [Romani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romani_language) and English, spoken widely by [Travellers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Travellers). Two sign languages have also been developed on the island, [Northern Irish Sign Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Sign_Language) and [Irish Sign Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Sign_Language); they have quite different bases.

Some other languages have entered Ireland with immigrants – for example, [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_language) is now the second most widely spoken language in Ireland after English, Irish being the third most commonly spoken language.[^cite_note-52]

## Food and drink

Main article: [Irish cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_cuisine)

### Pre-Medieval Ireland



Great Hall Kitchen in the [Carraig Phádraig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_of_Cashel)

There are many references to food and drink in early Irish literature. [Honey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honey) seems to have been widely eaten and used in the making of [mead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mead). The old stories also contain many references to banquets, although these may well be greatly exaggerated and provide little insight into everyday diet. There are also many references to *[fulacht fia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulacht_fiadh)*, which are archaeological sites commonly believed to have once been used for cooking [venison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venison). The *fulacht fia* have holes or troughs in the ground which can be filled with water. Meat can then be cooked by placing hot stones in the trough until the water boils. Many *fulach fia* sites have been identified across the island of Ireland, and some of them appear to have been in use up to the 17th century.

Excavations at the [Viking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking) settlement in the [Wood Quay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_Quay) area of Dublin have produced a significant amount of information on the diet of the inhabitants of the town. The main animals eaten were cattle, sheep and pigs, with pigs being the most common. This popularity extended down to modern times in Ireland. Poultry and wild geese as well as fish and shellfish were also common, as were a wide range of native berries and nuts, especially [hazel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazel). The seeds of [knotgrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygonum) and [goosefoot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goosefoot) were widely present and may have been used to make a [porridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porridge).

### Early-modern Ireland



Three men digging for potatoes in [Ahascragh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahascragh), County Galway (Circa 1900)

The [Tudor conquest of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_conquest_of_Ireland) led to significant changes in the Irish diet, as it introduced a new agro-alimentary system of intensive grain-based agriculture and led to large areas of land being turned over to grain production. The potato was introduced into Ireland in the second half of the 16th century, as a result of the [Columbian exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbian_exchange), initially as a garden crop; it eventually came to serve as the main food field crop of the tenant and labouring classes, which formed a majority of the population. Ireland also grew large quantities of [corned beef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corned_beef), though the vast majority of it was exported.[^cite_note-53] The over-reliance on potatoes as a staple crop in Irish cuisine meant that the people of Ireland were vulnerable to poor potato harvests. The [Irish Famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Famine_\(1740%E2%80%931741\)) of 1740 was the result of extreme cold weather, but the [Great Famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_\(Ireland\)) of 1845–1849 was caused by [potato blight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato_blight) which spread throughout the Irish crop which consisted largely of a single variety, the [Lumper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Lumper). During the famine, approximately one million people died and a million more emigrated elsewhere.[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55]

### Modern Ireland



An [Irish coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_coffee)&#x20;

In the 21st century, the modern selection of foods common to Western cultures has also to some extent been adopted in Ireland. Both US [fast food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_food) culture and continental European dishes have influenced the country, along with other world dishes introduced in a similar fashion to the Western world. In addition to Irish food, meals eaten in Ireland may include pizza, curry and Chinese food, with some west African dishes making an appearance lately. Supermarket shelves now contain ingredients for, among others, traditional, Polish, Mexican, Indian and Chinese dishes.

The proliferation of fast food has led to increasing public health problems including [obesity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obesity), and one of the highest rates of [heart disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_disease) in the world.[^cite_note-56] In Ireland, the [Full Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_breakfast#Full_Irish) has been particularly cited as being a major source for a higher incidence of [cardiac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiac) problems, quoted as being a *"heart attack on a plate"*. All the ingredients are [fried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frying), although more recently the trend is to [grill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grilling) as many of the ingredients as possible.

In tandem with these developments, the last quarter of the century saw the emergence of a new Irish cuisine based on traditional ingredients handled in new ways. This cuisine is based on fresh vegetables, fish, especially [salmon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmon) and [trout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trout), [oysters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oyster) and other shellfish, traditional [soda bread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soda_bread), the wide range of hand-made cheeses that are now being made across the country, and, of course, the potato. Traditional dishes, such as the [Irish stew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_stew), Dublin [coddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coddle), the [Irish breakfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_breakfast) and [potato bread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato_bread), have enjoyed a resurgence. Schools like the [Ballymaloe Cookery School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballymaloe_Cookery_School) have emerged to cater for the associated increased interest in cooking with traditional ingredients.

## Pub culture



A typical Irish pub in [County Donegal](45576.md)

"Pub culture" refers to the habit amongst Irish people of frequenting [public houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_house) (pubs). It extends beyond mere alcohol consumption (upon which it is not dependent), encompassing a variety of social traditions. Pubs vary widely according to the clientele they serve, and the area they are in. Best known, and loved amongst tourists is the traditional pub, with its [traditional Irish music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_music_of_Ireland) (or "trad music") often performed live, tavern-like warmness, memorabilia and traditional Irish ornamentation. Often such pubs will also serve Irish food, particularly during the day. Typically pubs are important meeting places, where people can gather and meet their neighbours and friends in a relaxed atmosphere; similar to the café cultures of other countries. Many modern pubs, not necessarily traditional, still emulate traditional pubs, only perhaps substituting traditional music for a DJ or non-traditional live music.

Many larger pubs in cities eschew such trappings entirely, opting for loud music, and focusing more on the consumption of drinks, which is not a focus of traditional Irish culture. Such venues are popular "pre-clubbing" locations. "[Clubbing](19321330.md)" has become a popular phenomenon amongst young people in Ireland during the [celtic tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_tiger) years. Clubs usually vary in terms of the type of music played, and the target audience. Belfast has a unique underground club scene taking place in settings such as churches, zoos, and crematoriums. The underground scene is mainly orchestrated by DJ [Christopher McCafferty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_McCafferty).[^cite_note-57] [^cite_note-58]

A significant recent change to pub culture in the Republic of Ireland has been the introduction of a [smoking ban](386818.md), in all workplaces, which includes pubs and restaurants. Ireland was the first country in the world to implement such a ban which was introduced on 29 March 2004.[^cite_note-59] A majority of the population support the ban, including a significant percentage of smokers. Nevertheless, the atmosphere in pubs has changed greatly as a result, and debate continues on whether it has boosted or lowered sales, although this is often blamed on the ever-increasing prices, or whether it is a "good thing" or a "bad thing". A similar ban, under the Smoking (Northern Ireland) Order 2006, came into effect in Northern Ireland on 30 April 2007.[^cite_note-60]

National and international organisations have labelled Ireland as having a problem with over-consumption of alcohol. In the late 1980s alcohol consumption accounted for nearly 25% of all hospital admissions. While this figure has been decreasing steadily, as of 2007, approximately 13% of overall hospital admissions were alcohol related.[^cite_note-61] In 2003, Ireland had the second-highest per capita alcohol consumption in the world, just below Luxembourg at 13.5 litres (per person 15 or more years old), according to the *[OECD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organisation_for_Economic_Co-operation_and_Development) Health Data 2009* survey.[^cite_note-62] According to the latest OECD figures, alcohol consumption in Ireland has dropped from 11.5 litres per adult in 2012 to 10.6 litres per adult in 2013. However, research showed that in 2013, 75% of alcohol was consumed as part of a drinking session where the person drank six or more standard units (which equates to three or more pints of beer). This meets the [Health Service Executive](4017657.md)'s definition of binge drinking.[^cite_note-63]

## Sport

Main article: [Sport in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_in_Ireland)



Gaelic football



Hurling ball (sliotar) and hurley (camán)

Sport on the island of Ireland is popular and widespread. A wide variety of sports are played throughout the island, with the most popular being [Gaelic football](37688.md), [hurling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurling), [soccer](10568.md), [rugby union](25405.md), and [golf](19568112.md). Four sports account for over 80% of event attendance: [Gaelic football](37688.md) is the most popular sport in Ireland in terms of match attendance and community involvement, and represents 34% of total sports attendances at events in the Republic of Ireland and abroad, followed by [hurling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurling) at 23%, soccer at 16% and rugby at 8%.[^cite_note-esri-64][^cite_note-65] and the [All-Ireland Football Final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-Ireland_Football_Final) is the most watched event in Ireland's sporting calendar.[^cite_note-66]

Swimming, golf, aerobics, soccer, cycling, Gaelic football, and billiards, pool and snooker, are the sporting activities with the highest levels of playing participation.[^cite_note-67] Other sports with material playing populations, including at school level, include [tennis](29773.md), [hockey](10886.md), [pitch and putt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_and_putt), rugby, basketball, boxing, [cricket](25675557.md) and [squash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squash_\(sport\)). Significant numbers attend horse racing meetings, and Ireland breeds and trains many racehorses; greyhound racing also has dedicated racecourses.

Soccer is the most popular sport involving national teams. The success of the Ireland team at the [1990 FIFA World Cup](157232.md) saw 500,000 fans in Dublin to welcome the team home.[^cite_note-68] The team's song "[Put 'Em Under Pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Put_'Em_Under_Pressure)" topped the Irish charts for 13 weeks.[^cite_note-69]

In Ireland most sports, including rugby union, Gaelic football, hurling and handball, cycling and golf, are organised on an all-island basis, with, where relevant, a single team representing the island of Ireland in international competitions. A few sports, such as soccer, have separate organising bodies in [Northern Ireland](21265.md) and the [Republic of Ireland](14560.md). Traditionally, those in the North who identify as Irish, predominantly Catholics and nationalists, support the Republic of Ireland team.[^cite_note-70] At the Olympics, a person from Northern Ireland can choose to represent either the *Great Britain* team or the *Ireland* team. Also as Northern Ireland is a [Home Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Nations) of the United Kingdom it also sends a [Northern Ireland Team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_at_the_Commonwealth_Games) to the [Commonwealth Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_Games) every four years.

## Media

### Print

Further information: [List of newspapers in the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_newspapers_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland)





Two types of traditional Irish boats, a [currach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currach) (above) and a [Galway hooker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galway_hooker) (below)

In the Republic of Ireland there are several daily newspapers: *[The Irish Independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Independent)*, *[The Irish Examiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Irish_Examiner)*, *[The Irish Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Irish_Times), [The Irish Daily Mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Daily_Mail),* *[The Irish Daily Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Daily_Star), [The Irish Mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Irish_Mirror),* *[The Evening Herald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Evening_Herald)* and *[The Irish Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Sun).* The best selling of these is the Irish Independen&#x74;*. The Irish Times* is Ireland's [newspaper of record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newspaper_of_record). There are also several news websites including [The Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TheJournal.ie), [RTÉ News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_News), BreakingNews.ie and [Extra.ie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extra.ie).

The leading Sunday newspaper in terms of circulation is The *[Sunday Independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunday_Independent_\(Ireland\))*. Other popular papers includ*e [The Sunday Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sunday_Times_\(UK\)) (Ireland Edition), [The Business Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sunday_Business_Post), [The Irish Mail on Sunday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Irish_Mail_on_Sunday)* and the *[Sunday World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunday_World).* Many of these are Irish versions of British newspapers.&#x20;

In [Northern Ireland](21265.md) the three main daily newspapers are [The News Letter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_News_Letter), which is [Unionist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unionism_in_Ireland) in outlook, [The Irish News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Irish_News), mainly [Nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_nationalism) in outlook and the [Belfast Telegraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belfast_Telegraph). Also widely available are the Northern Irish versions of the main UK wide daily newspapers and some Scottish dailies such as the [Daily Record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daily_Record_\(Scotland\)).

In terms of Sunday papers the Belfast Telegraph is the only one of the three main Northern Irish dailies that has a Sunday publication which is called the Sunday Life. Apart from this all the main UK wide Sunday papers such as [The Sun on Sunday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sun_on_Sunday) are widely available as are some Irish papers such as the Sunday world.

There are quite a large number of local weekly newspapers both North and South, with most counties and large towns having two or more newspapers. Curiously Dublin remains one of the few places in Ireland without a major local paper since the *[Dublin Evening Mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Evening_Mail)* closed down in the 1960s. In 2004 the *[Dublin Daily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Daily?action=edit\&redlink=1)* was launched, but failed to attract enough readers to make it viable. The Dublin Inquirer newspaper publishes a monthly paper and more regularly updates its website. There are also two weekly papers, the [Northside People](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northside_People) and [Southside People](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southside_People) that cover local topics in the capital.

The Irish magazine market is one of the world's most competitive, with hundreds of international magazines available in Ireland, ranging from *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_\(magazine\))* and *[The Economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Economist)* to *[Hello!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hello!_\(magazine\))* and *[Reader's Digest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reader's_Digest)*. This means that domestic titles find it very hard to retain readership. Among the best-selling Irish magazines are the *[RTÉ Guide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_Guide)*, *[Ireland's Eye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland's_Eye)*, *Irish Tatler*, *VIP*, *Phoenix* and *[In Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_Dublin_\(magazine\))*.

### Radio

The first known radio transmission in Ireland was a call to arms made from the [General Post Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Post_Office_\(Dublin\)) in O'Connell Street during the [Easter Rising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Rising). The first official radio station on the island was [2BE Belfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2BE_Belfast), which began broadcasting in 1924. This was followed in 1926 by [2RN Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2RN_Dublin?action=edit\&redlink=1) and [6CK Cork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/6CK_Cork?action=edit\&redlink=1) in 1927. 2BE Belfast later became [BBC Radio Ulster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_Ulster) and 2RN Dublin became [RTÉ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89). RTÉ have several stations including [RTÉ Radio 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_Radio_1) and [2fm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_2fm). The first commercial radio station in the Republic, [Century Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Century_Radio), came on air in 1989. The two main commercial stations in Ireland are [Newstalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newstalk) and [Today FM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Today_FM).

During the 1990s and particularly the early 2000s, dozens of local radio stations have gained licenses. This has resulted in a fragmentation of the radio broadcast market. This trend is most noticeable in Dublin where there are now 6 private licensed stations in operation.

### Television

Further information: [List of television channels available in the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_television_channels_available_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland)

Different television stations are available depending on location in Northern Ireland or the Republic of Ireland. In Northern Ireland the main terrestrial television stations are the main UK wide channels [BBC One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_One), [BBC Two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Two), [ITV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITV_\(TV_network\)), [Channel 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_4) and [Channel 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_5_\(UK\)). Both the BBC and ITV have local regional programing specific to Northern Ireland produced and broadcast through [BBC Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Northern_Ireland) and [UTV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UTV_\(TV_channel\)).

In terms of Satellite-carried channels in Northern Ireland these are the same as for the rest of the United Kingdom including all [Sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BSkyB) channels.

In the Republic of Ireland some areas first received signal from [BBC Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Wales) and then latter from [BBC Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Northern_Ireland) when it began broadcasting television programmes in 1959 before RTÉ Television opened in 1961. Today the Republic's main terrestrial channels are [RTÉ One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_One), [RTÉ Two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_Two), [TV3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TV3_\(Ireland\)) which began broadcasting in 1998 and [Teilifís na Gaeilge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teilif%C3%ADs_na_Gaeilge) (TnaG), now called [TG4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TG4) which started its Irish language service in 1996.

British and satellite-carried international television channels have widespread audiences in the Republic. The [BBC](19344654.md) and [ITV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITV_\(TV_network\)) families of channels are available free to air across the Republic and there is widespread availability of the four main UK channels (BBC1, BBC2, ITV1 and Channel Four) but only limited coverage from [Five](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_\(channel\)). [Sky One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_One), [E4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E4_\(channel\)), and several hundred satellite channels are widely available. Parts of the Republic can access the UK digital TV system *Freeview*.
