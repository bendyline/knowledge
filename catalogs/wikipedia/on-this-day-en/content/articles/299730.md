The **CONMEBOL Libertadores**, also known as the **Copa Libertadores de América** ([Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language): *Copa/Taça Libertadores da América*), and most commonly shortened to **Copa Libertadores**, is an annual continental club [football](10568.md) competition organized by [CONMEBOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CONMEBOL) since 1960. It is the highest level of competition in South American club football. The tournament is named after the *[Libertadores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libertadores)* (Spanish and Portuguese for "liberators"), the leaders of the [Spanish American wars of independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_American_wars_of_independence) and [Brazilian independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_of_Brazil),[^cite_note-Inicio-1] so a literal translation of its former name into English is "Liberators Cup".

The competition has had several formats over its lifetime. Initially, only the champions of the South American leagues participated. In 1966, the runners-up of the South American leagues began to join. In 1998, Mexican teams were invited to compete and competed regularly from 2000 until 2016. In 2000, the tournament was expanded from 20 to 32 teams. Today at least four clubs per country compete in the tournament, with [Argentina](18951905.md) and [Brazil](3383.md) having the most representatives (six and seven clubs, respectively). A group stage has always been used but the number of teams per group has varied.[^cite_note-Inicio-1][^cite_note-México-2]

In the present format, the tournament consists of eight stages, with the first stage taking place in late January. The four surviving teams from the first three stages join 28 teams in the group stage, which consists of eight groups of four teams each. The eight group winners and eight runners-up enter the knockout stages, which end with the final in November. The winner of the Copa Libertadores becomes eligible to play in the [FIFA Club World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FIFA_Club_World_Cup), [FIFA Intercontinental Cup](73290908.md) and the [Recopa Sudamericana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recopa_Sudamericana).[^cite_note-Reglamento-3]

[Independiente](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Club_Atl%C3%A9tico_Independiente) of Argentina is the most successful club in the cup's history, having won the tournament seven times. Argentine and Brazilian clubs have accumulated the most victories with 25 wins each, while Brazil has the largest number of winning teams, with 12 clubs winning the title. The cup has been won by 27 clubs; 15 of them have won it more than once, and seven clubs have won two years in a row. Brazilian clubs currently hold a record-breaking winning streak (seven Copa Libertadores in a row, starting in 2019).

The clashes for the [Copa Aldao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copa_Aldao) between the champions of Argentina and Uruguay kindled the idea of continental competition in the 1930s.[^cite_note-Inicio-1] In 1948, the [South American Championship of Champions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_American_Championship_of_Champions) (Spanish: *Campeonato Sudamericano de Campeones*), the most direct precursor to the Copa Libertadores, was played and organized by the Chilean club [Colo-Colo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colo-Colo) after years of planning and organization.[^cite_note-Inicio-1] Held in [Santiago](51572.md), it brought together the champions of each nation's top national leagues.[^cite_note-Inicio-1] The tournament was won by [Vasco da Gama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CR_Vasco_da_Gama) of [Brazil](3383.md).[^cite_note-Inicio-1][^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6][^cite_note-auto-7] The 1948 South American tournament began, in continent-wide reach, the "champions cup" model, resulting in the creation of the [European Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Cup) in 1955, as confirmed by [Jacques Ferran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Ferran) (one of the "founding fathers" of the [European Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Cup)), in a 2015 interview with a Brazilian TV sports programme.[^cite_note-8] The connection between the 1948 competition and the Copa Libertadores was recognised by Conmebol with the participation of Vasco da Gama in the 1997 [Supercopa Libertadores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercopa_Libertadores), a Conmebol competition that accepted the participation of Copa Libertadores winners only, and the 1948 championship is referred to at the Conmebol website as the competition that, 12 years later, would become the Copa Libertadores.[^cite_note-auto-7]

In 1958, the basis and format of the competition were created by [Peñarol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pe%C3%B1arol)'s board leaders. On October 8, 1958, [João Havelange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jo%C3%A3o_Havelange) announced, at a [UEFA](32332.md) meeting he attended as an invitee, the creation of *Copa de Campeones de America* (American Champions Cup, renamed in 1965 as Copa Libertadores), as a South American equivalent of the [European Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Cup), so that the champion clubs of both continental confederations could decide "the best club team of the world" in the [Intercontinental Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercontinental_Cup_\(1960%E2%80%932004\)).[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10] On March 5, 1959, at the 24th South American Congress held in [Buenos Aires](255919.md), the competition was ratified by the International Affairs Committee. In 1965, it was named in honor of the [heroes of South American liberation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libertadores), such as [Simón Bolívar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sim%C3%B3n_Bol%C3%ADvar), [José de San Martín](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_de_San_Mart%C3%ADn), [Pedro I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_I_of_Brazil), [Bernardo O'Higgins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernardo_O'Higgins), and [José Gervasio Artigas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Gervasio_Artigas), among others.[^cite_note-Inicio-1]

## Format

### Qualification

Most teams qualify for the Copa Libertadores by winning half-year tournaments called the [Apertura and Clausura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apertura_and_Clausura) tournaments or by finishing among the top teams in their championship.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3] The countries that use this format are Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador, Paraguay, Peru, Uruguay and Venezuela.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3] Peru and Ecuador have developed new formats for qualification to the Copa Libertadores involving several stages.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3] Argentina, Brazil and Chile are the only South American leagues to use a European league format instead of the Apertura and Clausura format.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3] However, one berth for the Copa Libertadores can be won by winning the domestic cups in these countries.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3]

Peru, Uruguay and Mexico formerly used a second tournament to decide qualification for the Libertadores (the "Liguilla Pre-Libertadores" between 1992 and 1997, the "Liguilla Pre-Libertadores de América" from 1974 to 2009, and the [InterLiga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/InterLiga) from 2004 to 2010, respectively).[^cite_note-México-2][^cite_note-Reglamento-3] Argentina used an analogous method only once in [1992](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1991%E2%80%9392_Argentine_Primera_Divisi%C3%B3n). Since 2011, the winner of the Copa Sudamericana has qualified automatically for the following Copa Libertadores.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3][^cite_note-11]

### Rules



The Copa Libertadores logo is shown on the centre of the [pitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_football_pitch) before every game in the competition.

Unlike most other football competitions around the world, the Copa Libertadores historically did not use [extra time](633242.md), or [away goals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Away_goals_rule).[^cite_note-Reglamento-3] From 1960 to 1987, two-legged ties were decided on points (teams would be awarded 2 points for a win, 1 point for a draw and 0 points for a loss), without considering goal differences. If both teams were level on points after two legs, a third match would be played at a neutral venue. Goal difference would only come into play if the third match was drawn. If the third match did not produce an outright winner, a [penalty shootout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penalty_shootout_\(football\)) was used to determine a winner.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3]

From 1988 onwards, two-legged ties were decided on points, followed by goal difference, with an immediate penalty shootout if the tie was level on aggregate after full-time in the second leg.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3] Starting with the 2005 season, CONMEBOL began to use the away goals rule.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3] In 2008, the finals became an exception to the away goals rule and employed extra time.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3] From 1995 onwards, the "[Three points for a win](4960100.md)" standard, a system adopted by FIFA in 1995 that places additional value on wins, was adopted in CONMEBOL, with teams now earning 3 points for a win, 1 point for a draw and 0 points for a loss.

### Tournament

The current tournament features 47 clubs competing over a six- to eight-month period. There are three stages: the first, the second and the knockout stage.

The first stage involves 12 clubs in a series of two-legged knockout ties.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3] The six survivors join 26 clubs in the second stage, in which they are divided into eight groups of four.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3] The teams in each group play in a double round-robin format, with each team playing home and away games against every other team in their group.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3] The top two teams from each group are then drawn into the knockout stage, which consists of two-legged knockout ties.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3] From that point, the competition proceeds with two-legged knockout ties to quarterfinals, semifinals, and the finals.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3] Between 1960 and 1987 the previous winners did not enter the competition until the semifinal stage, making it much easier to retain the cup.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3]

Between 1960 and 2004, the winner of the tournament participated in the now-defunct [Intercontinental Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercontinental_Cup_\(1960%E2%80%932004\)) or (after 1980) Toyota Cup, a football competition endorsed by [UEFA](32332.md) and CONMEBOL, contested against the winners of the [European Cup](44220.md) (since renamed the UEFA Champions League)[^cite_note-Reglamento-3] Since 2004, the winner has played in the [Club World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FIFA_Club_World_Cup), an international competition contested by the champion clubs from all six continental confederations. It is organized by the *Fédération Internationale de Football Association* ([FIFA](11049.md)), the sport's global governing body. Because Europe and South America are considered the strongest centers of the sport, the champions of those continents enter the tournament at the semifinal stage.[^cite_note-Reglamento-3] The winning team also qualifies to play in the Recopa Sudamericana, a two-legged final series against the winners of the [Copa Sudamericana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copa_Sudamericana).[^cite_note-Reglamento-3]

## Prizes

### Trophy

Main article: [Copa Libertadores (trophy)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copa_Libertadores_\(trophy\))

The tournament shares its name with the trophy, also called the *Copa Libertadores* or simply *la Copa*, which is awarded to the Copa Libertadores winner. It was designed by [goldsmith](323246.md) Alberto de Gasperi, an Italian-born immigrant to Peru, in Camusso Jewelry in [Lima](85423.md) at the behest of CONMEBOL.[^cite_note-Trofeo-12] The top of the laurel is made of [sterling silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sterling_silver), except for the football player at the top (which is made of [bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze) with a silver coating).[^cite_note-13]

The [pedestal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedestal), which contains badges from every winner of the competition, is made of [hardwood plywood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plywood#Hardwood_plywood). The badges show the season, the full name of the winning club, and the city and nation from which the champions hail. To the left of that information is the club logo. Any club which wins three consecutive tournaments has the right to keep the trophy. The current trophy is the third in the history of the competition.

Two clubs have kept the actual trophy after three consecutive wins:[^cite_note-14]

- [Estudiantes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estudiantes_de_La_Plata) after their third consecutive win in [1970](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1970_Copa_Libertadores). They won a fourth title in [2009](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Copa_Libertadores).

- [Independiente](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Club_Atl%C3%A9tico_Independiente) after their third consecutive win, and fifth overall, in [1974](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1974_Copa_Libertadores). They have since won two more titles, in [1975](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1975_Copa_Libertadores) and [1984](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984_Copa_Libertadores).

### Prize money

As of 2023, clubs in the Copa Libertadores receive US\$500,000 for advancing into the second stage and US\$1,000,000 per home match in the group phase, with an additional US\$300,000 awarded per match won in that stage. That amount is derived from television rights and stadium advertising. The payment per home match increases to US\$1,250,000 in the round of 16. The prize money then increases as each quarterfinalist receives US\$1,700,000, US\$2,300,000 is given to each semifinalist, US\$7,000,000 is awarded to the runner-up, and the winner earns US\$18,000,000.[^cite_note-Prizes_2023-15]

- Eliminated at the first stage: US\$400,000

- Eliminated at the second stage: US\$500,000

- Eliminated at the third stage: US\$600,000

- Group stage: US\$3,000,000

- Group stage win: US\$300,000

- Round of 16: US\$1,250,000

- Quarter-finals: US\$1,700,000

- Semi-finals: US\$2,300,000

- Runners-up: US\$7,000,000

- Champions: US\$18,000,000

## Cultural impact

The Copa Libertadores occupies an important space in South American culture. The folklore, fanfare, and organization of many football competitions around the world owe several aspects to the *Libertadores*.

### The "Sueño Libertador"



Estadio Centenario during a Copa Libertadores match.



Estudiantes fans celebrating their 2009 title win.



Since its inception, the Copa Libertadores has been an integral part of South American football culture.

The *Sueño Libertador* ("*Liberator Dream*") is a promotional [phrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrase) frequently used in sports journalism to refer to the pursuit or accomplishment of winning the Copa Libertadores.[^cite_note-16] When a team is eliminated from the competition, media outlets often report that the team has "awakened from the liberator dream." The project typically begins after a club wins its national league, securing qualification for the following edition of the tournament.

It is common for participating clubs to invest heavily in roster squad assembly to secure the trophy. For example, in 1998, [Vasco da Gama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CR_Vasco_da_Gama) spent \$10 million to construct a winning squad, while [Palmeiras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociedade_Esportiva_Palmeiras), managed by [Luiz Felipe Scolari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luiz_Felipe_Scolari), brought in [Júnior Baiano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%BAnior_Baiano) among other high-profile signings before winning the [1999 Copa Libertadores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_Copa_Libertadores).&#x20;

The tournament holds immense prestige among players and supporters across the continent. In 2010, players from [Guadalajara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C.D._Guadalajara) stated that they prioritized playing in the Copa Libertadores final over appearing in a friendly against the [Spanish national team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spain_men's_national_football_team), who were the reigning world champions at the time,[^cite_note-17] or domestic league fixtures.[^cite_note-18] Similarly, following their victory in the [2010 Copa do Brasil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Copa_do_Brasil), several [Santos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santos_FC) players expressed a desire to remain with the club to participate in the [2011 Copa Libertadores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Copa_Libertadores), despite lucrative offers from prominent [UEFA Champions League](44220.md) clubs such as [Chelsea](7473.md) and [Lyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympique_Lyonnais).[^cite_note-19]

Former [Boca Juniors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boca_Juniors) goalkeeper [Óscar Córdoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%93scar_C%C3%B3rdoba) stated that the Copa Libertadores was the most prestigious title he won during his career, placing its significance above his domestic league titles and the [Intercontinental Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercontinental_Cup).[^cite_note-20]

### "La Copa se mira y no se toca"

From its inception in 1960 through the late 1970s, the Copa Libertadores was won exclusively by clubs from South American countries with Atlantic coastlines: Argentina, Brazil, and Uruguay. [Olimpia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olimpia_Asunci%C3%B3n) of Paraguay became the first club outside these nations to win the tournament in 1979.

The first team from a country bordering the Pacific Ocean to reach a final was [Universitario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universitario_de_Deportes) of [Lima](85423.md), [Peru](170691.md), who lost in 1972 to [Independiente](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independiente_de_Avellaneda) of Argentina.[^cite_note-Independiente-21] The following year, Independiente defeated [Colo-Colo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colo-Colo) of [Chile](5489.md), another Pacific nation, giving rise to the popular taunt, *"La Copa se mira y no se toca"* (Spanish: *The Cup is to be seen, not touched*).[^cite_note-Independiente-21] [Unión Española](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uni%C3%B3n_Espa%C3%B1ola) became the third Pacific-based club to reach the final in 1975, though they were also defeated by Independiente.[^cite_note-Independiente-21]

[Atlético Nacional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atl%C3%A9tico_Nacional) of [Medellín](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medell%C3%ADn), Colombia, became the first Pacific-adjacent nation to win the tournament in 1989.[^cite_note-1989AN-22] Subsequently, [Colo-Colo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colo-Colo) became the first Chilean club to win the title in 1991, prompting supporters in Chile to adapt the popular phrase to *"La Copa se mira y se toca"* (Spanish: *The Cup is seen **and** touched*). Other clubs from Pacific-facing nations to win the title include [Once Caldas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Once_Caldas) of Colombia (2004) and [LDU Quito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LDU_Quito) of Ecuador (2008), while [Barcelona SC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barcelona_Sporting_Club) of Ecuador reached the final in 1990 and 1998.

### Performances by country

Brazil and Argentina are the most successful nations in the history of the competition with 25 titles each as of 2025. This is followed by Uruguay with 8 titles, Colombia and Paraguay with 3 titles each, and Chile and Ecuador with 1 title each. Peru has no titles but 2 runners-up. Venezuela and Bolivia have never reached a final. Beyond them, Mexico, as an invited country, got 3 runners up but no titles when the country participated from 1998 to 2016.
