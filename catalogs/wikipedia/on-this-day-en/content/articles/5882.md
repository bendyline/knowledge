**Cardiff**[^cite_note-4] is the [capital](181337.md) and [largest city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_urban_areas_in_the_United_Kingdom) of [Wales](69894.md). Cardiff had a population of 381,516 in 2025[^cite_note-popstats-2] and forms a [principal area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_areas_of_Wales) officially known as the **City and County of Cardiff** (Welsh: *Dinas a Sir Caerdydd*). The city is the [eleventh largest in the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_in_the_United_Kingdom). Located in the [southeast of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_East_Wales), the city sits within the [Cardiff Capital Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Capital_Region), which consists of 1.5 million people, accounting for roughly half the nation's total population. Cardiff is the [county town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_town) of the [historic county](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historic_counties_of_Wales) of [Glamorgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glamorgan) and from 1974 to 1996 of [South Glamorgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Glamorgan). It belongs to the [Eurocities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurocities) network of the largest European cities.[^cite_note-5] A small town until the early 19th century, its prominence as a port for coal when mining began in the region helped its expansion. In 1905, it was ranked as a city and in 1955 proclaimed [capital of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_of_Wales). The [Cardiff urban area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_urban_area) covers a larger area outside the county boundary, including the towns of [Dinas Powys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinas_Powys) and [Penarth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penarth).

Cardiff is the main commercial centre of Wales as well as the base for the [Senedd](238480.md), the Welsh Parliament. At the 2021 census, the unitary authority area population was put at 362,400,[^cite_note-6] an increase of 4.7% compared to the 2011 census when the population was 346,100.[^cite_note-\:2-7] The population of the wider urban area in 2011 was 479,000.[^cite_note-statswales.gov.wales-8] In 2011, it ranked sixth in the world in a [*National Geographic* magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Geographic_\(magazine\)) list of alternative tourist destinations.[^cite_note-9] It is the most popular destination in Wales with 21.3 million visitors in 2017.[^cite_note-10] It was voted as the best city in the UK in the 2023 Readers' Choice Awards.[^cite_note-11]

Cardiff is a major centre for television and film production (such as the 2005 revival of *[Doctor Who](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Who)*,[^cite_note-12] *[Torchwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torchwood)* and *[Sherlock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherlock_\(TV_series\))*) and is the Welsh base for the main national broadcasters.

[Cardiff Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Bay) contains the [Senedd building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senedd_building) and the [Wales Millennium Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_Millennium_Centre) arts complex. Work continues at Cardiff Bay and in the centre on projects such as [Cardiff International Sports Village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_International_Sports_Village), [BBC drama village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roath_Lock),[^cite_note-13] and a new business district.[^cite_note-14]

## History

Main articles: [History of Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Cardiff) and [Timeline of Cardiff history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_Cardiff_history)

### Etymology

*Caerdydd* (the [Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language) name of the city) derives from the [Middle Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Welsh) *Caerdyf*. The change from *-dyf* to *-dydd* shows the colloquial alteration of Welsh *f* [\[v\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Welsh) and *dd* [\[ð\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Welsh) and was perhaps also driven by [folk etymology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_etymology). This [sound change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_change) probably first occurred in the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages); both forms were current in the [Tudor period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_period). *Caerdyf* has its origins in [post-Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-Roman_Britain) [Brythonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brythonic_languages) words meaning "the fort of the [Taff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Taff)". The [fort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Roman_Fort) probably refers to that established by the [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Britain). *Caer* is Welsh for *fort* and *-dyf* is a form of *Taf* (Taff), the river which flows by Cardiff Castle, with the ⟨t⟩ showing [consonant mutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consonant_mutation) to ⟨d⟩, as is normal in compound words, and the vowel showing [affection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affection_\(linguistics\)) as a result of a (lost) [genitive case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genitive_case) ending.[^cite_note-hwo-15]

The [anglicised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglicised) *Cardiff* is derived from *Caerdyf*, with the Welsh *f* [\[v\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Welsh) borrowed as *ff* [/f/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English), as also happens in *Taff* (from Welsh *Taf*) and *[Llandaff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llandaff)* (from Welsh *Llandaf*).

The antiquarian [William Camden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Camden) (1551–1623) suggested that the name Cardiff may derive from \**Caer-Didi* ("the Fort of Didius"), a name supposedly given in honour of [Aulus Didius Gallus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aulus_Didius_Gallus), governor of a nearby province at the time when the Roman fort was established. Although some sources repeat this theory, it has been rejected on linguistic grounds by modern scholars such as Professor Gwynedd Pierce.[^cite_note-16]

### Origins

[Archaeological](18951655.md) evidence from sites in and around Cardiff show that people had settled in the area by at least around 6000 BC, during the early Neolithic: about 1,500 years before either [Stonehenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonehenge) or the [Great Pyramid of Giza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Pyramid_of_Giza) was completed.[^cite_note-Coflein_St_L-17][^cite_note-Coflein_Tink-18][^cite_note-Coflein_Cae-19][^cite_note-Coflein_Gwern-20][^cite_note-Stonehenge-21] These include the [St Lythans burial chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Lythans_burial_chamber) near [Wenvoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wenvoe), (approximately four miles or six km west of Cardiff city centre); the [Tinkinswood burial chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tinkinswood), near [St. Nicholas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Nicholas,_Vale_of_Glamorgan) (about six miles or ten km west of Cardiff city centre), the Cae'rarfau [Chambered Tomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chambered_tomb), [Creigiau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creigiau) (about six miles or ten km northwest of Cardiff city centre) and the Gwern y Cleppa [long barrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_barrow), near [Coedkernew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coedkernew), [Newport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newport,_Wales) (about eight miles or thirteen km northeast of Cardiff city centre). A group of five [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age_Britain) [tumuli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumulus) is at the summit of the [Garth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garth_Hill), within the county's northern boundary.[^cite_note-Coflein_Garth-22] Four [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Iron_Age) [hill fort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill_fort#Britain) and [enclosure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enclosure_\(archaeology\)) sites have been identified within Cardiff's county boundaries, including [Caerau Hillfort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caerau_Hillfort), an enclosed area of 5.1 hectares (12+1⁄2 acres).[^cite_note-Coflein_Graig-23][^cite_note-Coflein_Craigyparc-24][^cite_note-Coflein_Llwynda-25][^cite_note-Coflein_Caerau-26]



Front wall of [Cardiff Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Castle)\
part of the original Roman fort beneath the red stones

Until the [Roman conquest of Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_conquest_of_Britain), Cardiff was part of the territory of the [Silures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silures) – a [Celtic British tribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age_tribes_in_Britain) that flourished in the [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age) – whose territory included the areas that would become known as [Breconshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breconshire), [Monmouthshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monmouthshire_\(historic\)) and Glamorgan.[^cite_note-Wales_Hist_1-27] The 3.2 ha (8-acre) [fort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Roman_Fort) established by the [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_army) near the mouth of the [River Taff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Taff) in AD 75, in what would become the north western boundary of the centre of Cardiff, was built over an extensive settlement that had been established by the Romans in the 50s AD.[^cite_note-Coflein_2-28] The fort was one of a series of military outposts associated with *[Isca Augusta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isca_Augusta)* ([Caerleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caerleon)) that acted as border defences. The fort may have been abandoned in the early 2nd century as the area had been subdued. However, by this time a civilian settlement, or *[vicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vicus_\(Rome\))*, was established. It was likely made up of traders who made a living from the fort, ex-soldiers and their families. A [Roman villa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_villa) has been discovered at [Ely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ely,_Cardiff).[^cite_note-29] Contemporary with the [Saxon Shore forts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxon_Shore_forts) of the 3rd and 4th centuries, a stone fortress was established at Cardiff. Similar to the shore forts, the fortress was built to protect [Britannia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britannia) from raiders.[^cite_note-30] Coins from the reign of [Gratian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gratian) indicate that Cardiff was inhabited until at least the 4th century; the fort was abandoned towards the end of the 4th century, as the last Roman legions left the province of Britannia with [Magnus Maximus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnus_Maximus).[^cite_note-31][^cite_note-Visit_1-32]

Little is known of the fort and civilian settlement in the period between the Roman departure from Britain and the Norman Conquest. The settlement probably shrank in size and may even have been abandoned. In the absence of Roman rule, Wales was divided into small kingdoms; early on, [Meurig ap Tewdrig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meurig_ap_Tewdrig) emerged as the local king in [Glywysing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glywysing) (which later became [Glamorgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glamorgan)). The area passed through his family until the advent of the Normans in the 11th century.[^cite_note-33]

### Norman occupation and Middle Ages



The Norman keep

In 1081 [William I, King of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_the_Conqueror) visited Cardiff as part of an [armed pilgrimage across South Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_pilgrimage_across_South_Wales) and ordered work to begin on the castle keep within the walls of the old Roman fort.[^cite_note-Coflein_1-34] Cardiff Castle has been at the heart of the city ever since.[^cite_note-Cardiff_Visitors-35] The castle was substantially altered and extended during the Victorian period by [John Crichton-Stuart, 3rd Marquess of Bute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Crichton-Stuart,_3rd_Marquess_of_Bute), and the architect [William Burges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Burges).[^cite_note-Scheduled-36] Original Roman work can, however, still be distinguished in the wall facings.

A town grew up under the castle, consisting mainly of settlers from England.[^cite_note-Cardiffians-37] Cardiff had a population of between 1,500 and 2,000 in the Middle Ages – a normal size for a Welsh town in the period.[^cite_note-Tim_Lambert-short_history_of_Cardiff-38] It was the centre of the Norman [Marcher Lordship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcher_lord) of Glamorgan. By the end of the 13th century, Cardiff was the only town in Wales with a population exceeding 2,000, although it remained relatively small compared with notable towns in England and continued to be contained within its walls, which were begun as a wooden [palisade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palisade) in the early 12th century.[^cite_note-39] It was of sufficient size and importance to receive a series of charters, notably in 1331 from William La Zouche, Lord of Glamorgan through marriage with the [de Clare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Clare) family,[^cite_note-40] [Edward III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_III) in 1359,[^cite_note-calmview\.cardiff.gov.uk-41] then [Henry IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_IV_of_England) in 1400,[^cite_note-calmview\.cardiff.gov.uk-41] and later [Henry VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VI_of_England).

In 1404, [Owain Glyndŵr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owain_Glynd%C5%B5r) burned Cardiff and took possession of the Castle.[^cite_note-Bob_Sanders-period_up_to_1699-42] As many of the buildings were made of timber and tightly packed within the town walls, much of Cardiff was destroyed. The settlement was soon rebuilt on the same street plan and began to flourish again.[^cite_note-Tim_Lambert-short_history_of_Cardiff-38] (Glyndŵr's statue was erected in Cardiff Town Hall in the early 20th century, reflecting the complex, often conflicting cultural identity of Cardiff as capital of Wales.) Besides serving an important political role in the governance of the fertile south Glamorgan coastal plain, Cardiff was a busy port in the Middle Ages and declared an [English staple port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_staple_port) in 1327.

### County town of Glamorganshire



View of Caerdiffe Castle



[Cardiff old town hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Town_Hall) (1860)

In 1536, the [Laws in Wales Acts 1535–1542](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_in_Wales_Acts_1535%E2%80%931542) led to the creation of Glamorganshire and Cardiff was made the [county town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_town), it also became part of [Kibbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kibbor) [hundred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_\(county_subdivision\)),[^cite_note-43] around the same time the Herberts became the most powerful family in the area.[^cite_note-Cardiffians-37] In 1538, [Henry VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VIII) closed Cardiff's [Dominican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominican_Order) and [Franciscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franciscan) friaries, whose remains were used as building materials.[^cite_note-Tim_Lambert-short_history_of_Cardiff-38] A writer in this period noted: "The River Taff runs under the walls of his honours castle and from the north part of the town to the south part where there is a fair quay and a safe harbour for shipping."[^cite_note-Tim_Lambert-short_history_of_Cardiff-38]

Cardiff became a [borough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough) in 1542[^cite_note-Bob_Sanders-period_up_to_1699-42] and further [Royal Charters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Charter) were granted to it by Elizabeth I in 1600[^cite_note-44] and James I in 1608.[^cite_note-45] In 1573, it was made a head port for collection of customs duties.[^cite_note-Cardiffians-37] [Pembrokeshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pembrokeshire) historian [George Owen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Owen_of_Henllys) described Cardiff in 1602 as "the fayrest towne in Wales yett not the welthiest".[^cite_note-Cardiffians-37] It gained a second Royal Charter in 1608.[^cite_note-GoogoBits-46]



[John Speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Speed)'s map of Cardiff from 1610

A [disastrous flood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Channel_floods,_1607) in the [Bristol Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Channel) on 30 January 1607 (now believed to have been a tidal wave)[^cite_note-47] changed the course of the River Taff and ruined [St Mary's Parish Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mary's_Church,_Cardiff), which was replaced by a chapel of ease dedicated to St John the Baptist.[^cite_note-48]

During the [Second English Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_English_Civil_War) [St Fagans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Fagans), just to the west of the town, the [Battle of St Fagans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_St_Fagans), between [Royalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavalier) rebels and a [New Model Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Model_Army) detachment, was a decisive victory for the [Parliamentarians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundhead) that allowed [Oliver Cromwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Cromwell) to conquer Wales.[^cite_note-Bob_Sanders-period_up_to_1699-42] It was the last major battle in Wales, with about 200, mostly Royalist soldiers killed.[^cite_note-Cardiffians-37]

Cardiff was at peace throughout the ensuing century. In 1766, [John Stuart, 1st Marquess of Bute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Stuart,_1st_Marquess_of_Bute) married into the Herbert family and was later created [Baron Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron_Cardiff).[^cite_note-Cardiffians-37] In 1778, he began renovating Cardiff Castle.[^cite_note-49] A [racecourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racecourse), [printing press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_press), bank and [coffee house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee_house) opened in the 1790s and Cardiff gained a [stagecoach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagecoach) service to London. Despite these improvements, Cardiff's position in the Welsh [urban hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_hierarchy) declined over the 18th century. [Iolo Morganwg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iolo_Morganwg) called it "an obscure and inconsiderable place" and the [1801 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census_Act_1800) found a population of only 1,870, making it only the 25th largest town in Wales, well behind [Merthyr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merthyr) and [Swansea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swansea).[^cite_note-Encyclopedia_of_Wales-50]

### Building the docks

Main article: [Cardiff Docks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Docks)

In 1793, [John Crichton-Stuart, 2nd Marquess of Bute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Crichton-Stuart,_2nd_Marquess_of_Bute) was born. He spent his life building the Cardiff docks and was later hailed as "the creator of modern Cardiff".[^cite_note-Cardiffians-37] A twice-weekly boat service between Cardiff and [Bristol](36741.md) opened in 1815,[^cite_note-Bob_Sanders_2-51] and in 1821, the Cardiff Gas Works was established.[^cite_note-Bob_Sanders_2-51]

After the Napoleonic Wars Cardiff suffered some social and industrial unrest, starting with the trial and hanging of [Dic Penderyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dic_Penderyn) in 1831.[^cite_note-52]



Jubilee dock, Cardiff, from the eastern side (1849)

The town grew rapidly from the 1830s onwards, when the [Marquess of Bute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Crichton-Stuart,_2nd_Marquess_of_Bute) built a [dock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dock_\(maritime\)), which eventually linked to the [Taff Vale Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taff_Vale_Railway). Cardiff became the main port for coal exports from the [Cynon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynon_Valley), [Rhondda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhondda), and [Rhymney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhymney_Valley) valleys, and grew in population at a rate of nearly 80 per cent per decade between 1840 and 1870. Much of this was due to migration from within and outside Wales: in 1841, a quarter of Cardiff's population were English-born and more than 10 per cent born in Ireland.[^cite_note-53] By the 1881 census, Cardiff had overtaken Merthyr and Swansea to become the largest town in Wales.[^cite_note-54] Cardiff's status as the premier town in South Wales was confirmed when it was chosen as the site for the [University College of South Wales and Monmouthshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_of_South_Wales_and_Monmouthshire) in 1883.[^cite_note-Encyclopedia_of_Wales-50]

A permanent military presence was established with the completion of [Maindy Barracks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maindy_Barracks) in 1877.[^cite_note-55]

Cardiff faced a challenge in the 1880s when [David Davies of Llandinam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Davies_\(industrialist\)) and the [Barry Railway Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Railway_Company) promoted rival docks at [Barry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry,_Vale_of_Glamorgan). These had the advantage of being accessible in all [tides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide): David Davies claimed his venture would cause "grass to grow in the streets of Cardiff". From 1901 coal exports from Barry surpassed those from Cardiff, but the administration of the coal trade remained centred on Cardiff, in particular its [Coal Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_Exchange), where the price of coal on the British market was determined and the first million-pound deal was struck in 1907.[^cite_note-Encyclopedia_of_Wales-50] The city also strengthened its industrial base when the owners of the [Dowlais Ironworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dowlais_Ironworks) in Merthyr (who would later form part of [Guest, Keen and Nettlefolds](424537.md)) built a [steelworks](1143008.md) close to the docks at East Moors, which Lord Bute opened on 4 February 1891.[^cite_note-56]

### County Borough of Cardiff

Cardiff became a [county borough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_borough) on 1 April 1889 under the [Local Government Act 1888](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Government_Act_1888). The town had grown rapidly and had a population of over 123,000. It retained its county borough status until 1974.[^cite_note-57]

### City and capital city status

Main article: [Capital of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_of_Wales)



[St John the Baptist Church, Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John_the_Baptist_Church,_Cardiff), the only medieval building next to Cardiff Castle to still be in city centre. Seen here in 1852



[National Museum of Wales, Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_Cardiff)

King [Edward VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_VII) granted Cardiff [city status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_status_in_the_United_Kingdom) on 28 October 1905.[^cite_note-58] It acquired a Roman Catholic cathedral in 1916. Later, more national institutions came to the city, including the [National Museum of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_Cardiff), the [Welsh National War Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_National_War_Memorial), and the [University of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wales) Registry Building, but it was denied the [National Library of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Library_of_Wales), partly because the library's founder, Sir John Williams, considered Cardiff to have "a non-Welsh population".[^cite_note-Encyclopedia_of_Wales-50]

Following a brief post-war boom, Cardiff docks entered a prolonged decline in the [interwar period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interwar_period). By 1936, trade was at less than half its value in 1913, reflecting the slump in demand for [Welsh coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Wales_coalfield).[^cite_note-Encyclopedia_of_Wales-50] Bomb damage in the [Cardiff Blitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Blitz) of World War II included the devastation of [Llandaff Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llandaff_Cathedral), and in the immediate postwar years, the city's link with the Bute family came to an end.

On 20 December 1955, in reply to a question in the House of Commons, the [Home Secretary](149104.md), [Gwilym Lloyd George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwilym_Lloyd_George,_1st_Viscount_Tenby) said that due to the volume of support for the idea in Wales, the government would regard Cardiff as the [capital city of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_of_Wales), a step that would not require formal recognition.[^cite_note-59] [Caernarfon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caernarfon) had also vied for the title.[^cite_note-60] Welsh local authorities had been divided: only 76 out of 161 chose Cardiff in a 1924 poll organised by the *South Wales Daily News*.[^cite_note-Johnes_2012-61] The subject was not debated again until 1950, and meanwhile Cardiff took steps to promote its "Welshness". The stalemate between Cardiff and towns such as Caernarfon and Aberystwyth was not broken until Cardiganshire County Council decided to support Cardiff; and in a new local authority vote, 134 out of 161 voted for Cardiff.[^cite_note-Johnes_2012-61]

Cardiff therefore celebrated two important [anniversaries](477069.md) in 2005. The *Encyclopedia of Wales* notes that the decision to regard the city as the capital of Wales "had more to do with the fact that it contained marginal [Conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)) constituencies than any reasoned view of what functions a Welsh capital should have." Although the city hosted the [Commonwealth Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_Games) in 1958, Cardiff became a centre of national administration only with the establishment of the [Welsh Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Office) in 1964, which later prompted the creation of various other public bodies such as the [Arts Council of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arts_Council_of_Wales) and the [Welsh Development Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Development_Agency), most of which were based in Cardiff.



Redevelopment in the city's historic Cardiff Bay area

The East Moors Steelworks closed in 1978 and Cardiff lost population in the 1980s,[^cite_note-62] consistent with a wider pattern of counter-urbanisation in Britain. However, it recovered to become one of the few cities outside London where population grew in the 1990s.[^cite_note-63] During this period the [Cardiff Bay Development Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Bay_Development_Corporation) was promoting the [redevelopment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_renewal) of south Cardiff; an evaluation of the regeneration of Cardiff Bay published in 2004 concluded that the project had "reinforced the competitive position of Cardiff" and "contributed to a massive improvement in the quality of the built environment, although it had "failed "to attract the major inward investors originally anticipated".[^cite_note-64]

In the [1997 Welsh devolution referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Welsh_devolution_referendum), Cardiff voters rejected the establishment of the National Assembly for Wales by 55.4% to 44.2% on a 47% turnout, which Denis Balsom partly ascribed to a general preference in Cardiff and some other parts of Wales for a British rather than exclusively Welsh [identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_identity).[^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66] The relative lack of local support for the Assembly and difficulties between the Welsh Office and Cardiff Council in acquiring the originally preferred venue, [Cardiff City Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_City_Hall), encouraged other local authorities to bid to house the Assembly.[^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68] However, the Assembly was eventually located at [Tŷ Hywel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%B7_Hywel) in Cardiff Bay in 1999. In 2005, a new debating chamber on an adjacent site, designed by [Richard Rogers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Rogers), was opened.

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Cardiff)

The centre of Cardiff is relatively flat and bounded by hills to the east, north and west. Its location influenced its development as the world's largest coal port, notably its proximity and easy access to the coalfields of the [South Wales Valleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Wales_Valleys). The highest point in the local authority area is [Garth Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garth_Hill), 307 m (1,007 ft) [above sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Above_mean_sea_level).

Cardiff is built on reclaimed marshland on a bed of [Triassic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triassic) stones. This reclaimed marshland stretches from [Chepstow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chepstow) to the [Ely Estuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ely_Estuary),[^cite_note-82] which is the natural boundary of Cardiff and the Vale of Glamorgan. Triassic landscapes of this part of the world are usually shallow and low-lying, consistent with the flatness of the centre of Cardiff.[^cite_note-83] The classic Triassic [marl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marl), [sand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sand) and [conglomerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conglomerate_\(geology\)) rocks are used predominantly throughout Cardiff as building materials. Many of these Triassic rocks are purplish, especially the coastal marl found near Penarth. One of the Triassic rocks used in Cardiff is "Radyr Stone", a [freestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freestone_\(masonry\)) which as its name suggests is quarried in the Radyr district.[^cite_note-84] Cardiff has also imported some materials for buildings: [Devonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devonian) sandstones (the [Old Red Sandstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Red_Sandstone)) from the [Brecon Beacons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brecon_Beacons) has been used. Most famously, the buildings of [Cathays Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathays_Park), the civic centre in the centre of the city, are built of [Portland stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portland_stone) from Dorset.[^cite_note-85] A widely used building stone in Cardiff is the yellow-grey [Liassic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Jurassic) [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone) rock of the Vale of Glamorgan, including the rare "Sutton Stone", a conglomerate of lias limestone and [carboniferous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboniferous) limestone.[^cite_note-86]

Cardiff is bordered to the west by the rural district of the Vale of Glamorgan, also known as the Garden of Cardiff,[^cite_note-87] to the east by the city of Newport; to the north by the [South Wales Valleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Wales_Valleys), and to the south by the [Severn Estuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Severn) and [Bristol Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Channel). The River Taff winds through the city centre and together with the [River Ely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Ely) flows into the freshwater Cardiff Bay. A third river, the [Rhymney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhymney_River), flows through the east of the city directly into the Severn Estuary.

Cardiff lies near the [Glamorgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glamorgan) [Heritage Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heritage_Coast), stretching westward from Penarth and Barry – [commuter towns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commuter_town) of Cardiff – with striped yellow-blue [Jurassic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurassic) limestone cliffs. The Glamorgan coast is the only part of the [Celtic Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Sea) with exposed Jurassic ([blue lias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_lias)) geology. This stretch of coast with its reefs, sandbanks and serrated cliffs was a [ship graveyard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_graveyard); many ships sailing to Cardiff during the industrial era were wrecked on this hostile coastline during west/south-westerly gales. Smuggling, deliberate shipwrecking and attacks on ships were also common.[^cite_note-88]

"Inner Cardiff" consists of the wards of [Plasnewydd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasnewydd), [Gabalfa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabalfa), [Roath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roath), [Cathays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathays_\(electoral_ward\)), [Adamsdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adamsdown) and [Splott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Splott) ward on the north and east of the city centre, and [Butetown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butetown), [Grangetown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grangetown,_Cardiff), [Riverside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverside,_Cardiff) and [Canton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canton,_Cardiff) to the south and west.[^cite_note-89] The inner-city areas to the south of the [A4161 road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A4161_road), known as the "Southern Arc", are with the exception of Cardiff Bay some of the poorest districts of Wales, with low levels of economic activity.[^cite_note-90] On the other hand, Gabalfa, Plasnewydd and Cathays north of the 'arc' have large student populations,[^cite_note-91] and Pontcanna (north of Riverside and alongside Canton) is a favourite for students and young professionals. [Penylan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penylan), to the north east of Roath Park, is an affluent area popular with older parents and the retired.

To the west lie [Ely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ely,_Cardiff) and [Caerau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caerau,_Cardiff), which have some of the largest housing estates in the United Kingdom. With the exception of some outlying privately built estates at [Michaelston-super-Ely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michaelston-super-Ely), this is an economically disadvantaged area with high numbers of unemployed households. [Culverhouse Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culverhouse_Cross) is a more affluent western area of the city. [Fairwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairwater,_Cardiff), [Heath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heath,_Cardiff), [Birchgrove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birchgrove,_Cardiff), [Gabalfa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabalfa), [Mynachdy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mynachdy), [Llandaff North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llandaff_North), [Llandaff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llandaff), [Llanishen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llanishen), [Radyr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radyr), [Whitchurch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitchurch,_Cardiff) & [Tongwynlais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tongwynlais), [Rhiwbina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhiwbina), [Thornhill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thornhill,_Cardiff), [Lisvane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisvane) and [Cyncoed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyncoed) lie in an arc from the north-west to the north-east of the centre. Lisvane, Cyncoed, Radyr and Rhiwbina contain some of the most expensive housing in Wales.

Further east lie the wards of Pontprennau and Old St Mellons, St Mellons, Rumney, [Pentwyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentwyn,_Cardiff), [Llanrumney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llanrumney), [Llanedeyrn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llanedeyrn) and [Trowbridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trowbridge,_Cardiff). The last four are largely public housing stock, although much new private housing is being built in Trowbridge. [Pontprennau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontprennau) is the newest "suburb" of Cardiff, while [Old St Mellons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mellons) has a history going back to the 11th-century [Norman Conquest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Conquest).[^cite_note-92] The region that may be called "Rural Cardiff" contains the villages of [St Fagans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Fagans), Creigiau, [Pentyrch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentyrch), [Tongwynlais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tongwynlais) and [Gwaelod-y-garth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwaelod-y-garth).[^cite_note-93] In 2017, plans were approved for a new suburb of 7,000 homes between [Radyr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radyr) and [St Fagans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Fagans), known as [Plasdŵr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasdwr).[^cite_note-94] St Fagans, home to the [Museum of Welsh Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Welsh_Life), is protected from further development.[^cite_note-95]

Since 2000, there has been a marked change of scale and building height in Cardiff, with the development of the [city centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_city_centre)'s first purpose-built high-rise apartments.[^cite_note-96] [Tall buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_and_structures_in_Cardiff) have been built in the city centre and Cardiff Bay, and more are planned.[^cite_note-97]

Cardiff, in the [north temperate zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate_zone), has a [maritime climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_climate) ([Köppen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification): Cfb) marked by mild weather that is often cloudy, wet and windy.[^cite_note-Met_Off_1-98] Cardiff is one of the warmest and wettest cities in the UK, with an average annual temperature and rainfall of approximately 11°C and 1200mm respectively. Summers tend to be warm and sunny, with average maxima between 19 and 22 °C (66 and 72 °F). Winters are fairly wet, but excessive rainfall as well as frost are rare. Spring and autumn feel similar, with mild temperatures averaging around 15°C as daytime maxima. Rain is unpredictable at any time of year, although showers tend to be shorter in summer.[^cite_note-Airport_guide-99]

The northern part of the county, being higher and inland, tends to be cooler and wetter than the city centre.[^cite_note-OS_171-100]

Cardiff's maximum and minimum monthly temperatures average 21.8 °C (71.2 °F) (July) and 2.5 °C (36.5 °F) (January and February).\
For Wales, the temperatures average 19.3 °C (66.7 °F) (July) and 1.6 °C (34.9 °F) (February).[^cite_note-Met_Off_2-101][^cite_note-Met_Off_3-102]

Cardiff has 1,573 hours of sunshine in an average year (Wales 1,407.5 hours). Cardiff is sunniest in July, with an average 199.6 hours during the month (Wales 177 hours), and least sunny in December with 50.4 hours (Wales 41.4 hours).[^cite_note-Met_Off_2-101][^cite_note-Met_Off_3-102]

Cardiff experiences less rainfall than average for Wales. It falls on 153 days in an average year, with total annual rainfall of 1,203.3 mm (47.37 in). Monthly rainfall patterns show that from October to January, average monthly rainfall in Cardiff exceeds 100 mm (3.9 in) each month, the wettest month being December with 139.6 mm (5.50 in) and the driest from April to June, with average monthly rainfall fairly consistent between 70 and 80 mm (2.8 and 3.1 in).[^cite_note-Met_Off_2-101][^cite_note-Met_Off_3-102]

Following a period of decline in the 1970s and 1980s, Cardiff's population is growing again. It reached 362,400 in the [2021 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021%E2%80%932022_United_Kingdom_censuses),[^cite_note-111] compared to a [2011 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_United_Kingdom_census) figure of 346,100.[^cite_note-\:2-7] Between mid-2007 and mid-2008, Cardiff was the fastest-growing local authority in Wales, with growth of 1.2%.[^cite_note-2008_population-112] According to 2001 census data, Cardiff was the 21st largest [urban area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_conurbations_in_the_United_Kingdom) in the United Kingdom.[^cite_note-113] The Cardiff [Larger Urban Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larger_Urban_Zone) (a [Eurostat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostat) definition including the Vale of Glamorgan and a number of local authorities in [the Valleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Wales_Valleys)) has 841,600 people, the 10th largest LUZ in the UK.[^cite_note-114] The Cardiff and South Wales Valleys metropolitan area has a population of nearly 1.1 million.[^cite_note-espon.eu-115]



Residential areas of northern Cardiff

Official census estimates of the city's total population have been disputed. The city council published two articles arguing that the 2001 census seriously under-reported the population of Cardiff, and in particular the ethnic minority population of some inner city areas.[^cite_note-116][^cite_note-117]

The Welsh Government's official mid-year estimate of the population of the Cardiff local authority area in 2019 was 366,903.[^cite_note-118] At the 2011 census, the official population of the Cardiff Built Up Area (BUA) was put at 447,287.[^cite_note-119][^cite_note-120] The BUA is not contiguous with the local authority boundary and aggregates data at a lower level; for Cardiff this includes the urban part of Cardiff, Penarth/Dinas Powys, Caerphilly and Pontypridd.

Cardiff has an ethnically diverse population due to past trading connections, post-war [immigration](4599312.md) and large numbers of foreign students who attend university in the city. The ethnic make-up of Cardiff's population at the 2011 census was: 84.7% White, 1.6% mixed White and Black African/Caribbean, 0.7% mixed White and Asian, 0.6% mixed other, 8.1% Asian, 2.4% Black, 1.4% Arab and 0.6% other ethnic groups.[^cite_note-\:0-121] This means almost 53,000 people from a non-white ethnic group reside in the city. This diversity, especially that of the city's long-established African[^cite_note-122] and Arab[^cite_note-123] communities, has been recorded in cultural exhibitions and events, along with books published on this subject.[^cite_note-124][^cite_note-125]

### Health

Main article: [Cardiff and Vale University Health Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_and_Vale_University_Health_Board)



[University Hospital of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_Hospital_of_Wales)

There are seven NHS hospitals in the city, the largest being the [University Hospital of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_Hospital_of_Wales), which is the third largest hospital in the UK and deals with most accidents and emergencies.[^cite_note-126] The University Dental Hospital, which provides emergency treatment, is also located on this site. [Llandough Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llandough_Hospital) is located in the south of the city.

[St. David's Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_David's_Hospital,_Cardiff), the city's newest hospital, built behind the former building, is located in Canton and provides services for the elderly and children. [Cardiff Royal Infirmary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Royal_Infirmary) is on Newport Road, near the city centre. The majority of this hospital was closed in 1999, but the west wing remained open for clinic services, genitourinary medicine and rehabilitation treatment. [Rookwood Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rookwood_Hospital) and the [Velindre Cancer Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velindre_Cancer_Centre) are also located within Cardiff. They are administered by the [Cardiff and Vale University Health Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_and_Vale_University_Health_Board), with the exception of Velindre, which is run by a separate trust.[^cite_note-127][^cite_note-128] [Spire Healthcare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spire_Healthcare), a private hospital, is in Pontprennau.[^cite_note-129]

### Language

See also: [Cardiff dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_dialect)



Bilingual signs are commonplace in Cardiff.

Cardiff has a chequered linguistic history with [Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language), English, [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin), [Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse) and [Norman French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_language) preponderant at different times. Welsh was the majority language in Cardiff from the 13th century until the city's explosive growth in the [Victorian era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_era).[^cite_note-welsh-130] As late as 1850, five of the 12 [Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican) churches within the current city boundaries conducted their services exclusively in Welsh, while only two worshipped exclusively in English.[^cite_note-welsh-130] By 1891, the percentage of Welsh speakers had fallen to 27.9% and only [Lisvane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisvane), [Llanedeyrn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llanedeyrn) and [Creigiau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creigiau) remained as majority Welsh-speaking communities.[^cite_note-131] The Welsh language became grouped around a small cluster of chapels and churches, the most notable of which is Tabernacl in the city centre, one of four UK churches chosen to hold official services to commemorate the new millennium.

The city's first Welsh-language school (Ysgol Gymraeg Bryntaf) was established in the 1950s. Welsh has since regained ground.[^cite_note-132] Aided by [Welsh-medium education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh-medium_education) and migration from other parts of Wales, there are now many more Welsh speakers in the local authority: their numbers doubled between the 1991 and 2021 censuses, from 18,071 (6.6%) to 42,757 (12.2%) residents aged three years and above.[^cite_note-\:1-133][^cite_note-134] The [LSOA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LSOA) (Lower Layer Super Output Area) with the highest percentage of Welsh speakers in the city centre is found in [Victoria Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_Park,_Cardiff), at 28.7%.[^cite_note-135]

[Cardiff City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Cardiff_Council) adopted a five-year Welsh-language strategy in 2017, aimed at increasing the number of Welsh speakers (aged 3+) in Cardiff by 15.9%, from 36,735 in 2011 to 42,584 residents by the 2021 Census.[^cite_note-136] In the 2021 Census, 42,757 (12.2%) of Cardiff's population could speak Welsh.[^cite_note-\:1-133]

In addition to English and Welsh, the diversity of Cardiff's population (including foreign students) means that many other languages are spoken. One study has found that Cardiff has speakers of at least 94 languages, with [Somali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_language), [Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu), [Bengali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengali_language) and [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language) being the most commonly spoken foreign ones.[^cite_note-137]

The modern [Cardiff accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_dialect) is distinct from that of nearby South Wales Valleys. It is marked primarily by:

- Substitution of ⟨iə⟩ by ⟨jøː⟩

Due to its diversity and large student population, more people now come to the city to learn English. Cardiff's Muslim population is much above the Welsh average and the longest established in the UK, being started by Yemeni and Somali sailors settling in the 19th century.[^cite_note-150] Cardiff now has over 11,000 Muslims with various national affiliations[^cite_note-cityprofile-151] – nearly 52 per cent of the Muslim population in Wales.[^cite_note-152]

The proportion of Cardiff residents declaring themselves Hindu, Sikh and Jewish were all considerably higher than the Welsh averages, but lower than the UK figures. The city has had a Hindu community since Indian immigrants settled in the 1950s and 1960s. The first Hindu temple in the city was opened in [Grangetown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grangetown,_Cardiff) on 6 April 1979 on the site of an abandoned synagogue.[^cite_note-153] The 25th anniversary of the founding was celebrated in September 2007 with a parade of over 3,000 people through the city centre, including Hindus from across the United Kingdom and members of Cardiff's other religious communities.[^cite_note-154] There are over 2,000 Hindus in Cardiff, worshipping at three temples.[^cite_note-cityprofile-151]

In the 2001 census 18.8% of the city's population stated they had no religion, while 8.6% did not state a religion.[^cite_note-155]

## Economy

Main article: [Economy and industry of Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_and_industry_of_Cardiff)



[The Coal Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Coal_Exchange)

As the capital city of Wales, Cardiff is the main engine of growth in the Welsh economy. Though the population of Cardiff is about 10% of the Welsh population, the economy of Cardiff makes up nearly 20% of Welsh GDP and 40% of the city's workforce are daily in-commuters from the surrounding South Wales area.[^cite_note-156][^cite_note-157]

Industry has played a major part in Cardiff's development for many centuries. The main catalyst for its transformation from a small town into a big city was the demand for coal required in making iron and later steel, brought to sea by [packhorse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Packhorse) from [Merthyr Tydfil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merthyr_Tydfil). This was first achieved by building a 25-mile (40 km) canal from Merthyr (510 ft or 160 m above sea level) to the [Taff Estuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taff_Estuary) at Cardiff.[^cite_note-canal-158] Eventually the [Taff Vale Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taff_Vale_Railway) replaced the canal barges and massive marshalling yards sprang up as new docks were developed in Cardiff – all prompted by the soaring worldwide demand for coal from the South Wales valleys.

At its peak, Cardiff's port area, known as [Tiger Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiger_Bay), became the busiest port in the world and – for some time – the world's most important coal port.[^cite_note-Grauniad_Dinas-159][^cite_note-BBC_Dinas-160] In the years leading up to the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War), more than 10 million tonnes of coal was exported annually from [Cardiff Docks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Docks).[^cite_note-161] In 1907, Cardiff's [Coal Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_Exchange) was the first host to a business deal for a million [pounds Sterling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_Sterling).[^cite_note-162] The high demand for Welsh coal and specifically Welsh artificial fuel, named Patent Fuel, is shown by the numerous factories producing this fuel, with the same recipe, in the region of Cardiff. Most well known factories were the Star Patent fuel Co., the Crown Patent fuel, the Cardiff Patent fuel etc.[^cite_note-163] After a period of decline, due to low demand on coal, Cardiff's port has started to grow again – over 3 million tonnes of cargo passed through the docks in 2007.[^cite_note-164]



The 26-storey [Bridge Street Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridge_Street_Exchange), at 85 m (279 ft) in height, is the [tallest building in Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_Cardiff).

[^cite_note-Beeb-165]

Cardiff today is the main finance and business [services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_\(economics\)) centre in Wales, with strong representation of finance and business services in the local economy. This sector, combined with the public administration, education and health sectors, have accounted for about 75% of Cardiff's economic growth since 1991.[^cite_note-employ-166] The city was recently placed seventh overall in the top 50 European cities in the fDI 2008 Cities of the Future list published by the [fDi magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FDi_magazine), and ranked seventh in terms of attracting foreign investment.[^cite_note-167] Notable companies such as [Legal & General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_&_General), [Admiral Insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiral_Insurance), [HBOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HBOS), [Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zurich_Financial_Services), [ING Direct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ING_Direct), [The AA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Automobile_Association), [Principality Building Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality_Building_Society), [118118](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/118_118_\(UK\)), [British Gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrica_plc), [Brains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SA_Brain_&_Company_Ltd), [SWALEC Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SWALEC) and [BT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BT_Group), all operate large national or regional headquarters and contact centres in the city, some of them based in Cardiff's office towers such as [Capital Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_Tower,_Cardiff) and Brunel House. Other major employers include [NHS Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NHS_Wales) and the Senedd. On 1 March 2004, Cardiff was granted [Fairtrade City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairtrade_City) status.

Cardiff is one of the most popular tourist destinations in the United Kingdom, receiving 18.3 million visitors in 2010 and generating £852 million for the city's economy.[^cite_note-tourism-168] One result is that one in five employees in Cardiff are based in the distribution, hotels and restaurants sector, highlighting the growing retail and tourism industries in the city.[^cite_note-employ-166] The city has many hotels of varying sizes and standards, providing almost 9,000 available beds.[^cite_note-169]

Cardiff is home to the Welsh media and a large media sector with [BBC Cymru Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Cymru_Wales), [S4C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S4C) and [ITV Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITV_Wales_&_West) all having studios in the city.[^cite_note-media-170] There is a large independent TV production industry sector of over 600 companies, employing around 6,000, with a turnover estimated at £350 million.[^cite_note-media-170] Just to the north-west of the city, in [Rhondda Cynon Taff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhondda_Cynon_Taff), the first completely new film studios in the UK for 30 years are being built, to be named [Valleywood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valleywood). The studios are set to be the biggest in the UK. In 2011 the BBC completed the [Roath Lock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roath_Lock) studios in Cardiff Bay to film dramas such as *Casualty*, *Doctor Who*, and *Pobol y Cwm*.[^cite_note-171]

Cardiff has several regeneration projects, such as St David's 2 Centre and surrounding areas of the city centre, and the £1.4 billion International Sports Village in Cardiff Bay, which played a part in the [London 2012 Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics). It features the only [Olympic-standard swimming pool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic-size_swimming_pool) in Wales, the [Cardiff International Pool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_International_Pool), which opened on 12 January 2008.

According to the Welsh Rugby Union, the Principality Stadium contributed £1 billion to the Welsh economy in the ten years after it opened in 1999, with around 85% of that staying in the Cardiff area.[^cite_note-WRU_Mileniwm_1-172]

## Transport

Main article: [Transport in Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_in_Cardiff)

### Rail

Main article: [Rail transport in Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Cardiff)

[Cardiff Central railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Central_railway_station) is the largest railway station in Wales, with eight platforms coping with over 12.5 million passengers a year.[^cite_note-178][^cite_note-179] It provides direct services to [Bridgend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridgend_railway_station) and [Newport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newport_railway_station), long-distance, cross-Wales services to [Wrexham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrexham_General_railway_station) and [Holyhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holyhead_railway_station), and services to [Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Temple_Meads_railway_station), [Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_New_Street_railway_station), [Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Piccadilly_railway_station) and [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paddington_railway_station). Cardiff Central Station is situated within the southern border of what was known [Temperance Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperance_Town,_Cardiff), a former residential area within central Cardiff.

[Cardiff Queen Street railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Queen_Street_railway_station) is the second busiest in Wales and the hub for the [Valley Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valley_Lines) services that connect the [South Wales Valleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Wales_Valleys) and the Cardiff suburbs with the city centre. It is located at the eastern end of the city centre and provides services to Cardiff Bay. Cardiff has a [suburban rail system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commuter_rail_in_the_United_Kingdom) known as the [Valleys & Cardiff Local Routes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valleys_&_Cardiff_Local_Routes), operated by [Transport for Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_for_Wales_Rail). There are eight lines that serve 20 stations in the city, 26 in the wider urban area (including Taffs Well, [Penarth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penarth) and Dinas Powys) and more than 60 in the South Wales valleys and the [Vale of Glamorgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vale_of_Glamorgan).[^cite_note-180]

### Metro

The [South Wales Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Wales_Metro) is an integrated public transport system under development in south-east Wales, centered on Cardiff. The project is to include the electrification of some of the existing railway lines and the creation of multiple [light rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_rail) and [light rapid transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_rapid_transit) lines. Four lines are under construction with a further three planned. The first lines will link [Penarth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penarth) and [Cardiff Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Bay) to [Radyr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radyr_railway_station), [Treherbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treherbert_railway_station), [Aberdare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberdare_railway_station) and [Merthyr Tydfil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merthyr_Tydfil_railway_station), with plans to also serve [Pontyclun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontyclun), [St Mellons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mellons) and [Porth Teigr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porth_Teigr). Alongside this, current commuter services will be improved with a near-tripling in capacity on some routes to [Bridgend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridgend_railway_station) and [Rhymney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhymney_railway_station).

### Air

Main articles: [Cardiff Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Airport) and [Cardiff Heliport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Heliport)

Domestic and international air links to Cardiff and South & West Wales are provided from [Cardiff Airport (CWL)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Airport), the only international airport in Wales. The airport lies in the village of [Rhoose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhoose), 10 miles (16 km) west of the city. There are regular bus services linking the airport with Cardiff city centre, and a train service from [Rhoose Cardiff International Airport railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhoose_Cardiff_International_Airport_railway_station) to Cardiff Central.

### Road and bus

Main article: [Bus transport in Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus_transport_in_Cardiff)

The [M4 motorway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M4_motorway) connects Cardiff with [Swansea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swansea) to the west and [Newport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newport,_Wales) and London to the east, with four junctions on the M4, including one with the [A48(M)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A48\(M\)_motorway). The [A470](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A470_road) provides an important link from the city to the [Heads of the Valleys road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A465_road). When completed, the [A4232](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A4232_road) – also known as the Peripheral Distributor Road – will form part of the Cardiff ring-road system, along with the M4 motorway between junctions 30 and 33.[^cite_note-Ring_Roads-181]

Cardiff has a comprehensive bus network, whose providers include the [municipal bus company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipal_bus_companies) [Cardiff Bus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Bus) (routes within the city and to Newport, Barry and Penarth), [Adventure Travel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adventure_Travel_\(bus_company\)) (cross-city and to [Cardiff Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Airport)), [Stagecoach South Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagecoach_South_Wales) (to the [South Wales Valleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Wales_Valleys)) and [First Cymru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Cymru) (to Cowbridge and Bridgend). [National Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Express) operates direct services to major cities such as [Bristol](36741.md), London, [Newcastle upon Tyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcastle_upon_Tyne) and [Manchester](20206.md). Most bus services in the city use [Cardiff Bus Interchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Bus_Interchange) located next to Central Station, which opened in 2024 replacing an older structure on the same site, whilst intercity and coach services use the coach terminal located near [Sophia Gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophia_Gardens) in the north of the city centre.

### Cycle

Main article: [Cycling in Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycling_in_Cardiff)

The [Taff Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taff_Trail) is a [walking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walkway) and [cycle path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycle_path) running for 55 miles (90 km) between Cardiff Bay and [Brecon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brecon) in the [Brecon Beacons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brecon_Beacons) National Park. It runs through Bute Park, [Sophia Gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophia_Gardens) and many other green areas within Cardiff. It is possible to cycle the entire distance of the [Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trail) almost completely off-road, as it largely follows the [River Taff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Taff) and many of the disused railways of the Glamorganshire valleys.

[Nextbike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nextbike) previously operated a public bike-hire scheme in the city between March 2018 and January 2024,[^cite_note-182] with the scheme allegedly being scrapped due to theft. Cardiff Council are seeking a replacement operator.

### Water

The Aquabus water taxi runs every hour between the city centre (Taff Mead Embankment) and Cardiff Bay (Mermaid Quay), and between Cardiff Bay and Penarth [Cardiff Bay Barrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Bay_Barrage). Throughout the year, Cardiff Waterbus[^cite_note-183] sail between the Pierhead on The Waterfront and the Penarth end of the Cardiff Bay Barrage with short sightseeing cruises.

Between March and October boats depart from Cardiff Bay for [Flat Holm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_Holm) Island. The *[PS Waverley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PS_Waverley)* and *[MV Balmoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Balmoral_\(1949\))* sail from Britannia Quay (in Roath Basin) to various destinations in the [Bristol Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Channel).

## Telecommunications

See also: [List of Wales dialling codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Wales_dialling_codes)

**029** is the current telephone dialling code for Cardiff,[^cite_note-wales_online_02921-184] as well as for the neighbouring towns of Penarth, Dinas Powys and [Caerphilly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caerphilly). The dialling code is optional when dialling within the area: one can dial between any two phones within the 029 code using only the eight-digit local number.

Prior to the [Big Number Change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Number_Change) on 22 April 2000 the area had shorter, six-digit local numbers with an area code of 01222.[^cite_note-wales_online_02921-184] This was 0222 before May 1995, derived from 0 (indicating it was a trunk call), 22 (CA on a telephone pad, for CArdiff) and 2 (as 220 was used for CAmbridge and 221 for BAth). Before the introduction of automated trunk call dialling, non-local numbers were accessed through a system of manual telephone exchanges, in common with the rest of the United Kingdom.

There remains a common misconception that local numbers are still six digits long and that the code is 02920, even though there are newer Cardiff numbers in the ranges (029) 21xx xxxx and (029) 22xx xxxx.[^cite_note-wales_online_02921-184]

## Education

See also: [List of schools in Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_schools_in_Cardiff)

Cardiff is home to four major institutions of higher education: [Cardiff University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_University), [Cardiff Metropolitan University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Metropolitan_University), [University of South Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_South_Wales) and the [Royal Welsh College of Music & Drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Welsh_College_of_Music_&_Drama).

Cardiff University was founded by a [royal charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_charter) in 1883 as the University College of South Wales and Monmouthshire,[^cite_note-185] is a member of the [Russell Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Group) of leading research led universities, having most of its campus in [Cathays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathays) and the city centre. [Cardiff Metropolitan University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Metropolitan_University) (formerly UWIC) has campuses in the Llandaff, [Cyncoed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyncoed) and city centre areas, and is part of the confederal [University of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wales). The [Royal Welsh College of Music & Drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Welsh_College_of_Music_&_Drama) is a [conservatoire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_school) established in 1949 and is based in the grounds of Cardiff Castle. The [University of South Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_South_Wales)'s Cardiff campus, [Atrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrium_\(Cardiff\)), is home to the Cardiff School of Creative & Cultural Industries and is located in the city centre.

The total number of higher education students in the city is around 43,900.[^cite_note-186][^cite_note-187] The city also has two [further education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Further_education) colleges: [Cardiff and Vale College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_and_Vale_College) and [St David's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_David's_Catholic_College). The former is the result of a merger, completed in August 2011, between [Coleg Glan Hafren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coleg_Glan_Hafren) and [Barry College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_College). Further education is also offered at most high schools in the city.

Cardiff has three state nursery schools (one bilingual), 98 state primary schools (two bilingual, fifteen [Welsh medium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_medium_education)), and 19 state secondary schools (three Welsh medium).[^cite_note-188] There is a campaign to build a fourth Welsh medium secondary school in the south of the city.[^cite_note-189] There are also several independent schools in the city, including [St John's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Cardiff), [Llandaff Cathedral School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cathedral_School,_Llandaff), [Cardiff Sixth Form College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Sixth_Form_College), [Kings Monkton School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kings_Monkton_School) and [Howell's School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howell's_School_Llandaff), a single-sex girls' school (until sixth form). In 2013 Cardiff Sixth Form College came top of the independent senior schools in the UK, which were based on the percentage of A\* and A at [Advanced Level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GCE_Advanced_Level). Also in the top 100 were St John's College and Howell's School.[^cite_note-190]

Notable schools include [Whitchurch High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitchurch_High_School) (the largest secondary school in Wales),[^cite_note-191] [Fitzalan High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitzalan_High_School) (one of the most multi-cultural state schools in the UK),[^cite_note-192] and [Ysgol Gyfun Gymraeg Glantaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ysgol_Gyfun_Gymraeg_Glantaf) (the largest Welsh medium secondary school in Wales).

As well as academic institutions, Cardiff is also home to other educational and learning organisations such as [Techniquest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techniquest), a hands-on science discovery centre that now has franchises throughout Wales, and is part of the Wales Gene Park in collaboration with Cardiff University, [NHS Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NHS_Wales) and the [Welsh Development Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Development_Agency) (WDA).[^cite_note-193] Cardiff is also home to a regional office of the [International Baccalaureate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Baccalaureate) Organisation (IBO).[^cite_note-194]

## Landmarks and attractions

See also: [Architecture of Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_Cardiff) and [Listed buildings in Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Listed_buildings_in_Cardiff)



[Pierhead Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierhead_Building) is part of the [Senedd estate in Cardiff Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senedd_building#Senedd_estate_in_Cardiff_Bay).



[St John the Baptist Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John_the_Baptist_Church,_Cardiff) is the oldest [Church in Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_in_Wales) building in the city.



Cardiff Crown Court is part of the Wales Circuit of [His Majesty's Courts and Tribunals Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/His_Majesty's_Courts_and_Tribunals_Service).

Cardiff has many landmark buildings such as the Principality Stadium, [Pierhead Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierhead_Building), the Welsh National Museum and the Senedd building, the home of the Welsh Parliament. Cardiff is also known for Cardiff Castle, [St David's Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_David's_Hall), [St John the Baptist Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John_the_Baptist_Church,_Cardiff), [Llandaff Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llandaff_Cathedral) and the [Wales Millennium Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_Millennium_Centre).

[Cardiff Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Castle) is a major tourist attraction in the city and is situated in the heart of the [city centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_city_centre). The [National History Museum at St Fagans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Fagans_National_History_Museum) in Cardiff is a large open-air museum housing dozens of buildings from throughout Welsh history that have been moved to the site in Cardiff. The Civic Centre in [Cathays Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathays_Park) comprises a collection of [Edwardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwardian_architecture) buildings such as the [City Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_Hall,_Cardiff), [National Museum and Gallery of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_Cardiff), [Cardiff Crown Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Crown_Court), and buildings forming part of Cardiff University, together with more modern civic buildings. These buildings are laid out around the Queen Alexandra Gardens, a formal park which contains the [Welsh National War Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_National_War_Memorial) and a number of other, smaller memorials.

In addition to Cardiff Castle, [Castell Coch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castell_Coch) is a castle in [Tongwynlais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tongwynlais), in the north of the city. The current castle is an elaborately decorated Victorian [folly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folly) designed by [William Burges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Burges) for the Marquess and built in the 1870s, as an occasional retreat. However, the Victorian castle stands on the footings of a much older medieval castle possibly built by [Ifor Bach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ifor_Bach), a regional baron with links to Cardiff Castle also. The exterior has become a popular location for film and television productions. It rarely fulfilled its intended role as a retreat for the Butes, who seldom stayed there. For the Marquess, the pleasure had been in its creation, a pleasure lost following Burges's death in 1881.

Cardiff claims the largest concentration of [castles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castles) of any city in the world.[^cite_note-195] As well as Cardiff Castle and Castell Coch, there are the remains of two [motte-and-bailey castles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motte-and-bailey_castle) in [Morganstown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morganstown) and [Rhiwbina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhiwbina), known as [Morganstown Castle Mound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morganstown_Castle_Mound) and [Twmpath Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twmpath_Castle) or Twmpath Motte (also known as *Caer Cynwrig*) respectively.[^cite_note-Morganstown-196][^cite_note-Twmpath_Motte-197] [Twmpath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twmpath) (being a Welsh word for a small mound),[^cite_note-198] which along with a castle at Whitchurch (known as Treoda and destroyed by housing in the 1960s) formed an arc of fortifications which divided the Norman lordship from the Welsh lordship of Senghenydd.[^cite_note-199] Further up the Cefn Cibwr ridge on the boundary with Caerphilly there is also another ruined castle, known as [Morgraig Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morgraig_Castle) ([Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language): *Castell Morgraig*). Archaeological evidence suggests this castle was never finished, and it is debated whether the fortification was of Norman or Welsh origin. The concentration of castles indicates the moveable nature of the border between the Norman lordship of Glamorgan, centred at Cardiff, and its Welsh neighbours to the north.

There is also the ruined [Llandaff Bishop's Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop's_Palace,_Llandaff), also known as Llandaff Castle,[^cite_note-Britannica-200] which was the home of the medieval bishops, which was destroyed about 1403–1404 by the Welsh leader [Owain Glyndŵr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owain_Glynd%C5%B5r). Now only the ruined gatehouse remains.[^cite_note-Britannica-200] Not strictly a castle in the historical sense, [Saint Fagans Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Fagans_National_History_Museum) is a preserved 17th-century manor house, once the seat of the [Earls of Plymouth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_of_Plymouth).

Other major tourist attractions are the [Cardiff Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Bay) regeneration sites, which include the recently opened Wales Millennium Centre and the Senedd building, and many other cultural and sites of interest, including the [Cardiff Bay Barrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Bay_Barrage) and the famous [Coal Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_Exchange). The [New Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Theatre_\(Cardiff\)) was founded in 1906 and refurbished in the 1980s. Until the opening of the Wales Millennium Centre in 2004, it was the premier venue in Wales for touring theatre and dance companies. Other venues popular for concerts and sporting events include [Cardiff International Arena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_International_Arena), St David's Hall and the Principality Stadium. [Cardiff Story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Story), a museum documenting the city's history, has been open to the public since the spring of 2011.

Cardiff has over 1,000 listed buildings, ranging from the more prominent buildings such as the castles, to smaller buildings, houses and structures.[^cite_note-201] Cathedral Road was developed by the 3rd Marquis of Bute and is lined by fine villas, some backing on to Sophia Gardens.

Cardiff has walks of special interest for tourists and [ramblers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walking) alike, such as the [Centenary Walk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Centenary_Walk), which runs for 2+1⁄4 miles (3.5 km) within Cardiff city centre. This route passes through many of Cardiff's landmarks and historic buildings. The [Animal Wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_Wall), designed by [William Burges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Burges) in 1866, marks the south edge of Bute Park on Castle Street. It bears 15 carved animal statues.

## Culture and recreation

Main articles: [Culture and recreation in Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_and_recreation_in_Cardiff), [List of cultural venues in Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cultural_venues_in_Cardiff), and [List of public art in Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_public_art_in_Cardiff)



[Wales Millennium Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_Millennium_Centre)

Cardiff has many cultural sites varying from the historical Cardiff Castle and out of town [Castell Coch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castell_Coch) to the more modern [Wales Millennium Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_Millennium_Centre) and Cardiff Bay. Cardiff was a finalist in the [European Capital of Culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Capital_of_Culture) 2008.[^cite_note-202] In recent years Cardiff has grown in stature as a tourist destination, with recent accolades including Cardiff being voted the eighth favourite UK city by readers of *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)*.[^cite_note-203]

The city was also listed as one of the top 10 destinations in the UK on the official British tourist boards website Visit Britain,[^cite_note-204] and US travel guide Frommers have listed Cardiff as one of 13 top destinations worldwide for 2008.[^cite_note-205] Annual events in Cardiff that have become regular appearances in Cardiff's calendar include [Sparks in the Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparks_in_the_Park), [The Great British Cheese Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_British_Cheese_Festival), [Pride Cymru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pride_Cymru) (formerly Cardiff Mardi Gras), Cardiff Winter Wonderland, Cardiff Festival and [Made in Roath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Made_in_Roath).

### Music and performing arts

Main article: [Music of Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Cardiff)



[Utilita Arena Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_International_Arena)

A large number of concerts are held in the city, the larger ones at St David's Hall, Cardiff International Arena and occasionally the Principality Stadium. A number of festivals are also held in Cardiff, the largest being the [Cardiff Big Weekend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_big_weekend) Festival, held annually in the city centre in the summer and playing host to free musical performances (from artists such as [Ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ash_\(band\)), [Jimmy Cliff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Cliff), [Cerys Matthews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerys_Matthews), the [Fun Loving Criminals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fun_Loving_Criminals), [Soul II Soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_II_Soul) and [the Magic Numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magic_Numbers)), fairground rides and cultural events such as a Children's Festival that takes place in the grounds of Cardiff Castle. The annual festival claims to be the UK's largest free outdoor festival, attracting over 250,000 visitors in 2007.[^cite_note-206]

Cardiff hosted the [National Eisteddfod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Eisteddfod) in 1883, 1899, 1938, 1960, 1978, 2008 and 2018. Cardiff is unique in Wales in having two permanent [stone circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone_circles) used by the Gorsedd of [Bards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bard) during Eisteddfodau. The original circle stands in Gorsedd Gardens in front of the [National Museum Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_Cardiff) while its 1978 replacement is situated in [Bute Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bute_Park). Since 1983, Cardiff has hosted the [BBC Cardiff Singer of the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Cardiff_Singer_of_the_World) competition, a world-renowned event on the opera calendar which is held every two years. The city also hosts smaller events.

The [Wales Millennium Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_Millennium_Centre) hosts performances of opera, ballet, dance, comedy, musicals and is home to the [BBC National Orchestra of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_National_Orchestra_of_Wales). St David's Hall (which hosts the Singer of the World competition) has regular performances of classical music and ballet as well as music of other genres. The largest of Cardiff's theatres is the [New Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Theatre_\(Cardiff\)), situated in the city centre just off Queen Street. Other such venues include the [Sherman Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherman_Theatre), [Chapter Arts Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapter_Arts_Centre) and [the Gate Arts Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gate_Arts_Centre).

The Cardiff music scene is established and wide-ranging: home to the [BBC National Orchestra of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_National_Orchestra_of_Wales) and the [Welsh National Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_National_Opera); has produced several leading acts; has acted as a springboard for Welsh bands to become famous. Acts hailing from Cardiff include [Charlotte Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_Church), [Shirley Bassey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shirley_Bassey), [Iwan Rheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwan_Rheon), [the Oppressed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Oppressed), [Kids In Glass Houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kids_In_Glass_Houses), [Los Campesinos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Campesinos), [the Hot Puppies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hot_Puppies), [the School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_School_\(UK\)), [We're No Heroes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/We're_No_Heroes), [Budgie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budgie_\(band\)) and [Shakin' Stevens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakin'_Stevens). Also, artists such as [Stereophonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereophonics), [the Automatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Automatic),[^cite_note-207] [Manic Street Preachers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manic_Street_Preachers),[^cite_note-208] [Lostprophets](492820.md),[^cite_note-209] [Underworld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underworld_\(band\)), [Super Furry Animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Furry_Animals), [Catatonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catatonia_\(band\)) and [Bullet for My Valentine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullet_for_My_Valentine) have links with the city and are associated with the Cardiff music scene.[^cite_note-210] In 2010, Cardiff was named the UK's second "most musical" city by [PRS for Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PRS_for_Music).[^cite_note-211]

### Sporting venues

Cardiff's former municipal baths opened in 1862, as [Victorian Turkish baths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_Turkish_baths), and were taken over by the City Council in 1873, before closing over a century later.[^cite_note-213]

Sporting venues include the [Principality Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Stadium) – the national stadium and home of the [Wales national rugby union team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_national_rugby_union_team) – [Sophia Gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophia_Gardens_\(cricket_ground\)) for [Glamorgan County Cricket Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glamorgan_County_Cricket_Club), [Cardiff City Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_City_Stadium) for [Cardiff City F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_City_F.C.) and the [Wales football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_national_football_team), [Cardiff International Sports Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_International_Sports_Stadium), home of [Cardiff Amateur Athletic Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Amateur_Athletic_Club), [Cardiff Arms Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Arms_Park) for [Cardiff Blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Blues) and [Cardiff RFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_RFC) rugby union teams, and [Ice Arena Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_Arena_Wales) for [Cardiff Devils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Devils) ice hockey team. It hosted the [1958 British Empire and Commonwealth Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1958_British_Empire_and_Commonwealth_Games) and was dubbed European City of Sport for its role in international sporting events in 2009 and again in 2014.[^cite_note-214] The Principality Stadium hosted 11 football matches during the [2012 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics), including the opening event and the men's bronze medal match.[^cite_note-215]

### Recreation



[Bute Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bute_Park)

Cardiff has a strong nightlife. Most clubs and bars are situated in the city centre, especially St Mary Street. More recently Cardiff Bay has built up a strong night scene, with many modern bars and restaurants. The Brewery Quarter on St Mary Street is a recently developed venue for bars and restaurant with a central courtyard. Charles Street is also a popular part of the city.



The lake at [Roath Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roath_Park), including the lighthouse erected as a memorial to [Captain Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Falcon_Scott)

Cardiff is known for its extensive parks and other green spaces covering around 10% of the city's total area.[^cite_note-trends-216] In 2022 according to research by Drinking Straw Cardiff has the highest percentage of green spaces in the UK and the fourth highest in Europe.[^cite_note-217] Cardiff's main park, Bute Park (which was formerly the castle grounds) extends northwards from the top of one of Cardiff's main shopping street (Queen Street); when combined with the adjacent [Llandaff Fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llandaff_Fields) and Pontcanna Fields to the north-west it produces a massive open space skirting the River Taff. Other popular parks include [Roath Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roath_Park) in the north, donated to the city by the [3rd Marquess of Bute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Crichton-Stuart,_3rd_Marquess_of_Bute) in 1887, which includes a popular boating lake; [Victoria Park, Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_Park,_Cardiff)'s first official park; and [Thompson's Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thompson's_Park), formerly home to an [aviary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviary) removed in the 1970s. Wild open spaces include Howardian Local Nature Reserve, 32 acres (13 ha) of the lower Rhymney valley in Penylan noted for its orchids,[^cite_note-218] and [Forest Farm Country Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_Farm_Country_Park), over 150 acres (61 ha) along the River Taff in Whitchurch.

## Sport

Main article: [Sport in Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_in_Cardiff)

See also: [Leisure centres in Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leisure_centres_in_Cardiff), [List of stadia in Wales by capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_stadia_in_Wales_by_capacity), and [Rugby in Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_in_Cardiff)



Cardiff Arms Park

Cardiff hosts many high-profile sporting events at local, national and international level and in recognition of the city's commitment to sport for all was awarded the title of European Capital of Sport 2014.[^cite_note-221][^cite_note-222][^cite_note-223] Organised sports have been held in the city since the early 19th century.[^cite_note-Arms_Park_1-224] National home sporting fixtures are nearly always played in the city. All Wales's multi-sports agencies and many of the [country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countries_of_the_United_Kingdom)'s [sports governing bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governing_bodies_of_sports_in_Wales) have their headquarters in Cardiff and the city's many top quality venues have attracted world-famous sports events, sometimes unrelated to Cardiff or to Wales. In 2008/09, 61% of Cardiff residents regularly participated in sport and active recreation, the highest percentage in ll 22 local authorities in Wales.[^cite_note-225]

[Rugby union](25405.md) fans around the world have long been familiar with the old National Stadium, Cardiff Arms Park, and its successor the [Principality Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality_Stadium), which hosted the [FA Cup](11237.md) for six years (from 2001 to 2006) it took to rebuild [Wembley Stadium](8913012.md). In 2009, Cardiff hosted the first [Ashes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_cricket_team_in_England_in_2009) cricket test between England and Australia to be held in Wales. Cardiff hosted eight football matches of the [London 2012 Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_2012_Olympics).[^cite_note-226]



[Principality Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality_Stadium)

[Cardiff City F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_City_F.C.) (founded 1899 as Riverside AFC) played their home games at [Ninian Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ninian_Park) from 1910 until the end of the 2008–09 season. The club's new home is the [Cardiff City Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_City_Stadium), which they initially rented to the [Cardiff Blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Blues), the city's professional rugby union team, the Blues returning to the Arms Park in 2012. Cardiff City have played in the English [Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Football_League) since the 1920–21 season, climbing to Division 1 after one season.[^cite_note-1927_1-227][^cite_note-1927_2-228] Cardiff City are the only non-English team to have won the [FA Cup](11237.md), beating [Arsenal](2174.md) in the [1927 final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1927_FA_Cup_Final) at [Wembley Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wembley_Stadium_\(1923\)).[^cite_note-1927_2-228] They were runners up to [Portsmouth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portsmouth_F.C.) in the [2008 final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_FA_Cup_Final), losing 1–0 at the new Wembley Stadium.[^cite_note-1927_3-229] In the 2013/14 and 2018/19 seasons Cardiff City played in the English [Premier League](11250.md).

[Cardiff Metropolitan University F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Metropolitan_University_F.C.) of the Athletic Union of Cardiff Metropolitan University, based in Cyncoed, play in the [Cymru Premier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cymru_Premier), having been promoted from Welsh League Division One in 2016. They were winners of the [Welsh League Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_League_Cup) for the 2018–19 season. [^cite_note-1927_4-230]

Cardiff has numerous smaller clubs including [Bridgend Street A.F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridgend_Street_A.F.C.), [Caerau (Ely) A.F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caerau_\(Ely\)_A.F.C.), [Cardiff Corinthians F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Corinthians_F.C.), [Cardiff Draconians F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Draconians_F.C.), [Cardiff Grange Harlequins A.F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Grange_Harlequins_A.F.C.), and [Ely Rangers A.F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ely_Rangers_A.F.C.), which all play in the [Welsh football league system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_football_league_system).[^cite_note-Peldroed_1-231]



[Sport Wales National Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_Wales_National_Centre), Cardiff, headquarters of [Sport Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_Wales), the [Welsh Sports Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Sports_Association) and the [Federation of Disability Sport Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federation_of_Disability_Sport_Wales)

In addition to men's football teams [Cardiff City Ladies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_City_Ladies_F.C.) of the [FA Women's Premier League Southern Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FA_Women's_Premier_League_Southern_Division) are based in the city. Teams in the [Welsh Premier Women's Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Premier_Women's_Football_League) are [Cardiff Met. Ladies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Met._Ladies_F.C.), [Cyncoed Ladies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyncoed_Ladies_F.C.) and [Cardiff City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_City_F.C._\(women\)).

During the 1990s, London-based football club [Wimbledon FC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wimbledon_FC) expressed interest in relocating to Cardiff, having been without a home of their own since exiting [Plough Lane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plough_Lane_\(1912%E2%80%9398\)) stadium in 1991 and sharing with [Crystal Palace FC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_Palace_FC) at [Selhurst Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selhurst_Park). The relocation of the club to Cardiff did not happen; in 2003, the club moved to [Milton Keynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Keynes) and a year later rebranded as [Milton Keynes Dons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Keynes_Dons_FC).[^cite_note-Warren-232]

Cardiff Arms Park ([Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language): *Parc yr Arfau Caerdydd*), in central Cardiff, is among the world's most famous venues—being the scene of three Welsh [Grand Slams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Slam_\(rugby_union\)) in the 1970s ([1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_Five_Nations_Championship), [1976](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976_Five_Nations_Championship) and [1978](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1978_Five_Nations_Championship)) and six Five Nations titles in nine years—and was the venue for Wales's games in the [1991 Rugby World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1991_Rugby_World_Cup).[^cite_note-Arms_Park_2-233][^cite_note-rwc_1-234][^cite_note-Arms_Park_3-235][^cite_note-Arms_Park_4-236] The Arms Park has a sporting history dating back to at least the 1850s, when Cardiff Cricket Club (formed 1819) relocated to the site.[^cite_note-Arms_Park_1-224] The ground was donated to Cardiff CC in 1867 by the Marquess of Bute. Cardiff Cricket Club shared the ground with Cardiff Rugby Football Club (founded 1876) — forming [Cardiff Athletic Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Athletic_Club) between them — until 1966, when the cricket section moved to Sophia Gardens. Cardiff Athletic Club and the [Welsh Rugby Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Rugby_Union) established two stadia on the site—Cardiff RFC played at their stadium at the northern end of the site, and the Wales national rugby union team played international matches at the National Stadium, Cardiff Arms Park, which opened in 1970. The National Stadium was replaced by the 74,500 capacity Millennium Stadium ([Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language): *Stadiwm y Mileniwm*) in 1999—in time for the [1999 Rugby World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_Rugby_World_Cup)—and is home stadium to the [Wales national rugby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_national_rugby_union_team) and [football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_national_football_team) teams for international matches.[^cite_note-Arms_Park_1-224][^cite_note-Arms_Park_2-233][^cite_note-Mileniwm_1-237][^cite_note-Mileniwm_2-238] In addition to Wales's [Six Nations Championship](26547.md) and other international games, the Principality Stadium held four matches in the [2007 Rugby World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_Rugby_World_Cup) and six FA Cup finals (from the [2001–02](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001%E2%80%9302_FA_Cup) to [2005–06](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005%E2%80%9306_FA_Cup) seasons) while [Wembley Stadium](8913012.md) was being rebuilt.[^cite_note-rwc_1-234]



SWALEC Stadium

[Cardiff Cricket Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Cricket_Club) was formed in 1819 and [Glamorgan County Cricket Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glamorgan_CCC) has competed as a first-class county since 1921. Its headquarters and ground is the [SWALEC Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SWALEC_Stadium), [Sophia Gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophia_Gardens), since moving from Cardiff Arms Park in 1966. The Sophia Gardens stadium underwent multimillion-pound improvements since being selected to host the first ["England"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England_cricket_team) v [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia_national_cricket_team) [Test match](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_cricket) of the [2009 Ashes series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_cricket_team_in_England_in_2009).[^cite_note-Arms_Park_1-224][^cite_note-Sophia_1-239] [The Hundred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hundred_\(cricket\)) franchise team [Welsh Fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Fire) is also based at the stadium.

Cardiff has a long association with boxing, from ['Peerless' Jim Driscoll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Driscoll) — born in Cardiff in 1880 — to more recent, high-profile fights staged in the city.[^cite_note-Peerless_1-240] These include the [WBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Boxing_Council) [Lennox Lewis vs. Frank Bruno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lennox_Lewis_vs._Frank_Bruno) [heavyweight](591011.md) championship fight at the Arms Park in 1993, and many of [Joe Calzaghe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Calzaghe)'s fights, between 2003 and 2007.

Cardiff's professional ice hockey team, the [Cardiff Devils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Devils), plays in the 3,000-seat [Ice Arena Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_Arena_Wales) in the Cardiff International Sports Village. It plays in the 12-team professional [Elite Ice Hockey League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elite_Ice_Hockey_League). Founded in 1986, it was one of the most successful British teams in the 1990s.

Cardiff's only American-flag football team is the Hurricanes. It won the British Championship in 2014 after falling short by 2 points in a quarter-final to eventual winners, the London Rebels, the previous year. It is based at Roath Recreational Ground.



[Cardiff International Pool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_International_Pool) at the [International Sports Village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_International_Sports_Village), Cardiff Bay

The [1958 Commonwealth Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1958_British_Empire_and_Commonwealth_Games) were hosted by Cardiff. These involved 1,130 athletes from 35 national teams competing in 94 events.[^cite_note-Empire_1958-241] One of the venues for those Games—The Wales Empire Swimming Pool—was demolished in 1998 to make way for the Principality Stadium. The GBP32m [Cardiff International Pool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_International_Pool) in [Cardiff Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Bay), opened to the public on 12 January 2008 — part of the GBP1bn International Sports Village (ISV) — is the only Olympic-standard swimming pool in Wales. When complete, the ISV complex will provide Olympic standard facilities for sports including [boxing](4243.md) and [fencing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fencing), gymnastics, [judo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judo), [white water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_water) events (including [canoeing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitewater_canoeing) and [kayaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitewater_kayaking)) and [wrestling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrestling) as well as a [snow dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indoor_skiing) with real snow for [skiing](28478.md) and [snowboarding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowboarding), an [arena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Arena) for public [ice skating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_skating) and [ice hockey](14790.md) and a hotel.[^cite_note-Pool_1-242][^cite_note-ISV_1-243] Some of the sports facilities at the ISV were to be used as training venues for the [London 2012 Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_2012_Olympics).[^cite_note-ISV_2-244]



A stage of Wales Rally GB, hosted inside the [Principality Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality_Stadium)

The Principality Stadium hosts motor-sport events such as the [World Rally Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Rally_Championship), as part of [Wales Rally GB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_Rally_GB). The first indoor special stages of the World Rally Championship were held at the Principality Stadium in September 2005 and have been an annual event since.[^cite_note-Rali_1-245] The [British Speedway Grand Prix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speedway_Grand_Prix_of_Great_Britain), one of the World Championship events, is held at the Principality Stadium.[^cite_note-Mileniwm_2-238] While the track—a temporary, purpose built, shale oval—is not universally loved, the venue is considered the best of the World Championship's 11 rounds.[^cite_note-Speedway_1-246]

The [Cardiff International Sports Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_International_Sports_Stadium), opened 19 January 2009, replacing the [Cardiff Athletics Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Athletics_Stadium), demolished to make way for the Cardiff City Stadium. It has a 4,953 capacity as a multi sport/special event venue, offering certificated international [track and field athletics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Track_and_field_athletics) facilities, including an international standard external throws area.[^cite_note-Stadiwm_1-247][^cite_note-Stadiwm_2-248][^cite_note-Stadiwm_3-249] The stadium houses the Headquarters of [Welsh Athletics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Athletics), the sport's governing body for Wales.[^cite_note-Stadiwm_4-250] The city's indoor track and field athletics sports venue is the [National Indoor Athletics Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Indoor_Athletics_Centre), an international athletics and multi sports centre at the [University of Wales Institute, Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wales_Institute,_Cardiff) Campus, [Cyncoed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyncoed).[^cite_note-Stadiwm_5-251]

The [Cardiff Half Marathon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Half_Marathon) takes place each October and is one of the largest road races in the United Kingdom, attracting over 20,000 participants and many overseas visitors annually. The event is organised by the not-for-profit social enterprise Run 4 Wales, and has grown considerably since its establishment in 2003. It has hosted the World (2016) Commonwealth (2018) British (2014/2015) and Welsh (Annually) Half Marathon Championships and has held a [World Athletics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Athletics) Elite Road Race Label since 2017.[^cite_note-252] The race is also a part of the SuperHalfs, a series of leading international half marathon races which also includes Lisbon, Prague, Berlin, Valencia and Copenhagen.

## Notable people

Main article: [List of people from Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_people_from_Cardiff)

See also: [Category\:People from Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:People_from_Cardiff)

Many notable people have hailed from Cardiff, ranging from historical figures such as the 12th-century Welsh leader [Ifor Bach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ifor_Bach) to more recent figures such as [Roald Dahl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roald_Dahl), [Ken Follett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Follett), [Griff Rhys Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griff_Rhys_Jones), [Catrin Dafydd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catrin_Dafydd), and the former Blue Peter presenter [Gethin Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gethin_Jones).

Notable actors include [Ioan Gruffudd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ioan_Gruffudd) (*[Fantastic 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fantastic_4_\(2005_film\))*), [Iwan Rheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwan_Rheon) (*[Game of Thrones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_of_Thrones)*) and [Matthew Rhys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Rhys) (*[The Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Americans)).* Also notable is [Siân Grigg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Si%C3%A2n_Grigg), BAFTA winner and Oscar nominated Hollywood make-up artist.

The Abse family from Cardiff include the award-winning poet [Dannie Abse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dannie_Abse) and his brothers [Leo Abse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Abse), a prominent [Labour](19279158.md) MP, and [Wilfred Abse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilfred_Abse) a [psychoanalyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychoanalyst).

[Adam Phillips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Phillips_\(psychologist\)) the psychoanalyst, author and essayist was born in Cardiff.

The city has been the birthplace of sports stars such as [Tanni Grey-Thompson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanni_Grey-Thompson) and [Colin Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Jackson), as well as many [Premier League](11250.md), [Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Football_League) and international footballers, such as [Craig Bellamy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craig_Bellamy), [Gareth Bale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gareth_Bale), [Ryan Giggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryan_Giggs), [Joe Ledley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Ledley), and former managers of the [Wales national football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_national_football_team) [Terry Yorath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_Yorath) and [John Toshack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Toshack). International [rugby league](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_league) players from Cardiff include [Frank Whitcombe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Whitcombe), [Billy Boston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billy_Boston), [David Willicombe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Willicombe) and [Colin Dixon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Dixon). International [rugby union](25405.md) players include [Sam Warburton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Warburton), [Jamie Roberts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamie_Roberts), [Jamie Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamie_Robinson_\(rugby_union\)), [Nicky Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicky_Robinson_\(rugby_union\)), [Rhys Patchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhys_Patchell), and baseball internationals include [George Whitcombe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Whitcombe) and [Ted Peterson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Peterson).

[Saint Teilo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Teilo) (c. 500 – 9 February c. 560) is the patron saint of Cardiff. He was a British Christian monk, bishop, and founder of monasteries and churches. Reputed to be a cousin, friend, and disciple of Saint David, he was Bishop of Llandaff and founder of the first church at Llandaff Cathedral, where his tomb is. His Saint's Day is 9 February.

Cardiff is also well known for its musicians. [Ivor Novello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivor_Novello) inspired the [Ivor Novello Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivor_Novello_Awards). [Idloes Owen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idloes_Owen), founder of the [Welsh National Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_National_Opera), lived in Llandaff. Dame [Shirley Bassey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shirley_Bassey) was born and raised in Cardiff. [Charlotte Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_Church) is famous as a crossover classical/pop singer. [Shakin' Stevens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakin'_Stevens) was one of the top-selling male artists in the UK during the 1980s. [Tigertailz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigertailz), a popular [glam metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glam_metal) act in the 1980s, also hailed from Cardiff. A number of Cardiff-based bands, such as [Catatonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catatonia_\(band\)) and [Super Furry Animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Furry_Animals), were popular in the 1990s. Drum and bass artist Mr. Traumatik, born and raised in Cardiff, gained popularity in the 2010s.

Taff Groves, notably born and raised in Cardiff's Ely and Cathays districts is a [former SAS soldier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_former_Special_Air_Service_personnel). He gained recognition during the 2013 [Westgate shopping mall attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westgate_shopping_mall_attack) in [Nairobi](21482.md), [Kenya](188171.md), where he helped rescue hundreds of civilians while unarmed <https://www.walesonline.co.uk/news/wales-news/untold-story-welsh-sas-hero-27777696>.

## Namesakes
