The **sovereign citizen movement** (sometimes abbreviated as **SovCits**)[^cite_note-living-1][^cite_note-2] is a loose group of anti-government activists, [conspiracy theorists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conspiracy_theorists), [vexatious litigants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vexatious_litigant), [tax protesters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_protester) and financial [scammers](96637.md) found mainly in English-speaking [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law) countries—the [United States](3434750.md), [Canada](5042916.md), [Australia](4689264.md), the [United Kingdom](31717.md), and [New Zealand](4913064.md). Sovereign citizens have a [pseudolegal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudolegal) belief system based on misinterpretations of [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law), and claim not to be subject to any government statutes unless they consent to them.[^cite_note-Laird2014-3][^cite_note-SPLC2022-4] The movement appeared in the U.S. in the early 1970s and has since expanded to other countries; the similar [freeman on the land movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeman_on_the_land_movement) emerged during the 2000s in Canada before spreading to other [Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations) countries.[^cite_note-Netolitzky20182-5] Sovereign citizen ideas have also been incorporated by other fringe movements in the United States and abroad, such as the [*Reichsbürger*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichsb%C3%BCrger_movement) in [Germany](11867.md) and [Austria](26964606.md). The [FBI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI) has called sovereign citizens "anti-government extremists who believe that even though they physically reside in this country, they are separate or 'sovereign' from the United States".[^cite_note-FBI2010-6]

The sovereign citizen phenomenon is a primary contemporary source of pseudolaw. Sovereign citizens believe that courts or law enforcements authorities have no [jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction) over people and that certain procedures and [loopholes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loophole) can make one immune to government laws and regulations.[^cite_note-furiouspaper-7] They regard most forms of [taxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxation) as illegitimate and reject state documentation such as [Social Security numbers](22255742.md), [driver's licenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Driver's_license), and [vehicle registration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle_registration).[^cite_note-8] The movement's appeal to people facing financial or legal difficulties or wishing to resist perceived government oppression has helped it grow significantly during times of economic or social crisis.[^cite_note-wastingtime-9][^cite_note-\:1-10] Most schemes promoted by sovereign citizens aim to avoid paying taxes, ignore laws, eliminate debts, or extract money from the government.[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4] Sovereign citizen arguments have no basis in law and have never been successful in court.[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4][^cite_note-furiouspaper-7]

American sovereign citizens claim that the [United States federal government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_federal_government) is illegitimate,[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4][^cite_note-carey-11] and those outside the U.S. hold similar beliefs about their countries' governments. The movement can be traced to [American far-right groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_right_\(United_States\)) such as the [Posse Comitatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posse_Comitatus_\(organization\)) and the [constitutionalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutionalism_in_the_United_States) wing of the [militia movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Militia_movement).[^cite_note-NYT82313-12] It was originally associated with [White supremacy](33178.md) and [antisemitism](1078.md) but now attracts people of various ethnicities, including a significant number of [African Americans](2154.md).[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4] The latter sometimes belong to self-declared "[Moorish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moorish_sovereign_citizens)" sects.[^cite_note-SPLC2019-13]

Most sovereign citizens are not violent,[^cite_note-Laird2014-3][^cite_note-marple-14] but the methods the movement advocates are illegal. Sovereign citizens notably adhere to the fraudulent schemes promoted by the [redemption "A4V" movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redemption_movement). Many have been found guilty of offenses such as [tax evasion](2256795.md), forgery, threatening public officials, bank fraud, and traffic violations.[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4][^cite_note-FBI2010-6][^cite_note-adl-15] Two of the most important crackdowns by U.S. authorities on sovereign citizen organizations were the 1996 case of the [Montana Freemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montana_Freemen) and the 2018 sentencing of self-proclaimed judge [Bruce Doucette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Doucette) and his associates.[^cite_note-16]

Because some have engaged in armed confrontations with law enforcement,[^cite_note-Laird2014-3][^cite_note-ontherise-17] the FBI classifies "sovereign citizen extremists" as [domestic terrorists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestic_terrorism_in_the_United_States).[^cite_note-FBI2011-18] [Terry Nichols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_Nichols), one of the perpetrators of the 1995 [Oklahoma City bombing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City_bombing), subscribed to sovereign citizen ideology.[^cite_note-adl-15][^cite_note-\:2-19] In surveys conducted in 2014 and 2015, representatives of U.S. law enforcement ranked the risk of terrorism from the sovereign citizen movement higher than the risk from any other group.[^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21] In 2015, the Australian [New South Wales Police Force](563673.md) identified sovereign citizens as a potential terrorist threat.[^cite_note-NSW2015ABC-22]

## History

### Origin

See also: [Radical right (United States)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_right_\(United_States\)), [Posse Comitatus (organization)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posse_Comitatus_\(organization\)), [American militia movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_militia_movement), [Patriot movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriot_movement), [Christian Patriot movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Patriot_movement), and [Tax protester history in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_protester_history_in_the_United_States)

The sovereign citizen movement originated from a combination of [tax protester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_protester) ideas, 1960s–1970s radical and [racist](2162835.md) anti-government movements,[^cite_note-kent2015-23] and [pseudolaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudolaw), which has existed in the U.S. since at least the 1950s.[^cite_note-furiouspaper-7] The movement's belief in the illegitimacy of [federal income tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_income_tax) gradually expanded to challenging the legitimacy of the government.[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4]

The concept of a "sovereign citizen" whose rights are unfairly denied appeared in 1971 within the [Posse Comitatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posse_Comitatus_\(organization\)) as part of the teachings of [Christian Identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Identity) minister [William Potter Gale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Potter_Gale).[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4][^cite_note-carey-11] The Posse Comitatus, whose name derived from the [historical militias led by local sheriffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posse_comitatus),[^cite_note-24] was a [far-right](221220.md), anti-government movement[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4] that denounced [income tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_tax), [debt-based currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_theory_of_money), and [debt collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt_collection) as tools of [Jewish control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Jewish_conspiracy) over the United States.[^cite_note-balleck-25] The roots of the sovereign citizen movement were thus strongly associated with [white supremacist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_supremacist) and [antisemitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisemitism_in_the_United_States) ideologies.[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4][^cite_note-carey-11] Gale's racist beliefs were far from unique, but he innovated by devising a "legal" philosophy about the government's illegitimacy. Posse Comitatus members used the term "sovereign citizens" to convey the idea that they were entitled to enforce their interpretation of the Constitution.[^cite_note-carey-11]

After originating in that particular group, the sovereign citizen concept went on to influence the broader tax protester and [Christian Patriot movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Patriot_movement).[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4][^cite_note-carey-11] Until the 1990s, observers primarily classified the Posse Comitatus as a tax-protest movement rather than an outright far-right extremist group. The Posse Comitatus, Christian Identity, and militia movements did not fully overlap, but they shared members and influenced one another.[^cite_note-ascendant-26]

### Developments

See also: [Montana Freemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montana_Freemen) and [Moorish sovereign citizens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moorish_sovereign_citizens)

In the early 1980s, tax protester [Gordon Kahl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Kahl), a former [Posse Comitatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posse_Comitatus_\(organization\)) member, helped radicalize sovereign citizens' anti-government rhetoric. Kahl considered the government not only illegitimate but actively hostile to Americans' interests. After he was killed in 1983 during a shootout with law enforcement, the movement considered him a martyr, which helped amplify his views.[^cite_note-ascendant-26]

The movement garnered more support during the [American farm crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980s_farm_crisis) of the late 1970s and 1980s, which coincided with a general financial crisis in the U.S. and Canada.[^cite_note-kent2015-23] The farm crisis saw the rise of anti-government protesters selling sham [debt relief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt_relief) programs,[^cite_note-27] some of whom were associated with far-right groups. They included Roger Elvick,[^cite_note-28] a member of a successor organization of the Posse Comitatus. Elvick conceived the [redemption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redemption_movement) methods, a set of fraudulent debt and tax payment schemes[^cite_note-29] that became part of sovereign citizen ideology.[^cite_note-spl4-30]

As the Posse Comitatus movement evolved, its members created pseudolegal bodies that claimed to speak with the authority of "natural law" or "common law" and to supersede the government's legal system.[^cite_note-\:3-31] The most common tactic of these "common law courts" was to issue [false liens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_lien) against their enemies' property.[^cite_note-ascendant-26]

After the 1995 [Oklahoma City bombing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City_bombing), one perpetrator of which adhered to sovereign citizen ideology, observers categorized the Posse Comitatus as far-right extremism, sidelining the tax protester aspect.[^cite_note-\:2-19] Around the end of the decade, the term "Posse Comitatus" was supplanted by "sovereign citizen". This mirrored a change in the language adherents used, which reflected their increased focus on personal liberty secured through absolute ownership of personal property.[^cite_note-ascendant-26][^cite_note-\:1-10]

In 1996, the case of the [Montana Freemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montana_Freemen) brought the sovereign citizen movement to public attention. The Montana Freemen were [Christian Patriot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Patriot) sovereign citizens and direct ideological descendants of the Posse Comitatus:[^cite_note-carey-11] they used false liens to harass public officials[^cite_note-32] and committed [bank fraud](486777.md) with counterfeit checks and money orders.[^cite_note-33] The group surrendered in June 1996 after 81 days of armed standoff with the FBI.[^cite_note-34] Several members of the Montana Freemen received long prison sentences. The group's leader, LeRoy M. Schweitzer, died in prison in 2011.[^cite_note-35]

Over time, the movement expanded beyond its original [white nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_nationalist) environment to people of all backgrounds.[^cite_note-SeattleTimes-36] By the 1990s, sovereign citizen arguments had been adopted by [minority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Race_and_ethnicity_in_the_United_States) groups, notably the African American [Moorish sovereign citizens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moorish_sovereign_citizens).[^cite_note-SPLC2019-13][^cite_note-Cash-37] The Moorish sovereigns' beliefs derive, in part, from the [Moorish Science Temple of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moorish_Science_Temple_of_America), which has condemned this sovereign citizen offshoot.[^cite_note-SPLC2019-13]

Since the 1990s, the number of African American sovereign citizens has increased substantially. Various Black sovereign citizen groups have appeared, some [Islamic](6037917.md), others adhering to [New Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Age) philosophies.[^cite_note-adl-15] Sovereign citizen ideas have also been adopted by some groups within the [Hawaiian sovereignty movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_sovereignty_movement)[^cite_note-Laird2014-3] and various other fringe political or religious groups, such as [black separatists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_separatists) (including the [Nuwaubian Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuwaubian_Nation))[^cite_note-adl-15][^cite_note-isd-38][^cite_note-39] and the [Fundamentalist Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamentalist_Church_of_Jesus_Christ_of_Latter-Day_Saints).[^cite_note-adl-15]

American [pseudolaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudolaw) became well-established by 2000. Around that time, Elvick conceived the [strawman theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strawman_theory); it became a core sovereign citizen concept because it provided an overarching explanation for the movement's pseudolegal beliefs.[^cite_note-furiouspaper-7]

### Spread

See also: [Freeman on the land movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeman_on_the_land_movement)

In the late 1990s and early 2000s, sovereign citizen ideology was introduced into Canada and then gradually into other countries[^cite_note-furiouspaper-7] as the advent of the Internet facilitated communication between people sharing the same ideas.[^cite_note-kent2015-23] One influential American figure who helped spread sovereign citizen ideology abroad was Winston Shrout, who held seminars in Canada (until he was banned from the country), Australia, New Zealand, and the United Kingdom.[^cite_note-ADLShrout-40]

In Canada, sovereign citizen beliefs mixed with local tax protester concepts during the 2000s and gave birth to an offshoot, the [freeman on the land movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeman_on_the_land_movement), which eventually spread to other [Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations) countries.[^cite_note-lawless_ones-41]



[Cliven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliven_Bundy) and [Ammon Bundy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammon_Bundy), two American activists who became associated with the sovereign citizen movement during the 2010s

Since the late 2000s, the sovereign citizen movement has significantly expanded in the U.S. due to the [Great Recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession) and more specifically the [subprime mortgage crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subprime_mortgage_crisis).[^cite_note-lawless_ones-41][^cite_note-ascendant-26][^cite_note-Netolitzky2019-42][^cite_note-43] In 2010, the [Southern Poverty Law Center](27164.md) (SPLC) estimated that 100,000 Americans were "hard-core sovereign believers", with another 200,000 "just starting out by testing sovereign techniques for resisting everything from speeding tickets to drug charges".[^cite_note-44] According to another SPLC estimate, the number of sovereign citizen-influenced militia groups in the U.S. increased dramatically between 2008 and 2011, from 149 to 1,274, respectively.[^cite_note-ontherise-17]

Incidents such as the [2003 standoff in Abbeville, South Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_standoff_in_Abbeville,_South_Carolina), the 2007 [Edward and Elaine Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_and_Elaine_Brown) standoff, the [2010 West Memphis police shootings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_West_Memphis_police_shootings), the 2014 [Bundy standoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundy_standoff), the 2016 [occupation of the Malheur National Wildlife Refuge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_the_Malheur_National_Wildlife_Refuge) (also involving the Bundy family), the [2016 shooting of Baton Rouge police officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_shooting_of_Baton_Rouge_police_officers), and the 2021 [Wakefield standoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wakefield_standoff) (involving African American [Moorish sovereign citizens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moorish_sovereign_citizens)) attracted significant media attention. In 2022, the perpetrator of the [Waukesha Christmas parade attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waukesha_Christmas_parade_attack) brought further attention to the movement by using sovereign citizen arguments during his trial.[^cite_note-CBS58-45]



The site of the 2021 [Waukesha Christmas parade attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waukesha_Christmas_parade_attack), a violent crime linked to the sovereign citizen movement

There is significant overlap between the sovereign citizen and [QAnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QAnon) movements.[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4] A sovereign citizen group known as the Oath Enforcers attracted QAnon and [Donald Trump](4848272.md) supporters into the movement after the [2021 storming of the United States Capitol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_storming_of_the_United_States_Capitol).[^cite_note-46] In 2022, the [Anti-Defamation League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Defamation_League) reported that the sovereign citizen movement was attracting a growing number of QAnon adherents, whose belief in the illegitimacy of the [Biden administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biden_administration) is compatible with the sovereign citizens' broader anti-government views.[^cite_note-sovereign-citizen-ideology-47]

Videos of people attempting to use sovereign citizen-style arguments during [traffic stops](1430646.md), in [courtrooms](1766292.md), and in other public places are common on the Internet, where they are often considered a source of amusement. Researcher Christine Sarteschi has said that this may cause people to underestimate the movement's potential for violence and its links with criminal conduct. Several people charged with crimes such as [murder](20171.md) or [sexual assault](218407.md) have used sovereign citizen arguments as attempts to negate the court's jurisdiction over them.[^cite_note-NYLawJournal-48] In 2025, Sarteschi argued that we must better understand why people become sovereign citizens, and noted that while the movement itself is nonviolent, deep mistrust of authority can lead sovereign citizens to commit acts of violence anyway.[^cite_note-\:1-10][^cite_note-\:2-19]

The [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md) accelerated the spread of the movement. Sovereign citizens have been associated with broader [anti-mask](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-mask) and [anti-vaccine activism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-vaccine_activism) and took part in [protests over responses to the COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protests_over_responses_to_the_COVID-19_pandemic).[^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50][^cite_note-UKonnotice-51] An increase in sovereign citizens was observed in Australia and the United Kingdom during the pandemic.[^cite_note-UKonnotice-51][^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53] Several COVID-19-related incidents involving local sovereign citizens who refused to follow sanitary measures were also reported in [Singapore](27318.md).[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55] In June 2022, Sarteschi reported that the movement was rapidly expanding and could now be found in 26 countries.[^cite_note-conversationsarteschi-56]

### Government response

#### United States

After the 1995 [Oklahoma City bombing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City_bombing), [U.S. federal law enforcement](16252377.md) began cracking down on white supremacist groups, including sovereign citizen organizations. The [Montana Freemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montana_Freemen) incident occurred in that context.[^cite_note-carey-11] The bombing also led Congress to pass the [Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiterrorism_and_Effective_Death_Penalty_Act_of_1996), enhancing sentences for certain terrorism-related offenses.[^cite_note-57]

Hundreds, if not thousands, of sovereign citizens have been imprisoned as a result of their actions. Many have continued their activities behind bars, often spreading their ideologies among other inmates.[^cite_note-adl-15]

As of the 1990s, several hundred people involved in "common law courts" operated by sovereign citizens or, more broadly, by the [Patriot movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriot_movement) have been arrested for crimes such as fraud, [impersonating police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impersonating_police), intimidating or threatening officials, and in some cases, outright violence. In 1998, a number of U.S. states passed laws outlawing the activities of these "courts" or strengthening existing sanctions.[^cite_note-splc98-58]

To prevent state courts from being burdened by frivolous litigation, some states have heightened penalties for people who file baseless motions. Some courts choose to impose pre-filing injunctions against certain [*pro se*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro_se_legal_representation_in_the_United_States) serial litigants, to preclude them from filing new lawsuits or documents without prior leave.[^cite_note-wastingtime-9]

After incidents such as the [2010 West Memphis police shootings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_West_Memphis_police_shootings), [U.S. law enforcement agencies](4876283.md) advised officers on how to deal with sovereign citizens at traffic stops and elsewhere.[^cite_note-59]

#### Australia

In Australia, after the 2022 [Wieambilla police shootings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wieambilla_police_shootings), the [Australian Security Intelligence Organisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Security_Intelligence_Organisation) and the [Australian Federal Police](341221.md) indicated they would examine the groups more closely as their beliefs increasingly align with those of other extremists, with the AFP Joint Counter Terrorism Team now required to undergo training on sovereign citizen threats.[^cite_note-60][^cite_note-61]

Not all members of the movement call themselves "sovereign citizens", and some regard the term as an oxymoron.[^cite_note-SeattleTimes-36] Sovereign citizens may prefer to call themselves "state nationals",[^cite_note-reitz-63] "constitutionalists", "freemen",[^cite_note-64] "natural people", "living people",[^cite_note-living-1] "private persons",[^cite_note-dallasnews-65] or people "seeking the truth"[^cite_note-66] or "living on the land".[^cite_note-dallasnews-65] The name "American State National"[^cite_note-sovereign-citizen-ideology-47] (ASN) became popular among sovereign citizens in the early 2020s, especially among followers of the [QAnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QAnon) conspiracy theory.[^cite_note-SovcitQanon-67]

The sovereign citizen movement has no single universally accepted symbol or emblem, but sovereign citizen documents and signs often have distinctive identifying marks. Some of the most common ones are [postage stamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postage_stamp) and [thumbprints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fingerprint) on documents, and the addition of punctuation (dashes, hyphens, colons, or commas) to one's name, which sovereign citizens [believe has a legal effect](#Documents_and_formalities).[^cite_note-sovcitflag-62]

Groups such as [Moorish sovereigns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moorish_sovereign_citizens) and the [Washitaw Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washitaw_Nation) have their own specific flags and symbols. Some sovereign citizens use references to nonexistent "Republics" or to the [Uniform Commercial Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_Commercial_Code) (UCC) of 1-308, 1-207, and 1-103; variations on the [flag of the United States](11447.md); or religious symbols such as that of [Vatican City](32408.md), which are thought to establish "sovereignty".[^cite_note-ndsaa-68]

One common symbol of the American sovereign citizen movement is a version of the [U.S. flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._flag) with alternate colors and vertical stripes. Sometimes known as "the flag of peace" or "Title Four flag", it is based on a flag allegedly used by [American custom houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Customs_Service) for a brief period during the 19th century. Around the 2000s, some sovereign citizens began to claim that this is the true flag of the United States.[^cite_note-sovcitflag-62]

In Australia, some in the sovereign citizen movement have coopted the 1901 [Australian red ensign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_red_ensign) flag.[^cite_note-69]

The movement has no defining text, established doctrine, or centralized leadership,[^cite_note-wastingtime-9][^cite_note-roads-70] but there are common themes, generally implying that the legitimate government and legal system have been somehow replaced and that the current authorities are illegitimate. [Taxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax) and [licenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/License) are likewise thought to be illegitimate. A number of leaders, commonly called "gurus", develop their own variations.[^cite_note-wastingtime-9][^cite_note-lawless_ones-41] The movement's theories include influences from a variety of sources, some of them decades old, resulting in often confusing and incoherent narratives of U.S. history.[^cite_note-GWedu-71]

Sovereign citizens' legal theories reinterpret the [Constitution of the United States](31644.md) through the selective reading of law dictionaries (notably an obsolete version of [Black's Law Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black's_Law_Dictionary)), [state court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_court_\(United_States\)) opinions, or specific capitalization,[^cite_note-\:2-19][^cite_note-\:3-31] and incorporate other details from a variety of sources, including the [Uniform Commercial Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_Commercial_Code), the [Articles of Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Articles_of_Confederation), the [Magna Carta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_Carta), the [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible), and foreign treaties. They ignore the second clause of [Article VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_Six_of_the_United_States_Constitution) of the Constitution (the [Supremacy Clause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremacy_Clause)), which establishes the Constitution as the law of the land and the [United States Supreme Court](31737.md) as the ultimate authority to interpret it.[^cite_note-MLR-72][^cite_note-LEB-73][^cite_note-74] Most consider [county sheriffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_sheriff) the most powerful law enforcement officers in the country, with authority superior to that of any federal agent, elected official, or other local law enforcement official.[^cite_note-ForbesIII-75]

### Illegitimacy of laws and government

See also: [Anti-statism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-statism)

A widespread belief among sovereign citizens is that the [state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_\(polity\)) is not an actual government, but a [corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporation). American movement members believe that the corporation purporting to be the U.S. federal government is illegally controlling the republic via a territorial government in Washington, D.C.[^cite_note-reitz-63]

Sovereign citizens believe that sometime after the [Founding Fathers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Founding_Fathers) set up the government, [commercial law](77444.md) secretly replaced common law. This commercial law is generally understood to be [admiralty law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiralty_law), as sovereign citizens believe the current, illegitimate law is based on principles of [international commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_commerce).[^cite_note-GWedu-71][^cite_note-SPLC2022-4] Sovereign citizens also claim that the [gold fringes](11447.md#Decoration) on U.S. flags displayed in courtrooms is evidence that admiralty law is in effect.[^cite_note-spl4-30] This leads them to believe that U.S. judges and lawyers are actually agents of a foreign power,[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4] typically thought to be the [United Kingdom](31717.md): one pseudolegal conspiracy theory claims that [bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar_\(law\)) is an acronym for "British Accreditation Registry".[^cite_note-SovcitQanon-67]

Sovereign citizens, therefore, challenge the validity of the contemporary legal system and claim to answer only to [God's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God's_law) or to common law, by which they mean the system that supposedly existed before the conspiracy.[^cite_note-Laird2014-3]

There is no consensus among sovereign citizens on when the secret change to the political and legal system occurred; some believe it was during the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War) and others date it to 1933, when the U.S. abandoned the [gold standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard).[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4] According to one version, the vehicle for the change was the [District of Columbia Organic Act of 1871](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_of_Columbia_Organic_Act_of_1871), which sovereign citizens believe created a "United States corporation" to govern the [District of Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_of_Columbia) under commercial code; this form of corporate rule then extended to the entire country.[^cite_note-GWedu-71] Another theory has it that the country was secretly reorganized as a [post office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post_office) in 1789.[^cite_note-millions-76] One theory among Australian sovereign citizens is that public administrations became corporations when the [1999 republic referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_Australian_republic_referendum) was defeated.[^cite_note-ABCAustralia-77] Pseudolegal schemes attribute a particular power to the [Universal Postal Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Postal_Union)[^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78] and to the use of [postage stamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postage_stamp) on legal documents.[^cite_note-millions-76][^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78]

The beliefs that the government is a corporation and that people are secretly under a form of commercial law lead sovereign citizens to believe that [statutory law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statutory_law) is a [contract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contract_of_adhesion) binding people to the state. According to that theory, people are tricked into this contract by various methods, including Social Security numbers, [licenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/License), or ZIP Codes: thus, avoiding their use means immunity from government authority.[^cite_note-Knight2003-79][^cite_note-ValeriBorgeson2018-80][^cite_note-SeattleTimes-36][^cite_note-81] Another common belief among sovereign citizens is that they can opt out of the purported contract, making themselves immune from the laws they do not wish to follow, by declining to "consent": when confronted by police officers or other officials, sovereign citizens typically attempt to negate their authority by saying, "I do not consent".[^cite_note-living-1][^cite_note-ABCAustralia-77]

Many sovereign citizens believe that the [Uniform Commercial Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_Commercial_Code), which provides an interstate standard for documents that they believe apply only to their [strawman](#Dual_personas), is a codification of the illegitimate commercial law ruling the United States. Therefore, they think that exploiting supposed loopholes in the UCC will help them assert their rights or invoke their special privileges and powers as "common law citizens".[^cite_note-GWedu-71]

Adherents of the "American State National" concept believe that, through a specific procedure, they can renounce federal citizenship, make themselves immune from jurisdiction and arrest, avoid the [IRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IRS), and rescind voting registrations, marriages, or birth certificates.[^cite_note-\:2-19] In March 2023, Chase Allan, a man who subscribed to this notion and used a false passport and an illegal license plate, was shot dead by police at a traffic stop in [Utah](31716.md) during a confrontation with officers over his refusal to show an identification document.[^cite_note-SovcitQanon-67]

The belief that the current legal system is illegitimate has led some sovereign citizens to consider themselves "above the law" and commit crimes.[^cite_note-NYLawJournal-48]

### Dual personas

Main article: [Strawman theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strawman_theory)

One recurring idea in sovereign citizen ideology is that individuals have two personas: one of flesh and blood and the other a separate, secret [legal personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_person) (commonly called the "strawman"), created upon each person's birth and subject to the government.[^cite_note-\:2-19] Sovereign citizens claim it is possible to dissociate oneself from the "strawman" by certain procedures, thus becoming free of all debts, liabilities, and legal constraints.[^cite_note-furiouspaper-7][^cite_note-NYT82313-12][^cite_note-spl4-30][^cite_note-millions-76]

This theory is also connected to the [economic aspects](#Economics) of sovereign citizen tenets and more specifically to the [redemption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redemption_movement) concepts.[^cite_note-GWedu-71]

### Citizenship

See also: [Self-ownership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-ownership)



American activist David Zion Brugger, showing an irregular "[identity document](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_document)" asserting him to be a "[citizen of Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embassy_of_Heaven)"

American sovereign citizens posit that contemporary [United States citizenship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_citizenship) is somehow defective or fraudulent and that it curtails citizens' legitimate rights. Some sovereign citizens also claim that they can become immune to most or all laws of the United States by [renouncing citizenship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relinquishment_of_United_States_nationality) in a "federal corporation" and declaring themselves citizens only of the [state](18618239.md) where they reside: this process, which they call "expatriation", involves [filing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filing_\(law\)) or delivering a nonlegal document claiming their renunciation of citizenship to any county clerk's office that can be convinced to accept it.[^cite_note-82]

In the 1970s, one of the movement's originators, [white supremacist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_supremacist) ideologue [William Potter Gale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Potter_Gale), identified the [Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution](31666.md) as the act that converted "sovereign citizens" into "federal citizens" by their agreement to a contract to accept benefits from the federal government. Other commentators have identified other acts, including the [Emergency Banking Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_Banking_Act),[^cite_note-83] and the alleged suppression of the [Titles of Nobility Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titles_of_Nobility_Amendment).[^cite_note-84]

Likewise, sovereign citizen leader Richard McDonald claimed that there are two classes of citizens in the U.S.: the "original citizens of the states" (also called "states citizens" or "organic citizens")[^cite_note-spl6-85] and "U.S. citizens". According to McDonald, U.S. citizens, whom he calls "Fourteenth Amendment citizens", have civil rights, legislated to give the rights to freed black slaves after the [Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War): this benefit is received by consent in exchange for freedom. On the other hand, white state citizens have unalienable constitutional rights. On this view, state citizens must take steps to revoke and rescind their U.S. citizenship and reassert their *[de jure](43594.md)* common-law state citizen status. This involves removing oneself from [federal jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_jurisdiction_\(United_States\)) and relinquishing any evidence of consent to U.S. citizenship, such as a Social Security number, driver's license, [car registration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Car_registration), ZIP Code, [marriage license](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marriage_license), [voter registration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter_registration_in_the_United_States), or birth certificate. Also included is the refusal to pay state and federal income taxes because citizens not under U.S. jurisdiction are not required to pay them.[^cite_note-Feucht-86]

The concept of "14th Amendment citizens" is consistent with the movement's white supremacist origins in that it can cause adherents to believe that [African Americans](2154.md), having become citizens only after the Civil War, have far fewer rights than [Whites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Americans),[^cite_note-spl6-85] or that only [Black people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_people) have to pay federal taxes and abide by federal laws.[^cite_note-reitz-63]



Example illustration of a false "Moorish" license plate.

On the contrary, "[Moorish sovereign citizens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moorish_sovereign_citizens)" think that African Americans constitute an elite class within American society, with special rights and privileges that make them immune from federal and state authority. They commonly adopt an "Africanized" version of their names by adding "el", "Bey", or a combination of the two, and associate themselves with a particular "Moorish" group, claiming they are not culpable for acts committed under their former name and that their affiliation makes them immune to prosecution.[^cite_note-SPLC2019-13][^cite_note-87]

The underpinnings of sovereign citizens' theories of exemption vary. One belief is that the "Moors" were America's original inhabitants and are therefore entitled to be self-governing. They claim to be descendants of the Moroccan "Moors" and thus subject to the 1786 [Moroccan-American Treaty of Friendship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moroccan-American_Treaty_of_Friendship), which they believe exempts them from U.S. law. A variation of "Moorish" ideology is found in the [Washitaw Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washitaw_Nation), which claims rights through provisions in the [Louisiana Purchase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_Purchase) treaty granting privileges to Moors as early colonists and the nonexistent "United Nations Indigenous People's Seat 215".[^cite_note-SPLC2019-13] Various other groups claim special status and exemption from their countries' laws by purporting to belong to real or imaginary ethnic minorities.[^cite_note-adl-15]

Sovereign citizens may claim that their status in the United States is that of "non-resident aliens".[^cite_note-spl6-85] Only residents (resident aliens) of the states, not their citizens, are income-taxable, sovereign citizens argue. And as a state citizen landowner, one can bring forward the original land patent and file it with the county for absolute or [allodial property rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allodial_title). Such allodial ownership is held "without recognizing any superior to whom any duty is due on account thereof" (*[Black's Law Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black's_Law_Dictionary)*). Superiors include those who levy property taxes or who hold mortgages or liens against the property.[^cite_note-Feucht-86]

### Economics

See also: [Redemption movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redemption_movement)

Sovereign citizen texts often claim that "international bankers" are the source of a conspiracy that replaced the United States' legitimate government and legal system. In the movement's earlier form, these bankers were explicitly said to be [Jews](25955086.md). While this can still be implied in sovereign citizen literature, the movement's original [antisemitic conspiracy theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisemitic_conspiracy_theories) were diluted over time; most contemporary sovereign citizens tend to present greatly simplified versions of them, with no mention of Jewish conspiracies and only vague references to corrupt bankers.[^cite_note-GWedu-71]

Some sovereign citizens believe that the United States "corporation" is [bankrupt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bankrupt). This is often attributed to the 1933 abandonment of the gold standard.[^cite_note-GWedu-71] As a result, the illegitimate U.S. government is said to secretly use its citizens as [collateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collateralized_debt_obligation) against [foreign debt](322221.md), effectively enslaving Americans. Sovereign citizens believe that this sale of American citizens takes place at birth, through the issuance of [birth certificates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_certificate) and Social Security numbers.[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4][^cite_note-GWedu-71][^cite_note-millions-76]

The sovereign citizen movement overlaps with the [redemption movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redemption_movement) (also known as "A4V" after one of its schemes), which claims that a secret bank account is created for every citizen at birth as part of the process whereby the U.S. government uses its citizens as collateral.[^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78][^cite_note-millions-76] Several prominent sovereign citizens have advocated redemption schemes.[^cite_note-adl-15] The belief in a secret bank account is intertwined with the [strawman theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strawman_theory), since each person's fund is supposedly associated with their "strawman".[^cite_note-adl-15][^cite_note-GWedu-71]

"Redemption" theories assert that the vast sums of money in this account can be reclaimed through certain procedures and applied to financial obligations or even criminal charges.[^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78][^cite_note-millions-76] In some variations of this theory, the secret fund may be called a "Cestui Que Vie Trust".[^cite_note-SovcitQanon-67]

Pseudolegal economic theories also imply various misconceptions about currencies and financial institutions, for instance, that a borrower has no obligation to repay bank loans if the bank loans out more money than it has in reserve (i,e., in its customers' accounts) as this constitutes "creat\[ing] money from thin air", and that money is actually worthless when not [backed by gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard).[^cite_note-furiouspaper-7] Many sovereign citizens do not recognize [U.S. currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._currency) and demand payment in [gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_coin) or [silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_coin) coins.[^cite_note-88][^cite_note-Giordanos-89][^cite_note-90]

Some sovereign citizens also subscribe to the [NESARA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NESARA) conspiracy theory, according to which the U.S. Congress secretly created a new economic order and canceled all debt.[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4]

### Freedom of movement

See also: [Freedom of movement under United States law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_movement_under_United_States_law)



A car with an irregular sovereign citizen license plate.

Using arguments that rely on exacting definitions and word choice, sovereign citizens may assert a constitutional "right to travel" in a "conveyance", distinguishing it from driving an automobile in order to justify ignoring requirements for [license plates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/License_plates), vehicle registration, [insurances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle_insurance), and driver's licenses. The right to travel is claimed based on a variety of passages.[^cite_note-adl-15][^cite_note-roads-70][^cite_note-MLR-72]

One common argument of sovereign citizens is that they are "traveling" and not "driving" and hence do not need a driver's license because they are not transporting commercial goods or paying passengers.[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4]

### Other

See also: [QAnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QAnon)

Other pseudolegal theories commonly shared by sovereign citizens include that "silence means consent" for any sort of documents, that any claim or alleged statement of fact placed in a sworn document (known in pseudolegal jargon as an "[affidavit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affidavit) of truth") is proven true unless rebutted, and that there is no crime if there is no injured party.[^cite_note-furiouspaper-7]

Some sovereign citizens are involved in other forms of [conspiracy theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conspiracy_theories), including [QAnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QAnon).[^cite_note-91] Certain subgroups of the movement adhere to theories about [extraterrestrials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UFO_conspiracy_theories) and [reptilians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reptilian_conspiracy_theory).[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4] One advocate of sovereign citizen fraudulent [tax avoidance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_avoidance) schemes, [Sean David Morton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sean_David_Morton), was also active as a [psychic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychic) and [ufologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ufologist).[^cite_note-swindle-92] In [Quebec](7954867.md), sovereign citizen ideology was promoted by [Guylaine Lanctôt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guylaine_Lanct%C3%B4t), an [anti-vaccine activist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-vaccine_activist) and [AIDS denialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIDS_denialist).[^cite_note-dangereuse-93]

In 2022, the [Anti-Defamation League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Defamation_League) reported that sovereign citizen ideology was "increasingly seeping" into QAnon, as the movement's anti-government views were compatible with QAnon's belief in a worldwide "cabal" and in the illegitimacy of the [Biden administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biden_administration).[^cite_note-sovereign-citizen-ideology-47]

Sovereign citizen groups, notably that led in [Texas](29810.md) by "gurus" David and Bonnie Straight, a married couple, convinced parents whose children were removed from their custody that [Child Protective Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_Protective_Services) engages in [child trafficking](2045465.md), and encouraged them to kidnap their children.[^cite_note-NYLawJournal-48][^cite_note-vice21-94][^cite_note-SovcitQanon-67] The belief that child protection agencies are involved in crimes against children is also consistent with QAnon ideology.[^cite_note-SovcitQanon-67]

Several sovereign citizen "gurus" have made grandiose claims about the powers granted to them by their pseudolegal schemes. One American ideologue and ["Quantum Grammar"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Wynn_Miller#Constructed_language_and_linguistic_theories) advocate, Russell Jay Gould, claims that having signed a postal receipt in a specific way and [filed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filed) a document relating to [Title 4 of the United States Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_4_of_the_United_States_Code), at a moment when the country was supposedly bankrupt, makes him the "Postmaster-General" and legitimate ruler of the United States.[^cite_note-Cash-37] Another American guru, Heather Ann Tucci-Jarraf, claimed before her sentencing for fraud to have "[foreclosed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreclosed)" and "canceled" all banks and governments through [UCC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_Commercial_Code) filings.[^cite_note-bostonlawreview-95] Likewise, [Romana Didulo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romana_Didulo), a Canadian QAnon conspiracy theorist, uses sovereign citizen concepts to back her claims of being the rightful [Queen of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_of_Canada), and eventually the "Queen of the World".[^cite_note-conversationsarteschi-56][^cite_note-RedDeerians-96][^cite_note-Guar-97]

## Tactics

See also: [Civil disobedience](37073.md) and [Civil resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_resistance)



A homemade "[public notice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_notice)" with pseudolegal language used by a sovereign citizen in [Belfast](5046.md), Northern Ireland

Sovereign citizens may be categorized by their use and acceptance of pseudolaw. Some are simply naïve, some strategically adopt pseudolaw, some embrace and genuinely believe in the ideology, and some are "gurus" who may profit from spreading pseudolaw.[^cite_note-98]

Sovereign citizens may be affiliated with a group within the movement, follow the teachings of a specific "guru", or act entirely on their own. By disobeying rules they consider illegitimate, they regularly find themselves in conflict with all forms of government institutions, most commonly law enforcement, the [judiciary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judiciary), and the [revenue services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revenue_service).[^cite_note-adl-15] One sovereign citizen from [Montana](19978.md), Ernie Wayne terTelgte, became a local celebrity in 2013 by engaging in a protracted legal battle with authorities over the need to have a [fishing license](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing_license)[^cite_note-naturalman-99] and then having multiple conflicts with law enforcement over this matter, as well as his lack of a driver's license.[^cite_note-100]

Sovereign citizens often use flawed or invented legal arguments or irregular documents that may have been bought from other movement members as "proof" of their claims.[^cite_note-UKonnotice-51] It is common for sovereign citizen "gurus" to earn money by selling their followers standard documents such as template filings, scripts to recite at court appearances, or other "quick-fix" solutions to legal problems.[^cite_note-wastingtime-9] Some "gurus" sell "how-to" manuals explaining the movement's theories and schemes. One such manual is *Title 4 Flag Says You're Schwag: The Sovereign Citizen's Handbook*, which has been reprinted and updated several times.[^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78]

Sovereign citizens often use an unusual vocabulary[^cite_note-spl4-30] and twist the meaning of legal terms, or even commonplace phrases, for their convenience.[^cite_note-polar-101] This includes avoiding the use of expressions they think would create "[joinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joinder)",[^cite_note-furiouspaper-7] thus making them enter into a "contract" with the government. For example, when dealing with police, sovereign citizens will often avoid saying "I understand" and instead say "I comprehend", as they believe that the word "understand" acknowledges that one "stand\[s] under the jurisdiction", thus recognizing police authority.[^cite_note-polar-101] Other phrases and arguments commonly used by sovereign citizens may include "I do not consent" (in order [to deny police's or courts' authority](#Illegitimacy_of_laws_and_government)), "Do you have a claim against me?", "Am I being detained?", as well as claims of "diplomatic immunity" or references to irrelevant documents such as the Magna Carta.[^cite_note-102]

As they regard themselves as bound only by their own interpretation of common law, sovereign citizens have been setting up [militias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_militia_movement) of self-appointed "sheriffs",[^cite_note-NYLawJournal-48] as well as "common law courts", to handle matters regarding movement members. These "courts", which are [devoid of legal authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kangaroo_court), are frequently used to formalize the "declarations of sovereignty" of movement members, in a process often known as "asseveration".[^cite_note-spl4-30]

Sovereign citizens' conflicts with authorities have occasionally resulted in violence.[^cite_note-Laird2014-3][^cite_note-adl-15][^cite_note-NYLawJournal-48][^cite_note-ForbesIII-75]

### Documents and formalities

Sovereign citizens are known to create their own irregular, pseudolegal documents, including false passports, license plates, or birth certificates.[^cite_note-Quickguide-103] Sovereign citizen documents may include unusual formalities, such as [maxims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Latin_phrases_\(full\)) written in Latin, thumbprints, or stamps in certain places, as well as unconventional, sometimes incomprehensible pseudo-[legalese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legalese). Stamps are generally accompanied by signatures (with the sovereign citizen's name signed across them), initials or other markings.[^cite_note-wastingtime-9][^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78][^cite_note-Meads-104]

Signatures and thumbprints are likely to be in red ink or blood, since black and blue inks are believed to indicate corporations.[^cite_note-millions-76] As bonds are canceled using red ink in some U.S. states, sovereign citizens may sign in red ink to signify that they are canceling the bond attached to their birth certificate or to their strawman. Others use red ink because it represents the blood of the "flesh-and-blood person". Other methods to dissociate oneself from the strawman include unusual spelling and writing one's name in a different manner or with punctuation, e.g. "John of the family Doe" instead of "John Doe", or "John-Robert: Doe" instead of "John Robert Doe".[^cite_note-spl4-30]

Sovereign citizens often add the Latin phrase *[sui juris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sui_juris)* (meaning "of one's own right") to their names on legal documents to signify that they are reserving all the rights to which they are entitled as a free person.[^cite_note-spl4-30]

Postage stamps supposedly make pseudolegal documents authoritative, but their meaning varies depending on the "guru". One version has it that stamps grant sovereignty to pseudolaw affiliates: their use on documents purportedly makes one a "postmaster" with equal rights and peer status to [nation states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nation_state).[^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78]

When signing an official document such as a driver's license, mortgage document, or traffic ticket, sovereign citizens often add *under threat, duress, and coercion* (or a variation thereof, such as the initials *TDC*) after or under their name to signify that they are not signing the document voluntarily, which purportedly helps them avoid entering into a "contract" with the illegitimate government and falling under its jurisdiction. Some write *TDC* after their ZIP codes.[^cite_note-ADL_Identifiers-105]

People and groups linked to the movement have been using a [constructed language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructed_language) created by American theorist [David Wynn Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Wynn_Miller), who asserted that this unorthodox version of the English language, variously called "Parse-Syntax-Grammar", "Correct-Language",[^cite_note-ozy2020-106] "Truth Language"[^cite_note-messiah-107] or "Quantum Grammar",[^cite_note-wastingtime-9][^cite_note-bostonlawreview-95] guarantees success in legal proceedings where it constitutes the only "correct" form of communication.[^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78][^cite_note-ozy2020-106][^cite_note-messiah-107]

### Litigation and court cases

See also: [Frivolous litigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frivolous_litigation), [Vexatious litigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vexatious_litigation), [Paper terrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_terrorism), and [Abuse of process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abuse_of_process)

Cases involving sovereign citizens can cause law enforcement officers and court officials severe problems.[^cite_note-NYT82313-12] Sovereign citizens may challenge the laws, rules, or sentences they disagree with by engaging in the practice known as [paper terrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_terrorism),[^cite_note-\:1-10] which involves filing [complaints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complaint) with legal documents that may be bogus or simply misused. Minor issues such as [traffic violations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_violation) or disagreements over pet-licensing fees may provoke numerous court filings. Courts then find themselves burdened by having to process hundreds of pages of irregular, sometimes incomprehensible documents, straining their resources.[^cite_note-Laird2014-3][^cite_note-ForbesIII-75][^cite_note-adl-15][^cite_note-SPLC2022-4][^cite_note-wastingtime-9]

When involved in court cases, sovereign citizens generally act as their own lawyers, though sometimes a sovereign citizen "leader" may assist them in court. They often use uncommon or downright disconcerting pseudolegal tactics, and typically deny the court's jurisdiction over them.[^cite_note-wastingtime-9][^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78][^cite_note-Meads-104]

In May 2019, Kim Blandino, a felon residing in Nevada, was found guilty of traffic offenses. He threatened the judge who presided over his hearing that he would file complaints against him and demanded a monetary "settlement" from him.[^cite_note-108] Blandino was charged with extortion and impersonation of an officer. He then filed numerous motions to delay the proceedings and tried to disqualify almost every judge in the district. Blandino's motions required multiple reviews and countless hours of hearings.[^cite_note-wastingtime-9] In March 2022, Blandino was convicted in a jury trial. He then appealed his conviction with similar methods. On December 20, 2023, the Court of Appeals of Nevada affirmed the conviction, noting that Blandino's claims were "merely speculative" and that the court did not need to consider his argument as it was not "cogently argued".[^cite_note-findlaw-109]

### Anti-tax and other financial schemes

See also: [Tax resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_resistance), [Tax evasion](2256795.md), and [Mortgage elimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortgage_elimination)

Many sovereign citizens engage in various forms of tax resistance, causing disputes with government administrations.[^cite_note-ForbesIII-75][^cite_note-splc5-110] It is estimated that sovereign citizens and other tax protesters caused the U.S. about \$1 billion in public losses from 1990 to 2013.[^cite_note-swindle-92]

Sovereign citizens use a variety of fraudulent schemes, including filing false [securities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Securities), to avoid paying taxes, get "refunds" from the government, or eliminate their [debts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt) and [mortgages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortgages).[^cite_note-swindle-92] The belief that money is worthless since the [gold standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard) was abandoned has led sovereign citizens to create fictitious [financial instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_instrument). One of the first to use this method, in the 1980s, was tax protester and songwriter [Tupper Saussy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupper_Saussy), who created check-like instruments he called "Public Money Office Certificates".[^cite_note-Funny_Money-111]

Saussy issued these "certificates" primarily as a form of protest, but sovereign citizens have used false "[promissory notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promissory_note)", "[bills of exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bills_of_exchange)", "[coupons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupon)", "[bonds](60737.md)", or "[sight drafts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banker's_acceptance)" in attempts to pay taxes and [utility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_utilities) bills, purchase properties, or fight foreclosures. Some "gurus" have scammed adherents of the movement by selling them such counterfeit instruments.[^cite_note-Funny_Money-111] Other scams primarily target victims who are not part of the movement.[^cite_note-112]

Sovereign citizens may use the ineffective methods the [redemption movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redemption_movement) advocates for appropriating the sums from one's purported secret Treasury account: such schemes are sometimes called "money for nothing".[^cite_note-furiouspaper-7][^cite_note-Meads-104] For example, writing "Accepted for Value" or "Taken for Value" on bills or collection letters supposedly causes them to be paid with the strawman's secret fund[^cite_note-spl4-30][^cite_note-ADL_Identifiers-105] (this scheme is commonly known as "A4V").[^cite_note-Netolitzky20182-5][^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78][^cite_note-Meads-104] Purported methods for claiming the secret fund include filing a [UCC-1 financing statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCC-1_financing_statement) against one's strawman after "separating" from it.[^cite_note-spl4-30]

### False liens and other harassment tactics

Besides paper terrorism, sovereign citizens have used various techniques of [intimidation](494675.md) and [harassment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harassment) to achieve their goals.[^cite_note-adl-15] They will commonly demand vast sums of money from public officials or private individuals they believe have wronged them: after being pulled over at traffic stops or held in police custody, sovereign citizens may "charge" the officials involved for their time and inconvenience. One method of retaliation they use against public officials or other real or perceived enemies is the filing of [false liens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_lien).[^cite_note-adl-15]

Anyone can file a notice of lien against property such as real estate, vehicles, or other assets of another. In most U.S. states, the validity of liens is not investigated or inquired into at the time of filing. Notices of liens (whether legally valid or not) are a cloud on the title of the property and may affect the property owner's [credit rating](1194146.md) and ability to obtain home equity loans or refinance the property. Clearing up fraudulent notices of liens may be expensive and time-consuming.[^cite_note-NYT82313-12]

Illegitimate sovereign citizen common law courts also put enemies on "trial": on occasion, sovereign citizens have tried public officials in absentia and sentenced them to death for treason.[^cite_note-Laird2014-3]

Another tactic involves false [arbitration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbitration) entities operated by movement members that issue unilaterally, on their clients' behalf, "rulings" ordering the client's creditors or other victims to pay damages.[^cite_note-dallasnews-65][^cite_note-bogusarbitration-113][^cite_note-114] In 2022, the [Anti-Defamation League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Defamation_League) reported that although this particular tactic seems to have appeared around 2014, its use had intensified since 2019. According to the ADL's report, these sham rulings are designed, besides targeting specific victims, to clog the court system that sovereign citizens consider illegitimate.[^cite_note-bogusarbitration-113]

Some sovereign citizens have advocated and practiced [adverse possession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adverse_possession) of properties.[^cite_note-SPLC2022-4] Notably, Moorish Sovereigns have cited [reparations for slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reparations_for_slavery_in_the_United_States) as a justification for [squatting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squatting) homes and claiming other people's property as theirs, even though they also target the possessions of African Americans.[^cite_note-115]

In the United States, authorities have identified some people involved in [First Amendment audits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Amendment_audits) as sovereign citizens.[^cite_note-116]

## Legal status of theories

Sovereign citizens' tactics often succeed in delaying legal proceedings and occasionally confuse or exhaust public officials,[^cite_note-Laird2014-3][^cite_note-wastingtime-9][^cite_note-vice21-94] but their arguments are never upheld in court.[^cite_note-furiouspaper-7] Their claims have been consistently rejected by courts in various countries, including the U.S., Canada,[^cite_note-furiouspaper-7][^cite_note-Meads-104] Australia,[^cite_note-117] and New Zealand.[^cite_note-118] [Mark Pitcavage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Pitcavage), a scholar working for the [Anti-Defamation League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Defamation_League)'s Center on Extremism, has summed up sovereign citizen ideology as "[magical thinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magical_thinking)".[^cite_note-bizarre-119] One [state representative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Hampshire_House_of_Representatives) from New Hampshire, [Richard Marple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Marple), repeatedly tried to introduce legislation that would recognize sovereign citizen ideas, without success.[^cite_note-marple-14]

One crucial flaw of pseudolegal theories in general is that the "[common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law)" they cite is based not on historical precedent but instead on an erroneous perception of traditional [English law](85441.md).[^cite_note-furiouspaper-7][^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78]

In 2012, the [Court of Queen's Bench of Alberta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Queen's_Bench_of_Alberta)'s *Meads v. Meads* decision, pertaining to a contentious divorce case in which the husband used [freeman on the land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeman_on_the_land) arguments, compiled a decade of Canadian jurisprudence and American academic research about [pseudolaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudolaw). It went much further than the matters of the case by covering the most common pseudolegal arguments and tactics and refuting them in detail.[^cite_note-Meads-104][^cite_note-Netolitzky2019-42] *Meads v. Meads*, written by Associate Chief Justice John D. Rooke, has since been used as [case law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_law) by courts in Canada and in other [Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations) countries.[^cite_note-Netolitzky2019-42]

### Immunity from laws and taxes

See also: [Rule of law](25166191.md)

Pseudolegal documents and arguments claiming that one is personally immune from [jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction) or should not be paying taxes have never been accepted by any court.[^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78][^cite_note-Royals-120]

The belief that legal obligations are [contracts](19280537.md) that can be opted out of ignores that government and court [authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authority) is not a product of one's [consent](215934.md) and that the relationship between the state and an individual is not based on a contract.[^cite_note-OPCABC-121] The Canadian decision *Meads v. Meads* refuted the theory that laws are contracts, commenting:

> A claim that the relationship between an individual and the state is always one of contract is clearly incorrect. Aspects of that relationship may flow from mutual contract (for example a person or corporation may be hired by the government to perform a task such as road maintenance), but the state has the right to engage in unilateral action, subject to the [Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Charter_of_Rights_and_Freedoms), and the allocation and delegation of government authority.[^cite_note-Meads-104]

Author [Richard Abanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Abanes) has written that sovereign citizens fail to sufficiently examine the context of the case law they cite, and ignore adverse evidence, such as [Federalist No. 15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federalist_No._15), wherein [Alexander Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Hamilton) wrote that the [Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Constitution) places everyone personally under federal authority.[^cite_note-Feucht-86]

#### Taxation

The idea that one can avoid paying taxes in the country one resides in by renouncing or challenging the validity of one's citizenship and claiming to be a "non-resident alien" is legally baseless. The [Internal Revenue Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_Revenue_Service) has refuted in detail "frivolous tax arguments" such as this and the idea that filing [tax returns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_return) and paying [federal income tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_income_tax) are "voluntary".[^cite_note-122][^cite_note-123]

When he faced tax evasion charges in 2006, actor [Wesley Snipes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wesley_Snipes) adopted a sovereign-citizen defense, claiming to be a "non-resident alien" who should not be subject to income tax. He was eventually found guilty of three misdemeanor counts of failing to file federal income tax returns and sentenced to 36 months in prison.[^cite_note-124][^cite_note-125]

#### Jurisdiction

Arguments that one can be personally exempt from the jurisdiction of the courts are legally baseless. According to *United States v. Benabe* (2011), "Regardless of an individual's claimed status of descent, be it as a 'sovereign citizen', a 'secured-party creditor', or a 'flesh-and-blood human being', that person is not beyond the jurisdiction of the courts. These theories should be rejected summarily, however they are presented."[^cite_note-126] The sovereign citizen concept that [ordinary courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_court) are secretly [admiralty courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiralty_court) and thus have no jurisdiction over people has been repeatedly dismissed as [frivolous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frivolous_or_vexatious).[^cite_note-Benchmark-127][^cite_note-128]

In 1990, after Andrew Schneider was convicted and sentenced to five years in prison for making a threat by mail, he argued that he was a free, sovereign citizen and therefore not subject to the jurisdiction of federal courts. The [United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Seventh_Circuit) rejected his argument as having "no conceivable validity in American law".[^cite_note-129] In 2017, former [Subway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subway_\(restaurant\)) spokesman [Jared Fogle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jared_Fogle) similarly tried to overturn his convictions on [child sex tourism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_sex_tourism) and [child pornography](25211885.md) charges by denying the court's jurisdiction over him. The court dismissed Fogle's motions, reminding him that "the Seventh Circuit has rejected theories of individual sovereignty, immunity from prosecution, and their ilk".[^cite_note-130][^cite_note-131]

In a 2013 criminal case, the [United States District Court for the Western District of Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Western_District_of_Washington) responded to pseudolegal filings by sovereign citizen Kenneth Wayne Leaming with the following comments:

> The Court \[...] feels some measure of responsibility to inform Defendant that all the fancy legal-sounding things he has read on the internet are make-believe.[^cite_note-Royals-120] Defendant can call himself a "public minister" and "private attorney general", he may file "mandatory judicial notices" citing all his favorite websites, he can even address mail to the "Washington Republic". But at the end of the day, while sovereign citizens and Defendant cite things like "Universal Law Ordinances", they are subject to both state and federal laws, just like everyone else.[^cite_note-132][^cite_note-133]

In 2018, upon dismissing the arguments of sovereign citizen guru Heather Ann Tucci-Jarraf and her associate Randall Beane, the [United States District Court for the Eastern District of Tennessee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Eastern_District_of_Tennessee) found the idea that one cannot be charged by the judiciary without one's consent "incredible and absurd":

> Under \[that] reasoning, anyone can commit any criminal act (burglary, robbery, assault, drug trafficking, or even murder) but cannot be prosecuted, unless they consent to being prosecuted. Even the least educated among us would scoff at this notion. However, those intent upon preying on others would relish such a proposition. Such a system would be untenable, and no nation, country, state, or community could exist under such a preposterous proposition.[^cite_note-USvBeaneandTucci-Jarraf-134]

In 2021, Pauline Bauer, a Pennsylvania restaurant owner who was facing charges for participating in the [Capitol riot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitol_riot),[^cite_note-notaperson-135] used a sovereign citizen line of defense by claiming to be a "self-governed individual"[^cite_note-bizarre-119] and a "Free Living Soul"[^cite_note-polar-101] and thus immune to prosecution. She was jailed for one day for [contempt of court](7201.md)[^cite_note-bizarre-119][^cite_note-136] and later [remanded to jail pending trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remand_\(detention\)) for refusing to cooperate with the court or comply with the conditions of her release.[^cite_note-notaperson-135][^cite_note-137] In January 2023, Bauer was found guilty on all counts of misdemeanor and of the felony of [obstructing an official proceeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obstructing_an_official_proceeding).[^cite_note-Bauerguilty-138] In May, she was sentenced to 27 months in prison.[^cite_note-139] Bauer's co-defendant, who had pleaded guilty to a misdemeanor, was sentenced to probation and to a \$500 fine.[^cite_note-Bauerguilty-138]

In 2022, Darrell Brooks, the perpetrator of the [Waukesha Christmas parade attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waukesha_Christmas_parade_attack), claimed to be "sovereign"[^cite_note-CBS58-45] and used other pseudolegal arguments as part of his *[pro se](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro_se)* defense.[^cite_note-140][^cite_note-Vice-141][^cite_note-142] Judge [Jennifer Dorow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jennifer_Dorow) ruled that Brooks was not allowed to argue he was a sovereign citizen in court, saying the defense was without merit;[^cite_note-143] she said that sovereign citizen legal theories are "nonsense" and that the movement's tactics had no place in the judicial system.[^cite_note-FOX6_2-144] Brooks was found guilty on all counts[^cite_note-Brooksguilty-145] and sentenced to life without the possibility of parole.[^cite_note-146]

"Gurus" Bonnie and David Straight sold their adherents processes and documents (such as "noncitizen national passports" and illegal license plates) that purportedly gave them "American State National" status and made them immune to U.S. jurisdiction. The Straights' methods were proved ineffective in April 2023 when they were arrested and detained on several charges,[^cite_note-SovcitQanon-67] including driving without a license. Bonnie Straight, who had a warrant on a 2015 gun charge, was sentenced to five years' imprisonment. The court rejected her arguments that it did not have jurisdiction over her, and did not allow her husband to represent her in court.[^cite_note-147][^cite_note-148][^cite_note-149]

### Strawman theory and redemption schemes

See also: [Redemption movement § Heather Ann Tucci-Jarraf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redemption_movement#Heather_Ann_Tucci-Jarraf)

The core [redemption/A4V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redemption_movement) theory that people possess vast sums of money hidden from them by the government in a secret account, and that this money can be unlocked through specific means, has no basis in reality. Likewise, the [strawman theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strawman_theory) has been repeatedly dismissed by courts. Both theories are listed by the [FBI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI) as common [fraud](58790.md) schemes.[^cite_note-FBI-150] In 2021, the [District Court of Queensland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_Court_of_Queensland) dismissed an application that relied on the strawman theory, commenting that this argument "may properly be described as nonsense or gobbledygook".[^cite_note-gobbledygook-151] Redemption methods such as "Accepted for Value" are based on a misinterpretation of the [Uniform Commercial Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_Commercial_Code) and have no effect.[^cite_note-spl4-30]

Roger Elvick, the originator of the redemption movement, was convicted in 1991 in Hawaii of passing more than \$1 million in false [sight drafts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sight_draft), and of filing fraudulent [IRS forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IRS_tax_forms). He was sentenced to five years in federal prison. Upon his release, Elvick resumed his activities, conceiving the strawman theory at that point. In 2003, he was indicted in [Ohio](22199.md) on multiple [felony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felony) counts. During preliminary hearings, Elvick disrupted proceedings by denying his identity and claiming that the court had no jurisdiction over him or his "strawman". A judge ruled Elvick mentally unfit to stand trial and committed him to a correctional psychiatric facility. After nine months of treatment, Elvick stood trial and pleaded guilty; in April 2005, he was sentenced to four years in prison.[^cite_note-152]

Heather Ann Tucci-Jarraf, a licensed lawyer who had been at one point a state prosecutor, eventually joined the sovereign citizen movement: she built an online following as a "guru" and advocated the use of redemption methods to reclaim one's alleged secret fund from the banking system and the [Federal Reserve](10819.md). One of her followers, Randall Beane, used [Internet fraud](805626.md) to embezzle two million dollars, which he believed were part of his secret account; Tucci-Jarraf was aware of Beane's scheme and advised him throughout. Beane and Tucci-Jarraf were arrested and charged with [federal crimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_crimes). Both were found guilty of conspiracy to [launder money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launder_money) in 2018, with Beane also being convicted of [wire](905072.md) and [bank fraud](486777.md). The court ruled that Tucci-Jarraf, having used her legal training to assist Beane, was an aggravating circumstance.[^cite_note-153][^cite_note-154][^cite_note-155][^cite_note-156][^cite_note-USvBeaneandTucci-Jarraf-134] Beane was sentenced to 155 months in prison, and Tucci-Jarraf to 57 months.[^cite_note-bostonlawreview-95]

Creating and selling fictitious financial instruments is likewise a [scam](96637.md). People who purchased sovereign citizen instruments purported to help them pay off their debts or avoid foreclosures have worsened their situation by doing so.[^cite_note-Funny_Money-111] Winston Shrout, an influential sovereign citizen "guru" based in [Oregon](26811621.md), who advocated [tax resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_resistance_in_the_United_States) and redemption/A4V schemes, issued hundreds of fake "bills of exchange" for himself and others, and eventually mailed to a bank one quadrillion dollars in counterfeit [securities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Securities) supposedly to be honored by the [Treasury](53667.md).[^cite_note-splc5-110][^cite_note-Geezer-157] Shrout was charged in 2016 with 13 counts of using fictitious financial instruments.[^cite_note-ADLShrout-40] In 2017, he was found guilty of several counts of tax evasion and producing fraudulent documents. The next year, he was sentenced to 10 years in prison. Several of Shrout's followers who had tested his ideas, including his daughter, were also sentenced.[^cite_note-tinfoil-158][^cite_note-splc5-110][^cite_note-Geezer-157]

### Traffic

See also: [Traffic code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_code)

Sovereign citizens' argument that they do not need driver's licenses, license plates, and vehicle insurance has never been upheld in court.[^cite_note-roads-70] Sovereign citizens falsely claim that the [United States Supreme Court](31737.md) has upheld the right to travel as allowing operation of a motor vehicle without a driver's license. While the U.S. Constitution does grant the [right to "travel"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_movement_under_United_States_law) by foot, bike, or horse, operating a motor vehicle requires training and licensure.[^cite_note-159] The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that driver's licenses and traffic regulations are necessary for public safety.[^cite_note-160][^cite_note-161][^cite_note-162]

### Sovereign citizen entities

Sovereign citizens' "common law courts" and other "legal" entities lack any legitimacy. Some may be simply ignored by authorities: in 2015, sovereign citizen "guru" Anna Maria Riezinger aka Anna von Reitz, the self-proclaimed "judge" of a common law court in [Alaska](624.md),[^cite_note-SeattleTimes-36] published a letter calling for federal agents to arrest President [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama), the entire [Congress](31756.md) and the [Secretary of the Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_the_Treasury),[^cite_note-reitz-63] causing a minor Internet rumor. [Snopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snopes) debunked her claim by establishing that von Reitz was not a real judge and that her "orders" therefore had no force.[^cite_note-163]

Depending on the nature and severity of their actions, sovereign citizen "courts" may be disbanded and their leaders prosecuted.[^cite_note-splc98-58] In 2016, after [David Wynn Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Wynn_Miller)'s "Federal Postal court" issued a \$11.5 million judgment against a mortgage service company, a federal judge investigated that entity and ruled that it was "a sham and no more than a product of fertile imagination".[^cite_note-abajournal-164] Two years later, Leighton Ward, who worked as "clerk" of this false court[^cite_note-abajournal-164] and had used this capacity as part of a [mortgage elimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortgage_elimination) scheme based on the use of Miller's language,[^cite_note-165] was sentenced in Arizona to 23+1⁄2 years in prison for fraudulent schemes and artifices.[^cite_note-166][^cite_note-167][^cite_note-168]

In the 2010s, [Bruce Doucette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Doucette), a computer repair shop owner who styled himself a "Superior Court Judge" and led a group called "The People's Grand Jury in Colorado", traveled the country to help other sovereign citizens fight local governments and set up their own "common law courts".[^cite_note-denverpost-169][^cite_note-spl1-170][^cite_note-spl3-171][^cite_note-cpr-172] He and his followers attempted to intimidate public officials so they would dismiss criminal cases against other sovereign citizens.[^cite_note-spl2-173] When these efforts failed, Doucette's group retaliated by engaging in [paper terrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_terrorism) against them[^cite_note-spl1-170][^cite_note-westw-174] and threatening them with "arrest".[^cite_note-spl2-173] In May 2018, [Colorado](5399.md)'s [18th Judicial District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_district_courts) ruled that Doucette's network of "common law courts" was a [racketeering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racketeering) enterprise equivalent to [organized crime](22625.md). Doucette was found guilty of multiple felonies and sentenced to 38 years in prison.[^cite_note-spl2-173] Two of his co-defendants were sentenced to 36 and 22 years, respectively.[^cite_note-spl1-170] Colorado prosecutors said that with this verdict, they wished to send a message nationally to sovereign citizens and remind them that threats against local government officials are not tolerated.[^cite_note-cpr-172]

Randal Rosado, a Florida resident, created a series of false legal entities, including an "International Court of Commerce", and used them to file fictitious arrest warrants, court orders, and liens against public officials and lawyers, most of whom had been involved in [foreclosures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreclosure). In September 2019, Rosado was sentenced to 40 years in prison on numerous counts of unlawful retaliation against public officials and of [simulating the legal process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abuse_of_process).[^cite_note-175][^cite_note-176][^cite_note-177]

In August 2021, [Sitcomm Arbitration Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sitcomm_Arbitration_Association), the largest sovereign citizen "[arbitration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbitration)" entity,[^cite_note-Karlik-178] was held liable for a \$1,384,371.24 fine in a default judgment for violation of the [Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racketeer_Influenced_and_Corrupt_Organizations_Act).[^cite_note-179]

### Other arguments and schemes

The claim that the [District of Columbia Organic Act of 1871](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_of_Columbia_Organic_Act_of_1871) turned the United States into a business corporation is based on a misunderstanding of the term [municipal corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipal_corporation) used in the Act (which referred to the District of Columbia and not to the entire country)[^cite_note-180][^cite_note-factcheck-181] and on a misinterpretation of a provision in Title 28 of the [United States Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Code), which includes a definition of the United States as a "federal corporation" (meaning a group authorized to legally act as a single entity and not a business corporation).[^cite_note-GWedu-71]

The theories that "silence means consent" and that an unrebutted [affidavit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affidavit) stands as truth are based on misinterpretations of the [legal maxim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_maxim) "He who does not deny, admits".[^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78]

The idea that "there is no crime if there is no injured party" is based on a misinterpretation of [tort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tort) law[^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78] and fails to recognize the existence of different levels of [legal violations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrongdoing).

Filing fraudulent notices of [liens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lien) or documents is a crime in the United States.[^cite_note-NYT82313-12] Other forms of [paper terrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_terrorism) may be similarly punished by law: Brett Andrew Nelson, a sovereign citizen from Colorado, spent years filing "claims of damages" against judges and other public officials, as well as private citizens whom he felt had wronged him. His conflict with the judiciary started in 2017 over a child custody dispute. He later issued numerous false "judgements", demanding thousands of dollars from officials who had fined him for issues such as traffic violations and dog bites, and similarly harassed the mother of his child and people from his neighborhood. In April 2024, Nelson was sentenced to 12 years in prison.[^cite_note-182][^cite_note-183]

American courts have routinely dismissed documents written in [David Wynn Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Wynn_Miller)'s "Parse-Syntax-Grammar"/"Quantum Grammar" language, calling them unintelligible.[^cite_note-ozy2020-106][^cite_note-184][^cite_note-185][^cite_note-186] Canadian judge John D. Rooke commented, in his *Meads v. Meads* decision, that Miller's "bizarre form of 'legal grammar'" is "not merely incomprehensible in Canada, but equally so in any other jurisdiction".[^cite_note-Meads-104]

The [Universal Postal Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Postal_Union), which is often invoked as a supranational authority in sovereign citizen schemes,[^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78] has officially denied that it has "the authority to confer official recognition" upon sovereign citizens, "or to grant some kind of formal status to such individuals", also specifying that "the use of postage stamps on legal documents does not create an opportunity or obligation for the UPU to become involved in those matters".[^cite_note-187]

## Outside the United States

### English-speaking countries

See also: [Freeman on the land movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeman_on_the_land_movement)

With the advent of the Internet and continuing during the 21st century, people throughout the [Anglosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English-speaking_world) who share the core beliefs of these movements have been able to connect and share their ideas. While arguments specific to the history and laws of the United States are not used (except inadvertently, by litigants who use poorly adapted U.S. material),[^cite_note-Meads-104] many concepts have been incorporated or adopted by individuals and groups in English-speaking [Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations) countries.[^cite_note-kent2015-23][^cite_note-188]

In Canada, which has its own tradition of tax protesters,[^cite_note-HistoryCanada-189] fiscal misconceptions of US origin were gradually introduced during the 1980s and 1990s.[^cite_note-Netolitzky20182-5] In Commonwealth countries, pseudolaw advocates mostly used the name "freemen on the land" during the 2000s, but US-style sovereign citizen ideology has also reached these regions.[^cite_note-Netolitzky20182-5][^cite_note-Wagner-190][^cite_note-191] There is some crossover between Canadian freemen on the land and US sovereign citizens, as well as various others sharing similar beliefs, which may be loosely defined as "see\[ing] the state as a corporation with no authority over free citizens".[^cite_note-kent2015-23]

As of the 2010s, there are people identifying as sovereign citizens in Canada, Australia, New Zealand, the United Kingdom, Ireland, and South Africa.[^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78][^cite_note-kent2015-23][^cite_note-192] Sovereign citizens from the U.S. have gone on speaking tours to New Zealand and Australia, appealing to struggling farmers, and there are Internet presences in both countries.[^cite_note-kent2015-23]

#### Canada

Around 1999–2000, sovereign citizen and [redemption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redemption_movement) concepts were introduced into Canada by tax protester Eldon Warman, who adapted them to a Commonwealth context, launching the "Detaxer" movement. These ideas were further adapted by the [freeman on the land movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeman_on_the_land_movement), created by former stand-up comedian Robert Arthur Menard. That movement espouses an ideology broadly similar to that of sovereign citizens, but is aimed at a more [left-wing](18499.md) audience[^cite_note-Netolitzky20182-5] and assigns a special role to [section 7 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_7_of_the_Canadian_Charter_of_Rights_and_Freedoms).[^cite_note-HistoryCanada-189]

Whereas the more Canada-specific freeman-on-the-land movement has declined since the early 2010s,[^cite_note-Netolitzky20182-5] American-style sovereign citizen ideology has spread to Commonwealth countries during the same period, and the Canadian sovereign citizen movement has gained traction.[^cite_note-Moore_2013-193] Canada had an estimated 30,000 sovereign citizens in 2015, many of whom also associated with the freeman on the land movement.[^cite_note-194] There can be confusion between the two populations.[^cite_note-Netolitzky2019-42][^cite_note-195]

The 2012 ruling *Meads v. Meads* examined almost 150 cases involving [pseudolaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudolaw) and sovereign citizen or freeman of the land tactics, grouping them and characterizing them as "Organized Pseudolegal Commercial Arguments".[^cite_note-Meads-104][^cite_note-Netolitzky2018-78]

[Romana Didulo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romana_Didulo), one of Canada's most notorious QAnon conspiracy theorists during the early 2020s, used sovereign citizen rhetoric to persuade her followers not to pay their taxes, debts, or utility bills.[^cite_note-conversationsarteschi-56] In 2024, lawyer Naomi Arbabi resigned her license after being suspended by the Law Society of British Columbia for filing a frivolous lawsuit that used pseudolegal arguments similar to those of the sovereign citizen movement.[^cite_note-196]

#### Australia

See also: [Far-right terrorism in Australia § Others](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far-right_terrorism_in_Australia#Others)

Australia, which has its own tradition of pseudolaw, imported sovereign citizen ideas in the 1990s, even before the movement's 2000s resurgence. It later imported the more Commonwealth-specific freeman on the land movement.[^cite_note-Netolitzky20182-5] There is some cross-over between Australian freemen on the land,[^cite_note-stocken2016-197] local sovereign citizens groups, and some others.[^cite_note-kent2015-23][^cite_note-stocken2016-197] The core concept has been tested by several court cases, none successful for the "freemen".[^cite_note-lawland-198] In 2011, [climate denier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_denier) and political activist [Malcolm Roberts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_Roberts_\(politician\)) (later elected senator for [Pauline Hanson's One Nation](83013.md) party), wrote a letter to then Prime Minister [Julia Gillard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_Gillard) filled with characteristic sovereign citizen ideas and vocabulary, although he denied that he was a "sovereign citizen".[^cite_note-199][^cite_note-200]

From the 2010s, there has been a growing number of freemen targeting [Indigenous Australians](12598742.md), with groups using names like Tribal Sovereign Parliament of [Gondwana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gondwana) Land, the Original Sovereign Tribal Federation (OSTF), and the Original Sovereign Confederation. OSTF Founder Mark McMurtrie, an [Aboriginal](2912594.md) man, has produced YouTube videos speaking about "common law", which incorporates freemen beliefs. Appealing to other Aboriginal people by partly identifying with the [land rights movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_rights_in_Australia), McMurtrie played on their feelings of alienation and lack of trust in the systems which had not served Indigenous people well.[^cite_note-201][^cite_note-202]

In 2015, the [New South Wales Police Force](563673.md) identified "sovereign citizens" as a potential terrorist threat, estimating that there were about 300 sovereign citizens in the state at the time.[^cite_note-203] Freemen/sovereign citizen ideas have been promoted on the Internet by various Australian groups such as "United Rights Australia" (U R Australia).[^cite_note-kent2015-23][^cite_note-vox2018-204]

The [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Australia) has accelerated the spread of the movement in Australia; numerous incidents with law enforcement have since been reported, some of them violent such as the [Wieambilla (2022)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wieambilla_shootings) and [Porepunkah (2025)](80864208.md) shootings.[^cite_note-205]

#### New Zealand

New Zealand, which has imported foreign pseudolaw including Canadian freeman on the land ideology, has developed its own sovereign citizen movement.[^cite_note-Netolitzky20182-5] Many litigants using pseudolegal concepts in New Zealand are [Māori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people); invoking Indigineous Rights.[^cite_note-Netolitzky20182-5] Regardless of their ethnicity, some say that the [declaration of the Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_the_Independence_of_New_Zealand) or the [Treaty of Waitangi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Waitangi) make them exempt from the law. As in Australia, the movement gained traction there during the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_New_Zealand).[^cite_note-206] In 2024, police identified 1,400 New Zealanders as acting under the influence of sovereign citizen ideology.[^cite_note-207]

University of Otago Professor Stephen Young has coauthored with Harry Hobbs a book on the movement: **Outlawed**; published in August 2026 by *Penguin* [^cite_note-208]. The book says that most events have been in the Nelson-Tasman, Auckland and Waikato regions. Serious incidents include punching a police officer and kidnapping a meter reader.[^cite_note-209]

#### United Kingdom



A sovereign citizen "legal name fraud" billboard in the UK, 2016.

[^cite_note-210]

Sovereign citizen ideology reached the United Kingdom around 2010.[^cite_note-lawless_ones-41] British sovereign citizens have helped spread [COVID vaccine misinformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_misinformation) as well as various conspiracy theories – including [9/11 theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/9/11_conspiracy_theories) and one about [the Queen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_II) having been replaced by a satanic [cabal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabal) – and tried to set up their own [cryptocurrency](36662188.md).[^cite_note-UKonnotice-51]

### Germany and Austria

See also: [Reichsbürger movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichsb%C3%BCrger_movement)

The *Reichsbürger* (lit. 'imperial citizen') movement in Germany originated around 1985 and had approximately 19,000 members in 2019, more concentrated in the south and east. The originator claimed to have been appointed head of the post-World War I *[Reich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reich)*, but other leaders claim [imperial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire) authority. The movement consists of different, usually small groups. Some groups have issued passports and identification cards.[^cite_note-212][^cite_note-213]

The *Reichsbürger* movement claims that modern-day Germany is not a sovereign state but a corporation that the [Allied nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allied_nations) created after World War II. It has also expressed hope that Donald Trump will lead an army to restore the empire.[^cite_note-214][^cite_note-\:4-215] According to the [German domestic intelligence service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Office_for_the_Protection_of_the_Constitution), only a small number of groups in the Reich citizen movement are on the far right. Rather, the common denominator is the rejection of the [Federal Republic](11867.md) as a legal entity.[^cite_note-216]

The Reichsbürger movement has used language and techniques from the One People's Public Trust, an American sovereign citizen group operated by "guru" Heather Ann Tucci-Jarraf.[^cite_note-bostonlawreview-95] On December 7, 2022, 25 people connected to the movement were arrested in a nationwide raid by German police forces for their involvement in a [suspected terrorist plot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_German_coup_d'%C3%A9tat_plot) against the German government and institutions.[^cite_note-217] On May 13, 2025, the German government banned the main Reichsbürger organization, "Königreich Deutschland".[^cite_note-218][^cite_note-\:4-215]

In Austria, the group *Staatenbund Österreich* ('Austrian Commonwealth'), in addition to issuing its own passports and licence plates, had a written constitution.[^cite_note-Marko2020-219] The group, established in November 2015, also used language from the One People's Public Trust.[^cite_note-Hartleb2020-220] In 2019, its leader was sentenced to 14 years in jail after trying to order the army to [overthrow the government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Austrian_coup_d'%C3%A9tat_plot) and requesting foreign assistance from [Vladimir Putin](32817.md). Other members received lesser sentences.[^cite_note-221]

### Italy

As of the 2010s, incidents involving sovereign citizens have been reported in Italy, with various people purporting to opt out of Italian citizenship through nonlegal procedures and make themselves immune from Italian law. Members of one group attempt to do so by declaring themselves citizens of the "Sovereign Kingdom of [Gaia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaia)" (*Regno Sovrano di Gaia*) while others refer to themselves as the "People of Mother Earth" (*Popolo della Terra Madre*).[^cite_note-222][^cite_note-223]

Another group called "We is, I am" (*Noi è, Io sono*; also translated as "One People I Am") emerged in Italy in the early 2020s, inciting its followers to stop paying utility bills, taxes, and fines, and to reject passports and driver's licenses.[^cite_note-224] This movement is connected with American "guru" [Heather Ann Tucci-Jarraf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redemption_movement#Heather_Ann_Tucci-Jarraf)[^cite_note-225] and, according to Italian media, had about 10,000 followers in 2023.[^cite_note-226][^cite_note-227]

### Russia

Main article: [Union of Slavic Forces of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_of_Slavic_Forces_of_Russia)

A Russian movement of conspiracy theorists, known among other names as the Union of Slavic Forces of Russia (Союз славянских сил Руси, *Soyuz slavyanskikh sil Rusi*), or more informally as "Soviet Citizens", holds that the Soviet Union still exists *[de jure](43594.md)* and that the current Russian government and legislation are thus illegitimate. One of its beliefs is that the [government of the Russian Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_the_Russian_Federation) is an [offshore company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offshore_company) through which the United States illegally controls the country.[^cite_note-ng.ru-228][^cite_note-vesti.ru-229][^cite_note-Moscowtimes-230]

### France and Belgium

In France, pseudolegal arguments claiming that enacted laws were invalid became gradually popular during the 2010s among conspiracy theorists. They gained more traction during the [yellow vests protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_vests_protests), with claims that the [Constitution of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_France) was null and void.[^cite_note-contracte-231]

A [New Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Age)-oriented French group of conspiracy theorists called "One Nation" became known to the public in 2021 for their involvement in the [kidnapping of a child](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_incidents_involving_QAnon#Later). Later that year, they attempted to purchase a property in [Lot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lot_\(department\)), purportedly to create a "center for the arts" and a "research laboratory". The One Nation movement holds beliefs similar to those of American sovereign citizens and denies the legitimacy of the [French State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Fifth_Republic). They also share beliefs with [QAnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QAnon). The group translates the name "sovereign citizens" in French as *êtres souverains* (sovereign beings) or *êtres éveillés* (awakened beings).[^cite_note-232][^cite_note-233][^cite_note-234][^cite_note-235][^cite_note-236]

In 2021, people affiliated with One Nation were reported to be active in Belgium.[^cite_note-237] In February 2022, the group's French spokeswoman was sentenced to six months in prison for multiple traffic violations.[^cite_note-238] She was arrested and incarcerated in September of the same year.[^cite_note-239]

In 2024, sovereign citizen ideology became more familiar to the French general public due to the [viral video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viral_video) of an incident between a couple of conspiracy theorists and traffic police.[^cite_note-240][^cite_note-contracte-231] It was also reported that the movement was gaining more followers in Belgium.[^cite_note-241]

### Netherlands

In 2023, the sovereign citizen movement was reported to be gaining traction in the Netherlands. Dutch sovereign citizens are usually called "autonomen", but may also be called "soevereinen". People connected with a group called "Common Law Netherlands Earth" (*Common Law Nederland Earth*) organised themselves as "sheriffs" and rejected the rule of law in the Netherlands. In July 2024, a movement member was tried for attempting to acquire firearms illegally. Other Dutch sovereign citizens were reported to have made similar attempts, which caused concern in the Netherlands where anti-government radical movements are traditionally considered nonviolent. In 2024, Dutch intelligence and security services estimated the number of Dutch sovereign citizens at several tens of thousands.[^cite_note-242]

### Czech Republic

The movement was first covered by Czech media in 2022, when the government noticed an increasing number of people submitting a "[sworn declaration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sworn_declaration) of life" and demanding to terminate a contract with the "Czech Republic corporation".[^cite_note-243][^cite_note-hnuti-244] It gained further traction in the middle of 2023, when sovereign citizen movement followers tried to interrupt multiple court proceedings involving disseminators of COVID-19 and [Russo-Ukrainian War disinformation](70144390.md), demanding that the judges "identify" themselves.[^cite_note-245][^cite_note-246] The movement was also connected to a case of a family with two unregistered children living in a [yurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yurt) near [Náchod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N%C3%A1chod).[^cite_note-247]

Czech members of the movement maintain that they remain *[de jure](43594.md)* citizens of Czechoslovakia, based on a belief that the [dissolution of Czechoslovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_Czechoslovakia) was illegal.[^cite_note-hnuti-244] There are multiple active groups based on the sovereign citizen ideology, the most prominent one being the "Community of Legitimate Creditors of the Czech Republic" ([Czech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_language): *Společenství legitimních věřitelů České republiky*).[^cite_note-248]

### Lithuania

The sovereign citizen movement emerged in Lithuania during the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Lithuania) among people wishing to resist restrictions. The first Lithuanian sovereign citizen organization was an offshoot of the local [Krishna Consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krishna_Consciousness) movement. Some sovereign citizens have united in a [Naujieji Lažai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naujieji_La%C5%BEai) community that expresses nostalgia for the [Soviet era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic) and associates with pro-[Russia](25391.md) and pro-[Belarus](3457.md) initiatives.[^cite_note-249] Lithuanian sovereign citizens attracted more attention in 2023 when two parents belonging to the movement, whose family had no identity documents, kidnapped their children from foster care and attempted to escape with them to Belarus.[^cite_note-250]

### Norway

There is a small sovereign citizen movement in Norway.[^cite_note-251] One prominent member is Ingunn Sigurdsdatter (formerly Røiseland), who gained notoriety for renouncing her citizenship and declaring a sovereign state around her own house called "Niceland". She was evicted in 2014 when her house was foreclosed because she refused to pay interest or make down payments on her mortgage.[^cite_note-252] She also opposed the swine flu vaccine and was one of several people who sued the Norwegian Institute of Public Health for lying and fearmongering about the swine flu pandemic.[^cite_note-253] She has been an associate of controversial[^cite_note-254] princess [Märtha Louise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A4rtha_Louise).[^cite_note-255]

### Singapore

Sovereign citizen ideology has surfaced in Singapore, but it is not as widespread there as in some other countries. Two prominent cases have highlighted this phenomenon:

- During the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Singapore), Paramjeet Kaur, called the "Shunfu mart woman" by local media, gained notoriety for refusing to wear a mask and arguing with safe-distancing ambassadors. She cited sovereign citizen-like beliefs, claiming that the regulations did not apply to her. She was jailed for two weeks and fined S\$2,000.

- In 2023, Tarchandi Tan was convicted on multiple charges, including contempt of court and causing intentional harassment. She had repeatedly refused to comply with court orders, insisting that she was not bound by Singaporean law and invoking sovereign citizen rhetoric.
