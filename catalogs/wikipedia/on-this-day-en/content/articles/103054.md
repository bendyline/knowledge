The **Tuareg people**[^cite_note-11] or the **Tuaregs** are an [Amazigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berbers) ethnic group. They are traditionally [nomadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomad) [pastoralists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoralism) who principally inhabit the [Sahara](325363.md) in an area stretching from southwestern [Libya](17633.md) to southern [Algeria](358.md), [Niger](21373.md), [Mali](19127.md), [Burkina Faso](3470.md), and as far as northern [Nigeria](21383.md), with smaller communities in [Chad](5488.md) and [Sudan](27421.md) known as the *Kinnin*.[^cite_note-Shoup-12][^cite_note-13][^cite_note-Assal2022-14][^cite_note-Jay1996-15] Their population is estimated to be 4 to 5 million worldwide.[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17]

The Tuareg speak the [Tuareg languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuareg_languages), which include [Tamasheq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamasheq), [Tamahaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamahaq), [Tamajeq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamajeq) and [Tawellemmet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tawellemmet_language), all of which belong to the [Berber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_languages) branch of the [Afroasiatic family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afroasiatic_family).[^cite_note-SIL-18]

They are a semi-nomadic people who mostly practice [Islam](6037917.md), and are descended from the indigenous Berber communities of Northern Africa, whose ancestry has been described as a mosaic of local [Northern African](21714.md) ([Taforalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taforalt)), [Middle Eastern](19323.md), [European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_history_of_Europe) ([Early European Farmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_European_Farmers)), and [Sub-Saharan African](27067.md), prior to the [Muslim conquest of the Maghreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_the_Maghreb).[^cite_note-Levinson1996tua-19][^cite_note-20] Some researchers have tied the origin of the Tuareg ethnicity with the fall of the [Garamantes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garamantes), who inhabited the [Fezzan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fezzan) (Libya) from the 1st millennium BC to the 5th century AD.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22] Tuareg people are credited with spreading Islam in North Africa and the adjacent [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel) region.[^cite_note-Norris1976-23]

Tuareg social structure has traditionally included [clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clan) membership, social status and [caste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste) hierarchies within each political confederation.[^cite_note-Gates2010p499-24][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199516,_17–22,_38–44-25][^cite_note-Tamari_1991_221–222,_228–250-26] The Tuareg have controlled several [trans-Saharan trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Saharan_trade) routes and have been an important party to the conflicts in the Saharan region during the [colonial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa) and [post-colonial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decolonisation_of_Africa) eras.[^cite_note-Gates2010p499-24]

## Names

The origins and meanings of the name *Tuareg* have long been debated. It would appear that *Twārəg* is derived from the [broken plural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broken_plural) of *Tārgi*, a name whose former meaning was "inhabitant of *Targa*", the Tuareg name of the [Libyan](17633.md) region commonly known as [Fezzan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fezzan). *Targa* in Berber means "(drainage) channel".[^cite_note-27] Another theory is that Tuareg is derived from *Tuwariq*, the plural of the Arabic exonym *Tariqi*.[^cite_note-Shoup-12] Also compare [Hassaniya Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hassaniya_Arabic) *Ṭwāreg*, plural of *Ṭāreg*, ultimately derived from Berber *targa* 'drainage channel; Fezzan'.

The term for a Tuareg man is *Amajagh* (variants: *Amashegh*, *Amahagh*), the term for a woman *Tamajaq* (variants: *Tamasheq*, *Tamahaq*, *Timajaghen*). Spellings of the appellation vary by Tuareg dialect. They all reflect the same linguistic root, expressing the notion of "freemen". As such, the endonym strictly refers only to the Tuareg [nobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobility), not the artisanal client castes and the slaves.[^cite_note-28] Two other Tuareg self-designations are *Kel Tamasheq*, meaning "speakers of [Tamasheq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuareg_languages)", and *Kel Tagelmust*, meaning "veiled people" in allusion to the [tagelmust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagelmust) garment that is traditionally worn by Tuareg men.[^cite_note-Shoup-12]

The [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) exonym "Blue People" is similarly derived from the [indigo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigo) color of the tagelmust veils and other clothing, which sometimes stains the skin underneath giving it a blueish tint.[^cite_note-GearonEamonn-29] Another term for the Tuareg is *Imuhagh* or *Imushagh*, a cognate to the northern Berber self-name *Imazighen*.[^cite_note-Minahan418-30]

## Demography and languages



The traditional distribution of the Tuareg in the [Sahara](325363.md)

[^cite_note-Shoup-12]

The Tuareg today inhabit a vast area in the [Sahara](325363.md), stretching from far southwestern Libya to southern Algeria, Niger, Mali, Burkina Faso, and the far north of Nigeria.[^cite_note-Shoup-12] A small subgroup known as the Kinnin Tuareg have historically inhabited Northern Darfur, in what is now western Sudan, particularly around the region of Mellit. They are culturally and linguistically affiliated with the broader Tuareg group and have been documented in anthropological field studies.[^cite_note-Assal2022-14] Another community of Tuareg descent, also referred to as the Kinnin, has lived in and around the city of [Abéché](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ab%C3%A9ch%C3%A9) in eastern Chad since the early 20th century.[^cite_note-Jay1996-15] These groups trace their origin to migrations from Niger (especially Kel Denneg and Kel Gress tribes),[^cite_note-Jay1996-15] and while Tuareg customs are still present, many members have linguistically shifted to [Chadian Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chadian_Arabic) over time.[^cite_note-Jay1996-15]

Their combined population in these territories exceeds 2.5 million, with an estimated population in Niger of around 2 million (11% of inhabitants) and in Mali of another 0.5 million (3% of inhabitants).[^cite_note-nigertuareg-1][^cite_note-Imperato2008-31]

The Tuareg are the majority ethnic group in the [Kidal Region](2214311.md) of northeastern Mali.[^cite_note-rudolph381-32]

The Tuareg traditionally speak the [Tuareg languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuareg_languages), also known as *Tamasheq, Tamajeq* or *Tamahaq*, depending on the dialect.[^cite_note-33] These languages belong to the [Berber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_languages) branch of the [Afroasiatic family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afroasiatic_family).[^cite_note-SIL-18] According to *[Ethnologue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnologue)*, there are an estimated 1.2 million Tuareg speakers. Around half of this number consists of speakers of the eastern dialect (*Tamajaq*, *Tawallammat*).[^cite_note-SIL-18]

The exact number of Tuareg speakers per territory is uncertain. The [CIA](5183633.md) estimates that the Tuareg population in Mali constitutes approximately 0.9% of the national population (\~150,000), whereas about 3.5% of local inhabitants speak Tuareg (Tamasheq) as a primary language.[^cite_note-34] In contrast, Imperato (2008) estimates that the Tuareg represent around 3% of Mali's population.[^cite_note-Imperato2008-31]

## History

### Early history

Further information: [Azawad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azawad) and [Tin Hinan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin_Hinan)



An artist's representation of [Tin Hinan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin_Hinan), an ancient queen of the [Hoggar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoggar_Mountains)

In antiquity, the Tuareg moved southward from the [Tafilalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tafilalt) region into the [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel) under the Tuareg founding queen [Tin Hinan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin_Hinan), who is believed to have lived between the 4th and 5th centuries.[^cite_note-35] The matriarch's 1,500-year-old monumental [Tin Hinan tomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin_Hinan_tomb) is located in the Sahara at [Abalessa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abalessa) in the [Hoggar Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoggar_Mountains) of southern Algeria. Vestiges of an inscription in [Tifinagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tifinagh), the Tuareg's traditional Libyco-Berber writing script, have been found on one of the ancient sepulchre's walls.[^cite_note-36]

External accounts of interactions with the Tuareg are available from at least the 10th century onwards. [Ibn Hawkal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Hawkal) (10th century), [El-Bekri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Bakri) (11th century), [Edrisi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edrisi) (12th century), [Ibn Battutah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Battutah) (14th century), and [Leo Africanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Africanus) (16th century) all documented the Tuareg in some form, usually as Mulatthamin or "the veiled ones". Of the early historians, 14th-century scholar [Ibn Khaldûn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Khald%C3%BBn) probably wrote some of the most detailed commentary on the life and people of the Sahara, though he apparently never actually met them.[^cite_note-37]

### Colonial era

Further information: [Kaocen revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaocen_revolt) and [Kel Ajjer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kel_Ajjer)



Tuareg chief [Moussa Ag Amastan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moussa_Ag_Amastan) arriving in Paris, 1910

At the turn of the 19th century, the Tuareg territory was organised into confederations, each ruled by a supreme Chief (*Amenokal*), along with a council of elders from each tribe. These confederations were sometimes called "*Drum Groups*" after the Amenokal's symbol of authority, a drum. Clan (*Tewsit*) elders, called Imegharan (wisemen), were chosen to assist the chief of the confederation. Historically, there have been seven major confederations.[^cite_note-38]

- *[Kel Ajjer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kel_Ajjer)* or Azjar: centred in the oasis of [Aghat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghat,_Libya) (Ghat).

- *[Kel Ahaggar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kel_Ahaggar)*, in [Ahaggar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahaggar) mountains.

- *[Kel Adagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kel_Adagh)*, or *Kel Assuk*: [Kidal](1463133.md) and [Timbuktu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbuktu)

- *[Iwillimmidan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aulliminden) [Kel Ataram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kel_Ataram)* or *Western Iwillimmidan*: [Ménaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A9naka) and [Azawagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azawagh) regions (Mali)

- *[Iwillimmidan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aulliminden) [Kel Denneg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kel_Denneg)*, or *Eastern Iwillimmidan*: [Tchin-Tabaraden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tchin-Tabaraden), Abalagh, Teliya [Azawagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azawagh) (Niger).

- *[Kel Ayr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kel_Ayr)*: [Assodé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assod%C3%A9), [Agadez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agadez), [In Gal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In-Gall), [Timia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timia) and Ifrwan.

- *[Kel Gres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kel_Gres)*: [Zinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinder) and Tanut ([Tanout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanout)) and south into northern Nigeria.

- *[Kel Owey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kel_Owey)*: [Aïr Massif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%C3%AFr_Massif), seasonally south to [Tessaoua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessaoua) (Niger)

In the mid-19th century, descriptions of the Tuareg and their way of life were made by the English traveller [James Richardson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Richardson_\(explorer\)) in his journeys across the Libyan Sahara in 1845–1846.[^cite_note-39]

In pre-colonial times the *imuhagh* were the noble warrior caste of the Tuareg feared by their neighbors.[^cite_note-40] They proved their courage and skill in wars and raids.[^cite_note-41] Wars were rare, but raids, also known as *rezzous*, were frequent. A successful rezzou was profitable and brought much recognition, which was also reflected in the songs and poetry of the Tuareg.[^cite_note-42] The greatest glory was not achieved when great resistance had been overcome, but when the haul was great and the victims were taken by surprise through cunning.[^cite_note-43] The targets were often nomad camps of other Tuareg confederations with which they did not feel connected, but also the Chaambas, who nomadized in the northern Algerian Sahara, and the [Toubou people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toubou_people), [Hausa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausa_people), and [Fulani](102383.md) in the [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel) zone.[^cite_note-44]

The rezzou season usually began after the first rains. This ensured that the camels had enough food on the way there and back. Distant destinations were preferred, as rezzous often led to counter-rezzous, which were easier and quicker to organize over short distances.[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46] Everything that could be carried away was stolen, especially camels. However, through negotiations, it was possible for the victims to recover some or all of their loot later on.[^cite_note-47] From the middle of the 19th century, their vassals (*imaghad*) also took part in the wars and raids and organized their own rezzous. This was the time when they were also allowed to own camels. - However, the many raids by the northern Tuareg in the second half of the 19th century led to their impoverishment, because they had to hurry on their way back, and many animals were lost due to exhaustion or straying from the group. - During the Tuareg's military campaigns, prisoners were also taken and then kept as slaves or sold, regardless of their previous social status.[^cite_note-48] - During the period when their colonization was not yet complete (around 1900 to 1920), the French military commanders tolerated or authorized rezzous by ethnic groups that had submitted to them against those Tuareg tribes that resisted the French.[^cite_note-49] Afterwards, all rezzous and military campaigns on their own behalf were prohibited.

In the late 19th century, the Tuareg resisted the [French colonial invasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_French_colonial_empire) of their central Saharan homelands and annihilated a French expedition led by [Paul Flatters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Flatters) in 1881.[^cite_note-auto1-50] It was not until May 1902 that the French made a military advance into Hoggar territory. There they defeated the Kel Ahaggar in the Battle of Tit. This led to some of the Kel Ahaggar moving to Libya and into the territory of the Kel Ajjer. The others, under the leadership of the Amenocal Moussa ag Amastan, submitted to the French. - Some of the Kel Ajjer, who settled in south-eastern Algeria and south-western Libya, resisted colonization by France and were supported by the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md) and later by the [Senusiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senusiyya). Their leader was Sheikh Amoud Ag El Mokhtar. Well-armed, they fought fierce battles with the French between 1913 and 1920. Then their resistance collapsed.[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52] After numerous massacres on the part of the French and the southern Tuareg,[^cite_note-auto1-50] the Tuareg were defeated and forced to sign treaties in Mali in 1905 and Niger in 1917.

French colonial administration of the Tuareg was largely based on supporting the existing social hierarchy. The French concluded that Tuareg rebellions were largely the result of reform policies that undermined the traditional chiefs. The colonial authorities wished to create a protectorate operating, ideally, through single chieftains who ruled under French sovereignty, but were autonomous within their territories. Thus French rule, relying on the loyalty of the Tuareg noble caste, did not improve the status of the slave class.[^cite_note-53]



A Tuareg man in [Mali](19127.md) with his slave, 1974

### Post-colonial era

See also: [Tuareg rebellions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuareg_rebellion_\(disambiguation\))

When African countries achieved widespread independence in the 1960s, the traditional Tuareg territory was divided among a number of modern states: Niger, Mali, Algeria, Libya, and Burkina Faso. Political instability and competition for resources in the Sahel has since led to conflicts between the Tuareg and neighboring African groups. There have been tight restrictions placed on [nomadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomad) life because of high [population growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_growth). [Desertification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desertification) is exacerbated by over-exploitation of resources including firewood. This has pushed some Tuareg to experiment with farming; some have been forced to abandon herding and seek jobs in towns and cities.[^cite_note-peace-54]

Following the independence of Mali, a [Tuareg uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuareg_rebellion_\(1962%E2%80%931964\)) broke out in the Adrar N'Fughas mountains in the 1960s, joined by Tuareg groups from the [Adrar des Iforas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrar_des_Iforas) in northeastern Mali. The Malian Army suppressed the revolt, but resentment among the Tuareg fueled further uprisings.[^cite_note-peace-54]



Tuareg [separatist rebels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Movement_for_the_Liberation_of_Azawad) in Mali, January 2012

This [second (or third) uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuareg_rebellion_\(1990%E2%80%931995\)) was in May 1990. In the aftermath of a clash between government soldiers and Tuareg outside a prison in [Tchin-Tabaraden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tchin-Tabaraden), Niger, Tuareg in both Mali and Niger claimed independence for their traditional homeland: [Ténéré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%A9n%C3%A9r%C3%A9) in Niger, including their capital [Agadez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agadez), and the [Azawad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azawad) and [Kidal](1463133.md) regions of Mali.[^cite_note-auto-55]

Deadly clashes between Tuareg fighters, with leaders such as [Mano Dayak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mano_Dayak), and the military of both countries followed, with deaths into the thousands. Negotiations initiated by France and Algeria led to peace agreements in January 1992 in Mali and in 1995 in Niger, both arranging for decentralization of national power and the integration of Tuareg resistance fighters into the countries' national armies.[^cite_note-auto-55]

Major fighting between the Tuareg resistance and government security forces ended after the 1995 and 1996 agreements. As of 2004, sporadic fighting continued in Niger between government forces and Tuareg rebels. In 2007, a new [surge in violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuareg_rebellion_\(2007%E2%80%932009\)) occurred.[^cite_note-56]

The development of [Berberism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berberism) in North Africa in the 1990s fostered a Tuareg ethnic revival.[^cite_note-57]

Since 1998, three different flags have been designed to represent the Tuareg.[^cite_note-58] In Niger, the Tuareg people remain socially and economically marginalized, remaining poor and unrepresented in Niger's central government.[^cite_note-59]

On 21 March 2021, [IS-GS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_State_in_the_Greater_Sahara) militants [attacked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tillia_massacres) several villages around [Tillia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tillia), Niger, killing 141 people. The main victims of the massacres were the Tuaregs.[^cite_note-60]

During the [Mali War](36856978.md), in April 2026, Tuareg separatist group [Azawad Liberation Front](78996050.md) launched an [offensive](83033878.md) against the Malian Army, capturing the key commune of [Kidal](1463133.md) and parts of [Gao](1255721.md).[^cite_note-aj2-61][^cite_note-62]

## Religion

Further information: [Berber mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_mythology)



Tuaregs in prayer, 1973

The Tuareg traditionally adhered to the [Berber mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_mythology). Archaeological excavations of prehistoric tombs in the Maghreb have yielded skeletal remains that were painted with [ochre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ochre). Although this ritual practice was known to the [Iberomaurusians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberomaurusian), the custom seems instead to have been primarily derived from the ensuing [Capsian culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capsian_culture).[^cite_note-63] Megalithic tombs, such as the [jedar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jedars) sepulchres, were erected for religious and funerary practices. In 1926, one such tomb was discovered south of [Casablanca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casablanca). The monument was engraved with funerary inscriptions in the ancient Libyco-Berber writing script known as [Tifinagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tifinagh), which the Tuareg still use.[^cite_note-64]

During the medieval period, the Tuareg adopted [Islam](6037917.md) after its arrival with the [Umayyad Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate) in the 7th century.[^cite_note-Norris1976-23] In the 16th century, under the tutelage of El Maghili,[^cite_note-Brfo-65] the Tuareg embraced the [Maliki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maliki_school) school of [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni) Islam, which they now primarily follow.[^cite_note-Weissleder1978p17-66] The Tuareg helped spread Islam further into the [Western Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudan_\(region\)).[^cite_note-67] While Islam is the religion of the contemporary Tuareg, historical documents suggest that they initially resisted Islamization efforts in their traditional strongholds.[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69]

According to the anthropologist Susan Rasmussen, after the Tuareg had adopted the religion, they were reputedly lax in their prayers and observances of other Muslim precepts. Some of their ancient beliefs still exist today subtly within their culture and tradition, such as elements of pre-Islamic cosmology and rituals, particularly among Tuareg women, or the widespread "cult of the dead", which is a form of [ancestor veneration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veneration_of_the_dead). For example, Tuareg religious ceremonies contain allusions to matrilineal spirits, as well as to fertility, menstruation, the earth and ancestresses.[^cite_note-Levinson1996tua-19] Norris (1976) suggests that this apparent syncretism may stem from the influence of [Sufi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism) Muslim preachers on the Tuareg.[^cite_note-Norris1976-23]

The Tuaregs have been one of the influential ethnic groups in the spread of Islam and its legacy in North Africa and adjacent Sahel.[^cite_note-Norris1976-23] [Timbuktu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbuktu), an important Islamic center famed for its [ulama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulama), was established by Imasheghen Tuareg at the start of the 12th century.[^cite_note-70] It flourished under the protection and rule of a Tuareg confederation.[^cite_note-71][^cite_note-72] However, modern scholars believe that there is insufficient evidence to pinpoint the exact time of origin and founders of Timbuktu, although it is archeologically clear that the city originated from local trade between the Middle Niger Delta, on the one hand, and between the [pastoralists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoralists) of the Sahara, long before the first [hijra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hijri_year).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESaad19836-73] Monroe asserts, based on archaeological evidence, that Timbuktu emerged from an urban-rural dynamic, that is, aiming to provide services to its immediate rural [hinterland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinterland).[^cite_note-Monroe,_J._Cameron_2017-74]

In 1449, a Tuareg ruling house founded the [Tenere Sultanate of Aïr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Agadez) (Sultanate of Agadez) in the city of [Agadez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agadez) in the [Aïr Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%C3%AFr_Mountains).[^cite_note-Minahan418-30]

18th century Tuareg Islamic scholars such as [Jibril ibn 'Umar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jibril_ibn_Umar) later preached the value of revolutionary jihad. Inspired by these teachings, Ibn 'Umar's student [Usman dan Fodio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usman_dan_Fodio) led the [Sokoto jihads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jihad_of_Usman_dan_Fodio) and established the [Sokoto Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sokoto_Caliphate).[^cite_note-75]

## Society

Tuareg society has traditionally featured clan membership, social status and caste hierarchies within each political confederation.[^cite_note-Gates2010p499-24]

### Clans



A Tuareg from [Algeria](358.md)

Clans have been a historic part of the Tuaregs. The 7th century invasion of North Africa from the Middle East triggered an extensive migration of Tuaregs such as the Lemta and the Zarawa, along with other fellow [pastoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoralism) Berbers.[^cite_note-Levinson1996tua-19] Further invasions of [Banu Hilal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Hilal) and [Banu Sulaym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Sulaym) Arab tribes into Tuareg regions in the 11th century moved the Tuareg south into seven clans, which the [oral tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition) of Tuaregs claims are descendants of the same mother.[^cite_note-Levinson1996tua-19][^cite_note-Nicolaisen1963-76]

Each Tuareg clan (*tawshet*) is made up of family groups constituting a tribe,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199516,_17–22,_38–44-25] each led by its chief, the *amghar*. A series of *tawsheten* (plural of *tawshet*) may bond together under an *[Amenokal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amenokal)*, forming a *Kel* clan confederation. Tuareg self-identification is related only to their specific *Kel*, which means "those of". For example, *Kel Dinnig* (those of the east), *Kel Ataram* (those of the west).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199520–21-77]

The position of *amghar* is hereditary through a matrilineal principle; it is usual for the son of a sister of the incumbent chieftain to succeed to his position. The *amenokal* is elected in a ritual which differs between groups. The individual amghar who lead the clans making up the confederation usually have the deciding voice.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199520–21-77] The matrilineal inheritance and mythology among Tuareg clans, states Susan Rasmussen, is a cultural vestige from the pre-Islamic era of the Tuareg society.[^cite_note-Levinson1996tua-19]

According to Rasmussen, Tuareg society exhibits a blend of pre-Islamic and Islamic practices.[^cite_note-Levinson1996tua-19] Patrilineal Muslim values are believed to have been superimposed upon the Tuareg's traditional matrilineal society. Other apparently newer customs include close-cousin endogamous marriages and [polygyny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygyny) in conformity with Islamic tenets. Polygyny, which has been witnessed among Tuareg chiefs and Islamic scholars, is in turn thought to have been contrary to the pre-Islamic monogamous tradition of the nomadic Tuareg.[^cite_note-Levinson1996tua-19]

### Social stratification

Tuareg society has featured caste hierarchies within each clan and political confederation.[^cite_note-Gates2010p499-24][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199516,_17–22,_38–44-25][^cite_note-Tamari_1991_221–222,_228–250-26] These hierarchical systems have included nobles, clerics, craftsmen and unfree strata of people including widespread slavery.[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-Heath2005p7-79]

#### Nobility, vassals and clerics



Tuareg man from Algeria

Traditionally, Tuareg society is hierarchical, with [nobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobility) and vassals. The linguist [Karl-Gottfried Prasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl-Gottfried_Prasse) (1995) indicates that the nobles constitute the highest caste.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199516–17-80] They are known in the Tuareg language as *imušaɣ/imuhaɣ/imajăɣăn* "the proud and free".[^cite_note-Gates2010p499-24]

The nobles originally had a monopoly on carrying arms and owning camels, and were the warriors of the Tuareg regions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199516-81] They may have achieved their social status by subjugating other Tuareg castes, keeping arms to defend their properties and vassals. They have collected tribute from their vassals. This warrior nobility has traditionally married within their caste, not to individuals in strata below their own.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199516-81]

A collection of tribes, each led by a noble, forms a confederation whose chieftain, the *amănokal*, is elected from among the nobles by the tribal chiefs.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199516–17-80][^cite_note-Heath2005p7-79] The chieftain is the overlord during times of war, and receives tribute and taxes from tribes as a sign of their submission to his authority.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199520-82]

The vassal-herdsmen are the second free stratum within Tuareg society, occupying a position just below that of the nobles.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199517-83] They are known as *ímɣad* (*Imghad*, singular *Amghid*) in the Tuareg language.[^cite_note-Heath2005p7-79] Although the vassals were free, they did not own camels but instead kept donkeys and herds of goats, sheep and oxen. They pastured and tended their own herds as well those owned by the nobles of the confederation.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199517-83] The vassal strata have traditionally paid an annual *tiwse*, or tribute to the nobles as a part of their status obligations, and hosted any noble who was traveling through their territory.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199517–18-84]

In the late Medieval era, states Prasse, the previously existing weapon monopoly of the nobility broke down after regional wars took a heavy toll on the noble warrior strata, and thereafter the vassals carried weapons as well and were recruited as warriors.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199517–18-84] After the start of the French colonial rule, which deprived the nobles of their powers over war and taxation, the Tuaregs belonging to the noble strata disdained tending cattle and tilling the land, seeking instead soldiering or intellectual work.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199517–18-84]

A semi-noble stratum of the Tuareg people has been the endogamous religious clerics, the *[marabouts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marabout)* (Tuareg: *Ineslemen*, a loan word that means Muslim in Arabic).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199517–18-84] After the adoption of Islam, they became integral to the Tuareg social structure.[^cite_note-85] According to Norris (1976), this stratum of Muslim clerics has been a sacerdotal caste, which propagated Islam in North Africa and the Sahel between the 7th and 17th centuries.[^cite_note-Norris1976-23] Adherence to the faith was initially centered around this caste, but later spread to the wider Tuareg community.[^cite_note-86] The marabouts have traditionally been the judges (*qadi*) and religious leaders (*imam*) of a Tuareg community.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199517–18-84]



Tuareg men near [Tahoua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tahoua), [Niger](21373.md)

#### Castes



A Tuareg from a southern clan in typical clothing in [Timbuktu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbuktu), [Mali](19127.md)

According to anthropologist Jeffrey Heath, Tuareg artisans belong to separate [endogamous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endogamy) castes known as the *Inhăḍăn* (*[Inadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inadan_\(African_caste\))*).[^cite_note-Heath2005p7-79][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199518-87] These have included blacksmith, jeweler, wood worker and [leather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuareg_leatherwork) artisan castes.[^cite_note-Heath2005p7-79] They produced and repaired the saddles, tools, household items and other items for the Tuareg community. In Niger and Mali, where the largest Tuareg populations are found, the artisan castes were attached as clients to a family of nobles or vassals, carried messages over distances for their patron family, and traditionally sacrificed animals during Islamic [festivals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Festival).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199518-87]

These social strata, like caste systems found in many parts of West Africa, included singers, musicians and story tellers of the Tuareg, who kept their [oral traditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition).[^cite_note-88] They are called *Agguta* by Tuareg, have been called upon to sing during ceremonies such as weddings or funerals.[^cite_note-89] The origins of the artisanal castes are unclear. One theory posits a [Jewish](25955086.md) derivation, a proposal that Prasse calls "a much vexed question".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199518-87] Their association with fire, iron and precious metals and their reputation for being cunning tradesmen has led others to treat them with a mix of admiration and distrust.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199518-87]

According to Rasmussen, the Tuareg castes are not only hierarchical, as each caste differs in mutual perception, food and eating behaviors. For example, she relates an explanation by a smith on why there is endogamy among Tuareg castes in Niger. The smith explained, "nobles are like rice, smiths are like millet, slaves are like corn".[^cite_note-90]

The people who farm oases in some Tuareg-dominated areas form a distinct group known as *izeggaghan* (or *hartani* in Arabic). Their origins are unclear but they often speak both Tuareg dialects and Arabic, though a few communities are Songhay speakers.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199519–20-91] Traditionally, these local peasants were subservient to the warrior nobles who owned the oasis and the land. The peasants tilled these fields, whose output they gave to the nobles after keeping a fifth part of the produce.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199519–20-91] Their Tuareg patrons were usually responsible for supplying agricultural tools, seed and clothing. The peasants' origins are also unclear. One theory postulates that they are descendants of ancient people who lived in the Sahara before they were dominated by invading groups. In contemporary times, these peasant strata have blended in with freed slaves and farm arable lands together.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199519–20-91]

#### Slaves





*Bellah* constitute the historic slave stratum within Tuareg society.

[^cite_note-Hall2011p5-92]

The Tuareg confederations acquired slaves, often of [Nilotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilotic_peoples) origin,[^cite_note-93] as well as tribute-paying states in raids on surrounding communities.[^cite_note-Gates2010p499-24] They also took captives as war booty or purchased slaves in markets.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199518,_50–54-94] The slaves or servile communities are locally called *[Ikelan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikelan)* (or *Iklan*, *Eklan*), and slavery is inherited, with the descendants of the slaves known as *irewelen*.[^cite_note-Gates2010p499-24][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199518-87]

They often live in communities separate from other castes. The Ikelan's Nilotic extraction is denoted via the [Ahaggar Berber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamahaq_language) word *Ibenheren* (sing. *Ébenher*).[^cite_note-Nicolaisen-95]

The word *ikelan* is the plural form of "slave",[^cite_note-96] an allusion to most of the slaves.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199518,_50–54-94] In post-colonial literature, the alternate terms for *Ikelan* include "Bellah-iklan" or just "Bellah", derived from a [Songhay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songhay_languages) word.[^cite_note-Hall2011p5-92][^cite_note-97]

According to historian Priscilla Starratt (1981), the Tuareg evolved a system of slavery that was highly differentiated. They established strata among their slaves, which determined rules as to the slave's expected behavior, marriageability, inheritance rights if any, and occupation.[^cite_note-98] The *Ikelan* later became a bonded caste within Tuareg society, and they now speak the same Tamasheq language as Tuareg nobles and share many customs.[^cite_note-Nicolaisen-95] According to Heath, the *Bella* in Tuareg society were the slave caste whose occupation was rearing and herding livestock such as sheep and goats.[^cite_note-Heath2005p7-79]

French colonial governments stopped the acquisition of new slaves and slave trading in markets, but they did not remove or free domestic slaves from the Tuareg owners who had acquired them before French rule.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199519-99][^cite_note-100] In Tuareg society, like many others in West Africa, slave status was inherited, and the upper strata used slave children for domestic work, at camps and as dowry gifts to newlyweds.[^cite_note-101][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199549–54-102][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKarl_G._Prasse199550–51-103]



Tuareg from Agadez, Niger

According to Bernus (1972), Brusberg (1985) and Mortimore (1972), French colonial interests in the Tuareg region were primarily economic, and they had no intention of ending the slave-owning institution.[^cite_note-104] Historian [Martin A. Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_A._Klein) (1998) says that although French colonial rule indeed did not end domestic slavery in Tuareg society, the French reportedly attempted to impress upon the nobles the equality of the Imrad and Bella and to encourage the slaves to claim their rights.[^cite_note-105]

He suggests that there was a large scale attempt by [French West African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_West_Africa) authorities to liberate slaves and other bonded castes in Tuareg areas following the 1914–1916 Firouan revolt.[^cite_note-106] Despite this, French officials following the [Second World War](32927.md) reported that there were some 50,000 *Bella* under direct control of Tuareg masters in the Gao–Timbuktu area of [French Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Sudan) alone.[^cite_note-107] This was at least four decades after French declarations of mass freedom in other areas of the colony.

In 1946, a series of mass desertions of Tuareg slaves and bonded communities began in [Nioro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nioro_du_Sahel) and later in [Ménaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A9naka), quickly spreading along the Niger River valley.[^cite_note-108] In the first decade of the 20th century, French administrators in southern Tuareg areas of the French Sudan estimated that "free" to "servile" groups within Tuareg society existed at ratios of 1 to 8 or 9.[^cite_note-Klein1998ApI-109] At the same time, the servile *rimaibe* population of the [Masina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masina,_Kinshasa) [Fulbe](102383.md), roughly equivalent to the *Bella*, constituted between 70% and 80% of the Fulbe population, while servile Songhay groups around [Gao](1255721.md) made up some 2/3 to 3/4 of the total Songhay population.[^cite_note-Klein1998ApI-109] Klein concludes that approximately 50% of the population of French Sudan at the beginning of the 20th century was in some servile or slave relationship.[^cite_note-Klein1998ApI-109]

While post-independence states have sought to outlaw slavery, results have been mixed. Certain Tuareg communities still uphold the institution.[^cite_note-110] Traditional caste relationships have continued in many places, including slaveholding.[^cite_note-111][^cite_note-112] In Niger, where the practice of slavery was outlawed in 2003, according to ABC News, almost 8% of the population are still enslaved.[^cite_note-113] *[The Washington Post](102226.md)* reported that many slaves held by the Tuareg in Mali were liberated during 2013–14 when French troops intervened on behalf of the Malian government against Islamic radicals.[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115]

#### Chronology



Tuareg [blacksmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blacksmith)

Tuareg social stratification into noble, clerical and artisanal castes likely emerged after the 10th century, as a corollary of the rising slavery system.[^cite_note-DeCorse2001p17-116] Similar caste institutions are found in other communities in Africa.[^cite_note-117] According to anthropologist Tal Tamari, linguistic evidence suggests that the Tuareg blacksmith and bard [endogamous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endogamous) castes evolved under foreign contact with [Sudanic peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudanic_people?action=edit\&redlink=1), since the Tuareg terms for "blacksmith" and "bard" are of non-Berber origin.[^cite_note-118] The designation for the endogamous blacksmiths among the southern Tuareg is *gargassa*, a [cognate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognate) of the Songhay *garaasa* and [Fulani](102383.md) *garkasaa6e*, whereas it is *enaden* among the northern Tuareg, meaning "the other".[^cite_note-Tamari_1991p13c-119]

Archaeological work by Rod McIntosh and Susan Keech McIntosh indicates that long-distance trade and specialized economies existed in the Western Sudan at an early date. During the 9th and 10th centuries, Berbers and Arabs built upon these pre-existing [trade routes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_routes) and quickly developed trans-Saharan and sub-Saharan transport networks. Successive local Muslim kingdoms developed increasing sophistication in their martial capacity, slave raiding, holding and trading systems. Among these Islamic states were the [Ghana Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghana_Empire) (11th century), the [Mali Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mali_Empire) (13th and 14th centuries), and the [Songhay Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songhay_Empire) (16th century).[^cite_note-DeCorse2001p17-116] Slavery created a template for servile relationships, which developed into more complex castes and [social stratification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_stratification).[^cite_note-DeCorse2001p19-120]

## Culture

Further information: [Matrilineality § Tuareg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrilineality#Tuareg)



Tuareg [nomads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomad) in southern Algeria

Tuareg culture is largely [matrilineal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrilineality).[^cite_note-Haven07-121][^cite_note-122][^cite_note-Review66-123] Other distinctive aspects of Tuareg culture include clothing, food, education, language, religion, arts, astronomy, nomadic architecture, traditional weapons, music, films, games, and economic activities.

### Clothing

In Tuareg society women do not traditionally wear the [face veil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_veil), whereas men do.[^cite_note-Haven07-121][^cite_note-Review66-123] The most famous Tuareg symbol is the *[tagelmust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagelmust)* (also called éghéwed and, in Arabic, *[litham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litham)*), sometimes referred to as a cheche (pronounced "shesh"), a combined turban and veil, often indigo-blue colored. The men's facial covering originates from the belief that such action wards off evil spirits. It may have related instrumentally from the need for protection from the harsh desert sands as well.

It is a firmly established tradition, as is the wearing of [amulets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amulet) containing sacred objects and, recently, verses from the *[Qur'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qur'an)*. Taking on the veil is associated with the rite of passage to manhood. Men begin wearing a veil when they reach maturity. The veil usually conceals their face, excluding their eyes and the top of the nose.



A Tuareg woman from [Mali](19127.md) in traditional garb

Names for traditional clothing include:

- *[tagelmust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagelmust)*: turban – men

- *bukar*: black cotton turban – men

- *[tasuwart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasuwart?action=edit\&redlink=1)*: women's veil

- *[takatkat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takatkat?action=edit\&redlink=1)*: shirt – women and men

- *[takarbast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takarbast?action=edit\&redlink=1)*: short shirt – women and men

- *[akarbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akarbey?action=edit\&redlink=1)*: pants worn by men

- *[afetek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afetek?action=edit\&redlink=1)*: loose shirt worn by women

- *afer*: women's [pagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pagne)

- *tari*: large black pagne for winter season

- *[bernuz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnous)*: long woolen cloth for winter

- *[akhebay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akhebay?action=edit\&redlink=1)*: loose bright green or blue cloth for women

- *[ighateman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ighateman?action=edit\&redlink=1)*: shoes

- *[iragazan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iragazan?action=edit\&redlink=1)*: red leather sandals

- *[ibuzagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibuzagan?action=edit\&redlink=1)*: leather shoes

The traditional indigo turban is still preferred for celebrations, and generally Tuareg wear clothing and turbans in a variety of colors.

### Food

[Tagella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taguella) is a flatbread made from wheat flour and cooked on a [charcoal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charcoal) fire; the flat disk-shaped bread is buried under the hot sand. The bread is broken into small pieces and eaten with a meat sauce. [Millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet) [porridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porridge) called *a cink* or *a liwa* is a staple much like [ugali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ugali) and [fufu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fufu). Millet is boiled with water to make a pap and eaten with milk or a heavy sauce. Common dairy foods are goat and [camel milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camel_milk), called *akh*, as well as a cheese, *ta komart*, and *tona*, a thick [yogurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogurt), made from them. [Eghajira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eghajira) is a beverage drunk with a [ladle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ladle_\(spoon\)), made by pounding millet, [goat cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goat_cheese), [dates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Date_\(fruit\)), milk, and sugar, served at festivals.

The local popular tea, called *atay* or *ashay*, is made from [gunpowder green tea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunpowder_Tea) with much sugar added. After steeping, it is poured three times in and out of the teapot over the tea, mint leaves and sugar and served by pouring from a height of over a foot into small tea glasses with a froth on top.

### Education



1991: Nomadic children doing homework. Oued Samene, southeast Algeria

When raising their nomadic children, the Tuareg placed great importance on ensuring that they were able to establish many connections with the outside world and get to know as large a social network as possible. In 1998, seven-year-old Tuareg children were tested on this knowledge and it was astonishing how they were able to list the ancestors and relatives of a Targi unknown to them, regardless of his origin or geographical distance.[^cite_note-124] Other forms of knowledge in the socio-cultural sphere were also encouraged, e.g., the mastery of other languages, even including language stays with allies or customers. (This applied to boys, as men were responsible for relations with the outside world.) The promotion of memory was also evident when a Targi remembered a single encounter ten years ago on a desert track that lasted only a few minutes, in a specific place and context that was described in detail.

The pursuit of knowledge in many forms, but with the awareness that it should promote mobility, communication, and adaptability, has been noted by many observers. For example, a young Targia from a religious background said that she had received a dual education, in a medersa, a Muslim school in Arabic, and in a French school, in the 1990s. Her father justified this by saying that knowledge was different from religion. For him, knowledge could be found in every culture and had to be sought everywhere.[^cite_note-125][^cite_note-126]

### Language

Main article: [Tuareg languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuareg_languages)

The Tuareg natively speak the [Tuareg languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuareg_languages). A dialect cluster, it belongs to the [Berber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_languages) branch of the [Afroasiatic family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afroasiatic_family).[^cite_note-127] Tuareg is known as *[Tamasheq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamasheq_language)* by western Tuareg in Mali, as *[Tamahaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamahaq_language)* among Algerian and Libyan Tuareg, and as *[Tamajeq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Tamajeq_language)* in the Azawagh and Aïr regions of Niger.

French missionary [Charles de Foucauld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_de_Foucauld) compiled perhaps the earliest dictionary of the Tuareg language.[^cite_note-128] The Tuaregs compose a great deal of poetry, often elegiac, epigrammatic, and amatory. Charles de Foucauld, and other ethnographers have preserved thousands of these poems, many of which Foucauld translated into French.

### Arts



The [Cross of Agadez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agadez_Cross) in 21 modern variations, [Niger](21373.md), 2019

As in other rural Berber traditions, jewelry made of silver, coloured glass or iron is a special artform of the Tuareg people.[^cite_note-\:9-129][^cite_note-130] While in [other Berber cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewellery_of_the_Berber_cultures) in the Maghreb jewelry is mainly worn by women, Tuareg men also wear necklaces, amulets and rings.

Various pieces indicate a family's economic standing and prestige through both quality and quantity. Often passed through generations (typically from mother to daughter) and given as wedding gifts from the groom. A piece worn by both men and women called Grigri is usually either a leather pouch or amulet. And because this is a Muslim culture, the Grigri is rooted in religious tradition and are only made by holy men. There are gender specific pieces of jewelry as well; only men are allowed to wear arm bands (Eweki) and certain pieces of Tcherow. Tcherow were silver ornaments made by the Enaden that served the same protective purpose as the Grigri made by Islamic holy men.[^cite_note-131]

These traditional handicrafts are made by the *inadan wan-tizol* (makers of weapons and jewelry). Among their products are [tanaghilt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanaghilt?action=edit\&redlink=1) or [zakkat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zakkat?action=edit\&redlink=1) (the '[Agadez Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agadez_Cross)' or 'Croix d'Agadez'); the Tuareg sword ([takoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takoba)), gold and silver necklaces called 'takaza' as well as earrings called 'tizabaten'. Pilgrimage boxes with intricate iron and brass decorations are used to carry items. [Tahatint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tahatint?action=edit\&redlink=1) are made of goat skin.[^cite_note-132][^cite_note-133] Other such artifacts include metalwork for saddle decoration, called *trik*.

Most forms of the Agadez Cross are worn as [pendants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendant) with varied shapes that either resemble a cross or have the shape of a plate or shield. Historically, the oldest known specimens were made of stone or copper, but subsequently Tuareg blacksmiths also used iron and silver, in the [lost-wax casting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost-wax_casting) technique. According to the article "*The cross of Agadez*" by Seligman and Loughran (2006), this piece has become a national and African symbol for Tuareg culture and political rights.[^cite_note-134] Today, these pieces of jewellery are often made for tourists or as items of [ethnic-style fashion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fashion#Anthropological_perspective) for customers in other countries, with certain modern changes.[^cite_note-135]

Not only was lost wax casting used to create these pieces, the Enaden also pounded the metals into their desired shapes and would imprint different designs or even verses from the Quran. They were used as a method of spiritual and physical protection.[^cite_note-136] For example, certain materials impacted what they were used for. Silver was understood to bring happiness, various rare agates are thought to have healing properties, and even the shape of a triangle is a protective form from evil. Shells are also included in aiding in fertility when worn in Khomessa amulets. This goes the other way too, as gold is thought to bring misfortune when worn, which is why the primary material is the silver that comes from Austrian coins, 5 franc pieces, and various silver scraps.[^cite_note-137]

### Nomadic architecture

While living quarters are progressively changing to adapt to a more sedentary lifestyle, Tuareg groups are well known for their nomadic architecture ([tents](235363.md)). There are several documented styles, some covered with animal skin, some with mats. The style tends to vary by location or subgroup.[^cite_note-139] The tent is traditionally constructed for the first time during the marriage ceremony and is considered an extension of the union, to the extent that the phrase "making a tent" is a metaphor for becoming married.[^cite_note-academia.edu-140]

Because the tent is considered to be under the ownership of a married woman, sedentary dwellings generally belong to men, reflecting a patriarchal shift in power dynamics. Current documentation suggests a negotiation of common practice in which a woman's tent is set up in the courtyard of her husband's house.[^cite_note-141] It has been suggested that the traditional tent construction and arrangement of living space within it represent a microcosm of the greater world as an aide in the organization of lived experiences[^cite_note-academia.edu-140] so much so that movement away from the tent can cause changes in character for both men and women as its stabilizing force becomes faint.[^cite_note-142]

An old legend says the Tuareg once lived in grottoes, *akazam*, and they lived in foliage beds on the top [acacia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acacia) trees, *tasagesaget*. Other kinds of traditional housing include: *ahaket* (Tuareg goatskin red tent), *tafala* (a shade made of millet sticks), *akarban* also called *takabart* (temporary hut for winter), *ategham* (summer hut), *taghazamt* ([adobe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe) house for long stay), and *ahaket* (a dome-shaped house made of mats for the dry season and square shaped roof with holes to prevent hot air).

### Music

Further information: [Berber music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_music)

Traditional Tuareg music has two major components: the monochord violin *[anzad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anzad)* played often during night parties and a small *[tambour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanbur)* covered with goatskin called *[tende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tende_\(drum\))*, performed during camel and horse races, and other festivities. Traditional songs called *[Asak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asak_\(Tuareg\)?action=edit\&redlink=1)* and *[Tisiway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tisiway?action=edit\&redlink=1)* (poems) are sung by women and men during feasts and social occasions. Another popular Tuareg musical genre is *[Takamba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takamba)*, characterized by its Afro percussions.

**Vocal music**

- *tisiway*: poems

- *tasikisikit*: songs performed by women, accompanied by tende (drum); the men, on camel-back, circle the women as they sing.

- *asak*: songs accompanied by anzad monocord violin.

- *tahengemmit*: slow songs sung by elder men

In the 1980s rebel fighters founded [Tinariwen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tinariwen), a Tuareg band that fuses electric guitars and indigenous musical styles. Especially in areas that were cut off during the [Tuareg rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuareg_rebellion_\(1990%E2%80%931995\)) (e.g., Adrar des Iforas), they were practically the only music available, which helped them to regional success. They released their first CD in 2000, and toured in Europe and the United States in 2004. ### Economy



&#x20;Tuareg selling crafts to tourists in the [Hoggar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahaggar_Mountains) (Algeria)

Tuareg are distinguished in their native language as the *Imouhar*, meaning the free people;[^cite_note-158] the overlap of meaning has increased local cultural nationalism. Many Tuareg today are either settled agriculturalists or nomadic cattle breeders, while others are [blacksmiths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blacksmith) or [caravan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camel_train) leaders. The Tuareg are a [pastoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoral) people, having an economy based on livestock breeding, trading, and agriculture.[^cite_note-smithsonian1-159]

#### Caravan trade

Since prehistoric times, Tuareg peoples have been organising caravans for trading across the Sahara desert. The caravan in Niger from around Agadez to Fachi and Bilma is called *Tarakaft* or *Taghlamt* in Tamashek, and that in Mali from Timbuktu to Taoudenni, *Azalay*. These caravans used first oxen, horses and later camels as a means of transportation.

Salt mines or *salines* in the desert.

- Tin Garaban near Ghat in [Azjar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azjar), Libya

- Amadghor in Ahaggar, Algeria

- [Taoudenni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoudenni) in far northern Mali

- Tagidda N Tesemt in Azawagh, Niger

- [Fachi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fachi) in Ténéré desert, Niger

- [Bilma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilma) in Kawar, eastern Niger

A contemporary variant is occurring in northern Niger, in a traditionally Tuareg territory that comprises most of the uranium-rich land of the country. The central government in [Niamey](57061.md) has shown itself unwilling to cede control of the highly profitable mining to indigenous clans.[^cite_note-160] The Tuareg are determined not to relinquish the prospect of substantial economic benefit. The French government has independently tried to defend a French firm, Areva, established in Niger for fifty years and now mining the massive uranium deposit.[^cite_note-161]

Additional complaints against Areva are that it is: "...plundering...the natural resources and \[draining] the fossil deposits. It is undoubtedly an ecological catastrophe".[^cite_note-162] These mines yield uranium ores, which are then processed to produce *[yellowcake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellowcake)*, crucial to the nuclear power industry, as well as aspirational nuclear powers. In 2007, some Tuareg people in Niger allied themselves with the [Niger Movement for Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger_Movement_for_Justice) (MNJ), a rebel group operating in the north of the country.

In 2004–2007, U.S. Special Forces teams trained Tuareg units of the [Nigerien Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigerien_Army) in the Sahel region as part of the Trans-Sahara Counter-Terrorism Partnership. Some of these trainees are reported to have fought in [the 2007 rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Tuareg_Rebellion) within the MNJ. The goal of these Tuareg appears to be economic and political control of ancestral lands, rather than operating from religious and political ideologies.

Despite the Sahara's erratic and unpredictable rainfall patterns, the Tuareg have managed to survive in the hostile desert environment for centuries. Over recent years however, depletion of water by the uranium exploitation process combined with the effects of climate change are threatening their ability to subsist. Uranium mining has diminished and degraded Tuareg grazing lands. The mining industry produces radioactive waste that can contaminate crucial sources of ground water resulting in cancer, stillbirths, and genetic defects, and uses up huge quantities of water in a region where water is already scarce.

This is exacerbated by the increased rate of desertification thought to be the result of global warming. Lack of water forces the Tuareg to compete with southern farming communities for scarce resources and this has led to tensions and clashes between these communities. The precise levels of environmental and social impact of the mining industry have proved difficult to monitor due to governmental obstruction.

[E1b1b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_E-M215) is the most common paternal haplogroup among the Tuareg. Most belong to its [E1b1b1b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_E-Z827) (E-M81) subclade, which is colloquially referred to as the *Berber marker* due to its prevalence among [Mozabite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozabite_people), [Middle Atlas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Atlas), [Kabyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabyle_people) and other Berber groups. It reaches frequencies of up to 100 percent in some parts of the [Maghreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maghreb), and is dominated by its sub-clade E-M183. ### mtDNA

According to mtDNA analysis by Ottoni et al. (2010) in a study of 47 individuals, the Tuareg inhabiting the [Fezzan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fezzan) region in Libya predominantly carry the [H1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_H_\(mtDNA\)) haplogroup (61%). This is the highest global frequency found so far of the maternal clade. The haplogroup peaks among Berber populations. The remaining Libyan Tuareg mainly belong to two other West Eurasian mtDNA lineages, [M1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_M_\(mtDNA\)) and [V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_V_\(mtDNA\)).[^cite_note-168] M1 is today most common among other Afro-Asiatic speakers inhabiting East Africa, and is believed to have arrived on the continent along with the [U6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_U) haplogroup from the [Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_East) around 40,000 years ago.[^cite_note-Pereira2010-169] In 2009, based on 129 individuals, Libyan Tuareg were shown to have a maternal genetic pool with a "European" component similar to other Berbers, as well as a south Saharan contribution linked to Eastern Africa and Near Eastern populations.[^cite_note-170]

Pereira et al. (2010) in a study of 90 unrelated individuals observed greater matrilineal heterogeneity among the Tuareg inhabiting more southerly areas in the Sahel. The Tuareg in the Gossi environs in Mali largely bear the H1 haplogroup (52%), with the M1 lineage (19%), and various Sub-Saharan [L2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_L2) subclades (19%) next most common. Similarly, most of the Tuareg inhabiting Gorom-Gorom in Burkina Faso carry the H1 haplogroup (24%), followed by various L2 subclades (24%), the V lineage (21%), and haplogroup M1 (18%).[^cite_note-Pereira2010-169]

The Tuareg in the vicinity of Tanout in Maradi Region and westward to villages of Loube and Djibale in Tahoua Region in Niger are different from the other Tuareg populations in that a majority carry Sub-Saharan mtDNA lineages. In fact, the name for these mixed Tuareg-Haussa people is "Djibalawaa" named after the village of Djibale in Bouza Department, Tahoua Region of Niger. This points to significant assimilation of local West African females into this community. The most common maternal haplogroups found among the Tanout Tuareg are various L2 subclades (39%), followed by [L3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_L3) (26%), various [L1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_L1) sublineages (13%), V (10%), H1 (3%), M1 (3%), [U3a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_U) (3%), and [L0a1a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_L0) (3%).[^cite_note-Pereira2010-169]

### Autosomal DNA

Based on classical genetic markers, according to Cavalli-Sforza LL, Menozzi P, Piazza A. (1994), the Tuareg have genetic affinities with the [Beja people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beja_people), a minority ethnic group inhabiting parts of [Sudan](27421.md), [Egypt](8087628.md), and [Eritrea](17238590.md). The inferred ethnogenesis of the Tuareg people happened within a time period of 9,000 to 3,000 years ago, and most likely took place somewhere in [Northern Africa](21714.md).[^cite_note-171][^cite_note-172]

A 2017 study by Arauna et al. which analyzed existing genetic data obtained from Northern African populations, such as Berbers, described them as a mosaic of local Northern African (Taforalt), Middle Eastern, European (Early European Farmers), and Sub-Saharan African-related ancestries.[^cite_note-173]
