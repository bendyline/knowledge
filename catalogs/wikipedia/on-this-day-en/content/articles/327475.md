The Fifteenth Anniversary Egg, 1911

A **Fabergé egg** ([Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): яйцо Фаберже, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Russian): *yaytso Faberzhe*) is a jewelled egg first created by the [jewellery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewellery) firm [House of Fabergé](5874071.md), in [Saint Petersburg](24320051.md), Russia. As many as 69 eggs were created during the [Tsarist era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsarist_autocracy), of which 61 are known to have survived.[^cite_note-1] The most famous of the house creations are the 50 Imperial Easter eggs manufactured under the supervision of [Peter Carl Fabergé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Carl_Faberg%C3%A9), between 1885 and 1917,[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4][^cite_note-\:0-5] of which 44 are known to be in complete or partial physical existence, leaving the fate of those remaining unknown. The eggs are very highly sought-after collectors' items.[^cite_note-6]

The eggs were commissioned for the Russian tsar [Alexander III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_III_of_Russia) (10 eggs) and tsar [Nicholas II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_II) (40 eggs) as Easter gifts for Alexander's wife and Nicholas's mother, Empress [Maria Feodorovna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Feodorovna_\(Dagmar_of_Denmark\)), and Nicholas's wife [Tsaritsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsaritsa) [Alexandra Feodorovna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandra_Feodorovna_\(Alix_of_Hesse\)).[^cite_note-\:1-7] Fabergé eggs are worth large sums of money and have become symbols of opulence.[^cite_note-8]

Two more Fabergé Easter Imperial eggs (bringing the total to 52) were designed but were not delivered. One egg, known as the Karelian Birch Egg, has sketches but is not confirmed to have actually been made, and the other, the Blue Tsesarevich Constellation Egg, was only partially completed, due to the [Russian Revolution of 1917](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution).[^cite_note-\:0-5] In 2011, [Theo Fabergé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theo_Faberg%C3%A9), grandson of Peter Carl Fabergé, created a series of eggs as part of the [St. Petersburg Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Petersburg_Collection?action=edit\&redlink=1).[^cite_note-\:1-7]

Contemporary Fabergé eggs are made by [Fabergé UK Limited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faberg%C3%A9_UK_Limited), the reestablished jewellery house, which maintains a Fabergé Heritage Council involving Fabergé family members.

## History



The first Imperial Fabergé Egg – the Hen Egg, 1885

The [House of Fabergé](5874071.md) was founded by the goldsmith [Gustav Fabergé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Faberg%C3%A9) in 1842 in [Saint Petersburg](24320051.md), Russia, and the eggs were commissioned by the Russian [tsar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsar) after [Peter Carl Fabergé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Carl_Faberg%C3%A9) took the helm, in 1882.

For Easter in 1883, before his coronation, [Alexander III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_III_of_Russia) and [Maria Feodorovna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Feodorovna_\(Dagmar_of_Denmark\)) were given eggs, one of which contained a silver [dagger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagger) and two [skulls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skull).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFaber200814-9] The egg came with messages, including "[Christ is risen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paschal_greeting)" and "You may crush us—but we [Nihilists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_nihilist_movement) shall rise again!"[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFaber200814-9]



The Napoleonic egg, 1912

Prior to Easter 1885, Alexander III's brother [Grand Duke Vladimir Alexandrovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Duke_Vladimir_Alexandrovich_of_Russia) suggested that Peter Carl Fabergé create a jewelled egg.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFaber20088–9-10] This type of egg is believed to have been inspired by an [ivory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory) hen egg made for the Danish Royal Collection in the 18th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFaber200815-11] Known as the [Hen Egg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Hen_Egg), it had a 65 mm (2.5 in) outer [enamel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitreous_enamel) shell and a golden band around the middle.[^cite_note-\:2-12] The egg opens to reveal a golden "[yolk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yolk)" within, which in turn opens to expose a golden hen sitting on golden [straw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straw). Inside the hen lies a miniature diamond replica of the imperial crown and a ruby [pendant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendant),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFaber20083-13] though these two elements have been lost.[^cite_note-\:2-12] The egg cost 4,151 [rubles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruble).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFaber20088-14] Six weeks later, the emperor made Fabergé the supplier to the imperial court, following which, the egg designs became more elaborate.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFaber200816-15] Once Fabergé had approved an initial design, the work was carried out by a team of craftsmen, among them [Michael Perkhin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Perkhin), [Henrik Wigström](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrik_Wigstr%C3%B6m), and [Erik August Kollin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erik_August_Kollin).[^cite_note-16]

After Alexander III's death on 1 November 1894, his son, [Nicholas II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_II), presented a Fabergé egg to both his wife, Alexandra Feodorovna, and his mother, the [Dowager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dowager) Empress Maria Feodorovna. Records have shown that of the 50 Imperial Easter eggs, 20 were given to the former and 30 to the latter. Eggs were made each year except 1904 and 1905, during the [Russo-Japanese War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Japanese_War).[^cite_note-17]

The imperial eggs enjoyed great fame. Fabergé was commissioned to make similar eggs for a few private clients, including the [Duchess of Marlborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consuelo_Vanderbilt), the [Rothschild family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rothschild_family), and the [Yusupovs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Yusupov). He was also commissioned to make twelve eggs for the industrialist [Alexander Kelch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Kelch), though only seven appear to have been completed.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19] Following the [revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution) and the [nationalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalisation) of the Fabergé workshop in Saint Petersburg by the [Bolsheviks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolsheviks) in 1918, the Fabergé family left Russia. Their trademark was sold several times, and different companies retailed merchandise using the Fabergé name. From 1998 to 2009, the [Victor Mayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Mayer) jewellery company produced Fabergé eggs under [Unilever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unilever)'s license.

## Art of surprise



Kelch Hen Fabergé egg with surprises

Every Fabergé egg embodied a story and a surprise. The Hen Egg (1885) opened to reveal a golden egg and hen. The Rosebud Egg (1895) was the first egg made for [Alexandra Fedorovna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandra_Feodorovna_\(Alix_of_Hesse\)), wife of Tsar Nicholas II, and featured a yellow rose, believed to be the most noble colour, a miniature jewelled crown, and a [ruby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruby) pendant (both missing).[^cite_note-\:6-20]

The Danish Palace Egg (1890) contained a folding ten-panel screen depicting the Danish Imperial yacht *Polar Star*, [Amaliensborg Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amaliensborg_Palace?action=edit\&redlink=1), the estate of [Hvidøre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hvid%C3%B8re), [Fredensborg Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fredensborg_Palace), Bernsdorff Castle, [Kronberg Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kronberg_Castle), two views of Cottage Palace in Copenhagen, and Catching Palace, St Petersburg.[^cite_note-\:6-20]



Danish Palaces Egg surprise foldout frame, 1890

Techniques such as [guilloché](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guilloch%C3%A9) enamelling were introduced by Fabergé, and quality was overseen and approved by Peter Carl himself, or in his absence, by his eldest son Eugène, before it was placed into stock. The smallest of faults would result in rejection.[^cite_note-\:2-12]

## Fabergé eggs since 2007

The Fabergé trademarks were consolidated in 2007 and are owned by [Fabergé UK Limited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faberg%C3%A9_UK_Limited), which makes luxury fine jewellery.[^cite_note-Corder2011-21] Among their 21st-century collaborations was a series of pieces celebrating the [007](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/007) archive, which included the "007 Octopussy" capsule collection, inspired by the 1983 James Bond film, *[Octopussy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octopussy)*.[^cite_note-22]

In December 2023, Fabergé debuted "Journey in Jewels" on the *Seven Seas Grandeur*, a luxury cruise ship from [Regent Seven Seas Cruises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regent_Seven_Seas_Cruises).[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24]

## Location of eggs

Of the 52 known Fabergé eggs,[^cite_note-46] 46 have survived to the present day. Ten of the imperial Easter eggs are displayed at Moscow's [Kremlin Armory Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kremlin_Armory_Museum).[^cite_note-Fabergé_Eggs_–_the_fate_of_the_eggs-47] Of the 50 delivered[^cite_note-48] imperial eggs, 44 have survived, and there are photographs of three of the six lost eggs: the 1903 [Royal Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Danish_\(Faberg%C3%A9_egg\)) Egg, the 1909 [Alexander III Commemorative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_III_Commemorative_\(Faberg%C3%A9_egg\)) Egg, and the [Nécessaire Egg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N%C3%A9cessaire_Egg) of 1889.[^cite_note-academia1-36] The previously lost [Third Imperial Easter Egg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Imperial_Egg) of 1887 has since been found in the US and bought by [Wartski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wartski) for a private collector.[^cite_note-49] All six of the missing Imperial Eggs belonged to [Maria Feodorovna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Feodorovna_\(Dagmar_of_Denmark\)).[^cite_note-50]

After the [Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution_\(1917\)), the [Bolsheviks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolsheviks) nationalised the [House of Fabergé](5874071.md), and the Fabergé family fled to Switzerland, where [Peter Carl Fabergé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Carl_Faberg%C3%A9) died in 1920.[^cite_note-Fabergé_Eggs_–_the_fate_of_the_eggs-47] The [imperial family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Romanov)'s palaces were ransacked and their treasures moved to the [Kremlin Armoury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kremlin_Armoury) on order of [Vladimir Lenin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Lenin).[^cite_note-Fabergé_Eggs_–_the_fate_of_the_eggs-47]

In a bid to acquire more foreign currency, [Joseph Stalin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin) had many of the eggs sold in 1927, after their value had been appraised by [Agathon Carl Theodor Fabergé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agathon_Carl_Theodor_Faberg%C3%A9). Between 1930 and 1933, 14 imperial eggs left Russia. Many were sold to [Armand Hammer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armand_Hammer) (president of [Occidental Petroleum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occidental_Petroleum) and a personal friend of Lenin, whose father was founder of the [United States Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_USA)) and to [Emanuel Snowman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emanuel_Snowman) of the London antique dealers Wartski.

After the collection in the Kremlin Armoury, the largest gathering of Fabergé eggs was assembled by [Malcolm Forbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_Forbes) and displayed in New York City. Totalling nine eggs and approximately 180 other Fabergé objects, the collection was to be put up for auction at [Sotheby's](541267.md) in February 2004 by Forbes' heirs. However, before the auction began, the collection was purchased in its entirety by the [oligarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_oligarch#Post-Soviet_Russian_oligarchs) [Viktor Vekselberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Vekselberg).[^cite_note-51] In a 2013 [BBC Four](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Four) documentary, Vekselberg revealed he had spent just over US\$100 million purchasing the nine Fabergé eggs.[^cite_note-BBC-52] He claims never to have displayed them in his home, saying he bought them as they are important to Russian history and culture, and he believed them to be the best jewellery art in the world. In the same BBC documentary, Vekselberg revealed his plan to open a museum that would display the eggs in his collection,[^cite_note-BBC-52] which was built as a private [Fabergé Museum in Saint Petersburg, Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faberg%C3%A9_Museum_in_Saint_Petersburg,_Russia) on 19 November 2013.[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54]

In November 2007, a Fabergé clock, named by [Christie's](155688.md) auction house as the [Rothschild Egg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rothschild_\(Faberg%C3%A9_egg\)), sold at auction for £8.9 million (US\$16.5 million) (including commission). The price achieved by the egg set three auction records: it is the most expensive [timepiece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timepiece), Russian object, and Fabergé object ever sold at auction, surpassing the US\$9.6 million sale of the 1913 [Winter Egg](8912158.md) in 2002.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-55][^cite_note-Bloomberg-56] On 2 December 2025, the Winter Egg was sold at Christie's for £23 million.[^cite_note-57]

In 1989, as part of the San Diego Arts Festival, 26 Fabergé eggs were loaned for display at [the San Diego Museum of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_San_Diego_Museum_of_Art), the largest exhibition of Fabergé eggs anywhere since the Russian Revolution.[^cite_note-58] The eggs included eight from the Kremlin,[^cite_note-59] nine from the Forbes collection,[^cite_note-60] three from the [New Orleans Museum of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Orleans_Museum_of_Art),[^cite_note-61] two from the Royal Collection,[^cite_note-62] one from the [Cleveland Museum of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleveland_Museum_of_Art),[^cite_note-63] and three from private collections.[^cite_note-64]
