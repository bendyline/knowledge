The **Boeing KC-135 Stratotanker** is an American military [aerial refueling](237949.md) tanker aircraft that was developed from the [Boeing 367-80](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_367-80) prototype, alongside the [Boeing 707](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_707) airliner. It has a narrower fuselage and is shorter than the 707. Boeing gave the aircraft the internal designation of Model 717,[^cite_note-3] with the number later assigned to a [different Boeing aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_717).&#x20;

The KC-135 was the [United States Air Force](32090.md)'s (USAF) first jet-powered refueling tanker; it replaced the [KC-97 Stratofreighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_KC-97_Stratofreighter). The KC-135 was initially tasked with refueling strategic bombers and was used extensively in the [Vietnam War](32611.md) and later conflicts such as [Operation Desert Storm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Desert_Storm) and the [Iran war](82533582.md)[^cite_note-4] to extend the range and endurance of US tactical fighters and bombers.

The KC-135 entered service with the USAF in 1957. It is one of nine military fixed-wing aircraft (six American, three Russian) with over 60 years of continuous service[^cite_note-5] with its original operator. The KC-135 was supplemented by the larger [McDonnell Douglas KC-10 Extender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonnell_Douglas_KC-10_Extender). Studies have concluded that many of the aircraft could be flown until 2030, although maintenance costs have greatly increased. The KC-135 is to be partially replaced by the [Boeing KC-46 Pegasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_KC-46_Pegasus).

## Development

### Background

In 1950, the USAF operated the world's first production aerial tanker, the Boeing [KC-97 Stratofreighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KC-97_Stratofreighter), a gasoline-fueled, piston-engined [Boeing Stratocruiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_377) (USAF designation [C-97 Stratofreighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C-97_Stratofreighter)) with a Boeing-developed flying boom and extra kerosene (jet fuel) tanks feeding the boom. The Stratocruiser airliner was developed from the [Boeing B-29 Superfortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_B-29_Superfortress) bomber after [World War II](32927.md). In the KC-97, the mixed gasoline/kerosene fuel system was not desirable, and a jet-powered tanker aircraft obviously would be the next development, having a single type of fuel for both its own engines and for passing to receiver aircraft. The 230 mph (370 km/h) cruise speed of the slower, piston-engined KC-97 was also a serious issue, as using it as an aerial tanker forced the newer, jet-powered military aircraft to slow down to mate with the tanker's boom.

Like its sibling, the commercial [Boeing 707](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_707) [jet airliner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_airliner), the KC-135 was derived from the [Boeing 367-80](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_367-80) jet transport "proof of concept" demonstrator, which was commonly called the "Dash-80". The KC-135 is similar in appearance to the 707, but has a narrower [fuselage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuselage) and is shorter than the 707. The KC-135 predates the 707 and is structurally quite different from the civilian airliner. Boeing gave the future KC-135 tanker the initial designation Model 717.[^cite_note-6]



A KC-135A refueling a [B-52D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B-52D) during the [Cold War](325329.md): Both aircraft types were operated by the [Strategic Air Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_Air_Command).

In 1954, USAF's [Strategic Air Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_Air_Command) (SAC) held a competition for a jet-powered aerial-refueling tanker. In 1955, [Lockheed Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Corporation)'s tanker version of the proposed [Lockheed L-193](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_L-193) airliner with rear fuselage-mounted engines was declared the winner.[^cite_note-7] Since Boeing's proposal was already flying, the KC-135 could be delivered two years earlier and [Air Force Secretary](305210.md) [Harold E. Talbott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_E._Talbott) ordered 250 KC-135 tankers until Lockheed's design could be manufactured. In the end, orders for the Lockheed tanker were dropped rather than supporting two tanker designs. Lockheed never produced its jet airliner. Boeing eventually dominated the market with a family of airliners based on the 707.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9]

In 1954, the USAF placed an initial order for 29 KC-135As, the first of an eventual 820 of all variants of the basic C-135 family. The first aircraft flew in August 1956. The initial-production Stratotanker was delivered to [Castle Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle_Air_Force_Base), [California](5407.md), in June 1957. The last KC-135 was delivered to the USAF in 1965.



In November 1957, [Air Force Vice Chief of Staff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vice_Chief_of_Staff_of_the_United_States_Air_Force) General [Curtis LeMay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtis_LeMay) tested the first KC-135 on a [long-haul flight](8758086.md) from [Westover Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westover_Air_Reserve_Base), Massachusetts, to [Buenos Aires](255919.md), Argentina.

Developed in the early 1950s, the basic airframe is characterized by 35°, aft-swept wings and [tail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empennage), four underwing-mounted engine pods, a [horizontal stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_stabilizer) mounted on the fuselage near the bottom of the [vertical stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_stabilizer) with positive [dihedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihedral_\(aircraft\)) on the two horizontal planes, and a high-frequency radio antenna, which protrudes forward from the top of the vertical fin or stabilizer. These basic features make it strongly resemble the commercial Boeing 707 and 720 aircraft, although it is a different aircraft.

[Reconnaissance](146911.md) and command post variants of the aircraft, including the [RC-135 Rivet Joint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RC-135_Rivet_Joint) and [EC-135](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_EC-135) [Looking Glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Looking_Glass) aircraft were operated by SAC from 1963 to 1992, when they were reassigned to the [Air Combat Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Combat_Command). The USAF EC-135 Looking Glass was subsequently replaced in its role by the [U.S. Navy](20518076.md) [E-6 Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-6_Mercury) aircraft, a newly built airframe based on the Boeing 707-320B.

### Engine retrofits

All KC-135s were originally equipped with [Pratt & Whitney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney) [J57-P-59W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_J57) [turbojet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbojet) engines, which produced 10,000 lbf (44 kN) of thrust dry, and about 13,000 lbf (58 kN) of [thrust wet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_injection_\(engine\)). Wet thrust is achieved through the use of water injection on takeoff, as opposed to "wet thrust" when used to describe an [afterburning engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afterburner). 670 US gallons (2,500 L) of water are injected into the engines over the course of three minutes. The water is injected into the inlet and the diffuser case in front of the combustion case. The water cools the air in the engine to increase its density; it also reduces the turbine gas temperature, which is a primary limitation on many jet engines. This allows the use of more fuel for proper combustion and creates more thrust for short periods of time, similar in concept to "[war emergency power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_emergency_power)" in a [piston-engined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocating_engine) aircraft.

The fronts of several gray aircraft are centered in the image.

A nose-on view of several re-engined KC-135R aircraft [taxiing prior to takeoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elephant_walk_\(aeronautics\)): The new engines are [CFM56-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CFM_International_CFM56) high-bypass turbofans.

In the 1980s, the first modification program retrofitted 157 [Air Force Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Force_Reserve_Command) (AFR) and [Air National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_National_Guard) (ANG) tankers with the Pratt & Whitney [TF33-PW-102](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_JT3D) [turbofan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbofan) engines, from 707 airliners retired in the late 1970s and early 1980s. The modified tanker, designated the KC-135E, was 14% more fuel-efficient than the KC-135A and could offload 20% more fuel on long-duration flights. Only the KC-135E aircraft were equipped with [thrust reversers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust_reversal) for aborted takeoffs and shorter landing rollouts.&#x20;

The KC-135E fleet has since either been retrofitted as the R-model configuration or placed into long-term storage ("XJ"), as Congress has prevented the USAF from formally retiring them. The final KC-135E, tail number 56-3630, was delivered by the [101st Air Refueling Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/101st_Air_Refueling_Wing) to the [309th Aerospace Maintenance and Regeneration Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/309th_Aerospace_Maintenance_and_Regeneration_Group)  at [Davis–Monthan Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davis%E2%80%93Monthan_Air_Force_Base) in September 2009.[^cite_note-KC-135E-10]



The flight deck of a KC-135R. The instrument panel has been modified under the Pacer-CRAG program.

The second modification program retrofitted 500 aircraft with new [CFM International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CFM_International) [CFM56](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CFM_International_CFM56) (military designation: F108) high-bypass turbofan engines produced by [General Electric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric) and [Safran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safran_Aircraft_Engines).[^cite_note-11] The CFM56 engine produces 22,000 lbf (98 kN) of thrust,[^cite_note-12] nearly double compared to the original J57 engine. The modified tanker, designated KC-135R (modified KC-135A or E) or KC-135T (modified KC-135Q), can offload up to 50% more fuel (on a long-duration sortie), is 25% more fuel-efficient, and costs 25% less to operate than with the previous engines.[^cite_note-13] It is also significantly quieter than the KC-135A, with noise levels at takeoff reduced from 126 to 99 [decibels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decibel).[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15] This 27-decibel noise reduction results in a sound pressure level of about 5% of the original level. The KC-135R's operational range is 60% greater than the KC-135E for comparable fuel offloads, providing a wider range of basing options.

Upgrading the remaining KC-135Es into KC-135Rs is no longer in consideration. This would have cost around US\$3 billion, \$24 million per aircraft. According to USAF data, the KC-135 fleet had a total operation and support cost in fiscal year 2001 of about \$2.2 billion (\~\$3.71 billion in 2024). The older E model aircraft averaged total costs of about \$4.6 million per aircraft, while the R models averaged about \$3.7 million per aircraft. Those costs include personnel, fuel, maintenance, modifications, and spare parts.[^cite_note-16]

### Avionics upgrades



Block 45 [glass cockpit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass_cockpit)

To expand the KC-135's capabilities and improve its reliability, the aircraft has undergone a number of avionics upgrades. Among these was the Pacer-CRAG program (compass, radar, and GPS), which ran from 1999 to 2002 and modified all the aircraft in the inventory to eliminate the navigator position from the flight crew. The fuel-management system was also replaced. The program development was done by [Rockwell Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockwell_Collins) in [Iowa](26810748.md)[^cite_note-17] and installation was performed by [BAE Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BAE_Systems) at the [Mojave Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mojave_Spaceport) in California.[^cite_note-18]

Block 40.6 allows the KC-135 to comply with [global air-traffic management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_air-traffic_management). The latest block upgrade to the KC-135, the Block 45 program, is online with the first 45 upgraded aircraft delivered by January 2017. Block 45 adds a new [glass cockpit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass_cockpit) digital display, radio altimeter, digital autopilot, digital flight director, and computer updates. The original, no longer procurable analog instruments, including all engine gauges, were replaced.[^cite_note-19] Rockwell Collins again supplied the major avionic modules, with modification done at [Tinker AFB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tinker_AFB).[^cite_note-20]

### Further upgrades and derivatives

The KC-135Q variant was modified to carry [JP-7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JP-7) fuel necessary for the [Lockheed SR-71 Blackbird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_SR-71_Blackbird), by separating the JP-7 from the KC-135's own fuel supply. The body tanks carry JP-7, and the wing tanks carry [JP-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JP-4) or [JP-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JP-8). The tanker also had special fuel systems for moving the different fuels between  tanks.[^cite_note-21] When the KC-135Q model received the CFM56 engines, it was redesignated the KC-135T model, which was capable of separating the main body tanks from the wing tanks, where the KC-135 draws its engine fuel.&#x20;

The only external difference between a KC-135R and a KC-135T is the presence of a clear window on the underside of the empennage of the KC-135T, where a remote-controlled searchlight is mounted. It has two ground refueling ports, located in each rear wheel well, so ground crews can fuel both the body tanks and wing tanks separately.



A cutaway of the Flight Refueling Limited Mk.32B refueling pod

Eight KC-135R aircraft are [receiver-capable](237949.md#Multiple_systems) tankers, commonly referred to as KC-135R(RT). All eight aircraft were with the [22d Air Refueling Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/22d_Air_Refueling_Wing) at [McConnell AFB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McConnell_AFB), Kansas, in 1994.[^cite_note-22] They are primarily used for force extension and special operations missions and are crewed by highly qualified, receiver-capable crews. If not used for the receiver mission, these aircraft can be flown just like any other KC-135R.

The Multi-point Refueling Systems  modification adds refueling pods to the KC-135's wings. The pods allow refueling of U.S. Navy, U.S. Marine Corps and most NATO tactical jet aircraft, while keeping the tail-mounted refueling boom. The pods are Flight Refueling Limited MK.32B model pods, and refuel via the probe and drogue method common to Navy/Marine Corps tactical jets, rather than the primary "[flying boom](237949.md)" method used by Air Force fixed-wing aircraft. This allows the tanker to refuel two receivers at the same time, which increases throughput compared to the boom drogue adapter.

A number of KC-135A and KC-135B aircraft have been modified to EC-135, RC-135, and [OC-135](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OC-135) configurations for use in several different roles. These could also be considered variants of the [C-135 Stratolifter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_C-135_Stratolifter) family.

## Design



A USAF KC-135R [boom operator's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boom_operator_\(military\)) view from the boom pod

The KC-135R has four turbofan engines, mounted under 35° swept wings,[^cite_note-23] which power it to takeoffs at gross weights up to 322,500 pounds (146,300 kg). Nearly all internal fuel can be pumped through the tanker's [flying boom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_boom), the KC-135's primary fuel transfer method. A [boom operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boom_operator_\(military\)) stationed in the rear of the aircraft controls the boom while lying prone, viewing through a window at the bottom of the tail. Both the flying boom and operator's station are similar to those of the previous KC-97.&#x20;

A special shuttlecock-shaped drogue, attached to and trailing behind the flying boom, may be used to refuel aircraft fitted with probes. This apparatus is significantly more unforgiving of pilot error in the receiving aircraft than conventional trailing hose arrangements; an aircraft so fitted is incapable of refueling by the normal flying-boom method until the attachment is removed. A [Cargo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cargo) deck above the refueling system can hold a mixed load of passengers and cargo. Depending on fuel storage configuration, the KC-135 can carry up to 83,000 pounds (38,000 kg) of cargo.

## Operational history



An F-15 backs out after refueling from a KC-135R.

The KC-135 was introduced to support SAC bombers, but by the late 1960s, in the [Southeast Asia](32611.md) theater, the KC-135 Stratotanker's ability as a force multiplier came to the fore. Midair refueling of [F-105](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-105) and [F-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-4) fighter-bombers, as well as B-52 bombers, brought far-flung bombing targets within reach and allowed fighter missions to spend hours at the front, rather than a few minutes, which was usual due to their limited fuel reserves and high fuel consumption.&#x20;

KC-135 crews refueled USAF, Navy, and Marine Corps aircraft. They changed to [probe and drogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probe_and_drogue) adapters depending upon the mission, with the Navy and Marine Corps aircraft not having flying-boom receptacles, since the USAF boom system was impractical for aircraft carrier operations. Crews also helped to bring in damaged aircraft, which could sometimes fly while being fed by fuel to a landing site, or to ditch over the water, specifically those with punctured fuel tanks. KC-135s continued their tactical-support role in later conflicts such as [Operation Desert Storm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Desert_Storm) and current aerial strategy.

[Strategic Air Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_Air_Command) had the KC-135 Stratotanker in service with Regular Air Force SAC units from 1957 to 1992 and with SAC-gained ANG and AFR units from 1975 to 1992.[^cite_note-auto-24] Following a major USAF reorganization that resulted in the inactivation of SAC in 1992, most KC-135s were reassigned to the newly created AMC.[^cite_note-auto-24] A small number of KC-135s were also assigned directly to [United States Air Forces in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Air_Forces_in_Europe) (USAFE), [Pacific Air Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Air_Forces) (PACAF) and the [Air Education and Training Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Education_and_Training_Command) (AETC). All AFRC KC-135s and most of the ANG KC-135 fleet became operationally gained by AMC. [Alaska Air National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Air_National_Guard) and [Hawaii Air National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii_Air_National_Guard) KC-135s became operationally gained by PACAF.

AMC managed 396 Stratotankers, of which the AFR and ANG flew 243 in support of AMC's mission as of May 2018.[^cite_note-AF_fact_sheet-1] The KC-135 is one of a few military aircraft types with over 50 years of continuous service with its original operator as of 2009.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26]

Israel was offered KC-135s again in 2013, after turning down the aging aircraft twice due to expense of keeping them flying.[^cite_note-27] The IAF again rejected the offered KC-135Es, but said that it would consider up to a dozen of the newer KC-135Rs.[^cite_note-28]

As of 2025, the oldest active aircraft in the U.S military is KC-135 [57-1419](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/57-1419), in service with the [161st Air Refueling Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/161st_Air_Refueling_Wing), based out of [Goldwater Air National Guard Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldwater_Air_National_Guard_Base), Arizona. The aircraft was built in 1957, and as of 2013 had 22,300 flight hours.[^cite_note-29]

On 28 February 2026, KC-135s provided aerial refueling support during [Operation Epic Fury](82533582.md), a large-scale, joint US-Israeli military operation against Iran. The U.S. deployed over 30 KC-135 and [KC-46](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_KC-46_Pegasus) tankers to Israel in advance of the strikes alongside [F-22](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Martin_F-22_Raptor), [F-35](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-35) fighters, [B-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B-2) bombers and other planes.[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31] [United States Central Command](338482.md) (CENTCOM) described the operation as "the largest regional concentration of American military firepower in a generation".[^cite_note-32] On 12 March 2026, [a KC-135 crashed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Western_Iraq_mid-air_collision?action=edit\&redlink=1) in western Iraq killing all six crew members. The crash is thought to be the result of an incident involving another aerial refuelling aircraft.[^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34]

An Iranian missile and drone attack on [Prince Sultan Air Base](5553272.md) in Saudi Arabia left multiple US aircraft destroyed or damaged, including at least one KC-135.[^cite_note-35]

### Research usage



KC-135 [winglet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winglet) flight tests at [Armstrong Flight Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dryden_Flight_Research_Center)&#x20;

Besides its primary role as an inflight aircraft refueler, the KC-135, designated NKC-135, has assisted in several research projects at the [NASA](18426568.md) [Armstrong Flight Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armstrong_Flight_Research_Center) at [Edwards Air Force Base](107530.md), California. One such project occurred between 1979 and 1980, when special wingtip "[winglets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wingtip_device)", developed by [Richard Whitcomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_T._Whitcomb) of the [Langley Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langley_Research_Center), were tested at Armstrong, using an NKC-135A tanker lent to NASA by the USAF. Winglets are small, nearly vertical fins installed on an aircraft's wing tips. The results of the research showed that [drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_\(physics\)) was reduced and [range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Range_\(aircraft\)) could be increased by as much as 7% at cruise speeds.[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37] Winglets are now being incorporated into most new commercial and military transport/passenger jets, as well as business aviation jets.

NASA has operated several KC-135 aircraft, without the tanker equipment installed, as their famed [Vomit Comet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vomit_Comet) zero-gravity simulator aircraft. From 1973 to 1995, the longest-serving version was KC-135A, AF Ser. No. 59-1481, named *Weightless Wonder IV* and registered as N930NA.[^cite_note-heilig-38]

### Replacements

Main article: [KC-X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KC-X)



KC-135Rs at twilight on the flight line

Between 1993 and 2003, the amount of KC-135 depot maintenance work doubled, and the overhaul cost per aircraft tripled.[^cite_note-39] In 1996, it cost \$8,400 per flight hour for the KC-135. In 2002, this had grown to \$11,000. In 2004, the USAF's 15-year estimates projected significant cost growth to fiscal year 2017. KC-135 fleet operations and support costs were estimated to grow from about \$2.2 billion in fiscal year 2003 to \$5.1 billion (2003 dollars) in fiscal year 2017, an increase over 130%, which represented an annual operating cost growth rate of about 6.2%.[^cite_note-40]

The USAF projected that E and R models have lifetime flying hour limits of 36,000 and 39,000 hours, respectively. Accordingly, only a few KC-135s would reach these limits by 2040, when some aircraft would be about 80 years old. A 2005 USAF study estimated that KC-135Es upgraded to the R standard could remain in use until 2030.[^cite_note-41]

In 2006, the KC-135E fleet was flying an annual average of 350 hours per aircraft. The KC-135R fleet was flying an annual average of 710 hours per aircraft. In March 2009, the Air Force indicated that KC-135s would require additional skin replacement to allow their continued use beyond 2018.[^cite_note-42]



The view from the boom operator's hatch as a [F-35](11812.md) takes on fuel from a KC-135



A [JASDF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Air_Self-Defense_Force) F-35A Lightning II prepares to receive fuel from a USAF KC-135, 2025.

The USAF decided to replace the KC-135 fleet; it is large, so will need to be replaced gradually. The first batch of replacement planes was to be an air tanker version of the [Boeing 767](4165.md), leased from Boeing. In 2003, this was changed to contract where the USAF would purchase 80 KC-767 aircraft and lease 20 more.[^cite_note-43] In December 2003, the Pentagon froze the contract and in January 2006, the [KC-767](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_KC-767) contract was canceled. This move followed public revelations of corruption in how the contract was awarded and controversy regarding the original leasing rather than outright purchase agreement. Then-Secretary of Defense Rumsfeld stated that that move would in no way impair the Air Force's ability to deliver the mission of the KC-767, which would be accomplished by implementing continuing upgrades to the KC-135 and [KC-10 Extender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KC-10_Extender) fleet.

In January 2007, the USAF launched the [KC-X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KC-X) program with a [request for proposal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Request_for_proposal) (RFP). KC-X was the first phase of three acquisition programs meant to replace the KC-135 fleet.[^cite_note-44] In February 2008, the US Defense Department selected the [EADS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EADS)/[Northrop Grumman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_Grumman) ["KC-30"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A330_MRTT) (to be designated the [KC-45A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_Grumman_KC-45)) over the Boeing KC-767.[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46][^cite_note-47] In March 2008, Boeing protested the award, citing irregularities in the competition and bid evaluation.[^cite_note-48] In June 2008, the US [Government Accountability Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Accountability_Office) sustained Boeing's protest of the selection of the Northrop Grumman/EADS's tanker.[^cite_note-GAOdecision-49]

In February 2010, the USAF restarted the KC-X competition with the release of a revised RFP.[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-51] In February 2011, after evaluating bids, the USAF selected Boeing's 767-based tanker design as a replacement, with the military designation [KC-46](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_KC-46_Pegasus).[^cite_note-52] The first KC-46A Pegasus was delivered to the USAF in January 2019.[^cite_note-Boeing10jan2019-53]

Two export users of the KC-135, the [French Air and Space Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Air_and_Space_Force) and the [Republic of Singapore Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Singapore_Air_Force), took deliveries of Airbus A330 MRTTs as replacements for their Stratotankers.[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55]

Active KC-135 [aircraft liveries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_livery)

### KC-135A

Original production version powered by four Pratt & Whitney [J57s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_JT3C), 732 built. Initial production batch of 29 aircraft was given the company designation **Model 717-100A**, while the later batches of 68 and 635 aircraft were respectively designated **717-146** and **717-148**.[^cite_note-\:1-56]

### NKC-135A

Main article: [Boeing NC-135 § Readiness Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_NC-135#Readiness_Program)

Test-configured KC-135A.

### KC-135B

Main article: [Boeing EC-135 § Looking Glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_EC-135#Looking_Glass)

Airborne command post version equipped with turbofan engines, 17 built. Provided with in-flight refueling capability and redesignated EC-135C.[^cite_note-Encyl_aircr-57] Company designation **Model 717-166**.[^cite_note-\:12-58]

### KC-135D

All four [RC-135As](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_RC-135#RC-135A) (*Pacer Swan*) were modified to partial KC-135A configuration in 1979.[^cite_note-DoD_Models-59][^cite_note-autogenerated1-60] The four aircraft (serial numbers *63-8058, 63-8059, 63-8060* and *63-8061*) were given a unique designation KC-135D as they differed from the KC-135A in that they were built with a flight engineer's position on the flight deck.[^cite_note-61] The flight engineer's position was removed when the aircraft were modified to KC-135 standards. They retained their electrically powered wing flap secondary (emergency) drive mechanism and second air conditioning pack which had been used to cool the RC-135As on-board photo-mapping systems.[^cite_note-62] Later re-engined with Pratt & Whitney TF33 engines and a cockpit update to KC-135E standards in 1990 and were retired to the 309th AMARG at [Davis-Monthan AFB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davis-Monthan_AFB), AZ in 2007.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-60][^cite_note-63]

### KC-135E

Air National Guard and Air Force Reserve KC-135As re-engined with Pratt & Whitney TF33-PW-102 engines from retired 707 airliners (161 modified). All E model aircraft were retired to the 309th AMARG at [Davis-Monthan AFB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davis-Monthan_AFB) by September 2009 and replaced with R models.[^cite_note-KC-135E-10][^cite_note-64]

### NKC-135E

Main article: [Boeing NC-135 § Big Crow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_NC-135#Big_Crow)

Test-configured KC-135E aircraft, 55-3132 NKC-135E "Big Crow I" and 63-8050 NKC-135B "Big Crow II" were used as airborne targets for the [Boeing YAL-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_YAL-1) Airborne Laser carrier.[^cite_note-65]

### KC-135Q

KC-135As modified to carry [JP-7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JP-7) fuel necessary for the SR-71 Blackbird, 56 modified,[^cite_note-Encyl_aircr-57] survivors to KC-135T.

### KC-135R (1960s)

4 JC/KC-135As converted to *Rivet Stand* (Later *Rivet Quick*) configuration for reconnaissance and evaluation of above ground nuclear test (55-3121, 59–1465, 59–1514, 58–0126; 58-0126 replaced 59-1465 after it crashed in 1967). These aircraft were powered by Pratt & Whitney J57 engines and were based at [Offutt AFB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offutt_AFB), [Nebraska](21647.md).

### KC-135R



A Boeing KC-135R Stratotanker of the [Turkish Air Force](1799517.md) arrives at the 2016 [Royal International Air Tattoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_International_Air_Tattoo), [England](9316.md)&#x20;

KC-135As and some KC-135Es re-engined with [CFM56](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CFM56) engines, more than 417 converted.[^cite_note-AF_fact_sheet-1]

### KC-135R(RT)

Receiver-capable KC-135R Stratotanker; eight modified with either a Boeing or LTV receiver system and a secure voice SATCOM radio. Three of the aircraft (60-0356, -0357, and -0362) were converted to tankers from [RC-135Ds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_RC-135#RC-135D_Office_Boy_/_Rivet_Brass), from which they retained their added equipment.

### KC-135T

KC-135Q re-engined with CFM56 engines, 54 modified.

### C-135F

A new-built variant for [France](5843419.md) as dual-role tanker/cargo and troop carrier aircraft.[^cite_note-DoD_Models-59] 12 were built for the [French Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Air_Force) with the addition of a drogue adapter on the refueling boom. Given Boeing model numbers 717-164 and 717-165.[^cite_note-DrBob-66]

### C-135FR

11 surviving C-135Fs upgraded with CFM International F108 turbofans between 1985 and 1988. Later modified with MPRS wing pods.[^cite_note-DrBob-66]

### EC-135Y

Main article: [Boeing EC-135 § USCENTCOM Support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_EC-135#USCENTCOM_Support)

An airborne command post modified in 1984 to support [CINCCENT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USCENTCOM). Aircraft 55-3125 was the only EC-135Y. Unlike its sister EC-135N, it was a true tanker that could also receive in-flight refueling. Pratt & Whitney TF33-PW-102. Retired to 309th AMARG at Davis-Monthan AFB, AZ.

## Accidents

For accidents involving other C-135 variants, see [Boeing C-135 Stratolifter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_C-135_Stratolifter), [Boeing RC-135](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_RC-135), [Boeing EC-135](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_EC-135), [Boeing NC-135](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_NC-135), and [Boeing WC-135 Constant Phoenix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_WC-135_Constant_Phoenix).

Since entering into service in 1955, 52 Stratotankers have been lost to accidents, involving 385 fatalities.

27 June 1958

USAF KC-135A, [serial number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_military_aircraft_serials) *56-3599*, [stalled and crashed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westover_Air_Force_Base_KC-135_crash) at [Westover Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westover_Air_Force_Base) after the crew failed to extend the flaps on takeoff, killing all 15 on board. The aircraft was attempting a world speed record between New York and London.

[^cite_note-92]

31 March 1959

USAF KC-135A, *58-0002*, entered a thunderstorm near [Killeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killeen,_Texas), Texas. Two engines separated and one of the engines struck the tail, causing loss of control. The aircraft crashed on a hillside, killing all four crew on board. The aircraft had been delivered just six weeks before the accident.

[^cite_note-93]

15 October 1959

USAF KC-135A, *57-1513*, collided in mid-air with B-52F *57-0036* at 32,000 feet (9,800 m) over [Leitchfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leitchfield,_Kentucky), Kentucky, killing all six on board both aircraft.

[^cite_note-94]

3 February 1960

USAF KC-135A, *56-3628*, crashed on takeoff in extremely gusty [crosswind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crosswind) conditions at Roswell-[Walker AFB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walker_AFB), New Mexico. The airplane skidded into two other KC-135 tankers (57-1449 and 57–1457) and a hangar and burst into flames. The aircraft was on a training flight, but the instructor pilot was occupying the [jump seat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jump_seat) instead of one of the pilot seats as directed by the local commander. The destruction of three aircraft, along with the death of all six in the crew plus an additional two deaths on the ground made this a unique mishap.

[^cite_note-95]

18 November 1960

USAF KC-135A, *56-3605*, crashed on landing at [Loring Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loring_Air_Force_Base) due to an excessive sink rate, killing one of 17 on board.

[^cite_note-96]

USAF KC-135A, *56-3618*, crashed on takeoff from Loring Air Force Base due to engine failure, killing all six on board.

[^cite_note-97]

USAF KC-135A, *55-3144*, crashed on approach to Runway 11 at [Hanscom Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanscom_Field) in Bedford, Massachusetts, killing all three on board. Stock footage of this same aircraft had been used during the opening credits of the film *[Dr. Strangelove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dr._Strangelove)*.

10 September 1962

USAF KC-135A, *60-0352* on a flight from [Ellsworth Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellsworth_Air_Force_Base) to [Fairchild Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairchild_Air_Force_Base) crashed into a mountain just 20 miles (32 km) northeast of [Spokane](138268.md), Washington. The flight hit fog on approach to the air base and hit [Mount Kit Carson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Kit_Carson), a 5,271 ft (1,607 m) mountain. The crash killed all four crew and 40 passengers on board.

## Specifications (KC-135R)

3-view silhouette drawing of the Boeing KC-135A Stratotanker

3-view silhouette drawing of the Boeing KC-135A Stratotanker

*Data from* USAF Fact Sheet,[^cite_note-AF_fact_sheet-1] Boeing.com : KC-135[^cite_note-Boeing.com-2]

**General characteristics**

- **Crew:** 3 (pilot, co-pilot and boom operator; some KC-135 missions require the addition of a navigator.)

- **Capacity:** up to 80 passengers / 83,000 lb (38,000 kg) or 6 [463L pallets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/463L_master_pallet)

- **Length:** 136 ft 3 in (41.53 m)

- **Wingspan:** 130 ft 10 in (39.88 m)

- **Height:** 41 ft 8 in (12.70 m)

- **Wing area:** 2,433 sq ft (226.0 m

  2

  )

- **[Airfoil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airfoil):** **root:** BAC 310/311/312; **tip:** BAC 313

  [^cite_note-Selig-190]

- **Empty weight:** 98,392 lb (44,630 kg) \


- **Operating empty weight:** 124,000 lb (56,245 kg)

- **Gross weight:** 297,000 lb (134,717 kg)

- **Max takeoff weight:** 322,500 lb (146,284 kg)

- **Fuel capacity:** 200,000 lb (90,718 kg) / 29,850 US gal (112,995 L; 24,855 imp gal)

- **Powerplant:** 4 × [CFM International F108-CF-100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CFM_International_F108-CF-100) [turbofan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbofan) engines, 21,600 lbf (96.2 kN) thrust each

**Performance**

- **Maximum speed:** 504 kn (580 mph, 933 km/h)

- **Maximum speed:** Mach 0.9

- **Cruise speed:** 460 kn (530 mph, 850 km/h) at 30,000 ft (9,100 m)

- **Range:** 1,300 nmi (1,500 mi, 2,400 km) with 150,000 lb (68,039 kg) of transferable fuel

- **Ferry range:** 9,572 nmi (11,015 mi, 17,727 km)

- **Service ceiling:** 50,000 ft (15,000 m)

- **Rate of climb:** 4,900 ft/min (25 m/s)
