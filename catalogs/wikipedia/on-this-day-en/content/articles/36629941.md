**Assadism** ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): أسدية, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Arabic): *ʾAsadiyah*) or **Assadist Ba'athism** is a variant of [neo-Ba'athism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Ba'athism) based on the policies and thinking of the [Assad family](27334130.md), which governed [Syria](57134800.md) as a [totalitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totalitarian) [hereditary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynasty) dictatorship from 1971 to 2024.[^cite_note-tots-1][^cite_note-2] Assadism was characterized by [Arab nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_nationalism), [totalitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totalitarianism), [socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_socialism#Syrian_Ba'ath_constitution_and_practice) during the Cold War,[^cite_note-3] and a [cult of personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad's_cult_of_personality) around the Assad family, while [neoliberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoliberalism) was adopted after the end of the Cold War.[^cite_note-4]

This period spanned the successive regimes of [Hafez al-Assad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad) and his son [Bashar al-Assad](364813.md). The Assads rose to power as a result of the [1970 Syrian coup d'état](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrective_Movement_\(Syria\)), leading to the consolidation of [Alawite](436616.md) minority dominance within the [military](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Arab_Armed_Forces) and [security forces](19204147.md). Their governance was largely characterized by [nepotism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corruption_in_Ba'athist_Syria), [sectarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sectarianism#Syria), and [ethnic favoritism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnocentrism).[^cite_note-5]

The ideology enshrines the Assad family's leadership role in Syrian politics and presented the Assad regime in a very [personalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personalist_dictator) fashion, creating a government based upon and revolving around its leader. Under this system, the [Syrian Ba'ath Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Socialist_Ba'ath_Party_%E2%80%93_Syria_Region) portrayed the wisdom of Assad as "beyond the comprehension of the average citizen."[^cite_note-Kheir_2010_423-6] Syrian state propaganda cast Assadism as a neo-Ba'athist current that evolved [Ba'athist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba'athism) ideology with the needs of the modern era.[^cite_note-Dam_2011-7]

The Assad family cultivated extensive patronage networks, securing loyalty while monopolizing vast portions of the [Syrian economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_economy) and fostering widespread corruption.[^cite_note-8] The Syrian Ba'ath party used its control over Syria's political, social, economic, cultural, educational and religious spheres to enforce its neo-Ba'athist ideology in the wider society and preserve the Assad family's grip on power. Hafez al-Assad's goal upon coming to power was to consolidate the [socialist state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_state) with the Ba'ath party as its [vanguard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanguard) by establishing a "coup-proof" system that eliminated factional rivalries. As soon as he seized power, the armed forces, [secret police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_police), security forces, and bureaucracy were purged, subjugating them to party command by installing Alawite elites loyal to Assad.[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10]

To maintain control, the regime had shifted toward the use of brute force and relentless oppression by the early 1980s, though Assadism initially attempted to solve problems within the country through political maneuvering in 1970s, exemplified by the [Hama massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_Hama_massacre) in 1982 and the [several sectarian massacres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_massacres_during_the_Syrian_civil_war) over the course of the [Syrian civil war](30741795.md) since 2011.[^cite_note-11] Following the [fall of the Assad regime](78548066.md) in 2024 as a result of a [Syrian opposition offensive](78460726.md) amid the civil war, Assadists loyal to the former regime have engaged in [an insurgency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Syria_clashes_\(December_2024%E2%80%93present\)) across Alawite strongholds in western Syria.[^cite_note-12]

## History

### Pre-1970

Prior to Hafez al-Assad's seizure of power in 1970, the neo-Ba'athist movement in Syria had been dominated by strongman [Salah Jadid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salah_Jadid), who came to power after a [successful coup in 1966](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1966_Syrian_coup_d'%C3%A9tat).

Salah Jadid pursued a very radical [Marxist–Leninist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxist%E2%80%93Leninist) policy internally,[^cite_note-Seale_1995-13] and aggressive and [interventionist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interventionism_\(politics\)) externally.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16] In the end, his uncompromising radicalism and brutal imposition of his ideas alienated almost all layers of Syrian society, as well as members of the ruling party, who believed that the situation required a more moderate approach: The latter united around Hafez al-Assad, who was opposed to Jadid's adventurism.

Tensions between Jadid and Assad increased following the [Six-Day War](29328.md) in 1967 and [invasion of Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_invasion_of_Jordan) in 1970. The conflict was not limited to heated debates at party meetings and congresses, sometimes escalating into military clashes between supporters of both parties (in particular between pro-Assad elements in the army and the [Palestinian fedayeen](14068131.md) group [as-Sa'iqa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As-Sa'iqa), which supported the Jadid regime[^cite_note-17]), especially in 1969. Hafez al-Assad took advantage of his control over the military to dismantle Jadid's support network, before [carrying out a coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrective_Movement_\(Syria\)) and imprisoning Jadid and then Syrian president [Nureddin al-Atassi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nureddin_al-Atassi).[^cite_note-nytobi22-18]

### After 1970 coup

After Assad seized power, the ideology of neo-Ba'athism transformed into Assadism, with even greater [nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_nationalism), [militarism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Militarism#Syria) and the now established [cult of personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad's_cult_of_personality) of the Assad family. Assadism is very different from the ideas propagated by the original leaders of the [Ba'athist movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba'athism), [Michel Aflaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Aflaq) and [Salah al-Din al-Bitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salah_al-Din_al-Bitar), which caused them great dissatisfaction with such an ideological transformation.[^cite_note-Galvani_1974_3–16-19][^cite_note-20] Assad set upon a project of rapid institution-building, reopened parliament, and adopted a permanent constitution for the country, which had been ruled by military fiat and a provisional constitutional documents since 1963.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFederal_Research_Division2004213-21]

Political participation was limited to the [National Progressive Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Progressive_Front_\(Syria\)), the ruling coalition of Syrian Ba'ath and [Marxist–Leninist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism%E2%80%93Leninism) parties; entrenching itself firmly within the [Soviet Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Bloc). The Party also began building a personality cult around Assad and brought the elite of the [armed forces](27479.md) under Assad's grip and the officer corps were installed with [Alawite](436616.md) loyalists, further alienating the [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam_in_Syria) majority from the party.[^cite_note-22]

The Ba'athists initially pursued a very [militaristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Militarism) policy aimed at some kind of "mobilization of the nation to fight against the Israeli enemy." But under Assad, militarism reached new heights. Following the Syrian loss during the [Six-Day War](29328.md) with [Israel](9282173.md), Hafez initiated a huge expansion of the military to achieve military parity with Israel. Assad gave a high priority to building a strong military and preparing it for a confrontation with Israel, both for offensive and defensive purposes and to enable him to politically negotiate the return of the [Golan Heights](7515865.md) from a position of military strength. He allocated up to 70 percent of the annual budget to the military build-up and received large quantities of modern arms from the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union).[^cite_note-23] The [Syrian Arab Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Arab_Army), which was mainly a conscripted force, increased from 50,000 personnel in 1967 to 225,000 in 1973, and to over 350,000 by the 1990s.

Main article: [Yom Kippur War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yom_Kippur_War)



Hafez Assad declares war on Israel in his TV speech on October 6.

In 1973, Syria, together with [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Egypt_under_Anwar_Sadat), launched an [almost successful war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yom_Kippur_War) against Israel: despite facing stiffer resistance than their Egyptian allies, the [Syrian army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Arab_Armed_Forces) was able to break through the Israeli defenses.[^cite_note-Seale_1995-13] However, due to the lack of coordination and the Egyptian operational pause from October 7 to 14, Syria faced the full fury of the [Israeli forces](39237.md) (who learned of Egypt's modest strategy) and were forced to retreat.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeale1990205-24][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeale1990207-25] Israel again invaded Syrian territory, the [Bashan region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bashan_salient), hoping to reach [Damascus](8914.md). However, Syria was able to stop its advance and a [war of attrition began](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_Attrition_in_the_Bashan_Salient), which lasted until May 1974, when Syria signed a [disengagement agreement](40148472.md). Although Syria did not liberate the [Golan Heights](7515865.md), its army was not defeated, which earned Assad respect inside Syria and abroad.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeale1990211-26]

#### Islamist uprising

Main article: [Islamist uprising in Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamist_uprising_in_Syria)

Bitterness towards the Assadist regime and the [Alawite](436616.md) elite in the neo-Ba'ath and armed forces became widespread amongst the Sunni majority, laying the beginnings of a [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) Islamic resistance. Prominent leaders of [Muslim Brotherhood](20742.md) like [Issam al-Attar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Issam_al-Attar) were imprisoned and exiled. A coalition of the traditional Syrian Sunni *[ulema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulama)*, Muslim Brotherhood revolutionaries and [Islamist](15012.md) activists formed the Syrian Islamic Front in 1980 with objective of overthrowing Assad through [Jihad](16203.md) and establishing an [Islamic state](2646438.md). In the same year, Hafez officially supported Iran in its [war with Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War) and controversially began importing Iranian fighters and terror groups into [Lebanon](17771.md) and Syria. This led to rising social tensions within the country which eventually became a full-fledged [Islamist rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamist_uprising_in_Syria) in 1976–1982; led by the [Fighting Vanguard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fighting_Vanguard) and [local Muslim Brotherhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_Brotherhood_in_Syria) movement. The regime responded by [slaughtering the Sunni inhabitants in Hama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_Hama_massacre) and [Aleppo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Aleppo_\(1980\)) and bombarding numerous mosques, killing around 20,000–40,000 civilians. The uprising was brutally crushed and the Muslim Brotherhood armed movement was demolished.[^cite_note-27] After the uprising the government resumed its version of [militaristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Militarism) [Leninism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leninism), repealing liberalization introduced when Assad came to power.[^cite_note-\:2-28]

## Ideology's tenets

### Militarism

The Assadist regime was characterized by a very large-scale [militarization](2075211.md) of the entire Syrian society (both men and women, as can be seen from the military parades held in Syria) and a highly militaristic propaganda in the [media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_media_in_Ba'athist_Syria) and [education system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_Syria), mixed with the personality cult of Hafez al-Assad (and later Bashar). The degree of Assadist's militarism of was indecently high: Syria's [air force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_force) and [tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tank) fleets were not much smaller (if not larger) than those of large European countries. In 1979, Syria was one of the four largest arms importers in the world (between 1961 and 1979, it imported weapons worth \$7.4 billion, one of the highest figures).[^cite_note-29] Even if a person has not yet served in the army and is a student, he will eventually receive some [military training](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_education_and_training), such as assembling weapons, in schools run by Assadist [youth organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_youth_organizations) (such as the [Revolutionary Youth Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutionary_Youth_Union)), membership in which was mandatory.[^cite_note-\:0-30][^cite_note-31] These organizations would mobilize boys through enforced training and then membership of paramilitary groups.[^cite_note-\:1-32]



Hafez and Mustafa Tlass on the Golan front, October 1973.

Organizations like the RYU also carried out intensive [ideological training](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba'athism) and spread of Assadist ideas in schools, helping to create "ideologically correct" youth. School students also were taught Ba'athism and Assadism through a course known as "Political Arab Sociology".[^cite_note-33] Students sat through a lesson every week about how to become an "active Ba'athist" and how to show their love for both the nation and the leader, particularly through celebrating a physically strong body and military training. A compulsory 15-day summer camp gave to male students extra time to learn about the soldiers life, in an attempt to prepare them for compulsory army conscription when they finished high school. During enforced marches to celebrate the “great leader”, at the time, Bashar al-Assad's father, Hafez, Syrians learned by heart the slogan: “With blood and soul, we sacrifice ourselves for you, Hafez.”[^cite_note-\:1-32] According to the [Constitution of Syria of 1973](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_Constitution_of_Ba'athist_Syria), Chapter 3: Educational and Cultural Principles, Article 21, it is written:

> The educational and cultural system aims at creating a socialist nationalist Arab generation which is scientifically minded and attached to its history and land, proud of its heritage, and filled with the spirit of struggle to achieve its nation's objectives of unity, freedom, and socialism, and to serve humanity and its progress.[^cite_note-34]

### Neo-Ba'athism

Main articles: [Neo-Ba'athism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Ba'athism) and [Salah Jadid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salah_Jadid)

Neo-Ba'athism is a far-left version of Ba'athism that originated in Syria. Gradually, neo-Ba'athist military officers led by General [Salah Jadid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salah_Jadid) ousted less radical Ba'athists and Aflaqites from all important positions in the traditional civilian elites, government, army and intelligence services since 1963 coup, strengthening their power in the ruling party, but they finally consolidated their power after a [military coup in 1966](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1966_Syrian_coup_d'%C3%A9tat), overthrowing a [National Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Command_of_the_Ba'ath_Party), driving [Michel Aflaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Aflaq) and his supporters out of Syria.[^cite_note-35]



Syrians marching with gigantic portrait of the Syrian president in 1980s

Neo-Ba'athism is very different from the original ideas of the old Ba'athists, including the increased role of the military and the purging of the leadership from members of the old guard – Aflaq and [al-Bitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salah_al-Din_al-Bitar).[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-Galvani_1974_3–16-19] The Neo-Ba'athists were heavily influenced by the ideas of [Marxism–Leninism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism%E2%80%93Leninism), which put this ideology on the verge of [communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communism):[^cite_note-37] neo-Ba'athist regime espoused radical leftist doctrines such as [war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_communism) [Leninism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leninism) and [revolutionary socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutionary_socialism),[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-keegan2-39][^cite_note-40] prioritised "internal revolution", abandoned [pan-Arabism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Arabism), sought to strengthen ties with the Soviet Union, and came into conflict with such ideologies as [Arab nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_nationalism), [Nasserism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasserism), and [Iraqi Ba'athism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Socialist_Ba'ath_Party_%E2%80%93_Iraq_Region).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeale1990145-41]

The rise of the neo-Ba'athists to power in Syria caused the deepest split in the Ba'athist movement: the party divided into two factions, [Syrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Socialist_Ba'ath_Party_%E2%80%93_Syria_Region) and [Iraqi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Socialist_Ba'ath_Party_%E2%80%93_Iraq_Region), and the Syrian became independent from the National Command, which it overthrew. The neo-Ba'athists condemned Aflaq and accused him of "stealing" ideology from [Zaki al-Arsuzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaki_al-Arsuzi), sentencing him to death in absentia (along with al-Bitar),[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-revolution-dictatorship-43][^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46] while Iraqi Ba'athists continued to consider Aflaq the creator of that ideology.[^cite_note-revolution-dictatorship-43]

Domestically, the Jadid regime pursued a highly [anti-religious policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Syria): it imposed severe restrictions on religious freedom, persecuted the clergy,[^cite_note-47] labeled religious clerics as [class enemies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enemy_of_the_people),[^cite_note-\:02-48] and government officials and party mass media preached about the dangers of religion and its imminent demise through socialist revolution.[^cite_note-49] The Neo-Ba'athist regime also pursued very active attempts at radical socialist transformation, for example by confiscating the property of businessmen, merchants and landowners.[^cite_note-keegan2-39] Its relations with most of the [Arab world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world) remained largely poor: its interventionist policies and calls for the overthrow of [reactionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactionary) governments (especially [Jordan](7515964.md) and [Saudi Arabia](349303.md)) alienated most of its neighbors. The neo-Ba'athist regime was very active in supporting the [Maoist](46636.md) concept of "[people's war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_war)," which was expressed in its huge support for leftist [Palestinian fedayeen](14068131.md) groups, granting them considerable autonomy and allowing them to carry out attacks on Israel from Syrian territory.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeale1990144-50][^cite_note-51][^cite_note-keegan2-39]

But after his rise to power, Assad eased the government's repressive and radical policies.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeale1990171-52] Although Assad supported many of Jadid's ideas, he rejected the aggressive imposition of its radical ideas in Syria. He abolished persecution of religion and made overtures to the Writers' Union, rehabilitating those who had been forced underground, jailed or sent into exile for representing what radical Ba'athists called the [reactionary classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactionary).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeale1990170-53] He cut prices for basic foodstuffs 15 percent, which won him support from ordinary citizens,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeale1990171-52] and the confiscation of goods under Jadid was reversed.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeale1990171-52] The urban middle class, which had been hurt by the Jadid's policy, had new economic opportunities.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEReich199056-54] His reign was marked by the virtual abandonment of [Pan-Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Arabism) ideology; replacing it with the doctrine of socialist transformation and giving overriding priority in constructing [socialist society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism) within Syria.[^cite_note-55] However, despite the abandonment of pan-Arabism and Nasserism, Assad often represented himself as a successor to [Gamal Abdel Nasser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamal_Abdel_Nasser): he modelled his presidential system on Nasser's and hailed Nasser for his pan-Arab leadership.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEReich199057-56] In addition to Assad's ambition to turn Syria into a [regional power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_power) and for himself to become a pan-Arab leader, Assad calculated that working for Arab unity and stepping up the struggle against Israel were likely to strengthen his legitimacy and leadership among the various sections of the Syrian population.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEReich199056–57-57] The Assadists actively promoted the values of [Arab socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_socialism), but the regime's policy in this regard changed several times: from the implementation of socialism itself to ideas bordering on communism or on the contrary, reforms encouraging [capitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism). In the 1980s, Assad brought back the Leninist ideology that had existed under Jadid,[^cite_note-\:2-28] but with the [collapse of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union) in the 1990s, he began economic and political liberalization again.[^cite_note-58]

The neo-Ba'athist ideology continued to largely determine the regime's policies on socialism and nationalism, albeit with some differences. For example, already in the first three years of Assad’s rule, all attempts at [collectivization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_farming), proposed under Jadid, were stopped, and state farms were mostly disbanded.[^cite_note-59]

### Nationalism



Syrian artist paints a huge portrait of President Assad in 1974

Assadism is a complex mixture of several conflicting ideas: [Syrian nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_nationalism), Arab nationalism and pan-Arabism.

Formally, Hafez al-Assad declared the goals of achieving pan-Arab unity and Arab nationalism, but in reality these ideas faded into the background and remained in the ideology more for show. Syria's relations with a considerable number of [Arab countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world) were bad for a long period of time, which in itself did not allow pan-Arab beliefs to be realized in practice: Ba'athist Syria [invaded Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_invasion_of_Jordan), [Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_occupation_of_Lebanon) and [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War) in different periods of time. In addition, Assadist Syria became an important ally of non-Arab [Iran](14653.md) and sided with it in the [Iran-Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War), supporting it with arms supplies and closing Iraqi oil pipelines (apart from it, only [South Yemen](718749.md) and [Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Libya_under_Muammar_Gaddafi) supported Iran).[^cite_note-primer-60][^cite_note-61][^cite_note-SIPRI-62] Syria retained very aggressive views on the [Arab-Israeli conflict](7960202.md), although many other Arab countries reoriented their attention to Iran. In practice, the ideas of Assadism went towards a mixture of Syrian and [leftist nationalism](16066160.md).

[Jamal al-Atassi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamal_al-Atassi), co-founder of [Zaki al-Arsuzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaki_al-Arsuzi)'s early [Arab Ba'ath Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Ba'ath_Party_\(al-Arsuzi\)) and later Syrian dissident, stated that "Assadism is a false [nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism). It's the domination of a minority, and I'm not talking just of the Alawites, who control the society's nervous system. I include also the army and the *[mukhabarat](19204147.md)*... And despite its [socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist) slogans, the state is run by a class who has made a fortune without contributing—a *nouvelle [bourgeoisie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourgeoisie) parasitaire*."[^cite_note-63] The Assad family aligned itself with [Iran](14653.md) and its [Axis of Resistance](36658251.md) for much of its rule, contributing to an [inter-Ba'athist rivalry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assadist%E2%80%93Saddamist_conflict) with the [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam)-dominated [Saddamist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddamism) [Ba'ath Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Socialist_Ba'ath_Party_%E2%80%93_Iraq_Region) in [Iraq](26215470.md).[^cite_note-Nasr,_revival,1542-64] Describing Assadism as a [quasi-religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-religion) fostered by the Ba'athist state for mobilising the fealty and adulation of [Syrian citizens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_citizenship), Professor of [Middle Eastern Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Eastern_studies) at [Bonn International Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonn_International_Centre_for_Conflict_Studies) Dr. Esther Meininghaus wrote:

> "by drawing on religion, the Assad regime successfully sought to promote a value system ultimately rooted in the Baʿathist vision for Syrian society .... To this, we can indeed add the cult surrounding Presidents Hafez and Bashar al-Asad, whose pictures are displayed not only in public buildings and schools but taxis and shops, or ceremonies such as mass parades and/or the playing of the [national anthem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humat_ad-Diyar) during official celebrations. Also, official rhetoric has become increasingly infused with transcendental and metaphysical elements, in particular with regard to the President's personality cult. For instance, the President is addressed as the '**Eternal Leader**' who will guide his people to becoming the 'true' [Arab nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Nation). The recent slogan of '*Bashar, Allah, Suriyya wa-bas*' (*Bashar, God, and Syria – that's it*) possibly best epitomises how close the regime has come to creating a Syrian public religion in its own right. Whether the outward performance of 'regime rituals' was actually fully internalised or secretly mocked, it had to be practised and obeyed."[^cite_note-65]

### Al-infitah ala al-Sha'ab

Al-infitah ala al-Sha'ab was one of the central slogans and remarkable declared idea of Assadism a gave a significant support for Hafez al-Assad after coming to power.[^cite_note-66] The idea behind the slogan was to open the government to the people, and the initial, relaxing reforms succeeded in achieving greater citizen participation in governance and decision-making.[^cite_note-67] The slogan concerned both politics and economics:[^cite_note-68] the relaxing reforms of the 1970s were part of the policies of [infiraj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infiraj) and al-infitah ala al-Sha'ab, which were a part of [Corrective Movement program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrective_Movement_program_\(Syria\)).[^cite_note-69]

### Ta'addudiyya

Main article: [Ta'addudiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta'addudiyya)

Ta'addudiyya was the declared policy of Hafez al-Assad's government and his [Corrective Movement program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrective_Movement_program_\(Syria\)), emphasizing political and economic [pluralism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluralism_\(philosophy\)). The word "Ta'addudiyya" itself translates from Arabic as "pluralism."[^cite_note-70] The essence of the policy was Syria's shift away from a [one-party system](102459.md) toward a more "pluralistic" [multi-party system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-party_system): The Ba'ath Party banned all other political parties since [coming to power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1963_Syrian_coup_d'%C3%A9tat) in 1963.[^cite_note-\:3-71] This was expressed, for example, in the creation of a coalition of socialist parties known as the [National Progressive Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Progressive_Front_\(Syria\)).[^cite_note-\:3-71] In addition, Ta'addudiyya, along with the [infiraj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infiraj) policy, allowed private companies to invest in the public sector of the [Syrian economy](27476.md). As the government claimed, the policy of Ta'addudiyya provided greater representation for the people and greater investment opportunities for the economy. However, the Ba'ath Party still remained dominant in determining policy and social change, and the Ta'addudiyya reforms were largely directly controlled by it.[^cite_note-72]

### infiraj

Main article: [infiraj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infiraj)

[Al-infiraj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infiraj), sometimes called infitah, was a socialist policy of limited economic liberalization pursued intermittently by Hafez al-Assad. The goal of the infiraj policy was to attract foreign and private investment to Syria.

## Assadist policies

Further information: [Corrective Movement program (Syria)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrective_Movement_program_\(Syria\))

### Economic reforms

Main article: [Economy of Syria](27476.md)

The Assadists have changed the direction of their economic policy several times. By the mid-1960s, government-sponsored [land reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reform) and [nationalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalization) of major industries and foreign investments had confirmed the new [socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism) direction of Syria's economic policy.[^cite_note-CS3-73] As the state assumed greater control over economic decision-making by adopting [centralized planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planned_economy) and strictly regulating commercial transactions, Syria experienced a substantial loss of skilled workers, administrators, and their capital.[^cite_note-CS3-73] Despite the political upheavals, which undermined the confidence of landowners, merchants, and industrialists, the state successfully implemented large-scale development projects to expand industry, agriculture, and infrastructure.[^cite_note-CS3-73]

By the 1970s, 85% of agricultural lands were distributed to landless peasant populations and tenant farmers. Banks, oil companies, power production and 90% of large-scale industries were nationalized. By the end of the 1970s, the economy had shifted from its traditional agrarian base to an economy dominated by the service, industrial, and commercial sectors.[^cite_note-CS3-73] Massive expenditures for development of irrigation, electricity, water, road building projects, irisin plants and expansion of health services and education to rural areas contributed to prosperity. However, Syria remained noticeably dependent on foreign aid to support its rapidly growing military and arms spending. By the mid-1980s, the country's economic climate had shifted from prosperity to austerity, as a result of the rapid fall of world oil prices, lower export revenues, drought affecting agricultural production, and falling worker remittances.[^cite_note-CS3-73] Also, Arab aid levels decreased very much because of economic retrenchment in the oil-producing states and Syrian support for Iran in the [Iran–Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War).

In the 1990s, as the Cold War came to a close and the USSR collapsed, the Assad government instituted a series of economic reforms, although the economy remained highly regulated.[^cite_note-Central_Intelligence_Agency-74] These reforms began the end of Arab socialism in the Syrian state, favouring an opening up of the country to global investment and market reforms.[^cite_note-75][^cite_note-76][^cite_note-77] The Syrian economy experienced strong growth throughout the 1990s, and into the 2000s.[^cite_note-fred.stlouisfed.org-78] Following his assumption of power in 2000, [Bashar al-Assad](364813.md) sought to frame his leadership around modernizing and opening the economy. He emphasized, in particular, "the need to modernize the regulatory environment and the industrial base, activate and encourage the private sector, remove bureaucratic obstacles to investment, increase job opportunities, qualify cadres, improve education and expand information technology."[^cite_note-79] But Syria's economy has suffered a catastrophic collapse as a result of years of [civil war](30741795.md).

### Social reforms

When the Ba'ath Party took power in Syria in 1963, it pledged full equality between [women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_Syria) and men as well as full workforce participation for women.[^cite_note-Tohidi_1998_103-80] Assadists continued those policies and increased women's role in society and the army. Although women are generally not required to serve in the military, for a time in the 1980s they were subject to mandatory military conscription on an equal basis with men.[^cite_note-81] Women actively participated in military marches, demonstrations and in some military classes in schools (which is run by the [RYU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutionary_Youth_Union)).

Although the previous government of Salah Jadid had been extremely [anti-religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antireligion), the Assadists reversed its measures in this direction. Syria under the Assadists was one of the most secular and highly [Westernized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westernization) (in the civil sense) countries in the Arab world. The updated [constitution of 1973](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_Constitution_of_Ba'athist_Syria) confirmed the secular direction of the new regime. All schools are government-run and non-sectarian, although there is mandatory religious instruction, provided in [Islam](6037917.md) and/or [Christianity](5211.md). Political forms of Islam are not tolerated by the government. Syria has separate secular and religious courts. Civil and criminal cases are heard in secular courts, while the Sharia courts handle personal, family, and religious matters in cases between Muslims or between Muslims and non-Muslim.[^cite_note-82] Non-Muslim communities have their own religious courts using their own religious law.[^cite_note-83]

### Kurds policy

Further information: [Kurds in Syria](3821855.md) and [Arab Belt project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Belt_project)

The Assadist government's treatment of the [Kurds](17068.md) has been the subject of repeated outrage from international organizations such as [Amnesty International](18947898.md) and others.[^cite_note-84][^cite_note-85][^cite_note-86][^cite_note-87]



Kurds protest in Damascus against the decision to ban Nowruz celebrations, 1986

The [Kurds in Syria](3821855.md) had been victims of persecution and harassment by the government even before the Ba'ath Party or Assadists came to power, but it was they that toughened its policies in this regard. Although [Kurdish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdish_language) was the second most widely spoken language after [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic), it was officially banned from use,[^cite_note-88] as were a number of other things related to it: for example, Kurds were prohibited from registering children with Kurdish names, opening businesses with Kurdish names, publishing books or publications in Kurdish, and building Kurdish private schools.[^cite_note-89] Decree 768, introduced in 2000, additionally banned the sale of Kurdish-language cassettes and videos in stores.[^cite_note-90]

From 1973 to 1976, the Assad regime carried out [Arab Belt project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Belt_project) - an [Arabization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabization) campaign in the eastern [Syrian province](690220.md) of [al-Hasakah](1429110.md) to the detriment of local [ethnic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_Syria) (including Kurds), forcibly deporting the local population and settling Arab families there.[^cite_note-91][^cite_note-\:03-92] While that proposals had officially been accepted by the Ba'athist government as early as 1965, it was Hafez who ordered the implementation of the Arab Belt programme in 1973.[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94] The project's name was changed by the Assad government to "*Plan to establish model state farms in the Jazira region*"*.*[^cite_note-95] As a result of the campaign, tens of thousands of Kurds were deported from Syria without the possibility of returning and replaced by Arab families, mostly from the neighboring [Raqqa Governorate](5317143.md), and the Kurdish village names of the area were replaced by Arabic names not necessarily related to the traditions and history of the region.[^cite_note-\:12-96][^cite_note-\:03-92]

### Palestine policy

Assad, like all his predecessors, verbally spoke in unequivocal support of the [Palestinian movement](46216.md), against [Zionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zionism) and the state of Israel: the [Palestinian flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Palestine) became second only to the [Syrian flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Syria#United_Arab_Republic_and_Ba'athist_Syria) in Syria. However, in reality, his policy towards Palestine and support for [Palestinian militias](2646043.md) was much more ambiguous: Assadist Syria has been very selective in its support to the [Palestinians](23267.md).

Under Jadid's rule, leftist Palestinian groups were given a lot of support, but Assad already considered this a bad decision: in his opinion, the militants were given too much autonomy in attacks on Israel, which provoked the [Six-Day War](29328.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeale1990144-50]

The multi-factional organization known as the [Palestine Liberation Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine_Liberation_Organization) was the main militant Palestinian group in the early decades, but its leaders had poor relations with Assadist Syria. Hafez al-Assad was known for his hostility towards [Yasser Arafat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasser_Arafat) and [Faisal Husseini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faisal_Husseini), with attempts to divide the Palestinian leadership.[^cite_note-97]



Syrian troops in Beirut during [War of the Camps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Camps).

In 1975, a [civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Civil_War) broke out in neighboring [Lebanon](17771.md) between various factions, including the PLO. Assad attempted to steer Arafat and the PLO away from Lebanon, threatening him with a cutoff of Syrian aid, but in a result the two sides were unable to reach an agreement.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWinslow2012198-98] Already in 1976, Syria began a full-fledged [intervention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_intervention_in_the_Lebanese_Civil_War) in the war, siding with the [Maronites](39667585.md) and against most [Palestinian militants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestinians_in_Lebanon), which caused outrage not only among the Palestinians but also in the Arab world: one of the goals of the intervention was to return the PLO under Syrian influence. The [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) tried to prevent the conflict between its two important allies, but it did not succeed. Assad supported the [Palestine Liberation Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine_Liberation_Army), which was much more under his control, which participated in clashes with the PLO (the PLA however proved unreliable when ordered to fight other Palestinians, and suffered from mass defections).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPipes1992121-99] Syria's operations against PLO militants have effectively put it on the same side as Israel, which had already begun supplying Maronite militias with weapons since the war began.[^cite_note-100][^cite_note-101]

In the [1982 Lebanon War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_Lebanon_War), Syria fought alongside the PLO against [Israeli forces](39237.md), but after the war, this short-lived alliance collapsed again. Syria also actively supported the [Shiite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Shia_Muslims) [Amal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amal_Movement) militia throughout the war, and sided with them in the [War of the Camps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Camps), again fighting against PLO. Later, Assadist Syria prioritized supporting [Hamas](13913.md), a much more radical paramilitary group which are the enemy of PLO. The Assadists were also accused of massacres by their army against [Lebanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_people) (including Palestinians) who opposed the [Syrian occupation of Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_occupation_of_Lebanon).[^cite_note-LGIC_Terror-102]

### Cult of personality

Main articles: [Hafez al-Assad's cult of personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad's_cult_of_personality) and [Assadization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assadization)



Graffiti depicting Hafez al-Assad, 1980s

The [cult of personality of the Assad family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad's_cult_of_personality) in Syria was enormous. [Statues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue), [frescoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresco), [portraits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portrait) and other images of Hafez and Bashar al-Assad could be found everywhere. Hafez al-Assad developed his cult of personality in [Stalinist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalinism) style: ruling Ba'ath party initially manufactured Hafez al-Assad's cult of Arab socialist heroism in consultancy with [Soviet state propagandists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_propaganda), mimicking the pervasive personality cults prevalent across [Soviet Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Bloc) dictatorships like [Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Republic_of_Romania) and [North Korea](21255.md). Beginning as a tool to bind every Syrian citizen with the obligation of undying loyalty (*bay'ah*) to Assad in 1970s, the propaganda was further intensified and personalist depictions reached new heights during the 1980s. Through kindergarten, school books, educational institutions and [Ba'athist media](16602949.md); Assadist propaganda constructed the image of a homogenous [Arab nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_nation) protected by a fatherly leader revelling under the "cult of Saladin". Assad regime venerates Hafez al-Assad's [personalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personalist_dictator) [iconography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iconography) perpetually in the public and private spheres of everyday Syrian life.[^cite_note-gruber-103][^cite_note-104] Ba'athist propaganda portrayed Hafez al-Assad as a strong leader whose wisdom was "beyond the comprehension of the average citizen."[^cite_note-Kheir_2010_423-6] Syrian state propaganda also cast Assadism as a neo-Ba'athist current that evolved Ba'athist ideology with the needs of the modern era.[^cite_note-Dam_2011-7]

In schools, children were taught to sing songs of adulation about Hafez al-Assad. Teachers began each lesson with the song "Our eternal leader, Hafez al-Assad".[^cite_note-\:62-105] Syrian officials were made to refer to him as 'the sanctified one' (al-Muqaddas).[^cite_note-\:62-105] Assad was sometimes portrayed with apparently divine properties, and many places and objects were renamed in honor of the Assads. Fervently loyalist paramilitaries known as the *[Shabiha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shabiha)* (tr. ghosts) deify the Assad dynasty through slogans such as "*There is no God except Bashar!"* (Arabic: لا إله الا بشار) and pursue [psychological warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_warfare) against non-conformist populations.

[Middle East Insight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East_Insight) magazine wrote: "In no other country in recent memory ... not [Mao's China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maoist_China), nor [Tito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tito)'s [Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Yugoslavia), has the intensity of the personality cult reached such extremes. Asad's image, speaking, smiling, listening, benevolent or stern, solemn or reflective, is everywhere. Sometimes there are half a dozen pictures of him in a row. His face envelops telephone poles and trucks, churches and mosques. His is the visage a Syrian sees when he opens his newspaper."[^cite_note-106][^cite_note-107]

After the fall of the Assad regime, a phenomenon called [de-Assadization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De-Assadization) emerged[^cite_note-108] – the mass destruction of any symbols of the Assadist era.

### Human rights

Main article: [Human rights in Ba'athist Syria](78548501.md)

The Assad regime's [human rights](78548501.md) record has been widely criticized by international organizations for its virtual absence.[^cite_note-109][^cite_note-w\.r.p555-110][^cite_note-AmInt2009-111][^cite_note-112] The regime's [totalitarian measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totalitarianism) were intensified after the end of the Islamist uprising in the 1980s and again during the civil war. The Assad regime has consistently been ranked among the worst in the world for human rights.[^cite_note-FW-113][^cite_note-114] The Assadists' long history of arbitrary arrests and detentions extends to neighboring Lebanon, which was occupied for nearly 30 years. Over the decades of Assadist rule, more than 70,000 people have been forcibly disappeared, 40,000 killed and hundreds of thousands deported in Syria,[^cite_note-115] and 17,000 have gone missing in Lebanon during the occupation; the army has also targeted representatives of the [Red Cross organization](15487.md).[^cite_note-LGIC_Terror-102][^cite_note-HRW2-116] And that's not counting the Syrian civil war, when hundreds of thousands [were killed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casualties_of_the_Syrian_civil_war) and [millions fled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refugees_of_the_Syrian_civil_war).

## Opposition



Flag used by the anti-Assadist opposition which became an official symbol of Syria in 2025

There was also an [**opposition**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_opposition_\(2011%E2%80%932024\)) to the Assadist regime, especially in the later years of the [Ba'athist Syria](57134800.md) which included [Islamists](15012.md), [Pro-Iraqi Ba'thists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba'ath_Party_\(Iraqi-dominated_faction\)), [human rights activists](4358082.md), [ethnic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minority_group) and [religious minorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minority_religion) like [Kurds](17068.md) and [Druze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze) and other groups. The regime's crackdown on the Syrian dissidents caused the [Syrian revolution](39750126.md), [civil war](30741795.md), and the ultimate [fall of the Assad regime](78548066.md), ending the 61 years of the Ba'athist regime not only in Syria but worldwide. The opposition to the Assadist regime was also called **anti-Assadism**.
