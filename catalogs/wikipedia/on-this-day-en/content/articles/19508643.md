# Television show

"TV Show" redirects here. For the album by Sergey Lazarev, see [*TV Show* (album)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TV_Show_\(album\)).

For [OTT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Over-the-top_media_service) platform television shows, see [Streaming television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_television).



A live television show set and cameras

A **television show**, **TV program** ([British English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English_language): **programme**), or **TV show**, is content produced for viewing on a [television set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_set) that is transmitted via [over-the-air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_television), [satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_television), and [cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_television), or [distributed digitally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_distribution) on [streaming platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_platform).[^cite_note-Willcox-TV-1][^cite_note-Pedersen-2025-2] Shows are produced in-house on a television stage with multiple cameras or produced by contract with film production companies (many major entertainment companies have both film and television production divisions). Such content generally excludes [breaking news](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breaking_news) or [advertisements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_advertisement) that are aired between shows or between segments of a show.&#x20;

An [episodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episode) show, whether regularly recurring or limited run, is called a **television series.** An individual segment of such a series is called an **episode.** Episodes are usually broadcast in annual sets, which are called **seasons** in North America and **series** in other regions. A one-off television show may be called a [television special](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_special), while a show with a limited number of episodes is a [miniseries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miniseries).[^cite_note-5] A [television film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_film), or **telefilm**, is a [feature film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feature_film) produced for broadcast by a [terrestrial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_television) or cable network.

Television shows by terrestrial and cable networks are most often scheduled for broadcast ahead of time, as [broadcast programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_programming), and appear on [electronic guides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_program_guide) or other [TV listings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TV_listings). The rise of [streaming television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_television), however, has made television schedules less relevant than in earlier decades. Some programming may be [aired live](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_television)—that is, events are broadcast at the time they happen rather than at a later time or date—but the vast majority of programming is produced ahead of time. Originally, viewers had no practical way to record a show for later viewing; this changed with the advent of [home video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_video), first in the form of videotape recorded on [VCRs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VCR) and later in the form of [digital video recorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_video_recorder). Cable television providers began offering "pay-per-view" or [on-demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_on_demand) programming, with viewers paying a one-time fee to watch a program at a time of their own choosing. Streaming television lets viewers watch programming anytime with an [OTT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Over-the-top_media_service) subscription.

## History

Main article: [History of television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_television)

The first television shows were experimental, sporadic broadcasts viewable only within a very short range from the [broadcast tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_tower) starting in the 1930s. Televised events such as the [1936 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_Summer_Olympics) in Germany, the 1937 [coronation of King George VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronation_of_King_George_VI_and_Queen_Elizabeth#Television) in the United Kingdom, and [David Sarnoff's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Sarnoff#Early_history_of_television) famous introduction at the [1939 New York World's Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1939_New_York_World's_Fair) in the United States spurred growth in the medium, but [World War II](32927.md) put a halt to development until after the war. The [1947 World Series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1947_World_Series) inspired many Americans to buy their first television set, and then in 1948, the popular radio show *[Texaco Star Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texaco_Star_Theater)* made the move and became the first weekly televised [variety show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variety_show), earning host [Milton Berle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Berle) the name "Mr. Television", and demonstrating that the medium was a stable, modern form of entertainment that could attract [advertisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_advertising). The first national [live television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_television) broadcast in the US took place on September 4, 1951, when President [Harry Truman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_S._Truman)'s speech at the [Japanese Peace Treaty Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_San_Francisco) in [San Francisco](49728.md) was transmitted over [AT\&T](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AT\&T)'s transcontinental [cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coaxial_cable) and [microwave radio relay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave_radio_relay) system to broadcast stations in local markets.[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8]

The first national [color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_television) broadcast (the 1954 [Tournament of Roses Parade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tournament_of_Roses_Parade)) in the US occurred on January 1, 1954. During the following ten years, most network broadcasts, and nearly all local programming, continued to be in black-and-white. The color transition was announced for the fall of 1965, during which over half of all network prime-time programming would be broadcast in color. The first all-color prime-time season came just one year later. In 1972, the last holdout among daytime network shows converted to color, resulting in the first completely all-color network season.

Further information: [List of genres § Film and television genres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_genres#Film_and_television_genres)

Television shows are more varied than most other forms of media due to the wide variety of formats and genres that can be presented. A show may be [fictional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fictional) (as in [comedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_comedy), [animations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animated_series) and [dramas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramatic_programming)), or [non-fictional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-fictional) (as in [documentary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Documentary_television), [news](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_news), and [reality television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reality_television)). It may be topical (as in the case of a [local](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_programming) [newscast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newscast) and some [made-for-television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Made-for-television) films), or historical (as in the case of many documentaries and fictional series). They could be primarily [instructional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instructional_television), [educational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Educational_television), or entertaining, as is the case in [situation comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Situation_comedy) and [game shows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_show).

A drama program usually features a set of actors playing characters in a historical or contemporary setting. The program follows their lives and adventures. Before the 1980s, shows (except for [soap opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap_opera)-type [serials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_\(radio_and_television\))) typically remained static without [story arcs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Story_arc), and the main characters and premise changed little. If some change happened to the characters' lives during the [episode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episode), it was usually undone by the end. Due to this, the episodes could be broadcast in any order. Since the 1980s, many series feature progressive change in the plot, the characters, or both. For instance, *[Hill Street Blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill_Street_Blues)* and *[St. Elsewhere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Elsewhere)* were two of the first US prime time drama television series to have this kind of dramatic structure,[^cite_note-9] while the later series *[Babylon 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon_5)* further exemplifies such structure in that it had a predetermined story running over its intended five-season run.

In 2012, it was reported that television was growing into a larger component of major media companies' revenues than film.[^cite_note-wrap120606-10] Some also noted the increase in quality of some television programs. In 2012, Academy Award-winning film director [Steven Soderbergh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Soderbergh), commenting on ambiguity and complexity of character and narrative, stated: "I think those qualities are now being seen on television, and that people who want to see stories that have those kinds of qualities are watching television."[^cite_note-thr120629-11]

## Production

### Development

#### United States

When a person or company decides to create new content for television broadcast, they develop the show's elements, consisting of the [concept](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept), the [characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Character_\(arts\)), the [crew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_crew), and the cast. Then they often "pitch" it to the various [networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_network) in an attempt to find one interested enough to order a [prototype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prototype) for the first episode of the series, known as a [*pilot*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_pilot).[^cite_note-12] [Eric Coleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Coleman_\(producer\)), an animation executive at [Disney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney), told an interviewer, "One misconception is that it's very difficult to get in and pitch your show, when the truth is that development executives at networks want very much to hear ideas. They want very much to get the word out on what types of shows they're looking for."[^cite_note-13]

To create the pilot, the structure and team of the whole series must be put together. If audiences respond well to the pilot, the network will pick up the show to air it the next season. Sometimes they save it for mid-season or request rewrites and additional review. Other times, they *pass* entirely, forcing the show's creator to "shop it around" to other networks. Many shows never make it past the pilot stage.[^cite_note-14]

The method of "team writing" is employed on some longer dramatic series (usually running up to a maximum of around 13 episodes). The idea for such a program may be generated "in-house" by one of the networks; it could originate from an independent production company (sometimes a product of both). For instance, the [BBC](19344654.md)'s long-running soap opera *[EastEnders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EastEnders)* is wholly a BBC production, whereas its popular drama [*Life on Mars*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_on_Mars_\(UK_TV_series\)) was developed by [Kudos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kudos_\(production_company\)) in association with the broadcaster.

There are still a significant number of programs (usually [sitcoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sitcom)) that are built by just one or two writers and a small, close-knit production team. These are "pitched" in the traditional way, but since the creators handle all the writing requirements, there is a run of six or seven episodes per series once approval has been given. Many of the most popular British comedies have been made this way, including *[Monty Python's Flying Circus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monty_Python's_Flying_Circus)* (albeit with an exclusive team of six writer-performers), *[Fawlty Towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fawlty_Towers)*, *[Blackadder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackadder)* and [*The Office*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Office_\(British_TV_series\)).

The [production company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production_company) is often separate from the broadcaster. The [executive producer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_producer), often the show's creator, is in charge of running the show. They pick the [crew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_crew) and help cast the actors, approve and sometimes write series plots—some even write or direct major episodes—while various other producers help to ensure that the show runs smoothly. Very occasionally, the executive producer will cast themselves in the show. As with [filmmaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filmmaking) or other [electronic media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_media) production, producing of an individual episode can be divided into three parts: [pre-production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-production), [principal photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_photography), and [post-production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-production).

### Pre-production

Main article: [Pre-production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-production)



Arthur Schlesinger Jr.

Pre-production begins when a script is approved. A [director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_director) is chosen to plan the episode's final look. Pre-production tasks include storyboarding; construction of sets, props, and costumes; casting guest stars; budgeting; acquiring resources like lighting, special effects, stunts, etc. Once the show is planned, it must then be scheduled: scenes are often filmed out of sequence, and guest actors or even regulars may only be available at certain times. Sometimes the principal photography of different episodes must be done at the same time, complicating the schedule (a guest star might shoot scenes from two episodes on the same afternoon). Complex scenes are translated from storyboard to [animatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_animation#Animatic) to further clarify the action. Scripts are adjusted to meet altering requirements.

Some shows have a small stable of directors, but also usually rely on outside directors. Given the time constraints of broadcasting, a single show might have two or three episodes in pre-production, one or two episodes in principal photography, and a few more in various stages of post-production. The task of directing is complex enough that a single director can usually not work on more than one episode or show at a time, hence the need for multiple directors.

### Principal photography

Main article: [Principal photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_photography)

Principal photography is the actual filming of the episode. Director, actors, and crew gather at a [television studio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_studio) or on location for filming or [videoing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_production) a scene. A scene is further divided into shots, which should be planned during pre-production. Depending on scheduling, a scene may be shot in non-sequential order of the story. Conversations may be filmed twice from different [camera angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_angle), often using stand-ins, so one actor might perform all their lines in one set of shots, and then the other side of the conversation is filmed from the opposite perspective. To complete a production on time, a second unit may be filming a different scene on another set or location at the same time, using a different set of actors, an assistant director, and a second unit crew. A [director of photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Director_of_photography) supervises the lighting of each shot to ensure consistency.

Live events are usually covered by Outside Broadcast crews using mobile television studios, known as scanners or OB trucks. Although varying greatly depending on the era and subject covered, these trucks were normally crewed by up to 15 skilled operators and production personnel. In the UK for most of the 20th century, the BBC was the preeminent provider of outside broadcast coverage. BBC crews worked on almost every major event, including Royal weddings and funerals, major political and sporting events, and even drama programs.[^cite_note-15]

### Post-production

Main article: [Post-production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-production)

Once principal photography is complete, producers coordinate tasks to begin the [video editing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_editing). Visual and [digital video effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_video_effect) are added to the film; this is often outsourced to companies specializing in these areas. Often music is performed with the conductor using the film as a time reference (other musical elements may be previously recorded). An [editor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_editing#Film_Editor) cuts the various pieces of film together, adds the [musical score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incidental_music) and effects, determines scene transitions, and assembles the completed show.

## Running time

In the United States, dramas produced for hour-long time slots typically are 37–42 minutes in length (excluding advertisements), while [sitcoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sitcom) produced for 30-minute time slots typically are 18–21 minutes long. There are exceptions: subscription-based cable TV channels, such as [HBO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HBO), Starz, Cinemax, and Showtime, have episodes that are 45–48 minutes long, similar to the UK. Audience opinions of length have varied due to factors such as [content overload](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_overload#Information_Age).[^cite_note-16]

In Britain, dramas typically run from 46 to 48 minutes on commercial channels, and 57–59 minutes on the [BBC](19344654.md). Half-hour programs are around 22 minutes on commercial channels and around 28 minutes on the BBC. The longer duration on the BBC is due to the lack of advertising breaks.

In France, most television shows (whether dramas, game shows or documentaries) have a duration of 52 minutes. This is the same on nearly all French networks (TF1, France 2, France 5, M6, Canal+, etc.).[^cite_note-17]

The episode runtime of shows produced for [streaming television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_television) platforms, such as [Netflix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netflix) and [Hulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulu), is typically 30 minutes to one hour, though some run longer.[^cite_note-Volpe-18][^cite_note-Schneider-19][^cite_note-Porter-20] Internet-based series with episode runtime of less than 25 minutes are considered [web series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_series).[^cite_note-Volpe-18]

## Budgets and revenues

Most television networks throughout the world are 'commercial', dependent on selling advertising time or acquiring [sponsors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sponsor_\(commercial\)). Broadcasting executives' main concern over their programming is [audience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audience) size. In the past, the number of 'free to air' stations was restricted by the availability of [channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_channel) frequencies, but [cable TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_TV) (outside the United States, [satellite television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_television)) technology has allowed an expansion in the number of channels available to viewers (sometimes at premium rates) in a much more competitive environment.

In the United States, the average broadcast network drama costs \$3 million an episode to produce, while cable dramas cost \$2 million on average.[^cite_note-nyt100404-21] The [pilot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_pilot) episode may be more expensive than a regular episode. In 2004, [*Lost*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_\(2004_TV_series\))'s two-hour pilot cost \$10 to \$14 million, in 2008, [*Fringe*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fringe_\(TV_series\))'s two-hour pilot cost \$10 million, and in 2010, *[Boardwalk Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boardwalk_Empire)* was \$18 million for the first episode. In 2011, [*Game of Thrones*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_Is_Coming) was \$5 to \$10 million, [*Pan Am*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_Am_\(TV_series\)) cost an estimated \$10 million, while [*Terra Nova*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_Nova_\(TV_series\))'s two-hour pilot was between \$10 and \$20 million.[^cite_note-thr110926-22][^cite_note-nyt110828-23]

Many scripted [network television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_television) shows in the United States are financed through deficit financing: a studio finances the production cost of a show and a network pays a license fee to the studio for the right to air the show. This license fee does not cover the show's production costs, leading to the deficit. Although the studio does not make its money back in the original airing of the show, it retains ownership of the show. This allows the studio to make its money back and earn a profit through [syndication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_syndication) and sales of [DVDs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DVD) and [Blu-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blu-ray). This system places most of the financial risk on the studios; however, a hit show in the [syndication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_syndication) and home video markets can more than make up for the misses. Although deficit financing places minimal financial risk on the networks, they lose out on the future profits of big hits since they are only licensing the shows.[^cite_note-24]

Costs are recouped mainly by advertising revenues for broadcast networks and some cable channels, while other cable channels depend on subscriptions. In general, advertisers, and consequently networks that depend on advertising, are more interested in the number of viewers within the 18–49 age range than in the total number of viewers.[^cite_note-demos-25][^cite_note-pocketbook-26] Advertisers are willing to pay more to advertise on shows successful with young adults because they watch less television and are harder to reach.[^cite_note-reason-27] According to *Advertising Age*, during the 2007–08 season, *[Grey's Anatomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grey's_Anatomy)* was able to charge \$419,000 per commercial, compared to only \$248,000 for a commercial during *[CSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSI:_Crime_Scene_Investigation)*, despite CSI having almost five million more viewers on average.[^cite_note-demos2-28] Due to its strength with younger viewers, *[Friends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friends_\(TV_series\))* was able to charge almost three times as much for a commercial as *[Murder, She Wrote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder,_She_Wrote)*, even though the two series had similar total viewer numbers at that time.[^cite_note-demos-25] *[Glee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glee_\(TV_series\))* and *[The Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Office_\(American_TV_series\))* drew fewer total viewers than *[NCIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCIS_\(TV_series\))* during the 2009–10 season, but earned an average of \$272,694 and \$213,617 respectively, compared to \$150,708 for NCIS.[^cite_note-estadrates-29]

After production, the show is handed over to the [television network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_network), which sends it out to its [affiliate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_affiliate) [stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_station), which broadcast it in the specified [broadcast programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_programming) time slot. If the [Nielsen ratings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nielsen_ratings) are good, the show is kept alive as long as possible. If not, the show is usually [canceled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancellation_\(television\)). The show's creators are then left to shop around for remaining episodes, and the possibility of future episodes, on other networks. On especially successful series, the producers sometimes call a halt to a series on their own like *[Seinfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seinfeld)*, *[The Cosby Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cosby_Show)*, *[Corner Gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corner_Gas)*, and [*M\*A\*S\*H*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M*A*S*H_\(TV_series\)) and end it with a concluding episode, which sometimes is a big [series finale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Series_finale).

On rare occasions, a series that has not attracted particularly high ratings and has been canceled can be given a reprieve if [home video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_video) viewership has been particularly strong. This has happened in the cases of *[Family Guy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_Guy)* in the US and [*Peep Show*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peep_Show_\(British_TV_series\)) in the UK.

In the United States, if the show is popular or lucrative, and a minimum number of episodes ([usually 100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/100_episodes)) have been made, it can go into [broadcast syndication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_syndication), where rights to broadcast the program are then resold for cash or put into a barter exchange (offered to an outlet for free in exchange for airing additional commercials elsewhere in the station's broadcast day).

## &#x20;Seasons, series and strands

The terminology used to define a set of [episodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episode) produced for a television series varies from country to country.

### North American usage

See also: [Serial (radio and television)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_\(radio_and_television\))

In North American television, a series is a connected set of television program episodes that run under the same title, possibly spanning many seasons. During the 1950s, it was common for television seasons to consist of more than 30 episodes—however, the average length has been declining since.[^cite_note-30]

Until the 1980s, most new programs for the American [broadcast networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_network) debuted in the "fall season", which ran from September through March and nominally contained 24 to 26 episodes. These episodes were rebroadcast during the spring (or summer) season, from April through August. Because of [cable television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_television) and the Nielsen [sweeps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nielsen_ratings), the "fall" season now normally extends to May. Thus, a "full season" on a broadcast network now usually runs from September through May for at least 22 episodes.[^cite_note-NetworkShortOrders-31]

&#x20;A full season is sometimes split into two separate units with a hiatus around the end of the calendar year, such as the first season of [*Jericho*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jericho_\(2006_TV_series\)) on CBS. When this split occurs, the last half of the episodes are sometimes referred to with the letter B as in "The last nine episodes (of *[The Sopranos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sopranos)*) will be part of what is being called either 'Season 6, Part 2' or 'Season 6B'",[^cite_note-32] or "*[Futurama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Futurama)* is splitting its seasons similar to how *[South Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Park)* does, doing half a season at a time, so this is season 6B for them."[^cite_note-33] Since the 1990s, these shorter seasons also have been referred to as "split" or "half" seasons, which is done to increase profits, as seen with shows such as *[The Witcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Witcher_\(TV_series\))*.[^cite_note-34]

Since at least the 2000s, new broadcast television series are often ordered (funded) for just the first 10 to 13 episodes, to gauge audience interest. If a series is popular, the network places a "back nine order" and the season is completed to the regular 20 to 26 episodes. An established series that is already popular, however, will typically receive an immediate full-season order at the outset of the season. A [midseason replacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midseason_replacement) is a less-expensive short-run show of generally 10 to 13 episodes designed to take the place of an original series that failed to garner an audience and has not been picked up. A "series finale" is the last show of the series before the show is no longer produced. (In the UK, it means the end of a season, what is known in the United States as a "*season* finale".) Streaming services time finales to the next quarter to induce consumers to renew at least one more quarter.[^cite_note-35]

A standard television season in the United States runs predominantly during [autumn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autumn).[^cite_note-\:0-36] During the summer months of June through roughly mid-September, network schedules typically feature reruns of their flagship programs, first-run series with lower rating expectations, and other specials. First-run scripted series are typically shorter and of a lower profile than those aired during the main season and can also include [limited series](#Miniseries,_limited_series,_and_event_series) events. [Reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reality_show) and [game shows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_show) have also been fixtures of the schedule.[^cite_note-\:0-36]

In Canada, the commercial networks air most American programming in tandem with the American television season, but their original Canadian shows follow a model closer to British than American television production. Due to the smaller production budgets available in Canada, a Canadian show's season normally runs to a maximum of 13 episodes rather than 20 or more, although an exceptionally popular series such as *[Corner Gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corner_Gas)* or *[Murdoch Mysteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murdoch_Mysteries)* might receive 20-episode orders in later seasons. Canadian shows do not normally receive "back nine" extensions within the same season, however; even a popular series simply ends for the year when the original production order has finished airing, and an expanded order of more than 13 episodes is applied to the next season's renewal order rather than an extension of the current season. Only the public [CBC Television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBC_Television) normally schedules Canadian-produced programming throughout the year; the commercial networks typically now avoid scheduling Canadian productions to air in the fall, as such shows commonly get lost amid the publicity onslaught of the American fall season. Instead, Canadian-produced shows on the commercial networks typically air either in the winter as mid-season replacements for canceled American shows or in the summer (which may also improve their chances of being picked up by an American network for a summer run).[^cite_note-37]

#### Miniseries, limited series, and event series

While network orders for 13- or 22-episode seasons are still pervasive in the television industry, several shows have deviated from this traditional trend. Written to be closed-ended and of shorter length than other shows, they are marketed with a variety of terms.

- [Miniseries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miniseries): A very short, closed-ended series, typically six or more hours in two or more parts (nights), similar to an extended [television movie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_film). Many early miniseries were adaptations of popular novels of the day, such as [*The National Dream*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_National_Dream_\(miniseries\)) (1974), [*Roots*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_\(1977_miniseries\)) (1977), and [*North and South*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_and_South_\(miniseries\)) (1985). In recent years, as described by several television executives interviewed by *[The Hollywood Reporter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hollywood_Reporter)*, the term *miniseries* has grown to have negative [connotations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connotation) within the industry, having become associated with [melodrama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melodrama)-heavy works that were commonly produced under the format, while *limited series* or *event series* receive higher respect.

  [^cite_note-miniseries-38]

* [Limited series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited-run_series): Distinct from miniseries in that the production is seen to have potential to be renewed, but without the requirement of it having as many episodes as a typical order per season. [*Under the Dome*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Under_the_Dome_\(TV_series\)), *[Killer Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killer_Women)*, and [*Luther*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luther_\(TV_series\)) were marketed as limited series. Individual season-length stories of [anthology series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthology_series#Television) such as *[American Horror Story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Horror_Story)*, [*Fargo*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fargo_\(TV_series\)), and *[True Detective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_Detective)* are also described as "limited series". The [Primetime Emmys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primetime_Emmys) have had to make numerous changes to their [miniseries/limited series category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primetime_Emmy_Award_for_Outstanding_Limited_Series) to accommodate [anthology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthology) and other limited series.

  [^cite_note-Variety-Limited-39]

* Event series: Largely considered a marketing term, falling under the general category of [event television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event_television). The term can be applied to almost any new, short-run series, such as *[24: Live Another Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/24:_Live_Another_Day)*. It has also been used to describe [game shows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_show) like *[The Million Second Quiz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Million_Second_Quiz)* which aired for just two weeks.

  [^cite_note-miniseries-38]

In the United Kingdom and other countries, these sets of episodes are referred to as a "series". In Australia, the broadcasting may be different from North American usage. The terms *series* and *season* are both used and are the same. For example, *[Battlestar Galactica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battlestar_Galactica)* has an original series as well as a remake, both are considered a different series, each with their own number of individual seasons.

Australian television does not follow "seasons" in the way that American television does; for example, there is no "[fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autumn) season" or "fall schedule". For many years, popular night-time dramas in Australia would run for much of the year and would only go into recess during the summer period (December to February, as Australia is in the Southern Hemisphere), when ratings are not taken. Therefore, popular dramas would usually run from February through November each year. This schedule was used in the 1970s for popular dramas, including *[Number 96](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_96_\(TV_series\))*. Many drama series, such as *[McLeod's Daughters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McLeod's_Daughters)*, have received between 22 and 32 episodes per season.

Typically, [soap operas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap_opera), which have always run in season format in Australia, such as *[Home and Away](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_and_Away)*, would usually begin a new season in late January, while the season finale would air in late November, as the show is off air for two months, or sometimes longer, depending on the schedule. In recent years, a new season would begin in early February, and the season finale would broadcast in early December. Since *Home and Away*'s inception, it normally receives 230 episodes per season. Some seasons have seen between 205 and 235 episodes commissioned. During the [Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympics), *Home and Away* would often go on hiatus, which was referred to as an "Olympic cliffhanger". Therefore, the number of episodes would decrease. Australian [situation comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Situation_comedy) series' seasons are approximately 13 episodes long and premiere any time between February and November.

British television programmes have tended toward shorter series in recent years. For example, the [first series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Who_season_1_\(1963%E2%80%931964\)) of long-running [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction) show *[Doctor Who](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Who)* in 1963 featured forty-two 25‑minute episodes, and continued with a similar number each year until it was reduced to twenty-five [for 1970](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Who_season_7) to accommodate changes in production and significantly reducing the actors' workload) and continued to 1984. [For 1985](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Who_season_22) fewer but longer episodes were shown, but even after a return to shorter episodes in 1986, lack of support within the BBC meant fewer episodes were commissioned leading to only fourteen 25‑minute episodes up to [those in 1989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Who_season_26) after which it was cancelled. The revival of *Doctor Who* from 2005 has comprised thirteen 45‑minute installments.

There are some series in the UK that have a larger number of episodes, for example [*Waterloo Road*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterloo_Road_\(TV_series\)) started with 8 to 12 episodes, but from series three onward it increased to twenty episodes and series seven will contain 30 episodes. Recently, American non-cable networks have also begun to experiment with shorter series for some programs, particularly reality shows, such as [*Survivor*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survivor_\(American_TV_series\)). They often air two series per year, resulting in roughly the same number of episodes per year as a drama.

This is a reduction from the 1950s, in which many American shows (e.g. *[Gunsmoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunsmoke)*) had between 29 and 39 episodes per season. Actual storytelling time within a commercial television hour has also gradually reduced over the years, from 50 minutes out of every 60 to the current 44 (and even less on some networks), beginning in the early 21st century.

The usage of "season" and "series" differ for DVD and Blu-ray releases in both Australia and the UK. In Australia, many locally produced shows are termed differently on home video releases. For example, a set of the television drama series *[Packed to the Rafters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Packed_to_the_Rafters)* or [*Wentworth*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wentworth_\(TV_series\)) is referred to as "season" ("The Complete First Season", etc.), whereas drama series such as [*Tangle*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangle_\(TV_series\)) are known as a "series" ("Series 1", etc.). British-produced programmes such as *[Mrs. Brown's Boys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mrs._Brown's_Boys)* are referred to as "season" in Australia for the DVD and Blu-ray releases.

In the UK and Ireland, most programmes are referred to as 'series' while 'season' is starting to be used for some US and international releases.

### Egypt

The 1980s and 1990s was the golden age of television miniseries attracting millions of Egyptians. For example, *[The Family of Mr Shalash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Family_of_Mr_Shalash)* miniseries, starring [Salah Zulfikar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salah_Zulfikar) and [Laila Taher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laila_Taher), was the highest rated at the time.[^cite_note-40]
