**Spencer James Cox** (born July 11, 1975) is an American lawyer and politician serving since 2021 as the 18th [governor of Utah](252385.md). A member of the [Republican Party](32070.md), he served from 2013 to 2021 as the eighth [lieutenant governor of Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenant_Governor_of_Utah). In [Fairview, Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairview,_Utah), where Cox lives and was raised, he was elected to the [city council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_council) in 2004 and then as [mayor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayoralty_in_the_United_States) in 2005. In 2008, he was elected as a [Sanpete County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanpete_County,_Utah) [commissioner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_commission).[^cite_note-1]

He was elected to the [Utah House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utah_House_of_Representatives) in 2012. In October 2013, Governor [Gary Herbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Herbert) appointed Cox to replace [Greg Bell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greg_Bell_\(politician\)) as lieutenant governor; he was confirmed unanimously by the [Utah State Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utah_State_Senate). Cox was elected to the lieutenant governorship as Herbert's running mate in 2016 and was [elected governor in 2020](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Utah_gubernatorial_election). He was reelected in [2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Utah_gubernatorial_election).

## Early life and education

Cox was born on July 11, 1975, in [Mount Pleasant, Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Pleasant,_Utah). He grew up in the nearby town of Fairview and graduated from [North Sanpete High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sanpete_High_School) in Mount Pleasant. He enrolled at [Snow College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snow_College) in [Ephraim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephraim,_Utah), taking two years off to serve a [full-time mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission_\(LDS_Church\)) for [the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Church_of_Jesus_Christ_of_Latter-day_Saints) in Mexico. After his mission, he returned to Snow and married his high-school sweetheart, [Abby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abby_Cox), who also graduated from Snow College. After graduating with an [associate's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associate's_degree), he attended [Utah State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utah_State_University) (USU) in [Logan, Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logan,_Utah), graduating in 1998 with a [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts) in [political science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science).[^cite_note-farmboy-2]

Cox was accepted to [Harvard Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_Law_School) but instead chose to attend the [Washington and Lee University School of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_and_Lee_University_School_of_Law). He was a member of the *[Washington and Lee Law Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_and_Lee_Law_Review)*, and graduated in 2001 with a [Juris Doctor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juris_Doctor) degree with honors.[^cite_note-farmboy-2][^cite_note-fox-3]

## Career



Spencer Cox in 2018

### Early legal work

After graduating from law school, Cox was a [law clerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_clerk) to Judge [Ted Stewart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Stewart) of the [U.S. District Court for the District of Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Utah) from 2001 to 2002. He then entered private practice as an associate at the [Salt Lake City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_Lake_City) law firm Fabian & Clendenin (now Fabian VanCott). He returned to rural Utah and became a vice president of [Centracom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centracom).[^cite_note-\:0-4]

### Political career

Cox was elected as a city councilor of Fairview, Utah in 2004,[^cite_note-fox-3] and mayor the next year. In 2008, he was elected as a Sanpete County commissioner.[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6][^cite_note-farmboy-2] Cox was elected to the Utah House of Representatives in 2012 and became the first member to call for the [impeachment](1795376.md) of [John Swallow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Swallow), the [attorney general of Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attorney_General_of_Utah), over violations of [campaign finance](339302.md) laws.[^cite_note-sltrib-7] Cox and Lieutenant Governor Bell served as co-chairs of Governor Herbert's Rural Partnership Board.[^cite_note-deseret-8]

### Lieutenant governor of Utah

In October 2013, Herbert selected Cox to succeed Bell as lieutenant governor following Bell's resignation.[^cite_note-deseret-8] The Utah Senate's Government Operations Confirmation Committee unanimously approved his nomination on October 15.[^cite_note-committee-9] The next day, the full Utah Senate confirmed him unanimously, and he was sworn in.[^cite_note-10] As lieutenant governor, Cox produced a report on Swallow's financial interests, demonstrating that Swallow had failed to properly disclose all of his income and business interests. Swallow resigned before the report's release.[^cite_note-11] In the [2016 Utah gubernatorial election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Utah_gubernatorial_election), Cox was elected to a full term as lieutenant governor as Herbert's running mate.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13]

## Governor of Utah

See also: [2020 Utah gubernatorial election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Utah_gubernatorial_election)

Cox speaks on the [assassination](81040139.md) of conservative activist [Charlie Kirk](53245776.md); September 10, 2025.

On May 14, 2019, after Herbert announced that he would not seek reelection, Cox announced his candidacy for the Republican nomination for [governor of Utah](252385.md) in [2020](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Utah_gubernatorial_election).[^cite_note-14] With 36% of the vote in the primary, he defeated former governor [Jon Huntsman Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Huntsman_Jr.), former Utah GOP chair [Thomas Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Wright_\(Utah_politician\)), and former Utah House speaker [Greg Hughes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greg_Hughes).[^cite_note-15] In the general election, Cox defeated the [Democratic](5043544.md) nominee, [Chris Peterson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Peterson_\(law_professor\)), 63% to 30%.[^cite_note-16] In a break with tradition, Cox's January 4, 2021 inauguration (with precautions against the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md)) was held at the [Tuacahn Center for the Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuacahn) in [Ivins, Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivins,_Utah), a small town in Washington County. The stated purpose of this move was to express Cox's desire to be governor for the entire state as opposed to focusing on the [Wasatch Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasatch_Front) region.[^cite_note-17] Within days of his inauguration, he opened an office on [Southern Utah University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Utah_University)'s [Cedar City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cedar_City,_Utah) campus.[^cite_note-\:1-18]

Cox said early on that increasing the speed of the state's vaccine distribution was his administration's top priority. As of April 2021, Utah had administered more than 85% of the doses it had received, according to [CDC](6811.md) data.[^cite_note-19] In 2020, during the [COVID-19 pandemic in Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Utah), Cox faced criticism for the state's decision to award millions of dollars in no-bid contracts in the early days of the crisis and for the controversial purchase of an anti-malaria drug as a possible treatment for COVID-19. Cox says he had no role in approving the \$800,000 [hydroxychloroquine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroxychloroquine) order, which was later canceled.[^cite_note-\:1-18][^cite_note-20] In July 2022, Cox was elected vice chair of the [National Governors Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Governors_Association), succeeding New Jersey governor [Phil Murphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phil_Murphy), who was voted chair.[^cite_note-21] In March 2023, Cox signed two bills into law, including the [Utah Social Media Regulation Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utah_Social_Media_Regulation_Act), which bans social media platforms, such as [TikTok](56822861.md), [Instagram](31591547.md), and [Snapchat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snapchat), from allowing minors to create accounts without parental consent and blocks children's access during certain hours.[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24]

Cox designated September 10, 2026, "Charlie Kirk Day" in the state to mark the anniversary of Kirk's death.[^cite_note-25]

### Vetoes

Cox has vetoed five bills as of 2022, all of which were Republican-backed (Republicans have supermajorities in both of Utah's state legislative chambers). His first veto was of a bill sponsored by his brother-in-law, Senator [Mike McKell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_McKell), that sought to regulate how [social media](5897742.md) platforms moderate content.[^cite_note-26] Cox also vetoed Senate Bill 187: Local Education Agency Policies Amendments, sponsored by [Ronald Winterton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Winterton); Senate Bill 39: Hemp Regulation Amendments, sponsored by [David Hinkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hinkins); and House Bill 98: Local Government Building Regulation Amendments, sponsored by [Paul Ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Ray).[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29] In March 2022, Cox vetoed House Bill 11: Student Eligibility in Interscholastic Activities, sponsored by [Kera Birkeland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kera_Birkeland), which aimed to prevent [transgender youth athletes](46676322.md) from participating in women's sports. Cox noted that, of Utah's 75,000 student athletes, only four were transgender and only one competed in women's sports.[^cite_note-30] The legislature overrode his veto.[^cite_note-31]

## Political positions

Cox is frequently considered a moderate Republican.[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33] In October 2015, Cox endorsed [Marco Rubio](5502549.md) in the [2016 Republican presidential primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Party_presidential_primaries,_2016).[^cite_note-34] After Rubio withdrew, Cox endorsed [Ted Cruz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Cruz) in March 2016.[^cite_note-35] Cox said of [Donald Trump](4848272.md)'s [2016 campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump_2016_presidential_campaign) that Trump "does not represent neither goodness nor kindness."[^cite_note-36] He said he did not vote for [Hillary Clinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillary_Clinton) or Trump in the [2016 presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_States_presidential_election).[^cite_note-\:2-37][^cite_note-38]

Cox voted for a [write-in candidate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Write-in_candidate) in the [2020 presidential election](48410011.md).[^cite_note-\:2-37] After the [2021 United States Capitol attack](66307490.md), Cox said Trump was responsible for inciting the violence and called on him to resign.[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-\:2-37] Cox endorsed Trump in July 2024 for the [2024 presidential election](59568546.md) after an [attempted assassination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attempted_assassination_of_Donald_Trump_in_Pennsylvania) against Trump.[^cite_note-\:3-40] He wrote that he believed that God saved Trump in assassination attempt and that only Trump could unify and save the country.[^cite_note-\:3-40]

In April 2026, after public pushback against plans for a data center to be built in Utah, Cox said that every state had an obligation to allow for the construction of data centers.[^cite_note-41]

### Abortion

Cox describes himself as ["pro-life"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-abortion_movements).[^cite_note-42] He opposes abortion except in cases of rape, incest, or danger to the life of the mother.[^cite_note-43] Cox supported the [overturning of Roe v. Wade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overturning_of_Roe_v._Wade) in June 2022.[^cite_note-44] In March 2023, Cox signed a bill banning abortion clinics from operating in Utah and requiring abortions to be performed in a hospital.[^cite_note-45] The law was temporarily blocked by district court judge [Andrew H. Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_H._Stone) a day before it was to take effect in May 2023.[^cite_note-46]

### Environment

Cox opposes the use of the [Antiquities Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiquities_Act) to create [national monuments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_monument_\(United_States\)), saying that he believes monument designations hurt the landscapes they are meant to protect. He has expressed opposition to the restored boundaries of [Bears Ears National Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bears_Ears_National_Monument) and [Grand Staircase–Escalante National Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Staircase%E2%80%93Escalante_National_Monument), and to the creation of [Baaj Nwaavjo I'tah Kukveni – Ancestral Footprints of the Grand Canyon National Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baaj_Nwaavjo_I'tah_Kukveni_%E2%80%93_Ancestral_Footprints_of_the_Grand_Canyon_National_Monument).[^cite_note-47]

### Guns

In February 2021, Cox signed a [constitutional carry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_carry) bill to allow individuals to carry a firearm in public without a permit.[^cite_note-48] At a June 2022 press conference, Cox said he was open to discussing with the legislature a [red flag law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_flag_law) to permit a state court to order the temporary seizure of firearms from any person believed to present a danger.[^cite_note-49]

### Health

On March 28, 2025, Cox signed into law a bill introduced by Republican state lawmaker [Stephanie Gricius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephanie_Gricius) banning from Utah's public water the addition of [fluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluoride), a mineral added to public water since 1945 to prevent [cavities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tooth_decay), making Utah the first U.S. state to ban [water fluoridation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_fluoridation).[^cite_note-fluoban-50] Major medical associations and public health groups, such as the [American Academy of Pediatrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Pediatrics), the [American Dental Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Dental_Association), and the [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](6811.md), support water fluoridation.[^cite_note-fluosupp-51]

### LGBT rights

On June 13, 2016, Cox spoke at a vigil in Salt Lake City honoring those who died in the [Orlando nightclub shooting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlando_nightclub_shooting) the day before. He apologized for mistreating schoolmates and his lack of support for the [LGBT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGBT) community, and directed part of his speech at straight people.[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53]

In March 2022, Cox vetoed [HB11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utah_House_Bill_11), which would ban transgender youth from participating in high school sports. The legislature overrode his veto.[^cite_note-54] On June 1, 2022, Cox issued an official proclamation recognizing June as LGBTQ+ Pride Month and encouraged Utahns to be more welcoming and accepting of the LGBTQ community.[^cite_note-55] On January 28, 2023, Cox signed Senate Bill 16, which bans gender-affirming surgery for patients under 18.[^cite_note-56] On March 22, 2023, Cox signed HB228, codifying Utah's existing conversion therapy ban, previously approved through a 2020 administrative rule.[^cite_note-57][^cite_note-58]

In June 2024, Cox said his earlier declarations of Pride Month had created "division and hurt".[^cite_note-\:4-59] On August 5, 2024, he and other state leaders shared unconfirmed reports that Olympic boxer [Imane Khelif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imane_Khelif) was genetically male. At the time of these statements, Khelif was qualified as a woman by the International Olympic Committee but had been disqualified by the Russian-led International Boxing Association. The results of any genetic or other sex-related tests are confidential under medical privacy rules.[^cite_note-60][^cite_note-61]

Cox declared June 2026 "Fidelity Month", an initiative centered on the "principles of God, marriage, family, country and community".[^cite_note-\:4-59]

## Personal life



Cox with his wife Abby in 2024

Cox is the oldest of eight children and grew up on a farm in Fairview.[^cite_note-farmboy-2] He and his wife, [Abby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abby_Cox), have four children and live on their family farm in Fairview.[^cite_note-fox-3] His father, Eddie, served on the [Utah Transportation Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utah_Transportation_Commission) and was also a Sanpete County commissioner.[^cite_note-committee-9]

Cox plays [bass guitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bass_guitar) in a [garage band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garage_band).[^cite_note-sltrib-7][^cite_note-committee-9] His brother-in-law, Travis Osmond, the son of [Merrill Osmond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merrill_Osmond), taught him to play bass.[^cite_note-62] State Senator [Mike McKell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_McKell) is also a brother-in-law.[^cite_note-farmboy-2] Cox's fourth cousin, [Jon Cox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Cox_\(politician\)), succeeded him in the Utah House of Representatives.[^cite_note-63]

Cox is a fan of the band [The Killers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Killers) and in 2018 recorded a cover of their song "[Read My Mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Read_My_Mind_\(The_Killers_song\))" with his band. In 2024, The Killers' lead singer [Brandon Flowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandon_Flowers) performed at a fundraiser for Cox's campaign.[^cite_note-64]
