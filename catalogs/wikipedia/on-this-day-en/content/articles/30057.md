**Tokyo**,[^cite_note-12] officially the **Tokyo Metropolis**,[^cite_note-13] is the [capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_of_Japan) and [most populous city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_in_Japan) of [Japan](15573.md). The population of the [city proper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_proper) was over 14 million as of 2023. The [Greater Tokyo Area](354951.md), which includes Tokyo and parts of six neighboring [prefectures](74517.md), [is the third-most populous metropolitan area in the world](14649921.md), with 33 million residents as of 2025.[^cite_note-14]

Lying at the head of [Tokyo Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Bay), Tokyo is part of the [Kantō region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kant%C5%8D_region), on the central coast of [Honshu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honshu), Japan's largest island. It is Japan's economic center and the seat of the [Japanese government](30858300.md) and the [Emperor of Japan](10110.md). The [Tokyo Metropolitan Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metropolitan_Government) administers Tokyo's central [23 special wards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_wards_of_Tokyo), which formerly made up [Tokyo City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_City); various commuter towns and suburbs in [its western area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Tokyo); and two outlying island chains, the [Tokyo Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Islands). Although most of the world recognizes Tokyo as a city, since 1943 its governing structure has been more akin to that of a prefecture, with an accompanying [Governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor_of_Tokyo) and [Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metropolitan_Assembly) taking precedence over the smaller municipal governments that make up the metropolis. Special wards in Tokyo include [Chiyoda](47836792.md), the site of the [National Diet Building](925262.md) and the [Tokyo Imperial Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Imperial_Palace); [Shinjuku](147734.md), the city's administrative center; and [Shibuya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibuya), a hub of commerce and business.

Tokyo, originally known as [Edo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo), rose to political prominence in 1603 when it became the seat of the [Tokugawa shogunate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_shogunate), and by the mid-18th century, Edo had evolved from a small fishing village into one of the largest cities in the world, with a population surpassing one million. After the [Meiji Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration) (1868), the imperial capital in [Kyoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto) was moved to Edo, and the city was renamed Tokyo (lit. '[Eastern Capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_of_Japan)'). Tokyo was greatly damaged by the 1923 [Great Kantō earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1923_Great_Kant%C5%8D_earthquake) and by [Allied bombing raids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombing_of_Tokyo) during [World War II](32927.md). Beginning in the late 1940s, Tokyo underwent rapid reconstruction and expansion, which fueled the [Japanese economic miracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_economic_miracle), in which [Japan's economy](15578.md) became the [second-largest in the world at the time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_past_and_projected_GDP_\(nominal\)), behind [that of the United States](32022.md).[^cite_note-15] As of 2025, Tokyo is home to 26 of the world's 500 largest companies, as listed in the annual [*Fortune* Global 500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_Global_500).[^cite_note-\:7-16]

Tokyo was the first city in Asia to host the [Summer Olympics](27566.md) and [Paralympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summer_Paralympics), in 1964, and also hosted in 2021. It hosted three [G7](1196634.md) summits in [1979](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5th_G7_summit), [1986](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/12th_G7_summit), and [1993](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/19th_G7_summit). Tokyo is an international hub of [research and development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_and_development_in_Japan) and an academic center, with [several major universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_universities_in_Tokyo), including the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo), the top-ranking university in Japan.[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18] [Tokyo Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Station) is the central hub for the [Shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinkansen), the country's high-speed railway network, and the city's [Shinjuku Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinjuku_Station) is the world's busiest train station. [Tokyo Skytree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Skytree) is the world's tallest tower.[^cite_note-skytree-19] The [Tokyo Metro Ginza Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metro_Ginza_Line), which opened in 1927, is the [oldest underground metro line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_metro_systems) in Asia.[^cite_note-20]

Tokyo's nominal gross domestic output was 120.2 trillion yen (US\$887.9 billion) in FY2022 and accounted for 21.2% of Japan's economic output, which converts to 8.43 million yen or US\$62,291 per capita.[^cite_note-\:3-21] Including the [Greater Tokyo Area](354951.md), Tokyo is the [second-largest metropolitan economy in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_by_GDP) after [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_metropolitan_area), with a 2022 gross metropolitan product estimated at US\$2.08 trillion.[^cite_note-22] Although Tokyo's status as a leading global financial hub has diminished with the [Lost Decades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Decades) since the 1990s, when the [Tokyo Stock Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Stock_Exchange) (TSE) was the world's largest, with a market capitalization about 1.5 times that of the [NYSE](21560.md),[^cite_note-\:2-23] Tokyo is still a leading financial hub, and the TSE remains among the [world's top five major stock exchanges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_major_stock_exchanges).[^cite_note-NYCFintechAndFinancialCapitalWorld-24] Tokyo is categorized as an [Alpha+ city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_city) by the [Globalization and World Cities Research Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globalization_and_World_Cities_Research_Network). It ranked 14th in the 2024 edition of the [Global Livability Ranking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Liveability_Ranking),[^cite_note-25] and has been ranked as the safest city in the world by the [Economist Intelligence Unit](15303116.md).[^cite_note-26]

&#x20;Tokyo was originally known as *[Edo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_\(Tokyo\))* ([江戸](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%B1%9F%E6%88%B8)), a [kanji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanji) compound of [江](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%B1%9F#Japanese) (*e*, 'cove, inlet') and [戸](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%88%B8#Japanese) (*to*, 'entrance, gate, door').[^cite_note-naruto-san-27] The name, which can be translated as "[estuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estuary)", is a reference to the original settlement's location at the meeting of the [Sumida River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumida_River) and [Tokyo Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Bay). During the [Meiji Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration) in 1868, the name of the city was changed to *Tokyo* ([東京](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%9D%B1%E4%BA%AC); from [東](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%9D%B1#Japanese) *tō* 'east', and [京](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%BA%AC#Japanese) *kyō* 'capital'), when it became the new imperial capital,[^cite_note-28] in line with the [East Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinosphere) tradition of including the word for capital (京) in the name of the capital city (for example, [Kyoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto) (京都), [Keijō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keij%C5%8D) (京城), [Beijing](18603746.md) (北京), [Nanjing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanjing) (南京), and Xijing (西京)).[^cite_note-naruto-san-27] During the early [Meiji period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_period), the city was sometimes called *Tōkei*, an alternative pronunciation for the same characters representing "Tokyo", making it a [kanji homograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanji_homograph). Some surviving official English documents use the spelling "Tokei";[^cite_note-Tōkei-29] however, this pronunciation is now obsolete.[^cite_note-30]

## History

Main article: [History of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Tokyo)

For a chronological guide, see [Timeline of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_Tokyo).

### Pre-Tokugawa period

The site of Tokyo has been inhabited since ancient times, and there have been multiple [Paleolithic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Paleolithic) (around 40,000–16,000 BC) sites found in present-day Tokyo.[^cite_note-\:6-31] During the subsequent [Jomon period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C5%8Dmon_period), the [Holocene glacial retreat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene_glacial_retreat) caused sea levels in [Tokyo Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Bay) to rise by 120 cm, with the coastline running along the edge of what is now the [Imperial Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Imperial_Palace).[^cite_note-32] [Middens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midden) such as the [Omori Shell Mounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omori_Shell_Mounds) still mark where the coastline ran in those days.[^cite_note-33] The [Yayoi period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yayoi_period), during which agriculture spread across the country, is named after the [Yayoi 2-chōme Site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yayoi_2-ch%C5%8Dme_Site) in [Bunkyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunky%C5%8D), where the first example of [Yayoi pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yayoi_pottery) was excavated in 1884 by [Shozo Arisaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sh%C5%8Dz%C5%8D_Arisaka).[^cite_note-34]

In 534, a large-scale conflict was recorded in the region, as a result of which Kasahara no Omi, the victor, was appointed *[Kuni no Miyatsuko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuni_no_miyatsuko)* (provincial governor) by [Emperor Ankan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Ankan).[^cite_note-35] [Senso-ji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sens%C5%8D-ji) in Asakusa was founded in 645. Under the [*Ritsuryō* system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritsury%C5%8D) established during the [Asuka period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asuka_period), most of present-day Tokyo was part of [Musashi Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musashi_Province). Following the fall of the [Kingdom of Baekje](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baekje) after the [Battle of Baekgang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Baekgang) in the 660s, thousands of refugees were resettled in Musashi.[^cite_note-36] During the [Heian period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heian_period), Edo was first fortified by the [Edo clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_clan) in the late twelfth century, and in 1457, [Ōta Dōkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Cta_D%C5%8Dkan) built [Edo Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_Castle) to defend the region from the [Chiba clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiba_clan). After Dōkan was assassinated in 1486, the [Ohgigayatsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogigayatsu_Tomosada) branch of the [Uesugi clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uesugi_clan) took control of the castle and the surrounding area. However, the [Later Hōjō clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Later_H%C5%8Dj%C5%8D_clan) replaced them after the [Battle of Takanawahara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Takanawahara) in 1524, only to be defeated by [Toyotomi Hideyoshi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyotomi_Hideyoshi) during the [Siege of Odawara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Odawara_\(1590\)) in 1590.[^cite_note-37]

### 1590–1868: Tokugawa period

Main article: [Edo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo)



[Mitsukoshi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsukoshi) stores in [Nihonbashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nihonbashi), by [Hiroshige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiroshige), c. 1836

Following the siege of Odawara, [Tokugawa Ieyasu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_Ieyasu) was granted the [Kantō region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kant%C5%8D_region) and moved there from his ancestral land of [Mikawa Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikawa_Province). When he became *[shōgun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shogun)*, the *[de facto](43594.md)* ruler of the country, in 1603, the whole country came to be ruled by Edo. While the [Tokugawa shogunate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_shogunate) ruled the country in practice, the [Imperial House of Japan](3177105.md) based in [Kyoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto) was still the *[de jure](43594.md)* ruler, and the title of shōgun was granted by the Emperor as a formality.[^cite_note-Sorensen-38] <sup>: 16 </sup> During the [Edo period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_period), the city enjoyed a prolonged period of peace known as the *Pax Tokugawa*, and in the presence of such peace, the shogunate adopted a stringent policy of seclusion, which helped to perpetuate the lack of any serious military threat to the city.[^cite_note-39] The absence of war-inflicted devastation allowed Edo to devote the majority of its resources to rebuilding in the wake of consistent fires, [earthquakes](21161124.md) and other devastating natural disasters, and Edo grew into one of the largest cities in the world with a population reaching one million by the 18th century.[^cite_note-40]

However, this prolonged period of seclusion ended with the arrival of American Commodore [Matthew C. Perry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_C._Perry) in 1853. Commodore Perry forced the opening of the ports of [Shimoda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shimoda,_Shizuoka) and [Hakodate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakodate), leading to an increase in the demand for new foreign goods and subsequently a severe rise in inflation.[^cite_note-41] Social unrest mounted in the wake of these higher prices and culminated in widespread rebellions and demonstrations, especially in the form of the "smashing" of rice establishments.[^cite_note-42] Meanwhile, supporters of the Emperor leveraged the disruption caused by widespread rebellious demonstrations to further consolidate power, which resulted in the overthrow of the last Tokugawa shōgun, [Yoshinobu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_Yoshinobu), in 1867 and the end of the [Pax Tokugawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Tokugawa).[^cite_note-43] Despite Edo castle being handed over to the Emperor-supporting forces, some forces loyal to the shogunate kept fighting, ending with the final [Battle of Ueno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ueno) on July 4, 1868.[^cite_note-44]

### 1868–1941

Main articles: [Tokyo City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_City) and [Tokyo Prefecture (1868–1943)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Prefecture_\(1868%E2%80%931943\))

After being handed over to the Meiji government, Edo was renamed **Tokyo** (Eastern Capital) on September 3, 1868, and the capital was subsequently moved officially from [Kyoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto) to Tokyo by [Emperor Meiji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Meiji).[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46] The former Edo Castle became the new [Imperial Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Imperial_Palace), and Government ministries such as the [Ministry of Finance](870389.md) were relocated to Tokyo by 1871.[^cite_note-47] The 1870s also saw the establishment of other major institutions and facilities such as [Ueno Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ueno_Park) (1873), the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo) (1877) and the [Tokyo Stock Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Stock_Exchange) (1878), as well as the rise of public transportion such as the [first railway line in the country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_rail_transport_in_Japan#Stage_1:_early_development,_1872%E2%80%931906), connecting [Shimbashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinbashi) ([Shiodome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiodome)) and [Yokohama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yokohama) ([Sakuragicho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakuragich%C5%8D_Station)).[^cite_note-48] The rapid modernization of the country was driven from Tokyo, with its business districts such as [Marunouchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marunouchi) filled with modern brick buildings and the railway network serving as a means to help the large influx of labour force needed to keep the development of the economy.[^cite_note-49] The [City of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_City) was officially established on May 1, 1889. The [Imperial Diet](163847.md), the national legislature of the country, was established in Tokyo in 1889, and it has ever since been operating in the city.



The [Metropolitan Police](30871408.md) headquarters ablaze following the earthquake. The fire triggered by the earthquake caused [fire whirls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_whirl).

On September 1, 1923, the [Great Kanto Earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1923_Great_Kant%C5%8D_earthquake) struck the city, and the earthquake and subsequent fire killed an estimated 105,000 citizens. The loss amounted to 37 percent of the country's economic output.[^cite_note-50] On the other hand, the destruction provided an opportunity to reconsider the planning of the city, which had changed its shape hastily after the [Meiji Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration). The high survival rate of concrete buildings promoted the transition from timber and brick architecture to modern, earthquake-proof construction.[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52] The [Tokyo Metro Ginza Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metro_Ginza_Line) portion between [Ueno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ueno_Station) and [Asakusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asakusa_Station_\(Tokyo_Metro,_Toei,_Tobu\)), the first underground railway line built outside Europe and the American continents, was completed on December 30, 1927.[^cite_note-90th-2017-53] Although Tokyo recovered robustly from the earthquake and new cultural and liberal political movements, such as [Taishō Democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taish%C5%8D_Democracy), spread, the 1930s saw an economic downturn caused by the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression) and major political turmoil. Two attempted military *coups d'état* happened in Tokyo, the [May 15 incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_15_incident) in 1932 and the [February 26 incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_26_incident) in 1936. This turmoil eventually allowed the military wings of the government to take control of the country, leading to Japan joining the [Second World War](32927.md) as an [Axis power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_powers). Due to the country's political isolation on the international stage caused by its [military aggression in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Sino-Japanese_War) and the increasingly unstable geopolitical situations in Europe, Тоkуо had to give up hosting the [1940 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1940_Summer_Olympics) in 1938.[^cite_note-54] [Rationing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationing) started in June 1940 as the nation braced itself for another world war, while the 26th Centenary of the Enthronement of [Emperor Jimmu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Jimmu) celebrations took place on a grand scale to boost morale and increase the sense of national identity in the same year. On December 7, 1941, [Japan attacked the American bases at Pearl Harbor in Hawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attack_on_Pearl_Harbor), entering the Second World War against the [Allied Powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II). The wartime regime greatly affected life in the city.[^cite_note-55]

### 1942–1945

Main article: [Bombing of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombing_of_Tokyo)



A birds-eye view over the Ningyōchō district after the air raid of March 10, 1945

In 1943, [Tokyo City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_City) merged with [Tokyo Prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Prefecture_\(1868%E2%80%931943\)) to form the **Tokyo Metropolis** (東京都, *Tōkyō-to*), a reorganization aimed to create a more centralized and efficient administrative structure to better manage resources, urban planning, and civil defence during wartime.[^cite_note-56] The Tokyo Metropolitan Government thus became responsible for both prefectural and city functions while administering cities, towns, and villages in the suburban and rural areas. Although Japan enjoyed significant success in the initial stages of the war and rapid expansion of its sphere of influence, the [Doolittle Raid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doolittle_Raid) on April 18, 1942 marked the first direct foreign attack on Tokyo. Although the physical damage was minimal, the raid demonstrated the vulnerability of the Japanese mainland to air attacks and boosted American morale.[^cite_note-57]

Large-scale Allied air bombing of cities in the Japanese home islands, including Tokyo, began in late 1944 when the US seized control of the [Mariana Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariana_Islands). From these islands, newly developed long-range [B-29 bombers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_B-29_Superfortress) could conduct return journeys. The bombing of Tokyo in 1944 and 1945 is estimated to have killed between 75,000 and 200,000 civilians and left more than half of the city destroyed.[^cite_note-58] The deadliest night of the war came on March 9–10, 1945, the night of the American "[Operation Meetinghouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Meetinghouse)" raid.[^cite_note-59] Nearly 700,000 incendiary bombs were dropped on the east end of the city ([Shitamachi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamanote_and_Shitamachi), 下町), an area with a high concentration of factories and working-class houses. Two-fifths of the city were completely burned, more than 276,000 buildings were destroyed, 100,000 civilians were killed, and 110,000 more were injured.[^cite_note-60][^cite_note-61] Numerous Edo and Meiji-era buildings of historical significance were destroyed, including the main building of the [Imperial Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Imperial_Palace), [Sensō-ji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sens%C5%8D-ji), [Zōjō-ji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C5%8Dj%C5%8D-ji), [Sengaku-ji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sengaku-ji) and [Kabuki-za](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabuki-za). Between 1940 and 1945, the population of Tokyo dwindled from 6,700,000 to less than 2,800,000, as soldiers were sent to the front and children were evacuated.[^cite_note-62]

### 1945–1972



[Marunouchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marunouchi) in the 1950s. It was not until the height restriction was lifted in the 1960s that skyscrapers began to dominate the skyline of Tokyo.



The 1964 Olympics in Tokyo symbolized the transition of the city from bombed-out ruins to a modern metropolis.

After the war, Tokyo became the base from which the [Allied Occupation Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Commander_for_the_Allied_Powers), under [Douglas MacArthur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_MacArthur), an American general, administered Japan for six years. The original rebuilding plan of Tokyo was based on a plan modelled after the [Metropolitan Green Belt of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Green_Belt), devised in the 1930s but canceled due to the war.[^cite_note-\:1-63] However, due to the monetary contraction policy known as the [Dodge Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodge_Line), named after [Joseph Dodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Dodge), the neoliberal economic advisor to MacArthur, the plan had to be reduced to a minimal one focusing on transport and other infrastructure. In 1947, the 35 pre-war special wards were reorganized into the [current 23 wards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_wards_of_Tokyo). Tokyo did not experience fast economic growth until around 1950, when heavy industry output returned to pre-war levels.[^cite_note-Sorensen-38][^cite_note-\:1-63]

Since around the time the [Allied occupation of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Japan) ended in 1952, Tokyo's focus shifted from rebuilding to developing beyond its pre-war stature. From the 1950s onwards, Tokyo's [Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_subway) and [railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_in_Greater_Tokyo#Rail) network saw significant expansion, culminating in the launch of the world's first dedicated high-speed railway line, the [Shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinkansen), between Tokyo and Osaka in 1964. The same year saw the development of other transport infrastructure, such as the [Shuto Expressway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuto_Expressway) to meet the increased demand brought about by the [1964 Tokyo Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_Summer_Olympics), the first Olympic Games held in Asia. Around this time, the 31-metre height restriction, imposed on all buildings since 1920, was relaxed due to the increased demand for office buildings and advancements in earthquake-proof construction.[^cite_note-64] Starting with the [Kasumigaseki Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kasumigaseki_Building) (147 metres) in 1968, skyscrapers began to dominate Tokyo's skyline. During this period of rapid rebuilding, Tokyo celebrated its 500th anniversary in 1956[^cite_note-65] and the [Ogasawara Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonin_Islands), which had been under control of the US since the war ended, were returned in 1968.[^cite_note-66] [Ryokichi Minobe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryokichi_Minobe), a Marxian economist who served as the governor for 12 years starting in 1967, is remembered for his welfare state policy, including free healthcare for the elderly and financial support for households with children, and his 'war against pollution' policy, as well as the large government deficit they caused.[^cite_note-67]

### 1973–present



Shinjuku's development as a business district started in the 1970s.

Although the [1973 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis) put an end to the rapid post-war recovery and development of Japan's economy, its became the [world's second-largest economy at the time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_past_and_projected_GDP_\(nominal\)) during that decade, remaining so until 2010 when it was surpassed by [China](5405.md).[^cite_note-68] Tokyo's development was sustained by its status as the economic, political, and cultural hub of such a country. In 1978, after years of the intense [Sanrizuka Struggle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanrizuka_Struggle), [Narita International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narita_International_Airport) opened as the new gateway to the city, while [Haneda Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haneda_Airport) switched to primarily domestic flights.[^cite_note-69] [West Shinjuku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nishi-Shinjuku), which had been occupied by the vast [Yodobashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yodobashi) Water Purification Centre until 1965, became the site of an entirely new business district characterized by skyscrapers surpassing 200 metres during this period.[^cite_note-70]

The American-led [Plaza Accord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_Accord) in 1985, which aimed to depreciate the US dollar, had a devastating effect on Japan's manufacturing sector, particularly affecting small to mid-size companies based in Tokyo.[^cite_note-71] This led the government to adopt a domestic-demand-focused economic policy, ultimately causing an [asset price bubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_asset_price_bubble). Land redevelopment projects were planned across the city, and real estate prices skyrocketed. By 1990, the estimated value of the [Imperial Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Imperial_Palace) surpassed that of the entire [state of California](5407.md).[^cite_note-72] The [Tokyo Stock Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Stock_Exchange) became the largest stock exchange in the world by market capitalization, with the Tokyo-based [NTT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nippon_Telegraph_and_Telephone) becoming the most highly valued company globally.[^cite_note-\:2-23][^cite_note-\:4-73]



The 2020 Olympics were postponed and held in 2021 due to the COVID-19 pandemic.

After the bubble burst in the early 1990s, Japan experienced a prolonged economic downturn called the "[Lost Decades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Decades)", characterized by extremely low or negative economic growth, deflation, and stagnant asset prices.[^cite_note-74] Tokyo's status as a world city is said to have depreciated greatly during these three decades. Nonetheless, Tokyo still saw new urban developments during this period. Recent projects include [Ebisu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebisu,_Shibuya) Garden Place, [Tennōzu Isle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenn%C5%8Dzu_Isle), [Shiodome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiodome), [Roppongi Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roppongi_Hills), [Shinagawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinagawa,_Tokyo), and the [Marunouchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marunouchi) side of [Tokyo Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Station). [Land reclamation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reclamation) projects in Tokyo have also been going on for centuries. The most prominent is the [Odaiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odaiba) area, now a major shopping and entertainment center. In the 1990s, various plans were proposed[^cite_note-75] for transferring national government functions from Tokyo to secondary capitals in other regions of Japan, to spread population and investment more evenly.[^cite_note-76] Such plans were met with fierce opposition, and then-prime minister [Junichiro Koizumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junichiro_Koizumi) decided to close parliamentary discussions in 2003.[^cite_note-77][^cite_note-78] The [Agency for Cultural Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agency_for_Cultural_Affairs) relocated from [Kasumigaseki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kasumigaseki) to [Kyoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto) in 2023, making it the only central organ of the [Civil Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_service_of_Japan) to move out of Tokyo so far.[^cite_note-79]

On September 7, 2013, the [IOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IOC) selected Tokyo to host the [2020 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Summer_Olympics). Thus, Tokyo became the first Asian city to host the Olympic Games twice.[^cite_note-80] However, the 2020 Olympic Games were postponed and held from July 23 to August 8, 2021, as a result of the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Japan).[^cite_note-81]

## Administration

Main article: [Tokyo Metropolitan Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metropolitan_Government)



[Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metropolitan_Government_Building), Shinjuku, designed by [Kenzo Tange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenz%C5%8D_Tange)

### Local government

Under [Japanese law](483566.md), the prefecture of Tokyo is designated as a *to* (都), translated as *[metropolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolis)*.[^cite_note-82] Tokyo Prefecture is the most populous prefecture and the densest, with 6,100 inhabitants per square kilometer (16,000/sq mi); by geographic area it is the third-smallest, above only [Osaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osaka_Prefecture) and [Kagawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kagawa_Prefecture). Its administrative structure is similar to that of Japan's other [prefectures](74517.md). The [23 special wards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_wards_of_Tokyo) (特別区, *tokubetsu-ku*), which until 1943 constituted the [city of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_City), are self-governing [municipalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipalities_of_Japan), each having a mayor, a council, and the status of a city.

In addition to these 23 special wards, Tokyo also includes 26 more cities ([市](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%B8%82#Japanese) *-shi*), five towns ([町](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E7%94%BA#Japanese) *-chō* or *machi*), and eight villages ([村](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%9D%91#Japanese) *-son* or *-mura*), each of which has a local government. The [Tokyo Metropolitan Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metropolitan_Government) administers the whole metropolis, including the 23 special wards and the cities and towns that constitute the prefecture. It is headed by a publicly elected governor and metropolitan assembly. Its [headquarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metropolitan_Government_Building) is in [Shinjuku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinjuku,_Tokyo) Ward.

The governor of Tokyo is elected every four years. The incumbent governor, [Yuriko Koike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuriko_Koike), was elected in 2016, following the resignation of her predecessor, [Yoichi Masuzoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y%C5%8Dichi_Masuzoe). She was re-elected in 2020 and 2024. The legislature of the Metropolis is called the [Metropolitan Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metropolitan_Assembly), and it has one house with 127 seats. The assembly is responsible for enacting and amending prefectural ordinances, approving the budget (8.5 trillion yen in fiscal 2024),[^cite_note-83] and voting on important administrative appointments made by the governor, including the vice governors. Its members are also elected on a four-year cycle.[^cite_note-84]

Since the completion of the [Great Mergers of Heisei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipal_mergers_and_dissolutions_in_Japan#Process) in 2001, Tokyo consists of 62 [municipalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipalities_of_Japan): 23 [special wards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_wards_of_Tokyo), 26 [cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cities_of_Japan), 5 [towns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Towns_of_Japan) and 8 [villages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villages_of_Japan). All municipalities in Japan have a directly elected mayor and a directly elected assembly, each elected on independent four-year cycles. The 23 Special Wards cover the area that had been [Tokyo City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_City) until 1943, 30 other municipalities are located in the [Tama area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tama_area), and the remaining 9 are on Tokyo's outlying islands.

- The [special wards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_wards_of_Tokyo) (特別区, *tokubetsu-ku*) of Tokyo comprise the area formerly incorporated as [Tokyo City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_City). Each special ward has used the word "city" in its official English name in recent times (e.g., Chiyoda City), but its status is more akin to boroughs in London or New York. Certain municipal functions, such as waterworks, sewerage, and fire-fighting, are handled by the Tokyo Metropolitan Government rather than each ward. To pay for the added administrative costs, the Metropolitan Government collects municipal taxes, which would usually be levied by each ward.

  [^cite_note-85]

  &#x20;The "three central wards" of Tokyo – Chiyoda, Chūō, and Minato – are the business core of the city, with a daytime population more than seven times higher than their nighttime population.

  [^cite_note-population-86]

  &#x20;Chiyoda Ward is occupied by many major [Japanese companies](76562.md) and is also the seat of the [national government](30858300.md), and the [Emperor of Japan](10110.md), yet is one of the least populated wards.

  [^cite_note-87]

- To the west of the special wards, Tokyo Metropolis consists of cities, towns, and villages that enjoy the same legal status as those elsewhere in Japan. While serving as "[bed towns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedroom_community)" for those working in central Tokyo, some of them also have a local commercial and industrial base, such as [Tachikawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tachikawa). Collectively, these are often known as the Tama area or [Western Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Tokyo). The far west of the Tama area is occupied by the district (*gun*) of [Nishi-Tama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nishitama_District,_Tokyo). Much of this area is mountainous and unsuitable for urbanization. The highest mountain in Tokyo, [Mount Kumotori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Kumotori), is 2,017 m (6,617 ft) high; other mountains in Tokyo include Takanosu (1,737 m (5,699 ft)), Odake (1,266 m (4,154 ft)), and [Mitake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Mitake_\(Tokyo\)) (929 m (3,048 ft)). [Lake Okutama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Okutama), on the [Tama River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tama_River) near [Yamanashi Prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamanashi_Prefecture), is Tokyo's largest lake and serves as the primary reservoir for Tokyo's water supply. The district is composed of three towns ([Hinode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinode,_Tokyo), [Mizuho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mizuho,_Tokyo) and [Okutama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okutama,_Tokyo)) and one village ([Hinohara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinohara,_Tokyo)). The Tokyo Metropolitan Government has designated Hachiōji, Tachikawa, Machida, Ōme, and [Tama New Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tama_New_Town) as regional centers of the Tama area.

  [^cite_note-88]

Tokyo has numerous outlying islands, which extend as far as 1,850 km (1,150 mi) from central Tokyo. Because of the islands' distance from the administrative headquarters of the Tokyo Metropolitan Government in Shinjuku, local sub-prefectural branch offices administer them. The [Izu Islands](728303.md) are a group of volcanic islands and form part of the [Fuji-Hakone-Izu National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuji-Hakone-Izu_National_Park). The islands in order from closest to Tokyo are [Izu Ōshima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izu_%C5%8Cshima), [Toshima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toshima_Island), [Nii-jima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nii-jima), [Shikine-jima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shikine-jima), [Kōzu-shima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%8Dzu-shima), [Miyake-jima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miyake-jima), [Mikurajima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikurajima), [Hachijō-jima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hachij%C5%8D-jima), and [Aogashima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aogashima). The Izu Islands are grouped into three subprefectures. Izu Ōshima and Hachijojima are towns. The remaining islands are six villages, with Niijima and Shikinejima forming [one village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niijima,_Tokyo). The [Ogasawara Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonin_Islands) include, from north to south, [Chichi-jima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chichi-jima), [Nishinoshima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nishinoshima_\(Ogasawara\)), [Haha-jima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haha-jima), [Kita Iwo Jima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Iwo_Jima), [Iwo Jima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwo_Jima), and [Minami Iwo Jima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minami_Iwo_Jima). Ogasawara also administers two small outlying islands: [Minami Torishima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minami_Torishima), the easternmost point in Japan and at 1,850 km (1,150 mi) the most distant island from central Tokyo, and [Okinotorishima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okinotorishima), the southernmost point in Japan.[^cite_note-89] Japan's claim on an [exclusive economic zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclusive_economic_zone) (EEZ) surrounding Okinotorishima is contested by [China](5405.md) and [South Korea](27019.md) as they regard Okinotorishima as uninhabitable rocks which have no EEZ.[^cite_note-90] The Iwo chain and the outlying islands have no permanent population, but hosts [Japan Self-Defense Forces](2785204.md) personnel. Local populations are only found in Chichi-Jima and Haha-Jima. The islands form both [Ogasawara Subprefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogasawara_Subprefecture) and the village of [Ogasawara, Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogasawara,_Tokyo).

#### Environmental policies

Tokyo has enacted a measure to cut greenhouse gases. Governor [Shintaro Ishihara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shintaro_Ishihara) created Japan's first [emissions cap system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emissions_cap_system), aiming to reduce [greenhouse gas emission](9528025.md) by a total of 25% by 2020 from the 2000 level.[^cite_note-92] Tokyo is an example of an [urban heat island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island), and the phenomenon is especially serious in its special wards.[^cite_note-barry-93][^cite_note-94] According to the Tokyo Metropolitan Government,[^cite_note-95] the annual mean temperature has increased by about 3 °C (5.4 °F) over the past 100 years. Tokyo has been cited as a "convincing example of the relationship between urban growth and climate".[^cite_note-barry-93]

In 2006, Tokyo enacted the "10-Year Project for Green Tokyo" to be realized by 2016. It set a goal of increasing roadside trees in Tokyo to 1 million (from 480,000), and adding 1,000 ha (2,500 acres) of green space, 88 ha (220 acres) of which would be a new park named "Umi no Mori" to replace a landfill.[^cite_note-96] From 2007 to 2010, 436 ha (1,080 acres) of the planned 1,000 ha of green space was created and 220,000 trees were planted, bringing the total to 700,000. As of 2014, roadside trees in Tokyo have increased to 950,000, and a further 300 ha (740 acres) of green space have been added.[^cite_note-97]

In 2023, the Tokyo Metropolitan Government launched the Tokyo Green Biz Project, an initiative seeking to increase and preserve the city's greenery over the next 100 years, create an online map designed to increase collaboration between residents and the local government, and create and promote green infrastructure.[^cite_note-98] They have also increased the footage of parks,[^cite_note-99] and encourage citizens to get involved in pond cleaning, tree planting, and working as park guides.

In 2018 the [Kasai Marine Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kasai_Marine_Park) became the first wetland in Tokyo to be registered under the [Ramsar Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsar_Convention).[^cite_note-100] It opened in 1989.[^cite_note-101]

Tokyo is the seat of all [three branches of government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_of_powers): the legislature ([National Diet](163847.md)), the executive ([Cabinet](573784.md) led by the [Prime Minister](24833.md)), and the judiciary ([Supreme Court of Japan](2251732.md)), as well as the [Emperor of Japan](10110.md), the head of state. Most government ministries are concentrated in the [Kasumigaseki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kasumigaseki) district in Chiyoda, and the name Kasumigaseki is often used as a [metonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metonymy) for the [Japanese national civil service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_service_of_Japan).[^cite_note-102] Tokyo has 25 constituencies for the [House of Representatives](217778.md), 18 of which were won by the ruling [Liberal Democrats](164231.md) and 7 by the main opposition [Constitutional Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_Democratic_Party_of_Japan) in the [2021 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Japanese_general_election).[^cite_note-103] Apart from these seats, through the [Tokyo proportional representation block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_proportional_representation_block), Tokyo sends 17 more politicians to the House of Representatives, 6 of whom were members of the ruling LDP in the 2021 election. The [Tokyo at-large district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_at-large_district), which covers the entire metropolis, sends 12 members to the [House of Councillors](217779.md).

Tokyo is the founding member of the [Asian Network of Major Cities 21](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_Network_of_Major_Cities_21) and is a member of the [Council of Local Authorities for International Relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Local_Authorities_for_International_Relations). Tokyo was also a founding member of the [C40 Cities Climate Leadership Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C40_Cities_Climate_Leadership_Group).[^cite_note-104]

## Geography

The mainland portion of Tokyo lies northwest of [Tokyo Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Bay) and measures about 90 km (56 mi) east to west and 25 km (16 mi) north to south. The average elevation in Tokyo is 40 m (131 ft).[^cite_note-105] [Chiba Prefecture](179796.md) borders it to the east, [Yamanashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamanashi_Prefecture) to the west, [Kanagawa](61757.md) to the south, and [Saitama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saitama_Prefecture) to the north. Mainland Tokyo is further subdivided into the special wards (occupying the eastern half) and the Tama area (多摩地域) stretching westwards. Tokyo has a [latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latitude) of 35.65 (near the [36th parallel north](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/36th_parallel_north)), which makes it more southern than [Rome](25458.md) (41.90), [New York City](645042.md) (40.71) and [Beijing](18603746.md) (39.91).[^cite_note-106]

Within the administrative boundaries of Tokyo Metropolis are two island chains in the Pacific Ocean directly south: the [Izu Islands](728303.md), and the [Ogasawara Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogasawara_Islands), which stretch more than 1,000 km (620 mi) away from the mainland. Because of these islands and the mountainous regions to the west, Tokyo's overall population density figures far under-represent the real figures for the urban and suburban regions of Tokyo.[^cite_note-107]

### Climate

The former city of Tokyo and the majority of Tokyo prefecture lie in the [humid subtropical climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humid_subtropical_climate) zone ([Köppen climate classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification): *Cfa*),[^cite_note-108] with hot, humid summers and cool winters with occasional cold spells. The region, like much of Japan, experiences a one-month [seasonal lag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seasonal_lag). The warmest month is August, which averages 26.9 °C (80.4 °F). The coolest month is January, averaging 5.4 °C (41.7 °F). The record low temperature was −9.2 °C (15.4 °F) on January 13, 1876. The record high was 39.5 °C (103.1 °F) on July 20, 2004. The record highest low temperature is 30.4 °C (86.7 °F), on August 11, 2013.[^cite_note-109]

Annual rainfall averages nearly 1,600 millimeters (63.0 in), with a wetter summer and a drier winter. The growing season in Tokyo lasts for about 322 days from around mid-February to early January.[^cite_note-110] Snowfall is sporadic and occurs almost annually.[^cite_note-111] Tokyo often sees typhoons every year, though few are strong. The wettest month since records began in 1876 was October 2004, with 780 millimeters (30 in) of rain,[^cite_note-112] including 270.5 mm (10.65 in) on the ninth of that month.[^cite_note-113] The most recent of four months on record to observe no precipitation is December 1995.[^cite_note-JMArecords-114] Annual precipitation has ranged from 879.5 mm (34.63 in) in 1984 to 2,229.6 mm (87.78 in) in 1938.[^cite_note-JMArecords-114]

#### Offshore territories

The climates of Tokyo's offshore territories vary significantly from those of the city. The climate of [Chichijima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chichijima) in [Ogasawara village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogasawara,_Tokyo) is on the boundary between the [tropical savanna climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_savanna_climate) (Köppen classification: *Aw*) and the [tropical rainforest climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_rainforest_climate) (Köppen classification: *Af*). It is approximately 1,000 km (621 mi) south of the [Greater Tokyo Area](354951.md), resulting in much different climatic conditions. Tokyo's easternmost territory, the island of [Minamitorishima](193362.md) in Ogasawara village, is in the tropical savanna climate zone (Köppen classification: *Aw*). Tokyo's Izu and Ogasawara islands are affected by an average of 5.4 typhoons a year, compared to 3.1 in mainland Kantō.[^cite_note-122]

### Natural disasters

#### Earthquakes



The [Great Kanto Earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1923_Great_Kant%C5%8D_earthquake) in 1923 killed more than 100,000 citizens in Tokyo.

Tokyo is near the [boundary of three plates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boso_triple_junction), making it an extremely active region for smaller quakes and [slippage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slow_earthquake) which frequently affect the urban area with swaying as if in a boat, although epicenters within mainland Tokyo (excluding Tokyo's 2,000 km (1,243 mi)–long island jurisdiction) are quite rare. It is not uncommon in the metro area to have hundreds of these minor quakes (magnitudes 4–6) that can be felt in a single year, something residents merely brush off but can be a source of anxiety not only for foreign visitors but for Japanese from elsewhere as well. They rarely cause much damage (sometimes a few injuries) as they are either too small or far away, as quakes tend to dance around the region. Particularly active are offshore regions and to a lesser extent [Chiba](179796.md) and [Ibaraki](181029.md).[^cite_note-125]

Tokyo has been hit by powerful [megathrust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megathrust) earthquakes in 1703, 1782, 1812, 1855, 1923, and much more indirectly (with some [liquefaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_liquefaction) in landfill zones) in [2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_T%C5%8Dhoku_earthquake_and_tsunami);[^cite_note-126][^cite_note-127] the frequency of direct and large quakes is a relative rarity. The [1923 earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1923_Great_Kant%C5%8D_earthquake), with an estimated magnitude of 7.9, killed more than 100,000 people, the last time the urban area was directly hit.[^cite_note-128][^cite_note-129][^cite_note-130]

#### Volcanic eruptions



[Mount Fuji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Fuji) has posed the primary volcanic threat to Tokyo's citizens for centuries.

[Mount Fuji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Fuji) is about 100 km (62 mi) southwest of Tokyo. There is a low risk of eruption. The last recorded was the [Hōei eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C5%8Dei_eruption_of_Mount_Fuji) which started on December 16, 1707, and ended about January 1, 1708 (16 days).[^cite_note-ShizuokaUni-131] During the Hōei eruption, the ash amount was 4 cm in southern Tokyo (bay area) and 2 cm to 0.5 cm in central Tokyo.[^cite_note-Hoei-eruption-map-132] [Kanagawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanagawa) had 16 cm to 8 cm ash and [Saitama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saitama_Prefecture) 0.5 to 0 cm.[^cite_note-Hoei-eruption-map-132] If the wind blows north-east it could send [volcanic ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_ash) to Tokyo metropolis.[^cite_note-fuji-eruption-133] According to the government, less than a millimeter of the volcanic ash from a Mount Fuji eruption could cause power grid problems such as blackouts and stop trains in the Tokyo metropolitan area.[^cite_note-fuji-eruption-133] A mixture of ash with rain could stick to cellphone antennas and power lines and cause temporary power outages.[^cite_note-fuji-eruption-133] The affected areas would need to be evacuated.[^cite_note-fuji-eruption-133]

#### Floods

&#x20;Tokyo is located on the [Kantō Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kant%C5%8D_Plain) with five river systems and dozens of rivers that expand during each season.[^cite_note-flood-protection-134] Important rivers are [Edogawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_River), [Nakagawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naka_River_\(Saitama_Tokyo\)), [Arakawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arakawa_River_\(Kant%C5%8D\)), [Kandagawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanda_River), [Megurogawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meguro_River) and [Tamagawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tama_River).[^cite_note-flood-tokyo-135] In 1947, [Typhoon Kathleen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoon_Kathleen) struck Tokyo, destroying 31,000 homes and killing 1,100 people.[^cite_note-flood-protection-134] In 1958, [Typhoon Ida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoon_Ida_\(1958\)) dropped 400 mm (16 in) of rain in a single week, causing streets to flood.[^cite_note-flood-protection-134] In the 1950s and 1960s, the [government](30858300.md) invested 6–7% of the national budget on disaster and risk reduction.[^cite_note-flood-protection-134] A huge system of dams, levees, and tunnels was constructed.[^cite_note-flood-protection-134] The purpose is to manage heavy rain, [typhonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoon) rain, and river floods.[^cite_note-flood-protection-134]



The [MAOUDC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Area_Outer_Underground_Discharge_Channel) is the world's largest underground floodwater diversion facility.

Tokyo has currently the world's largest underground floodwater diversion facility called the [Metropolitan Area Outer Underground Discharge Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Area_Outer_Underground_Discharge_Channel) (MAOUDC).[^cite_note-maoudc-136][^cite_note-flood-protection-134] It took 13 years to build and was completed in 2006. The MAOUDC is a 6.3 km (3.9 mi) long system of tunnels, 22 meters (72 ft) underground, with 70-meter (230 ft) tall cylindrical tanks, each tank being large enough to fit a Space Shuttle or the Statue of Liberty.[^cite_note-flood-protection-134] During floods, excess water is collected from rivers and drained to the [Edo River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_River).[^cite_note-flood-tokyo-135] Low-lying areas of [Kōtō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%8Dt%C5%8D), [Edogawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edogawa,_Tokyo), [Sumida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumida,_Tokyo), [Katsushika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katsushika), [Taitō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tait%C5%8D) and [Arakawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arakawa,_Tokyo) near the [Arakawa River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arakawa_River_\(Kanto\)) are most at risk of flooding.[^cite_note-flood-tokyo-135]

### Architecture

See also: [Architecture of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_Tokyo)

Tokyo's buildings are too diverse to be characterized by any specific architectural style, but it can be generally said that a majority of extant structures were built in the past hundred years.[^cite_note-spatial-137] Twice in recent history has the metropolis been left in ruins: first in the [1923 Great Kantō earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1923_Great_Kant%C5%8D_earthquake) and later after [extensive firebombing in World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombing_of_Tokyo).[^cite_note-spatial-137]

#### Early modern (1407–1868)

Extant pre-17th century structures in Tokyo



[Shōfuku-ji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sh%C5%8Dfuku-ji_\(Higashimurayama\)), built in 1407



A lookout tower at the [Imperial Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Imperial_Palace), formerly [Edo Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_Castle)

The oldest known extant building in Tokyo is [Shofukuji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sh%C5%8Dfuku-ji_\(Higashimurayama\)) in [Higashi-Murayama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higashimurayama,_Tokyo). The current building was constructed in 1407, during the [Muromachi period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muromachi_period) (1336–1573).[^cite_note-138] Although greatly reduced in number by later fires, earthquakes, and air raids, a considerable number of Edo-era buildings survive to this day. The [Tokyo Imperial Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Imperial_Palace), which was occupied by the [Tokugawa Shogunate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_shogunate) as [Edo Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_Castle) during the [Edo Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_period) (1603–1868), has many gates and towers dating from that era, although the main palace buildings and the [tenshu tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenshu) have been lost.[^cite_note-139]

&#x20;Numerous temple and shrine buildings in Tokyo date from this era: the [Ueno Toshogu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ueno_T%C5%8Dsh%C5%8D-g%C5%AB) still maintains the original 1651 building built by the third shogun [Iemitsu Tokugawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_Iemitsu).[^cite_note-140] Although partially destroyed during the Second World War, [Zojo-ji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C5%8Dj%C5%8D-ji), which houses the Tokugawa family mausoleum, still has grand Edo-era buildings such as the Sangedatsu gate.[^cite_note-141] [Kaneiji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kan'ei-ji) has grand 17th-century buildings such as the five-storey pagoda and the Shimizudo. The [Nezu Shrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nezu_Shrine) and [Gokokuji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gokoku-ji) were built by the fifth shogun [Tsunayoshi Tokugawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_Tsunayoshi) in the late 1600s. All feudal lords ([daimyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daimyo)) had large Edo houses where they stayed when in Edo; at one point, these houses amounted to half the total area of Edo.[^cite_note-142] None of the grand Edo-era daimyo houses still exist in Tokyo, as their vast land footprint made them easy targets for redevelopment programs for modernization during the [Meiji Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_era). Some gardens were immune from such fates and are today open to the public; [Hamarikyu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hama-riky%C5%AB_Gardens) ([Kofu Tokugawa family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%8Dfu_Domain)), [Shibarikyu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ky%C5%AB_Shiba_Riky%C5%AB_Garden) ([Kishu Tokugawa family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%AB_Tokugawa_family)), [Koishikawa Korakuen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koishikawa-K%C5%8Drakuen) ([Mito Tokugawa family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mito_Tokugawa_family)), [Rikugien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rikugi-en_Gardens) ([Yanagisawa family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yanagisawa_Yoshiyasu)), and [Higo Hosokawa Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higo_Hosokawa_Garden) ([Hosokawa family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hosokawa_clan)). The [Akamon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akamon_\(Tokyo\)), which is now widely seen as a symbol of the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo), was originally built to commemorate the marriage of a shogun's daughter into the [Maeda clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maeda_clan), one of the most affluent of the feudal lords, while the campus itself occupies their former edo estate.[^cite_note-143]

#### Modern (1869–1945)

Extant brick or stone buildings in Tokyo



[Akasaka State Guest House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akasaka_Palace), originally the Crown Prince's residence, built in 1909



[Tokyo Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Station), built in 1914

The [Meiji era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_era) saw a rapid modernization in architectural styles as well; until the [Great Kanto Earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1923_Great_Kant%C5%8D_earthquake) in 1923 exposed their weakness to seismic shocks, grand brick buildings were constantly built across the city. [Tokyo Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Station) (1914), the [Ministry of Justice building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Ministry_of_Justice_Building) (1895), the [International Library of Children's Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Library_of_Children's_Literature) (1906), and [Mitsubishi building one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Ichigokan_Museum,_Tokyo) (1894, rebuilt in 2010) are some of the few brick survivors from this period. It was regarded as fashionable by some members of the [Japanese aristocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazoku) to build their Tokyo residences in grand and modern styles, and some of these buildings still exist, although most are in private hands and open to the public on limited occasions. Aristocratic residences today open to the public include the [Marquess Maeda residence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komaba_Park) in [Komaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komaba), the [Baron Iwasaki residence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ky%C5%AB-Iwasaki-tei_Garden) in Ikenohata, and the [Baron Furukawa residence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ky%C5%AB-Furukawa_Gardens) in Nishigahara.

Extant concrete buildings from the interwar period



[Meiji Insurance Headquarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Seimei_Kan), completed in 1934



[National Diet Building](925262.md), built between 1920 and 1936

The Great Kanto Earthquake in 1923 ushered in an era of concrete architecture.[^cite_note-144] Surviving reinforced concrete buildings from this era include the [Meiji Insurance Headquarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Seimei_Kan) (completed in 1934), the Mitsui Headquarters (1929), [Mitsukoshi Nihonbashi flagship store](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsukoshi) (1914, refurbished in 1925), [Takashimaya Nihonbashi flagship store](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takashimaya) (1932), [Wako](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wako_\(retailer\)) in Ginza (1932) and [Isetan Shinjuku flagship store](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isetan) (1933). This spread of earthquake and fire-resistant architecture reached council housing too, most notably the [Dōjunkai apartments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C5%8Djunkai).[^cite_note-145]

The 1930s saw the rise of styles that combined characteristics of both traditional Japanese and modern designs. [Chuta Ito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It%C5%8D_Ch%C5%ABta) was a leading figure in this movement, and his extant works in Tokyo include [Tsukiji Hongan-ji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsukiji_Hongan-ji) (1934). The [Imperial Crown Style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Crown_Style), which often features Japanese-style roofs on top of elevated concrete structures, was adopted for the [Tokyo National Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_National_Museum) in Ueno and the Kudan Hall in [Kudanminami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kudanminami).[^cite_note-146]

#### Contemporary (1946–present)

See also: [List of tallest buildings in Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_Tokyo)

Contemporary buildings in Tokyo



[Yoyogi National Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoyogi_National_Gymnasium), completed in 1964



[National Art Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Art_Center,_Tokyo), completed in 2007

Since the 30-metre height restriction was lifted in the 1960s, Tokyo's most dense areas have been dominated by skyscrapers. As of May 2024, at least 184 buildings are exceeding 150 metres (492 feet) in Tokyo. Apart from these, [Tokyo Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Tower) (333m) and [Tokyo Skytree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Skytree) (634m) feature high-elevation observation decks; the latter is the tallest tower in both Japan and the world, and the third tallest structure in the world.[^cite_note-skytree-19] With a scheduled completion date in 2027, [Torch Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torch_Tower_\(Japan\)) (385m) will overtake [Azabudai Hills Mori JP Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azabudai_Hills) (325.2m) as the tallest building in Tokyo.

[Kenzo Tange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenz%C5%8D_Tange) designed notable contemporary buildings in Tokyo, including [Yoyogi National Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoyogi_National_Gymnasium) (1964), [St. Mary's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Mary's_Cathedral,_Tokyo) (1967), and the [Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metropolitan_Government_Building) (1991). [Kisho Kurokawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kisho_Kurokawa) was also active in the city, and his works there include the [National Art Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_National_Art_Center,_Tokyo) (2005) and the [Nakagin Capsule Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nakagin_Capsule_Tower) (1972). Other notable contemporary buildings in Tokyo include the [Tokyo Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Dome), [Mode Gakuen Cocoon Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_Gakuen_Cocoon_Tower), [Roppongi Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roppongi_Hills), [Tokyo International Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_International_Forum), and [Asahi Beer Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asahi_Beer_Hall).

As of October 2012, the official [intercensal estimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercensal_estimate) showed 13.506 million people in Tokyo, with 9.214 million living within Tokyo's 23 wards.[^cite_note-population2015-148] During the daytime, the population swells by over 2.5 million as workers and students commute from adjacent areas. This effect is even more pronounced in the three central wards of [Chiyoda](47836792.md), [Chūō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C5%AB%C5%8D,_Tokyo), and [Minato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minato,_Tokyo), whose collective population as of the 2005 National Census was 326,000 at night, but 2.4 million during the day.[^cite_note-population-86]

According to April 2024 official estimates, [Setagaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Setagaya) (942,003), [Nerima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nerima) (752,608), and [Ota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Cta,_Tokyo) (748,081) were the most populous wards and municipalities in Tokyo. The least inhabited of all Tokyo municipalities are remote island villages such as [Aogashima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aogashima,_Tokyo) (150), [Mikurajima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikurajima,_Tokyo) (289), and [Toshima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To-shima,_Tokyo) (306).[^cite_note-149]

### Age structure and average age

In 2021, Tokyo's average and median ages were both 45.5 years old. This is below the national median age of 49.0, placing Tokyo among the youngest regions in Japan. 16.8% of the population was below 15, while 34.6% was above 65.[^cite_note-150] In the same year, the youngest municipalities in Tokyo were [Mikura-jima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikura-jima) (average age 40.72), Chūō (41.92), and Chiyoda (42.07), while the oldest included [Okutama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okutama,_Tokyo) (59.11) and [Miyake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miyake,_Tokyo) (53.82).[^cite_note-151]

### Immigration

In 1889, the [Home Ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Ministry) recorded 1,375,937 people in [Tokyo City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_City) and a total of 1,694,292 people in [Tokyo-fu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Prefecture_\(1868%E2%80%931943\)).[^cite_note-152] In the same year, a total of 779 foreign nationals were recorded as residing in Tokyo. The most common nationality was English (209 residents), followed by American (182) and Chinese nationals (137).[^cite_note-153]

As of January 2024, Tokyo had 647,416 foreign nationals registered as residents, with China, South Korea, Vietnam, the Philippines, Nepal, Taiwan, and the United States each having more than 20,000 nationals living there as citizens.[^cite_note-pop-detail-154] Since the COVID-19 pandemic ended, Tokyo's foreign population has increased significantly, now nearly 20% above the January 2022 population of 546,436.[^cite_note-155] There is no official survey of race or place of birth as of June 2024.

### Dialects

See also: [Tokyo dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_dialect)

[Japanese](15606.md) is the primary language spoken throughout the metropolis, though regional and socio-economic differences can be heard. Traditionally, dialects in Tokyo are classified into two groups: the Yamanote dialect and the Shitamachi dialect. The former has traditionally been spoken in the upper- and upper-middle-class residential area of Yamanote, which includes [Bancho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banch%C5%8D), [Kojimachi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%8Djimachi), [Koishikawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koishikawa), [Kudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kudankita), [Yotsuya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yotsuya), [Azabu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azabu), and [Akasaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akasaka,_Tokyo). During the [Edo period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_period), these neighborhoods were occupied by *[Daimyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daimyo)* and other powerful [samurai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samurai) families, and the dialect evolved largely based on their way of speech. Standard Japanese pronunciation is largely based on this accent and spread across the country with the introduction of [radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio). The Shitamachi dialect, in contrast, has been associated with the *[Chōnin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C5%8Dnin)* district of Shitamachi and retains many characteristics of the accents spoken there in the Edo era.[^cite_note-156] However, socio-economic changes in the post-war period and the large influx of people moving from other areas have largely blurred these distinctions in recent years. It has been reported that young generations are not as aware of the differences in dialects as their parents' and grandparents' generations were.[^cite_note-157]

The [Hachijō dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hachij%C5%8D_language), spoken primarily in [Hachijōjima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hachij%C5%8D-jima) and [Aogashima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aogashima), descended from 6th-8th century [Eastern Old Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Old_Japanese) and has fewer than 1,000 speakers.[^cite_note-158][^cite_note-159] [Bonin English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonin_English) is a creole spoken in the [Ogasawara Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonin_Islands), derived from English and Japanese,[^cite_note-Long2006-160] as the islands' population historically consisted of people of Japanese, British, American, Hawaiian, and Polynesian origins, mostly mixed-race.[^cite_note-\:12-161]

## Economy



Sector breakdown of the 2021 GDP of Tokyo

Tokyo's gross regional product was 120.2 trillion yen or US\$887.9 billion in FY2022 and accounted for 21.2% of the country's total economic output, which converts to 8.43 million yen or US\$62,291 per capita.[^cite_note-\:3-21] By sector, wholesale and retail was the largest contributor, accounting for 21.5% of the total output. This was followed by real estate (13.5%), professional, scientific and technical (12.2%), information and communications (11.7%), finance and insurance (7.6%), manufacturing (7.0%), and healthcare (6.7%). Agriculture, forestry and fishery, and mining combined accounted for less than 0.1% of the economic output.[^cite_note-162] As these numbers suggest, Tokyo's economy is heavily dependent on the tertiary sector. As the [Greater Tokyo Area](354951.md), it has the [second-largest metropolitan economy in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_by_GDP), after [Greater New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_metropolitan_area), with a gross metropolitan product estimated at US\$2 trillion. The area's economy is slightly smaller than [Canada's economy](5195.md) while being slightly larger than [Mexico's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Mexico), according to IMF estimates from the same year.[^cite_note-163]

Tokyo's business districts are concentrated in four central wards: [Chiyoda](47836792.md) ([Marunouchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marunouchi), [Otemachi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Ctemachi), [Kasumigaseki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kasumigaseki)), [Chuo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C5%AB%C5%8D,_Tokyo) ([Nihombashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nihonbashi), [Kyobashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ky%C5%8Dbashi,_Tokyo), [Yaesu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaesu)), [Minato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minato,_Tokyo) ([Shimbashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinbashi), [Shiodome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiodome), [Toranomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toranomon)), and [Shinjuku](147734.md) ([West Shinjuku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nishi-Shinjuku)). The 23 Special Wards of Tokyo had 73.5 million m<sup>2</sup> of office space as of January 2022.[^cite_note-164]

In 2025, 26 of the *[Fortune Global 500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_Global_500)* companies were headquartered in Tokyo.[^cite_note-\:7-16] Notably, around 20 of them are based in Marunouchi, such as [MUFG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_UFJ_Financial_Group), [Mitsubishi Corp.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Corporation) and [Hitachi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitachi).[^cite_note-165] Tokyo was rated by the [Economist Intelligence Unit](15303116.md) as the most expensive (highest [cost-of-living](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost-of-living_index)) city in the world for 14 years in a row ending in 2006, when it was replaced by [Oslo](22309.md), and later [Paris](22989.md).[^cite_note-166][^cite_note-167] However, years of deflation and an extremely weak yen starting in 2022 due to Japan's low interest rates made the cost of living in Tokyo 31% cheaper than in [New York City](645042.md) in 2023, which is roughly the same as in [Beijing](18603746.md) and [Manchester](20206.md) according to the 2023 EIU rankings.[^cite_note-168]

### Finance

Tokyo is a major international finance center, housing the headquarters of several of the world's largest [investment banks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Investment_bank) and insurance companies, and serves as a hub for Japan's transportation, publishing, [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics), and broadcasting industries.[^cite_note-169] During the centralized growth of Japan's economy following [World War II](32927.md), many large firms moved their headquarters from cities such as [Osaka](52230.md) (the historical commercial capital) to Tokyo, in an attempt to take advantage of better access to the government.&#x20;



The [Tokyo Stock Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Stock_Exchange), Chuo, Tokyo

Tokyo emerged as a leading international [financial center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_centre) (IFC) in the 1960s and has been described as one of the three "command centers" for the [world economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_economy), along with New York City and [London](17867.md).[^cite_note-170] In the 2020 [Global Financial Centers Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Financial_Centres_Index), Tokyo was ranked as having the fourth most competitive financial center in the world, and second most competitive in Asia (after Shanghai).[^cite_note-GFCI2-171] [Mitsubishi UFJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_UFJ_Financial_Group), [Sumitomo-Mitsui Banking Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumitomo_Mitsui_Banking_Corporation), [Mizuho Financial Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mizuho_Financial_Group), all among the [top 20 banks in the world by total assets in 2023](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_banks), are headquartered in Tokyo.

The Japanese financial market opened up slowly in 1984 and accelerated its internationalization with the "Japanese Big Bang" in 1998.[^cite_note-172] Despite the emergence of Singapore and Hong Kong as competing financial centers, the Tokyo IFC manages to keep a prominent position in Asia. The [Tokyo Stock Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Stock_Exchange) is Japan's largest [stock exchange](28168.md), and third largest in the world by [market capitalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_capitalization), and the fourth largest by share turnover. In 1990, at the end of the [Japanese asset price bubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_asset_price_bubble), it accounted for more than 60% of the world stock market value.[^cite_note-173]

### Agriculture, fishery, and forestry



[Toyosu Market](58650701.md), Koto

The [Toyosu Market](58650701.md) in Tokyo is the largest wholesale fish and seafood market in the world since it opened on October 11, 2018.[^cite_note-178] It is also one of the largest wholesale food markets of any kind. It is located in the [Toyosu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyosu) area of [Kōtō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%8Dt%C5%8D) ward. The Toyosu Market holds strong to the traditions of its predecessor, the [Tsukiji Fish Market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsukiji_Fish_Market) and [Nihonbashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nihonbashi) fish market, and serves some 50,000 buyers and sellers every day. Retailers, wholesalers, auctioneers, and public citizens alike frequent the market, creating a unique microcosm of organized chaos that continues to fuel the city and its food supply after over four centuries.[^cite_note-179] Tokyo had 8,460 hectares (20,900 acres) of agricultural land as of 2003,[^cite_note-180] according to the [Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries](1327960.md), placing it last among the nation's prefectures. The farmland is concentrated in Western Tokyo. Perishables such as vegetables, fruits, and flowers can be conveniently shipped to the markets in the eastern part of the prefecture.

With 36% of its area covered by forest, Tokyo has extensive growths of [cryptomeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptomeria) and [Japanese cypress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamaecyparis_obtusa), especially in the mountainous western communities of Akiruno, Ōme, Okutama, Hachiōji, Hinode, and Hinohara. Decreases in the price of timber, increases in the cost of production, and advancing old age among the forestry population have resulted in a decline in Tokyo's output. In addition, pollen, especially from cryptomeria, is a major [allergen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seasonal_allergies) for the nearby population centers. Tokyo Bay was once a major source of fish. Most of Tokyo's fish production comes from the outer islands, such as Izu Ōshima and Hachijō-Jima. [Skipjack tuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skipjack_tuna), [nori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nori), and *[aji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carangidae)* are among the ocean products.

## Transportation

Main article: [Transport in Greater Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_in_Greater_Tokyo)

Tokyo, which is the center of the [Greater Tokyo Area](354951.md), is Japan's largest domestic and international hub for rail and ground transportation. Public transportation within Tokyo is dominated by an extensive network of "clean and efficient"[^cite_note-181] trains and subways run by a variety of operators, with buses, monorails and trams playing a secondary feeder role. There are up to 62 electric train lines and more than 900 train stations in Tokyo.[^cite_note-182] [Shibuya Crossing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibuya_Crossing) is the "world's busiest pedestrian crossing", with around 3,000 people crossing at a time.[^cite_note-asahi_2016-04-22-183][^cite_note-cnn_2019-08-25-184][^cite_note-185]

### Rail



[JR East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Japan_Railway_Company) operates the largest commuter train network in Tokyo as well as intercity services.



The [Shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinkansen) connects major cities around the country to Tokyo.

Rail is the primary mode of transportation in Tokyo,[^cite_note-186] which has the most extensive urban railway network in the world and an equally extensive network of surface lines. [JR East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Japan_Railway_Company) operates Tokyo's largest railway network, including the [Yamanote Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamanote_Line) loop that circles central Tokyo. It operates rail lines throughout the entire metropolitan area of Tokyo and the rest of northeastern Honshu. JR East is also responsible for the [Shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinkansen) high-speed rail lines that link Tokyo and the Northeastern cities of Japan ([Joetsu Shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C5%8Detsu_Shinkansen), [Tohoku/Hokkaido Shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%8Dhoku_Shinkansen), [Yamagata Shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamagata_Shinkansen), [Akita Shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akita_Shinkansen), [Hokuriku Shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hokuriku_Shinkansen)).[^cite_note-187] The [Tokaido Shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokaido_Shinkansen), which links Tokyo and [Osaka](52230.md) via [Nagoya](61720.md) and [Kyoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto), as well as western cities beyond, is operated by [JR Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Japan_Railway_Company). The [Chuo Shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C5%AB%C5%8D_Shinkansen), the first-ever long-distance high-speed floating maglev line currently under construction, will also be operated by JR Central. Both JR companies were created from the privatization of [Japan National Railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_National_Railways) in 1987. [JR Freight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Freight_Railway_Company) does not own any part of the railway network but operates freight trains on the [JR network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Railways_Group). Two different entities operate Tokyo's underground railway network: the privatized [Tokyo Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metro), which operates Tokyo Metro lines, and the governmental [Tokyo Metropolitan Bureau of Transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metropolitan_Bureau_of_Transportation), which operates Toei lines. Tokyo Metro is a listed private company, although the [Japanese Government](30858300.md) and the [Tokyo Metropolitan Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metropolitan_Government) still jointly hold a 50% stake due to its history as a public entity called the *Imperial Capital Rapid Transit Authority* from 1941 to 2004.[^cite_note-188][^cite_note-189] Other major railway operators in Tokyo include [Odakyu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odakyu_Electric_Railway), [Tokyu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyu_Corporation), [Keio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keio_Corporation), [Seibu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seibu_Railway), [Tobu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobu_Railway), and [Keisei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keisei_Electric_Railway). Although each operator directly owns its railway lines, services that travel across different lines owned by different operators are common.

Tokyo once had an extensive tram network, with a total distance of 213 km (*[Tokyo Toden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Toden)*). However, similar to other major cities worldwide, the age of motorization since the 1950s has made it considered unfit to share busy roads with cars. Today, only one line, the [Arakawa line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toden_Arakawa_Line), remains.[^cite_note-190]

### Roads



[Shuto Expressway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuto_Expressway) near [Harumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harumi,_Tokyo)

Tokyo has the lowest car ownership rate among all prefectures in Japan, with 0.416 cars per household compared to the national average of 1.025 per household. This is despite Tokyo being one of the most affluent areas in the country.[^cite_note-191] A 2021 survey found that 81% of respondents without a car were satisfied with public transport and saw no need to own one.[^cite_note-192]

Each road in Tokyo falls into one of the following categories depending on the type of ownership: private roads, municipal roads, metropolitan roads, and expressways. As of April 1, 2022, the total length of roads in Tokyo is approximately 24,741 km (including 2,370 km of metropolitan roads), with a total area of approximately 190.31 km<sup>2</sup> (including 46.30 km<sup>2</sup> of metropolitan roads).[^cite_note-193] Intercity expressways in and around Tokyo are managed by [NEXCO East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Nippon_Expressway_Company), while expressways that serve only within the Greater Tokyo Area ([Shuto Expressway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuto_Expressway)) are operated by the Metropolitan Expressway Company. Tolls are collected based on the distance travelled. The total length of the Shuto Expressway is 337.8 km, with speed limits usually set at 80 km/h or 60 km/h to reduce noise pollution and accommodate the relatively winding road shapes.[^cite_note-194]

### Aviation



Aerial view of [Haneda Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haneda_Airport)

The mainland portion of Tokyo is served by two international airports: [Haneda Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haneda_Airport) in [Ōta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Cta,_Tokyo) and [Narita International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narita_International_Airport) in neighboring [Chiba Prefecture](179796.md). Haneda has served as the primary airport for Tokyo since 1931. However, the Jet Age saw an exponential increase in flights, prompting the government to build a second airport. Narita was chosen as the site for this second airport in 1966, but local farmers and left-wing activists who sympathized with them protested vehemently for more than a decade (the [Sanrizuka Struggle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanrizuka_Struggle)), delaying the new airport's opening until 1978. Almost all international flights were transferred to Narita Airport upon its completion, and Haneda became primarily a domestic airport.[^cite_note-195]

The situation changed when it was decided to expand Haneda Airport and build new runways in 2001. The new runway, Runway D, was constructed partly as a pier-like structure rather than a landfilled structure to avoid obstructing the flow of water in the bay.[^cite_note-196] Its opening in 2010 marked the return of international flights to Haneda, which is much closer to central Tokyo. In 2023, Haneda handled 17.9 million international passengers and 60.8 million domestic passengers,[^cite_note-197] while Narita was used by 25.4 million international passengers and 7.7 million domestic passengers.[^cite_note-198] According to a 2023 survey, Haneda is the fifth [busiest airport in the world by passenger traffic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_busiest_airports_by_passenger_traffic).

[Hachijō-jima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hachij%C5%8D-jima) ([Hachijojima Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hachijojima_Airport)), [Kōzu-shima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%8Dzu-shima) ([Kōzushima Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%8Dzushima_Airport)), [Miyakejima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miyakejima) ([Miyakejima Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miyakejima_Airport)), [Nii-jima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nii-jima) ([Niijima Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niijima_Airport)), and [Izu Ōshima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izu_%C5%8Cshima) ([Oshima Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oshima_Airport)), located on the [Izu Islands](728303.md), which are governed by the Tokyo Metropolis have services to Haneda and the [Chōfu Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C5%8Dfu_Airport) located in [Chōfu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C5%8Dfu).

### Water transport



A [Mitsui O.S.K. Lines](401425.md) container ship at the [Port of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Tokyo)



A [Jetfoil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_929_Jetfoil) operated by the [Tōkai Kisen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%8Dkai_Kisen), which serves between Tokyo and the [Izu Islands](728303.md)

Water transport is the primary means of importing and exporting goods as well as connecting the [Tokyo Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Islands) to the mainland. According to [Lloyd's List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lloyd's_List), the [Port of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Tokyo) handled 4,430,000 TEU of containers in 2022, making it the 46th largest port in the world.[^cite_note-199] The Greater Tokyo Area is served by other major ports such as the [Port of Yokohama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Yokohama) and the [Port of Chiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Chiba) as well. Takeshiba Pier (竹芝埠頭) in [Minato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minato,_Tokyo) is used by [Tōkai Kisen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%8Dkai_Kisen), which serves islands such as [Izu Ōshima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izu_%C5%8Cshima), [Miyakejima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miyake-jima), [Hachijojima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hachij%C5%8D-jima), [Kozushima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%8Dzu-shima),[^cite_note-200] and Ogasawara Kaiun, which serves the [Ogasawara Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonin_Islands).[^cite_note-201] Many of these islands are accessible only by ocean routes and helicopters, as they are too small or undulating for a landing ground, making these ships the primary means of inter-island transport. There are ferry routes that connect landmarks within the mainland portion of Tokyo as well; the [Tokyo Cruise Ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Cruise_Ship) and the [Tokyo Mizube Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Mizube_Line) operate several routes between tourist attractions such as [Asakusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asakusa), [Hamarikyu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hama-riky%C5%AB_Gardens), [Odaiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odaiba), and Shinagawa Aquarium.[^cite_note-202] The Symphony Cruise operates two large restaurant ships that can also be hired as party venues.[^cite_note-203]

## Education

Main article: [Education in Tokyo](11799203.md)

Tokyo is the educational, academic, and cultural hub of Japan. From primary to tertiary levels, numerous educational institutions operate in the city to cater to a diverse range of pupils and students.

### Tertiary education

See also: [List of universities in Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_universities_in_Tokyo)



[Yasuda Auditorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasuda_Auditorium), University of Tokyo, Bunkyō

Tokyo is the heartland of tertiary education in the country, home to 143 authorized universities in 2020.[^cite_note-204] This number includes the nation's most prestigious and selective universities, such as, the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo) (*[QS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QS_World_University_Rankings) National*:1st), [Tokyo Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Institute_of_Technology) (4th), [Waseda University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waseda_University) (9th), [Keio University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keio_University) (10th) and [Hitotsubashi University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitotsubashi_University) (15th).[^cite_note-205] Additionally, [Tokyo University of the Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_University_of_the_Arts) is widely regarded as the most prestigious painting, sculpture, crafts, and music school in the country.[^cite_note-206] The [United Nations University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_University), which is the academic arm of the [United Nations](31769.md), is headquartered in [Shibuya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibuya), Tokyo. In 2024, *QS Best Student Cities* ranked Tokyo as the second-best city for university students, after [London](17867.md).[^cite_note-207] The ranking noted that Tokyo is ideal for "those who favour total immersion in local culture rather than living in a "student bubble'", stating that despite having high-ranking universities and large global companies offering internships as well as rich culture, Tokyo still has a very low international student population ratio.[^cite_note-208]

### Primary and secondary education



[Hibiya High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibiya_High_School), Chiyoda

At the secondary level, 429 senior high schools are located in Tokyo, six of which are national, 186 are public, and 237 are private.[^cite_note-\:0-209] Some senior high schools, often prestigious national or private ones, run jointly with their affiliated junior high schools, providing six-year educational programs (*Chūkō Ikkan Kyōiku*). The [Kaisei Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaisei_Academy),[^cite_note-210] [Komaba Junior & Senior High School, University of Tsukuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komaba_Junior_and_Senior_High_School,_University_of_Tsukuba),[^cite_note-211] [Azabu High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azabu_High_School), and [Oin Junior and Senior High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oin_Junior_and_Senior_High_School),[^cite_note-212] the largest sources of successful applicants to the nation's top university, the University of Tokyo,[^cite_note-213] are some examples of such. At the primary level, there are 1332 elementary schools in Tokyo. Six of them are national, 1261 are public, and 53 are private.[^cite_note-\:0-209]

Early-modern-established academies such as [Gakushuin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gakush%C5%ABin) and [Keio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keio_Gijuku_\(Gakk%C5%8D_H%C5%8Djin\)) provide all-through educational programs from primary schools to universities, originally to cater to the needs of traditionally affluent and powerful families.[^cite_note-214] There are international and ethnic schools that abide by the national curricula of their respective countries or international curricula rather than the Japanese one as well, such as the [British School in Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_British_School_in_Tokyo), [Tokyo Chinese School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Chinese_School), the [American School in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_School_in_Japan), and the [Tokyo International School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_International_School).

### Learned societies

Almost all major Japanese learned societies are based in Tokyo. The [Japan Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Academy), the country's [academy of sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_sciences), was established in 1879 to bring together leading scholars in various disciplines.[^cite_note-215] The [Japan Art Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Art_Academy) was established in 1919 with a similar purpose.[^cite_note-216] These two national academies are headquartered in [Ueno Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ueno_Park). The newest national academy, the [Science Council of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_Council_of_Japan), was established in 1949 to promote scientific research and the application of research findings to civilian life. It is located in [Roppongi, Minato.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roppongi)

## Culture

### Museums, art galleries, libraries, and zoos



[Tokyo National Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_National_Museum), Ueno



[Tokyo Sea Life Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Sea_Life_Park), Edogawa

See also: [List of museums in Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_museums_in_Tokyo)

Tokyo is home to a wide array of museums, art galleries, and libraries, catering to various interests. [Ueno Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ueno_Park) has the [Tokyo National Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_National_Museum), the country's largest museum specializing in traditional Japanese art,[^cite_note-217] the [National Museum of Western Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Western_Art), whose building designed by [Le Corbusier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Corbusier) is a [world heritage site](44940.md),[^cite_note-218] and the [National Museum of Nature and Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Nature_and_Science). [Ueno Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ueno_Zoo) is also located within the park, near the [Shinobazu Pond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinobazu_Pond). It is famous for being one of the three zoos in Japan to have [giant pandas](12713.md), with a population of 4 as of May 2024.[^cite_note-219]

Other notable museums include the [Artizon Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artizon_Museum) in Chūō, the [National Museum of Emerging Science and Innovation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miraikan) in Odaiba, and the [Edo-Tokyo Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo-Tokyo_Museum) in Sumida, which provides insights into the history and culture of Tokyo. The [Edo-Tokyo Open Air Architectural Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo-Tokyo_Open_Air_Architectural_Museum) preserves various buildings that have existed throughout the history of Tokyo. The [Nezu Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nezu_Museum) in Aoyama has a collection of pre-modern Japanese and East Asian art. Located near the [Imperial Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Imperial_Palace), the [National Diet Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Diet_Library), the [National Archives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Archives_of_Japan), and the [National Museum of Modern Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Modern_Art,_Tokyo) are also notable cultural institutions. Additionally, the [Mori Art Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mori_Art_Museum) in [Roppongi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roppongi) and the [Sumida Hokusai Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumida_Hokusai_Museum) in Sumida ward are notable for their contemporary and ukiyo-e art collections, respectively. The [Sompo Museum of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sompo_Museum_of_Art) in Shinjuku is best known for owning one of Van Gogh's [*Sunflowers*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunflowers_\(Van_Gogh_series\)). The [Tokyo Metropolitan Garden Art Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metropolitan_Teien_Art_Museum) in [Minato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minato,_Tokyo) features the former Tokyo House of [Yasuhiko, Prince Asaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Yasuhiko_Asaka), which was built in an opulent [Art Deco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Deco) style in 1933. The [Railway Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_Museum_\(Saitama\)), which used to be located in [Kanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanda,_Tokyo), has relocated to a larger site in [Omiya, Saitama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Cmiya,_Saitama) and stores 42 train carriages and locomotives of historical importance.[^cite_note-220] The [Tobacco and Salt Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_and_Salt_Museum) in Sumida has one of the world's most extensive collections of different types of tobacco and salt. Major aquariums in Tokyo include: [Tokyo Sea Life Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Sea_Life_Park), [Sunshine Aquarium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunshine_Aquarium) and [Sumida Aquarium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumida_Aquarium).

### Leisure and entertainment



[Kabukicho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabukich%C5%8D), a nightlife district in [Shinjuku](147734.md)

Tokyo offers a diverse array of leisure and entertainment options. The city is home to numerous theatres. The [National Noh Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Noh_Theatre) and [Kabuki-za](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabuki-za) are dedicated to traditional Japanese plays. The [New National Theatre Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_National_Theatre_Tokyo) in Shibuya serves as a central venue for opera, ballet, contemporary dance, and drama.[^cite_note-about-nntt-221] Other major play and concert venues include: the [National Theatre of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Theatre_of_Japan), the [Imperial Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Theatre_\(Japan\)), the [Meiji-za](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji-za), the [NHK Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NHK_Hall), the [Tokyo Metropolitan Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metropolitan_Theatre), [Tokyo Opera City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Opera_City_Tower) and the [Tokyo International Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_International_Forum). Two sports venues, the [Nippon Budokan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nippon_Budokan) and the [Tokyo Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Dome), are usually used to host concerts by popular pop artists.[^cite_note-222]

The nightlife district of Tokyo is centered around areas in the west of the city, such as [Shibuya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibuya), [Shinjuku](147734.md), and [Roppongi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roppongi), with a high concentration of bars, clubs, [host and hostess clubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_and_hostess_clubs), and live music venues.[^cite_note-223] Tokyo is also known for its festivals, such as the [Sannō Matsuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sann%C5%8D_Matsuri) at [Hie Shrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hie_Shrine), the Sanja Festival at Asakusa Shrine, and the biennial [Kanda Matsuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanda_Matsuri), which features parades with elaborately decorated floats. [Harajuku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harajuku), located in Shibuya, is internationally famous for its youth fashion and street culture, with trendy shops, cafes, and Takeshita Street.[^cite_note-224] [Akihabara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akihabara), known as "Electric Town", is a hub for electronics and [otaku culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otaku) such as [anime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anime) and [computer games](5363.md), with numerous shops selling anime, manga, and gaming merchandise.[^cite_note-225] [Ginza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginza) and [Nihombashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nihonbashi) are two of Tokyo's most notable shopping districts. Ginza is known for its high-end shopping, featuring luxury brand stores, boutique shops, and department stores such as [Mitsukoshi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsukoshi) and [Wako](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wako_\(retailer\)). It is also home to numerous fine dining places and art galleries, making it a cultural and commercial hub. Nihombashi, historically a center of commerce, has long-established shops and the Mitsukoshi department flagship store, Japan's first department store, founded in 1673.[^cite_note-226] [Jinbōchō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinb%C5%8Dch%C5%8D,_Tokyo) is known for its concentration of bookstores, publishing houses, and literary cafes, and its links to a large number of famous literary figures.[^cite_note-227]

Modern attractions in Tokyo include the [Tokyo Skytree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Skytree) in Sumida, the tallest structure in Japan, which provides panoramic views of the city from its observation decks. [Odaiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odaiba), a man-made island in Tokyo Bay, features shopping, dining, and entertainment attractions such as the [teamLab Planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TeamLab_Planets_TOKYO_DMM.com) digital art museum and [Joypolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joypolis) indoor amusement park.[^cite_note-228] The [Tokyo Disney Resort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Disney_Resort) and its two theme parks [Tokyo Disneyland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Disneyland) and [Tokyo DisneySea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_DisneySea) are major destinations for family entertainment. Although these Disney theme parks bear the name Tokyo, they are located in nearby [Urayasu, Chiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urayasu), just east of Tokyo.

### Food

In November 2007, [Michelin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelin_guide) released their first guide for fine dining in Tokyo, awarding 191 stars in total, or about twice as many as Tokyo's nearest competitor, Paris. As of 2017, 227 restaurants in Tokyo have been awarded (92 in Paris). Twelve establishments were awarded the maximum of three stars (Paris has 10), 54 received two stars, and 161 earned one star.[^cite_note-229]

### Recreation



[Ogasawara National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogasawara_National_Park), a [UNESCO](21786641.md) World Natural Heritage Site

Natural settings for outdoor activities include [Okutama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okutama,_Tokyo) and [Mount Takao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Takao), which are known for their hiking trails and scenic views. [Kasai Seaside Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kasai_Rinkai_Park) provides coastal leisure activities. [Ueno Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ueno_Park) houses several museums and a zoo, and is famous for its cherry blossoms. [Inokashira Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inokashira_Park) in Kichijoji features a pond, a zoo, and in its vicinity the [Ghibli Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghibli_Museum). [Yoyogi Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoyogi_Park), located near Shibuya, is popular for picnics and outdoor events. [Shinjuku Gyoen National Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinjuku_Gyo-en), [Koishikawa Korakuen Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koishikawa-K%C5%8Drakuen), [Rikugien Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rikugi-en_Gardens), [Hamarikyu Gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hama-riky%C5%AB_Gardens), [Kiyosumi Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiyosumi_Garden), [Kyu Shiba Rikyu Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ky%C5%AB_Shiba_Riky%C5%AB_Garden), [Chinzanso Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinzan-s%C5%8D_Garden), [Happo-en Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Happo-en_Garden?action=edit\&redlink=1),[^cite_note-230] [Mukojima-Hyakkaen Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muk%C5%8Djima-Hyakkaen_Garden) and [Meiji Jingu Inner Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Shrine_Inner_Garden) are popular traditional Japanese gardens in Tokyo, some of which originally belonged to members of the *[kazoku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazoku)* nobility. Botanical gardens in Tokyo include the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo)'s [Koishikawa Botanical Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koishikawa_Botanical_Garden), the [Yumenoshima Tropical Greenhouse Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yumenoshima_Tropical_Greenhouse_Dome), and the [Institute for Nature Study Nature Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Nature_Study).

#### National parks

As of March 31, 2008, 36% of the total land area of the prefecture was designated as [Natural Parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefectural_Natural_Park) (second only to [Shiga Prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiga_Prefecture)), namely the [Chichibu Tama Kai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chichibu_Tama_Kai_National_Park), [Fuji-Hakone-Izu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuji-Hakone-Izu_National_Park), and [Ogasawara National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogasawara_National_Park) (the last a UNESCO [World Heritage Site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Heritage_Sites_in_Japan)); [Meiji no Mori Takao Quasi-National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_no_Mori_Takao_Quasi-National_Park); and [Akikawa Kyūryō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akikawa_Ky%C5%ABry%C5%8D_Prefectural_Natural_Park), [Hamura Kusabana Kyūryō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamura_Kusabana_Ky%C5%ABry%C5%8D_Prefectural_Natural_Park), [Sayama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayama_Prefectural_Natural_Park_\(Tokyo\)), [Takao Jinba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takao_Jinba_Prefectural_Natural_Park), [Takiyama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takiyama_Prefectural_Natural_Park), and [Tama Kyūryō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tama_Ky%C5%ABry%C5%8D_Prefectural_Natural_Park) Prefectural Natural Parks.[^cite_note-231]

### In popular culture



[Consumer electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_electronics) stores in Tokyo in 1971: the 'city lights at night' urban landscape of Tokyo inspired the development of the [cyberpunk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyberpunk) genre.

[^cite_note-232]

Tokyo has long been a popular setting in both Japanese and Western creative works. In literature, the city has featured since the [Edo period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_period), while the modern city appears in the works of authors such as [Natsume Sōseki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natsume_S%C5%8Dseki) and [Mori Ōgai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mori_%C5%8Cgai), who depicted life in Tokyo during the industrialization period in famous novels such as *[Sanshirō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanshir%C5%8D_\(novel\))* (1908) and *[The Wild Geese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wild_Geese_\(Mori_novel\))* (1911). They spent their undergraduate days at [Tokyo University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo), and many protagonists of such novels are students who moved to Tokyo for higher education, contrasting Tokyo with their hometowns.[^cite_note-233][^cite_note-234] In later years, writers who have used Tokyo as the setting for their major works include: [Yasunari Kawabata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasunari_Kawabata), who depicted the 'decadent allure' of Tokyo in the 1930s in his series *[The Scarlet Gang of Asakusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Scarlet_Gang_of_Asakusa)* (1930);[^cite_note-235] [Kenzaburō Ōe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenzabur%C5%8D_%C5%8Ce), who centred his early works around [turbulent political movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anpo_protests) in the city in the 1960s;[^cite_note-236] and [Haruki Murakami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haruki_Murakami), who has set many of his works in [Shinjuku](147734.md) and [Shibuya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibuya), as symbols of youth culture as well as urban loneliness. Western authors have also set their literary works in Tokyo, such as [Ian Fleming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Fleming)'s [*James Bond* series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_James_Bond_novels_and_short_stories) novel *[You Only Live Twice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/You_Only_Live_Twice_\(novel\))* and [David Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Mitchell_\(author\))'s *[number9dream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number9dream)*.

The city has also appeared frequently in film and television. Japanese directors like [Akira Kurosawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akira_Kurosawa) and [Yasujirō Ozu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasujir%C5%8D_Ozu) have used Tokyo as the backdrop for narratives that examine postwar change and the complexities of modern society. In the *[kaiju](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiju)* (monster movie) genre, landmarks of Tokyo are usually destroyed by giant monsters such as [Godzilla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Godzilla) and [Gamera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamera).[^cite_note-237] Western films, from earlier examples such as *[Tokyo Joe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Joe_\(film\))* to more recent titles like *[Lost in Translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_in_Translation_\(film\))* and *[The Fast and the Furious: Tokyo Drift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Fast_and_the_Furious:_Tokyo_Drift)*, often depict Tokyo as an exotic urban landscape where cultures intersect. In addition, Tokyo appears frequently in [anime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anime), [manga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manga), and [video games](5363.md), where its real and reimagined districts provide the setting for stories ranging from realistic dramas to fantastical adventures.

## Sports

Main article: [Sports in Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_in_Tokyo)



[Japan National Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_National_Stadium)



Ryogoku Kokugikan Tsuriyane 05212006.jpg

Panorama of the Tokyo Dome

Panorama of the Tokyo Dome

Tokyo, with a diverse array of sports, is home to two professional baseball teams, the [Yomiuri Giants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yomiuri_Giants), who play at the [Tokyo Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Dome), and [Tokyo Yakult Swallows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Yakult_Swallows) at [Meiji-Jingu Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji-Jingu_Stadium). The [Japan Sumo Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Sumo_Association) is also headquartered in Tokyo at the [Ryōgoku Kokugikan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ry%C5%8Dgoku_Kokugikan) sumo arena where three official [sumo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumo) tournaments are held annually (in January, May, and September). Soccer clubs in Tokyo include [FC Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FC_Tokyo) and [Tokyo Verdy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Verdy), both of which play at [Ajinomoto Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajinomoto_Stadium) in [Chōfu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C5%8Dfu,_Tokyo), and [Machida Zelvia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FC_Machida_Zelvia) at [Machida GION Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machida_GION_Stadium) in [Machida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machida,_Tokyo). [Rugby Union](25405.md) is also played in Tokyo, with multiple [Japan Rugby League One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Rugby_League_One) clubs based in the city including: [Black Rams Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Rams_Tokyo) ([Setagaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Setagaya,_Tokyo)), [Tokyo Sungoliath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Sungoliath) ([Fuchū](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuch%C5%AB,_Tokyo)) and [Toshiba Brave Lupus Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toshiba_Brave_Lupus_Tokyo) ([Fuchū](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuch%C5%AB,_Tokyo)).

Basketball teams include the [Hitachi SunRockers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitachi_SunRockers), [Toyota Alvark Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyota_Alvark_Tokyo), and [Tokyo Excellence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Excellence).

Tokyo hosted the [1964 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_Summer_Olympics), thus becoming the first Asian city to host the [Summer Games](27566.md). The National Stadium, also known as the [Olympic Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Stadium_\(Tokyo,_1958\)), was host to several international sporting events. In 2016, it was to be replaced by the [New National Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_National_Stadium). With several world-class sports venues, Tokyo often hosts national and international sporting events such as basketball tournaments, women's volleyball tournaments, tennis tournaments, swim meets, marathons, rugby union and sevens rugby games, soccer exhibition games, [judo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judo), and [karate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karate). [Tokyo Metropolitan Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metropolitan_Gymnasium), in [Sendagaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sendagaya), Shibuya, is a large sports complex that includes swimming pools, training rooms, and a large indoor arena. According to *[Around the Rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Around_the_Rings)*, the gymnasium has played host to the October 2011 Artistic Gymnastics World Championships, despite the International Gymnastics Federation's initial doubt in Tokyo's ability to host the championships after the [2011 Tohoku earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_T%C5%8Dhoku_earthquake_and_tsunami) hit Japan.[^cite_note-238] Tokyo was also selected to host several games for the [2019 Rugby World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Rugby_World_Cup), and to host the [2020 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Summer_Olympics) and [Paralympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Summer_Paralympics), which had to be rescheduled to the summer of 2021 due to [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Japan).
