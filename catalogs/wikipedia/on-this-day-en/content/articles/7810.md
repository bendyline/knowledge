The **Church of the Holy Sepulchre**,[^cite_note-sepname-1][^cite_note-2] also known as the **Church of the Resurrection**,[^cite_note-resname-3] is a fourth-century church in the [Christian Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Quarter) of the [Old City](302972.md) of [Jerusalem](16043.md). The church is simultaneously the seat of the [Armenian Patriarchate of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Patriarchate_of_Jerusalem), [Greek Orthodox Patriarchate of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Patriarchate_of_Jerusalem), and the [Catholic](606848.md) [Latin Patriarchate of Jerusalem](231524.md).[^cite_note-4] It is the holiest site in [Christianity](5211.md), and it has been an important pilgrimage site for Christians since the [fourth century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_the_4th_century).

According to traditional beliefs dating to the fourth century, the church contains the site of the [Crucifixion of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crucifixion_of_Jesus)[^cite_note-mcmahon-5] at [Calvary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvary), or Golgotha, and the location of [Jesus](1095706.md)'s empty [tomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Jesus) where he was [buried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burial_of_Jesus) and [resurrected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resurrection_of_Jesus). Both locations are considered immensely holy sites by most Christians.[^cite_note-sacred-6] Scholars note that there are two or three independent accounts of the burial of Jesus in a tomb, and archeologists are in general agreement that this is likely the location.[^cite_note-Moropoulou-7][^cite_note-Bow_Komo-8][^cite_note-Gibson-9] The Gospel of John and archaeological finds show at least seven corroborations in mundane details with the Church of the Holy Sepulchre.[^cite_note-Wahlde-10] After the crucifixion Emperor Hadrian covered the tomb of Jesus and erected a Temple of Aphrodite on top of it (ca. 135 AD), where it remained until Constantine rediscovered the tomb around 325 AD.[^cite_note-Moropoulou-7] The church and rotunda were built under [Constantine the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_the_Great) in the 4th century, confirmed by scientific testing,[^cite_note-Moropoulou-7] and [destroyed in 1009](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destruction_of_the_Church_of_the_Holy_Sepulchre) by [al-Hakim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hakim_bi-Amr_Allah). Al-Hakim's son allowed Emperor [Constantine IX Monomachos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_IX_Monomachos) to reconstruct the church, which was completed in 1048. After it was [captured](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_\(1099\)) by the [crusaders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades) in 1099, it continued to undergo modifications, resulting in a significant departure from the original structure.[^cite_note-\:02-11] Several renovations and restorations were made under the Ottomans.[^cite_note-Murphy56-12] The tomb itself is enclosed by a 19th-century shrine called the Aedicule.

Within the church proper are the last four [stations of the Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stations_of_the_Cross) of the [Via Dolorosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Dolorosa), representing the final episodes of the [Passion of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passion_of_Jesus). Since its creation the church has been a major [Christian pilgrimage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_pilgrimage) destination as the traditional site of the resurrection of Christ, thus its original Greek name, Church of the Anastasis (Resurrection).

The [Status Quo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Status_Quo_\(Jerusalem_and_Bethlehem\)), an understanding between religious communities dating to 1757, applies to the site.[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14] Control of the church itself is shared among several [Christian denominations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_denominations) and secular entities in complicated arrangements essentially unchanged for over 160 years, and some for much longer. The main denominations sharing property over parts of the church are the [Roman Catholic](606848.md), [Greek Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Church), [Armenian Apostolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Apostolic_Church), [Coptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_Orthodox_Church_of_Alexandria), [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox_Church), and [Ethiopian Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Orthodox_Tewahedo_Church). Directly adjacent to the Church of the Holy Sepulchre is the [Church of the Redeemer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_Redeemer,_Jerusalem), marking a [Lutheran](23371382.md) presence at the site.[^cite_note-Kroll2024-15]

## Name

The **Church of the Holy Sepulchre**[^cite_note-sepname-1][^cite_note-16] is also known as the **Basilica of the Holy Sepulchre**[^cite_note-17] or simply as the **Holy Sepulchre**, named for the [tomb of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Jesus), the focal point of [his resurrection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resurrection_of_Jesus) according to Christians. [Eastern Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Christianity) also directly call it the **Church of the Resurrection** or the **Church of the Anastasis**[^cite_note-resname-3] – *anástasis* (*ἀνάστασις*) being Greek for *resurrection*.

## History

See also: [§ Connection to Roman temple](#Connection_to_Roman_temple)

### Background (1st–4th centuries)

After the [siege of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_\(70_AD\)) in AD 70 during the [First Jewish–Roman War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Jewish%E2%80%93Roman_War), Jerusalem had been reduced to ruins. In AD 130, the Roman emperor [Hadrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadrian) began the building of a Roman colony, the new city of *[Aelia Capitolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aelia_Capitolina)*, on the site. About AD 135, he ordered that a cave containing a [rock-cut tomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock-cut_tombs_in_ancient_Israel)[^cite_note-22] be filled in to make a flat foundation for a temple dedicated to [Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus_\(mythology\)) or Aphrodite.[^cite_note-sacred-6][^cite_note-paul-23] [The temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Venus_\(Aelia_Capitolina\)) remained until the early fourth century.[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-Corbo-25]

After the death of Jesus, Emperor Hadrian covered the tomb and erected a Temple of Aphrodite on top of it around 135 AD and it remained until Constantine rediscovered the tomb around 325 AD.[^cite_note-Moropoulou-7] Physical testing of a mortar layer between the original older limestone tomb and later marble slab, revealed that the original shrine construction around the actual tomb could be securely dated to the time of [Constantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_the_Great), as historical records indicate on its rediscovery.[^cite_note-Romey2017-26][^cite_note-Moropoulou-7]

### Constantine and Macarius: context for the first sanctuary

After seeing a vision of a cross in the sky in 312,[^cite_note-bible-27] [Constantine the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_the_Great) began to favour [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_the_Great_and_Christianity) and signed the [Edict of Milan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edict_of_Milan) legalizing the religion. The Bishop of Jerusalem [Macarius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macarius_of_Jerusalem) asked Constantine for permission to dig for the tomb. With the help of [Eusebius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eusebius) (a Bishop of Caesarea) and Macarius, three crosses were found near a tomb; one, which was said to have [cured people near death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helena,_mother_of_Constantine_I#True_Cross), was presumed to be the [True Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_Cross), on which Jesus was [crucified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crucifixion_of_Jesus), leading the Romans to believe that they had found [Calvary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvary).[^cite_note-bible-27][^cite_note-28]

About 326, Constantine ordered that the temple to Jupiter or Venus be replaced by a church.[^cite_note-sacred-6] After the temple was torn down and its ruins removed, the soil was removed from the cave, revealing a rock-cut tomb that Macarius identified as the burial site of Jesus.[^cite_note-mcmahon-5][^cite_note-constantine-29][^cite_note-30][^cite_note-socrates-31]

The Church of the Holy Sepulchre site has been recognized since early in the fourth century as the place where Jesus was crucified, buried, and rose from the dead.[^cite_note-commemoration-39][^cite_note-41] The church was consecrated on 13 September 335.[^cite_note-commemoration-39][^cite_note-42]

In 327, Constantine and Helena separately commissioned the [Church of the Nativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_Nativity) in [Bethlehem](4312.md) to commemorate the birth of Jesus.

The Constantinian sanctuary in Jerusalem was destroyed by a fire [in May of 614](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_conquest_of_Jerusalem), when the [Sassanid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sassanid_Empire), under [Khosrow II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khosrow_II),[^cite_note-bible-27] invaded Jerusalem and captured the True Cross. In 630, the Emperor [Heraclius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclius) rebuilt the church after recapturing the city.[^cite_note-Kroesen-43]

### Early Islamic period (7th–10th centuries)

See also: [First Muslim conquest of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Muslim_conquest_of_Jerusalem)

After [Jerusalem came under Islamic rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Jerusalem_during_the_Middle_Ages), it remained a Christian church, with the early Muslim rulers protecting the city's Christian sites, prohibiting their destruction or use as living quarters. A story reports that the caliph [Umar ibn al-Khattab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umar) visited the church and stopped to pray on the balcony, but at the time of prayer, turned away from the church and prayed outside. He feared that future generations would misinterpret this gesture, taking it as a pretext to turn the church into a mosque. [Eutychius of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutychius_of_Alexandria) adds that Umar wrote [a decree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umar's_Assurance) saying that Muslims would not inhabit this location. The building suffered severe damage from an earthquake in 746.[^cite_note-Kroesen-43]

Early in the ninth century, another earthquake damaged the dome of the Anastasis. The damage was repaired in 810 by [Patriarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Patriarch_of_Jerusalem) [Thomas I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_I_of_Jerusalem). In 841, the church suffered a fire. In 935, the Christians prevented the construction of a Muslim mosque adjacent to the Church. In 938, a new fire damaged the inside of the basilica and came close to the rotunda. In 966, due to a defeat of Muslim armies in the region of Syria, a riot broke out, which was followed by reprisals. The basilica was burned again. The doors and roof were burnt, and Patriarch [John VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_VII_of_Jerusalem) was murdered.[^cite_note-44]

### Fatimid destruction and reconstruction (11th century)

See also: [Destruction of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destruction_of_the_Church_of_the_Holy_Sepulchre)

On 18 October 1009, [Fatimid caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimid_Caliphate) [al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hakim_bi-Amr_Allah) ordered the [complete destruction of the church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destruction_of_the_Church_of_the_Holy_Sepulchre) as part of a more general campaign against Christian and Jewish places of worship in Palestine and Egypt,[^cite_note-45] He was also hostile toward [Sunni Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam).[^cite_note-46] The damage was extensive, with few parts of the early church remaining, and the roof of the rock-cut tomb damaged; the original shrine was destroyed.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDK201699-33] The tomb itself was almost completely destroyed, with only portions of the northern wall containing the burial bench and the southern wall surviving.[^cite_note-47] Some partial repairs followed.[^cite_note-Morris_2005-48] Christian Europe reacted with shock: it was a spur to [expulsions of Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expulsions_of_Jews) and, later on, used as a justification for the [Crusades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades).[^cite_note-49]

In wide-ranging negotiations between the Fatimids and the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) in 1027–1028, an agreement was reached whereby the new Caliph [Ali az-Zahir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_az-Zahir) (al-Hakim's son) agreed to allow the rebuilding and redecoration of the church.[^cite_note-Lev_1991-50] The rebuilding was finally completed during the tenures of Emperor [Constantine IX Monomachos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_IX_Monomachos) and [Patriarch Nicephorus of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicephorus_I_of_Jerusalem) in 1048, during the reign of [al-Mustansir Billah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mustansir_Billah) (Ali az-Zahir's son).[^cite_note-Foakes-Jackson-51] As a concession, the mosque in [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople) was reopened and the [khutba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khutba) sermons were to be pronounced in az-Zahir's name.[^cite_note-Lev_1991-50] Muslim sources say a by-product of the agreement was the renunciation of Islam by many Christians who had been forced to convert under al-Hakim's persecutions. In addition, the Byzantines, while releasing 5,000 Muslim prisoners, made demands for the restoration of other churches destroyed by al-Hakim and the reestablishment of a [patriarch](175409.md) in Jerusalem. Contemporary sources credit the emperor with spending vast sums in an effort to restore the Church of the Holy Sepulchre after this agreement was made.[^cite_note-Lev_1991-50] Still, "a total replacement was far beyond available resources. The new construction was concentrated on the rotunda and its surrounding buildings: the great basilica remained in ruins."[^cite_note-Morris_2005-48]

The rebuilt church site consisted of "a court open to the sky, with five small chapels attached to it."[^cite_note-Fergusson-52] The chapels were east of the court of resurrection (when reconstructed, the location of the tomb was under open sky), where the western wall of the great basilica had been. They commemorated scenes from the passion, such as the location of the prison of Christ and his flagellation, and presumably were so placed because of the difficulties of free movement among shrines in the city streets. The dedication of these chapels indicates the importance of the pilgrims' devotion to the suffering of Christ. They have been described as "a sort of [Via Dolorosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Dolorosa) in miniature" since little or no rebuilding took place on the site of the great basilica. Western pilgrims to Jerusalem during the 11th century found much of the sacred site in ruins.[^cite_note-Morris_2005-48] Control of Jerusalem, and thereby the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, continued to change hands several times between the Fatimids and the [Seljuk Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuq_dynasty) (loyal to the [Abbasid caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate) in [Baghdad](4492.md)) until the Crusaders' arrival in 1099.[^cite_note-53]

### Crusader period (1099–1187)

See also: [Art of the Crusades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_of_the_Crusades)

#### Background

Many historians maintain that the main concern of [Pope Urban II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Urban_II), when calling for the [First Crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Crusade), was the threat to Constantinople from the [Seljuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuk_Empire) invasion of [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor) in response to the appeal of Byzantine Emperor [Alexios I Komnenos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexios_I_Komnenos). Historians agree that the fate of Jerusalem and thereby the Church of the Holy Sepulchre was also of concern, if not the immediate goal of papal policy in 1095. The idea of taking Jerusalem gained more focus as the Crusade was underway. The rebuilt church site was taken from the Fatimids (who had recently taken it from the Abbasids) by the knights of the First Crusade on 15 July 1099.[^cite_note-Morris_2005-48]

The First Crusade was envisioned as an [armed pilgrimage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_pilgrimage), and no crusader could consider his journey complete unless he had prayed as a pilgrim at the Holy Sepulchre. In light of this mentality, Crusader leader [Godfrey of Bouillon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Godfrey_of_Bouillon) (who became the first [Latin ruler of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Jerusalem)) decided not to use the title "king" during his lifetime, and declared himself *[Advocatus Sancti Sepulchri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Title_of_Godfrey_of_Bouillon)* ('Protector \[or Defender] of the Holy Sepulchre').

According to the German priest and pilgrim [Ludolf von Sudheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludolf_von_Sudheim), the keys to the Chapel of the Holy Sepulchre were in hands of the "ancient [Georgians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgians)", and the food, alms, candles and oil for lamps were given to them by the pilgrims at the south door of the church.[^cite_note-54]

#### Crusaders: reconstruction (12th century) and ownership

By the Crusader period, a [cistern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cistern) under the former basilica was rumoured to have been where Helena had found the True Cross, and began to be venerated as such; the cistern later became the [Chapel of the Invention of the Cross](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Grotto%20of%20the%20Holy%20Cross), but there is no evidence of the site's identification before the 11th century, and modern archaeological investigation has now dated the cistern to 11th-century repairs by Monomachos.[^cite_note-Corbo-25]

[William of Tyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Tyre), chronicler of the Crusader Kingdom of Jerusalem, reports on the rebuilding of the church in the mid-12th century. The Crusaders investigated the eastern ruins on the site, occasionally excavating through the rubble, and while attempting to reach the cistern, they discovered part of the original ground level of Hadrian's temple enclosure; they transformed this space into a [chapel dedicated to Helena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapel_of_Saint_Helena,_Jerusalem), widening their original excavation tunnel into a proper staircase.[^cite_note-Savage37-55]



Crusader [graffiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graffiti) in the church: crosses engraved in the staircase leading down to the [Chapel of Saint Helena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapel_of_Saint_Helena,_Jerusalem)

[^cite_note-madainhelenachapel-56]

The Crusaders began to refurnish the church in [Romanesque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanesque_architecture) style and added a [bell tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_tower).[^cite_note-Savage37-55] These renovations unified the small chapels on the site and were completed during the reign of [Queen Melisende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Melisende) in 1149, placing all the holy places under one roof for the first time.[^cite_note-Savage37-55]

The church became the seat of the first [Latin patriarchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Patriarch_of_Jerusalem) and the site of the kingdom's [scriptorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scriptorium).[^cite_note-Savage37-55]

&#x20;Eight 11th- and 12th-century Crusader leaders ([Godfrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Godfrey_of_Bouillon), [Baldwin I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldwin_I_of_Jerusalem), [Baldwin II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldwin_II_of_Jerusalem), [Fulk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulk,_King_of_Jerusalem), [Baldwin III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldwin_III_of_Jerusalem), [Amalric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amalric_of_Jerusalem), [Baldwin IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldwin_IV_of_Jerusalem) and [Baldwin V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldwin_V_of_Jerusalem) – the first eight [rulers of the Kingdom of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rulers_of_the_Kingdom_of_Jerusalem)) were buried in the south transept and inside the [Chapel of Adam](#Chapel_of_Adam).[^cite_note-57][^cite_note-58][^cite_note-Pringle65-59] The royal tombs were looted during the [Khwarizmian sack of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_\(1244\)) in 1244 but probably remained mostly intact until 1808 when a fire damaged the church. The tombs may have been destroyed by the fire, or during renovations by the Greek Orthodox custodians of the church in 1809–1810. The remains of the kings may still be in unmarked pits under the church's pavement.[^cite_note-reem-60]

### Ayyubid, Mamluk and Ottoman periods

The church was lost to [Saladin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saladin),[^cite_note-Savage37-55] along with the rest of the city, in 1187, although the treaty established after the [Third Crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Crusade) allowed Christian pilgrims to visit the site. [Emperor Frederick II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Frederick_II) (r. 1220–50) regained the city and the church by treaty in the 13th century while under a ban of [excommunication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excommunication), with the consequence that the holiest church in Christianity was laid under [interdict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interdict).[^cite_note-61] The church seems to have been largely in the hands of Greek Orthodox patriarch [Athanasius II of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athanasius_II_of_Jerusalem) (c. 1231–47) during the last period of Latin control over Jerusalem.[^cite_note-62] Both city and church were captured by the [Khwarezmians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khwarezm) and the Ayyubids in 1244.[^cite_note-Savage37-55]

There was certainly a recognisable ‘[Nestorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nestorian)’ ([Church of the East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_East)) presence at the Holy Sepulchre from the years 1348 through 1575, as contemporary [Franciscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franciscan) accounts indicate.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELuke192446–56-63] The [Franciscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franciscan) friars renovated the church in 1555, as it had been neglected despite increased numbers of pilgrims. The Franciscans rebuilt the [Aedicule](#Aedicule), extending the structure to create an [antechamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vestibule_\(architecture\)).[^cite_note-Murphy56-12] A marble shrine commissioned by Friar [Boniface of Ragusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boniface_of_Ragusa) was placed to envelop the remains of Christ's tomb,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDK201699-33] probably to prevent pilgrims from touching the original rock or taking small pieces as souvenirs.[^cite_note-natgeo-34] A marble slab was placed over the limestone burial bed where Jesus's body is believed to have lain.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDK201699-33]



[Eastern Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox) icon (c. 1600) commemorating a church renovation

After the renovation of 1555, control of the church oscillated between the Franciscans and the Orthodox, depending on which community could obtain a favourable *[firman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firman_\(decree\))* from the "[Sublime Porte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublime_Porte)" at a particular time, often through outright bribery. Violent clashes were not uncommon. There was no agreement about this question, although it was discussed at the negotiations to the [Treaty of Karlowitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Karlowitz) in 1699.[^cite_note-Mailáth-64]

In the mid-17th century, [Evliya Çelebi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evliya_%C3%87elebi), the renowned Ottoman traveler and a devout Muslim, visited the church and wrote that despite its "beautiful adornment", he found it lacking in "spirituality", comparing it instead to something like a "tourist attraction". After touring the building, he wrote that he performed "two prostrations in a corner" and expressed the hope that "it one day become a Muslim place of worship". He also objected to what he saw of religious practice there, complaining about the many statues at the church, and about what he described as priests deceiving believers.[^cite_note-65]

During the [Holy Week](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Week) of 1757, Orthodox Christians reportedly took over some of the Franciscan-controlled church. This may have been the cause of the sultan's *[firman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firman)* (decree) later developed into the [Status Quo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Status_Quo_\(Jerusalem_and_Bethlehem\)).[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-ladder-67]

A fire severely damaged the structure again in 1808,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDK201699-33] causing the dome of the rotunda to collapse and smashing the Aedicule's exterior decoration. The rotunda and the Aedicule's exterior were rebuilt in 1809–10 by architect Nikolaos Ch. Komnenos of [Mytilene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mytilene) in the contemporary [Ottoman Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_architecture#Baroque_period) style.[^cite_note-68] The interior of the antechamber, now known as the [Chapel of the Angel](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Chapel%20of%20the%20Angel),[^cite_note-69] was partly rebuilt to a square ground plan in place of the previously semicircular western end.

Another decree in 1853 from the sultan solidified the existing territorial division among the communities and solidified the Status Quo for arrangements to "remain in their present state", requiring consensus to make even minor changes.[^cite_note-cohen-70][^cite_note-ladder-67]



Floorplan in 1857

The dome was restored by Catholics, Greeks, and Turks in 1868, being made of iron ever since.[^cite_note-cruise-72]

### British Mandate period

By the time of the British [Mandate for Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_for_Palestine) following the end of [World War I](4764461.md), the [cladding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cladding_\(construction\)) of red limestone applied to the [Aedicule](#Aedicule) by Komnenos had deteriorated badly and was detaching from the underlying structure; from 1947 until restoration work in 2016–17, it was held in place with an exterior scaffolding of iron girders installed by the [British authorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandatory_Palestine).[^cite_note-NG16-73]

### Jordanian and Israeli period



Diagram of the modern church showing the traditional site of [Calvary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvary) and the [Tomb of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Jesus)

In 1948, Jerusalem was divided between Israel and Jordan and where the church was located, in the [Old City](302972.md), were made part of Jordan. In 1967, Israeli forces captured East Jerusalem in the [Six Day War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six_Day_War), and that area has remained under Israeli control ever since. Under Israeli rule, legal arrangements relating to the churches of [East Jerusalem](343508.md) were maintained in coordination with the Jordanian government. The dome at the Church of the Holy Sepulchre was restored again in 1994–97 as part of extensive modern renovations that have been ongoing since 1959. During the 1970–78 restoration works and excavations inside the building, and under the nearby [Muristan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muristan) bazaar, it was found that the area was originally a quarry, from which white *[meleke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meleke)* [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone) was struck.[^cite_note-Hesemann170-74] Blocks of [Meleke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meleke) [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone) were extracted from the quarry and their traces can be seen on the surfaces of floors, walls and the ceiling in the Chapel of St. Vartan[^cite_note-\:1-75]

#### Chapel of St. Vartan

East of the Chapel of Saint Helena, the excavators discovered a void containing a fourth-century drawing of a Roman pilgrim ship,[^cite_note-madainvartan-76] two low walls supporting the platform of Hadrian's second-century temple, and a higher fourth-century wall built to support Constantine's basilica.[^cite_note-Murphy56-12][^cite_note-lancaster-77] After the excavations of the early 1970s, the Armenian authorities converted this archaeological space into the Chapel of [Saint Vartan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Vartan), and created an artificial walkway over the quarry on the north of the chapel, so that the new chapel could be accessed (by permission) from the Chapel of Saint Helena.[^cite_note-lancaster-77]

#### Aedicule restoration

After seven decades of being held together by steel girders, the [Israel Antiquities Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_Antiquities_Authority) (IAA) declared the visibly deteriorating [Aedicule](#Aedicule) structure unsafe. A restoration of the Aedicule was agreed upon and executed from May 2016 to March 2017. Much of the \$4 million project was funded by the [World Monuments Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Monuments_Fund), as well as \$1.3 million from Mica Ertegün and a significant sum from [King Abdullah II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdullah_II_of_Jordan) of [Jordan](7515964.md).[^cite_note-NG16-73][^cite_note-2017restoration-78] The existence of the original limestone cave walls within the Aedicule was confirmed, and a window was created to view this from the inside.[^cite_note-natgeo-34] The presence of moisture led to the discovery of an underground shaft resembling an [escape tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escape_tunnel) carved into the bedrock, seeming to lead from the tomb.[^cite_note-secret-21][^cite_note-79] For the first time since at least 1555, on 26 October 2016, marble cladding that protects the supposed burial bed of Jesus was removed.[^cite_note-natgeo-34][^cite_note-ls-80] Members of the [National Technical University of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Technical_University_of_Athens) were present. Initially, only a layer of debris was visible. This was cleared in the next day, and a partially broken marble slab with a Crusader-style cross carved was revealed.[^cite_note-secret-21] By the night of 28 October, the original limestone burial bed was shown to be intact. The tomb was resealed shortly thereafter.[^cite_note-natgeo-34] Mortar from just above the burial bed was later dated to the mid-fourth century.[^cite_note-81]

#### 2020 pandemic

On 25 March 2020, Israeli health officials ordered the site closed to the public due to the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Israel). According to the Joudeh al-Goudia family who are hereditary keepers of the keys, it was the first such closure since 1349, during the [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death).[^cite_note-82] Clerics continued regular prayers inside the building, and it reopened to visitors two months later, on 24 May.[^cite_note-83]

#### Crusader altar slab discovered (2022)

During church renovations in 2022, a stone slab covered in modern graffiti was moved from a wall, revealing [Cosmatesque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmatesque)-style decoration on one face. According to an [IAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_Antiquities_Authority) archaeologist, the decoration was once inlaid with pieces of glass and fine marble; it indicates the object was the [frontal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antependium#Types_of_antependia) of the church's [high altar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_altar) from the [Crusader era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusader_states) (c. 1149), which was later used by the [Greek Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Church) until being damaged in the 1808 fire.[^cite_note-84]

### Bell tower



Bell tower left of the entrance

The 12th-century Crusader [bell tower](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Holy%20Sepulchre%20bell%20tower) is just south of the Rotunda, to the left of the entrance.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDK201694–97-91] Its uppermost level was lost in a 1545 collapse.[^cite_note-custodia-92] In 1719, another two storeys were lost.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDK201697-87]

### Façade and entrance



Entrance door

The façade and entrance have undergone significant changes since their construction in the 12th century. The entrance doors were originally topped with ornate [lintels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lintel), and their tympanums were decorated with mosaics. The doors themselves were likely decorated with sculpture. Today, the original lintels are on display at the [Rockefeller Archeological Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_Archeological_Museum), the mosaics are completely missing, and nothing of the original doors remains.[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94] Only the left-hand entrance is currently accessible, as the right doorway has long since been bricked up. The entrance to the church leads to the south [transept](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transept), through the crusader [façade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fa%C3%A7ade) in the [parvis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parvis) of a larger courtyard.[^cite_note-95][^cite_note-96] This is found past a group of streets winding through the outer [Via Dolorosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Dolorosa) by way of a [souq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Souq) in the [Muristan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muristan). This narrow way of access to such a large structure has proven to be hazardous at times. For example, when a fire broke out in 1840, dozens of pilgrims were trampled to death.[^cite_note-sun-97]

The church is open to pilgrims and visitors, and entrance is generally free. Because it remains an active place of worship, access to some chapels may be affected by liturgies, processions, or denominational services. Visitors are generally expected to dress modestly, and queues often form for entry into the Aedicule, especially during major pilgrimage seasons.[^cite_note-98][^cite_note-99]

According to their own family lore, the Muslim [Nuseibeh family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nusaybah_family) has been responsible for opening the door as a neutral party to the church's denominations already since the seventh century.[^cite_note-Nus-100][^cite_note-101][^cite_note-102] However, they themselves admit the documents held by various Christian denominations only mention their role since the 12th century in the time of Saladin, which is the more generally accepted date.[^cite_note-Nus-100][^cite_note-Harash-103][^cite_note-Sherwood-104] After [retaking Jerusalem from the Crusaders in 1187](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_\(1187\)), Saladin entrusted the Joudeh family with the key to the church, which is made of iron and 30 centimetres (12 in) long; the Nuseibehs either became or remained the doorkeepers.[^cite_note-Nus-100][^cite_note-Harash-103][^cite_note-Sherwood-104]

The '[immovable ladder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immovable_ladder)' stands beneath a window on the façade.

### Calvary (Golgotha)



The Altar of the Crucifixion, where The Rock of Calvary (bottom) is encased in glass



Chapel of Adam in the Church of the Holy Sepluchre

Just inside the church entrance is a stairway leading up to Calvary (Golgotha), traditionally regarded as the site of Jesus's crucifixion[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDK201698-105] and the most lavishly decorated part of the church. The exit is via another stairway opposite the first, leading down to the [ambulatory](#Ambulatory). Golgotha and its chapels are just south of the main altar of the [catholicon](#Catholicon).

Calvary is split into two chapels: one Greek Orthodox and one Catholic, each with its own altar. On the left (north) side, the Greek Orthodox chapel's altar is placed over the supposed rock of Calvary (the [12th Station](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Twelfth%20Station) of the Cross), which can be touched through a hole in the floor beneath the altar.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDK201698-105] The rock can be seen under protective glass on both sides of the altar. The softer surrounding stone was removed when the church was built.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDK201698-105] The Roman Catholic (Franciscan) Chapel of the Nailing to the Cross (the [11th Station](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Eleventh%20Station) of the Cross) stretches to the south. Between the Catholic Altar of the Nailing to the Cross and the Orthodox altar is the Catholic Altar of the [Stabat Mater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stabat_Mater),[^cite_note-106] which enshrines an 18th-century bust of the Virgin Mary with numerous *[ex-votos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ex-voto)*; this middle altar marks the [13th Station](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Thirteenth%20Station) of the Cross.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDK201698-105]

&#x20;On the ground floor, just underneath the Golgotha chapel, is the [Chapel of Adam](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Adam's%20Chapel).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDK201698-105] According to tradition, Jesus was crucified over the place where Adam's skull was buried.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDK201698-105] According to some, the blood of Christ ran down the cross and through the rocks to fill Adam's skull.[^cite_note-107] Through a window at the back of the 11th-century [apse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apse), the rock of Calvary can be seen with a crack traditionally held to be caused by the earthquake that followed Jesus's death;[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDK201698-105] some scholars claim it is the result of quarrying against a natural flaw in the rock.[^cite_note-Patriarchate-108]

Behind the Chapel of Adam is the [Greek Treasury](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Greek%20Treasury%20\(Church%20of%20the%20Holy%20Sepulchre\)) (Treasury of the Greek Patriarch). Some of its relics, such as a 12th-century crystal [mitre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitre), were transferred to the Greek Orthodox Patriarchate Museum (the Patriarchal Museum) on [Greek Orthodox Patriarchate Street](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Greek%20Orthodox%20Patriarchate%20Street).[^cite_note-109][^cite_note-110][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDK2016104-111]

### Stone of Unction



The Stone of Unction, where Jesus's body is said to have been anointed before burial

Just inside the entrance to the church is the Stone of Unction (also the Stone of Anointing or Stone of the Anointing), which tradition holds to be where Jesus's body was prepared for burial by [Joseph of Arimathea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_of_Arimathea), though this tradition is only attested since the Crusader era (notably by the Italian [Dominican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominican_Order) pilgrim [Riccoldo da Monte di Croce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riccoldo_da_Monte_di_Croce) in 1288), and the present stone was only added in the 1810 reconstruction.[^cite_note-Murphy56-12]

The wall behind the stone is defined by its striking blue balconies and [taphos symbol](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Taphos%20symbol)–bearing red banners (depicting the insignia of the [Brotherhood of the Holy Sepulchre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brotherhood_of_the_Holy_Sepulchre)), and is decorated with lamps. [The modern mosaic](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Mosaic%20at%20the%20Stone%20of%20Anointing) along the wall depicts the anointing of Jesus's body, preceded on the right by the [Descent from the Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descent_from_the_Cross), and succeeded on the left by the [Burial of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burial_of_Jesus).[^cite_note-\:0-112]

The wall was a temporary addition to support the arch above it, which had been weakened after the damage in the 1808 fire; it blocks the view of the rotunda, separates the entrance from the [catholicon](#Catholicon), sits on top of four of the now empty and desecrated [Crusader graves](#Royal_tombs)[^cite_note-Pringle39-113] and is no longer structurally necessary. Opinions differ as to whether it is to be seen as [the 13th Station of the Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stations_of_the_Cross), which others identify as the [lowering of Jesus from the cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descent_from_the_Cross) and located between the 11th and 12th stations on [Calvary](#Calvary).[^cite_note-\:0-112]

The lamps that hang over the Stone of Unction, adorned with cross-bearing chain links, are contributed by Armenians, [Copts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copts), Greeks and [Latins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Christianity).[^cite_note-\:0-112]

Immediately inside and to the left of the entrance is [a bench](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Divan%20\(Church%20of%20the%20Holy%20Sepulchre\)) (formerly a [divan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divan_\(furniture\)))[^cite_note-114] that has traditionally been used by the church's Muslim doorkeepers, along with some Christian clergy, as well as electrical wiring. To the right of the entrance is a wall along the [ambulatory](#Ambulatory) containing the staircase leading to [Golgotha](#Golgotha). Further along the same wall is the entrance to the [Chapel of Adam](#Chapel_of_Adam).

The [rotunda](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Rotunda%20of%20the%20Church%20of%20the%20Holy%20Sepulchre%20\(Jerusalem\)) is the building of the larger dome located on the far west side.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDK201694–97-91] In the centre of the rotunda is a small chapel called the [Aedicule](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Aedicule%20\(Holy%20Sepulchre\)) in English, from the Latin *[aedicula](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/aedicula#Latin)*, in reference to a small shrine.[^cite_note-115] The Aedicule has two rooms: the first has a pedestal with a relic called the Angel's Stone, which is believed to be a fragment of the large stone that sealed the tomb; the second, smaller room contains the tomb of Jesus. Possibly to prevent pilgrims from removing bits of the original rock as souvenirs, by 1555, a surface of marble cladding was placed on the tomb to prevent further damage to the tomb.[^cite_note-natgeo-34] In October 2016, the top slab of the burial bed was pulled back to reveal an older, partially broken marble slab with a Crusader-style cross carved in it. Beneath it, the limestone burial bed was revealed to be intact.[^cite_note-natgeo-34]

Under the [Status Quo](#Status_Quo), the [Eastern Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox), [Roman Catholic](606848.md), and [Armenian Apostolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Apostolic) Churches all have rights to the interior of the tomb, and all three communities celebrate the [Divine Liturgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Liturgy) or [Holy Mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_\(liturgy\)) there daily. It is also used for other ceremonies on special occasions, such as the [Holy Saturday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Saturday) ceremony of the [Holy Fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Fire) led by the Greek Orthodox patriarch (with the participation of the Coptic and Armenian patriarchs).[^cite_note-holyfire-116] To its rear, in the [Coptic Chapel](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Coptic%20Chapel), constructed of iron [latticework](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latticework), lies the altar used by the [Coptic Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_Orthodox_Church_of_Alexandria).[^cite_note-117] Historically, the [Georgians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_Orthodox) also retained the key to the Aedicule.[^cite_note-Janin-118][^cite_note-Desandoli-119][^cite_note-120]

To the right of the sepulchre on the northwestern edge of the rotunda is the [Chapel of the Apparition](#Franciscan_area_north_of_the_Aedicule), which is reserved for Roman Catholic use.[^cite_note-121]

Though not within the Church of the Holy Sepulchre compound, directly adjacent to it is the [Church of the Redeemer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_Redeemer,_Jerusalem), marking a [Lutheran](23371382.md) presence at the site.[^cite_note-Kroll2024-15]

### Catholicon



East end of the Greek Orthodox [catholicon](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:catholicon), with [its iconostasis](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:iconostasis%20of%20the%20catholicon%20of%20the%20Church%20of%20the%20Holy%20Sepulchre)

In the central [nave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nave) of the Crusader-era church, just east of the larger rotunda,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDK201694–97-91] is the Crusader structure housing the main altar of the Church, today the Greek Orthodox *[catholicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katholikon)*. Its dome is 19.8 metres (65 ft) in diameter,[^cite_note-cruise-72] and is set directly over the centre of the [transept crossing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossing_\(architecture\)) of the [choir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choir_\(architecture\)) where the *compas* is situated, an [omphalos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omphalos) ("navel") stone once thought to be the center of the world and still venerated as such by Orthodox Christians (associated with the site of the Crucifixion and the Resurrection).

Since 1996, the main dome is topped by the monumental Golgotha Crucifix, which then-Greek Patriarch [Diodoros I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diodoros_I) of Jerusalem had consecrated. It was the initiative of Israeli professor Gustav Kühnel to erect a new crucifix at the church that would not only be worthy of the singularity of the site, but also symbolise the efforts of unity among Christians.[^cite_note-hammers-122]

The catholicon's [iconostasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iconostasis) demarcates the Orthodox sanctuary behind it, to its east.[^cite_note-123] The iconostasis is flanked to the front by two episcopal thrones: the southern seat ([cathedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedra)) is the patriarchal throne of the [Greek Orthodox patriarch of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_patriarch_of_Jerusalem), and the northern seat is for an archbishop or bishop.[^cite_note-Dowling-124][^cite_note-Wright-125] There is also a popular claim that both are patriarchal thrones, with the northern one being for the [patriarch of Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_of_Antioch)[^cite_note-126] – this has since been dispelled.[^cite_note-Dowling-124]

### Armenian monastery south of the Aedicule

South of the [Aedicule](#Aedicule) is the "Place of the [Three Marys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Three_Marys)",[^cite_note-madainthreemary-127] marked by a stone canopy (the [Station of the Holy Women](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Station%20of%20the%20Holy%20Women)) and a large modern [wall mosaic](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Mosaic%20at%20the%20Station%20of%20the%20Holy%20Women). ### Syriac Chapel with Tomb of Joseph of Arimathea



Altar in the Syriac chapel

West of the Aedicule, to the rear of the Rotunda, is the [Syriac Chapel](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Chapel%20of%20the%20Syrians%20\(Holy%20Sepulchre,%20Jerusalem\)) with the Tomb of Joseph of Arimathea, located in a Constantinian apse and containing an opening to an ancient Jewish rock-cut tomb. This chapel is where the [Syriac Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox_Church) celebrate their Liturgy on Sundays.

The [Syriac Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox_Church) Chapel of [Saint Joseph of Arimathea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Joseph_of_Arimathea) and [Saint Nicodemus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Nicodemus). On Sundays and feast days it is furnished for the celebration of Mass. It is accessed from the Rotunda, by a door west of the Aedicule.

#### First-century tomb

On the far side of the chapel is the low entrance to an almost complete first-century Jewish tomb, initially holding six *[kokh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock-cut_tomb#Kokh)*-type funeral shafts radiating from a central chamber, two of which are still exposed. Although this space was discovered relatively recently[^cite_note-\:1-75] and contains no identifying marks, some believe that Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus were buried here.[^cite_note-128] Since Jews always buried their dead outside the city, the presence of this tomb seems to prove that the Holy Sepulchre site was indeed outside the city walls at the time of the Crucifixion.[^cite_note-natgeo-34][^cite_note-129]

### Prison of Christ



Prison of Christ before renovation

In the northeast side of the complex, there is the [Prison of Christ](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Prison%20of%20Christ%20in%20the%20Church%20of%20the%20Holy%20Sepulchre), alleged to be where Jesus was held.[^cite_note-135] The [Greek Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox) are showing pilgrims yet another place where Jesus was allegedly held, the similarly named Prison of Christ in their [Monastery of the Praetorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monastery_of_the_Praetorium?action=edit\&redlink=1), located near the [Church of Ecce Homo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convent_of_the_Sisters_of_Zion), between the Second and Third Stations of the [Via Dolorosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Dolorosa). ## Status Quo

### Ottoman decrees

Main article: [Status Quo (Jerusalem and Bethlehem)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Status_Quo_\(Jerusalem_and_Bethlehem\))



As a result of the [Status Quo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Status_Quo_\(Jerusalem_and_Bethlehem\)), the [Immovable Ladder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immovable_Ladder) placed before 1728 remains in place to this day.

[^cite_note-frozen-71]

An Ottoman decree of 1757 helped establish a *[status quo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Status_quo)* upholding the state of affairs for various Holy Land sites. The *status quo* was upheld in Sultan [Abdülmecid I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd%C3%BClmecid_I)'s *firman* (decree) of 1852/53, which pinned down the now-permanent statutes of property and the regulations concerning the roles of the different denominations and other custodians.[^cite_note-139]

The primary custodians are the [Roman Catholic](606848.md), [Greek Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox) and [Armenian Apostolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Apostolic_Church) churches. The Greek Orthodox act through the [Greek Orthodox Patriarchate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Patriarchate_of_Jerusalem) as well as through the [Brotherhood of the Holy Sepulchre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brotherhood_of_the_Holy_Sepulchre). Roman Catholics act through the [Franciscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franciscans) [Custody of the Holy Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Custody_of_the_Holy_Land). In the 19th century, the [Coptic Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_Orthodox_Church_of_Alexandria), the [Ethiopian Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Orthodox_Tewahedo_Church) and the [Syriac Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox_Church) also acquired lesser responsibilities, which include shrines and other structures in and around the building.

None of these controls the main entrance. In 1192, [Saladin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saladin) assigned door-keeping responsibilities to the Muslim [Nusaybah family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nusaybah_family). The Joudeh al-Goudia (also *al-Ghodayya*) family were made custodians of the keys to the Holy Sepulchre by Saladin in 1187.[^cite_note-Sherwood-104][^cite_note-140] Despite occasional disagreements, religious services take place in the Church with regularity and coexistence is generally peaceful. An example of concord between the Church custodians is the full restoration of the Aedicule from 2016 to 2017.

### Interdenominational issues

The establishment of the modern Status Quo in 1853 did not halt controversy and occasional violence. In 1902, 18 friars were hospitalized and some monks were jailed after the Franciscans and Greeks disagreed over who could clean the lowest step of the [Chapel of the Franks](#Chapel_of_the_Franks). In the aftermath, the Greek patriarch, Franciscan [custos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Custody_of_the_Holy_Land), Ottoman governor and French [consul general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consul_general) signed a convention that both denominations could sweep it.[^cite_note-141] On a hot summer day in 2002, a [Coptic monk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_monasticism) moved his chair from its agreed spot into the shade. This was interpreted as a hostile move by the Ethiopians and eleven were hospitalized after the resulting fight.[^cite_note-142] In another incident in 2004, during Orthodox celebrations of the [Exaltation of the Holy Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feast_of_the_Cross), a door to the Franciscan Chapel was left open. This was taken as a sign of disrespect by the Orthodox and a fistfight broke out. Some people were arrested, but no one was seriously injured.[^cite_note-143]

On [Palm Sunday](24178.md) in April 2008, a brawl broke out when a Greek monk was ejected from the building by a rival faction. Police were called to the scene but were also attacked by the enraged brawlers.[^cite_note-144] On Sunday, 9 November 2008, a clash erupted between Armenian and Greek monks during celebrations for the [Feast of the Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feast_of_the_Cross).[^cite_note-145][^cite_note-146]

## Issues with Israeli authorities

### Tax and land disputes

In February 2018, the church was closed following a tax dispute over €152,000,000 of uncollected taxes on church properties. The Jerusalem city hall stressed that the Church of the Holy Sepulchre and all other churches are exempt from the taxes, with the changes only affecting establishments like "hotels, halls and businesses" owned by the churches.[^cite_note-i24-147]

There was a lock-in protest against an Israeli legislative proposal which would expropriate church lands that had been sold to private companies since 2010, a measure which church leaders assert constitutes a serious violation of their property rights and the Status Quo. In a joint official statement the church authorities protested what they considered to be the peak of a systematic campaign in:

> a discriminatory and racist bill that targets solely the properties of the Christian community in the Holy Land ... This reminds us all of laws of a similar nature which were enacted against the Jews during dark periods in Europe.[^cite_note-148]

The 2018 taxation affair does not cover any church buildings or religious related facilities (because they are exempt by law),[^cite_note-Notredame-149] but commercial facilities such as the Notre Dame Hotel which was not paying the [municipal property tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rates_\(tax\)#Israel), and any land which is owned and used as a commercial land.[^cite_note-Notredame-149] The church holds the rights to land where private homes have been constructed, and some of the disagreement had been raised after the [Knesset](74656.md) had proposed a bill that will make it harder for a private company not to extend a lease for land used by homeowners.[^cite_note-jpost2-150] The church leaders have said that such a bill will make it harder for them to sell church-owned lands. According to *[The Jerusalem Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jerusalem_Post)*:

> The stated aim of the bill is to protect homeowners against the possibility that private companies will not extend their leases of land on which their houses or apartments stand.[^cite_note-jpost2-150]

### Land sale to Israeli settlers

In 2017, [NPR](29697232.md) reported that the Greek Orthodox Church owns many properties across Jerusalem, Israel and the [West Bank](33209.md). It owns some 30% of the land in the [Old City](302972.md). But, due to property sales, these properties are diminishing every year. The decision to sell is made by Greek leaders, even though most local followers of the church are [Arab Palestinians](23267.md). The [Palestinian Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestinian_Christians) have raised concerns about selling these properties to [Israeli settler](15123.md)-affiliated organizations.[^cite_note-151]

In June 2019, a number of Christian denominations in Jerusalem raised their voice against the [Supreme Court](1666510.md)'s decision to uphold the sale of three properties by the [Greek Orthodox Patriarchate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Patriarchate_of_Jerusalem) to [Ateret Cohanim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ateret_Cohanim) – an organization that seeks to increase the number of Jews living in the Old City and East Jerusalem. The church leaders warned that if the organization gets to control the sites, Christians could lose access to the Church of the Holy Sepulchre.[^cite_note-152] In June 2022, the Supreme Court upheld the sale and ended the legal battle.[^cite_note-153]

### Preventing Palm Sunday celebrations

On 29 March 2026, the [Latin Patriarch](231524.md), [Cardinal](6221.md) [Pierbattista Pizzaballa](54051059.md), and the [custos of the Holy Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Custody_of_the_Holy_Land), [Fr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friar) Francesco Ielpo, were en route to the Church of the Holy Sepulchre without a procession when Israeli authorities stopped them from entering and thereby preventing the celebration of [Palm Sunday](24178.md).[^cite_note-154][^cite_note-155] The Patriarchate later released a joint [press release](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Press_release) calling the incident "a grave precedent" which disrespects the [Status Quo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Status_Quo_\(Jerusalem_and_Bethlehem\)).[^cite_note-156]

The dispute stemmed from a safety measure that prohibited all religious gatherings of more than 50 people at Old City holy sites including the [Western Wall](75225.md), Church of the Holy Sepulchre, and the [Dome of the Rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dome_of_the_Rock). The safety precautions were instituted as a result of a missile strike days earlier due to the [ongoing war with Iran](82533582.md).&#x20;

This event drew criticism from Italian Prime Minister [Giorgia Meloni](29228976.md) and the Spanish Prime Minister [Pedro Sanchez](43297512.md) who both called it an attack against [religious freedoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_freedoms),[^cite_note-\:2-157][^cite_note-158] from French President [Emmanuel Macron](43671127.md) who likewise condemned the move and said it came in the context of "the worrying increase in violations of the status of the Holy Places in Jerusalem",[^cite_note-159] and from U.S. Ambassador to Israel [Mike Huckabee](301237.md) who called the incident "an unfortunate overreach already having major repercussions around the world".[^cite_note-160] The Spanish Foreign Affairs Minister [Jose Manuel Albares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Manuel_Albares) has publicly said that this event "cannot happen again". Both the Italian and Spanish Prime Minister summoned their respective country's Israeli representative to answer for the incident, namely [Jonathan Peled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Peled) and [Dana Erlich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dana_Erlich).[^cite_note-\:2-157][^cite_note-161]

The [Israel Police](745595.md) later issued a statement confirming they have approved a limited prayer arrangement at the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in coordination with a representative of the Latin Patriarch. Furthermore, Israeli officials, including Prime Minister [Benjamin Netanyahu](56472.md), later clarified that a small contingent of Catholic leaders should have been allowed to enter the Church despite the safety concerns, and that the barring of their entry was “unfortunate.”[^cite_note-162]

## Connection to Roman temple



Jerusalem after being rebuilt by Hadrian. Two main [east–west roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decumanus_\(Roman_city\)) were built rather than the typical one, due to the awkward location of the Temple Mount, blocking the central east–west route.

The site of the church had been a temple to Jupiter or Venus built by Hadrian before Constantine's edifice was built. Hadrian's temple had been located there because it was the junction of the [main north–south road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardo) with one of the two [main east–west roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decumanus_Maximus) and directly adjacent to the [forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forum_\(Roman\)) (now the location of the [Muristan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muristan), which is smaller than the former forum). The forum itself had been placed, as is traditional in Roman towns, at the junction of the main north–south road with the other main east–west road (which is now El-Bazar/David Street). The temple and forum together took up the entire space between the two main east–west roads (a few above-ground remains of the east end of the temple precinct still survive in the Alexander Nevsky Church complex of the Russian Mission in Exile).[^cite_note-163]

From the archaeological excavations in the 1970s, it is clear that construction took over most of the site of the earlier temple enclosure and that the *Triportico* and *Rotunda* roughly overlapped with the temple building itself; the excavations indicate that the temple extended at least as far back as the Aedicule, and the temple enclosure would have reached back slightly further. [Virgilio Canio Corbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgilio_Canio_Corbo), a Franciscan priest and archaeologist, who was present at the excavations, estimated from the archaeological evidence that the western retaining wall of the temple itself would have passed extremely close to the east side of the supposed tomb; if the wall had been any further west any tomb would have been crushed under the weight of the wall (which would be immediately above it) if it had not already been destroyed when foundations for the wall were made.[^cite_note-Corbo-25]

Other archaeologists have criticized Corbo's reconstructions. [Dan Bahat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Bahat), the former city archaeologist of Jerusalem, regards them as unsatisfactory, as there is no known temple of [Aphrodite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphrodite) (Venus) matching Corbo's design, and no archaeological evidence for Corbo's suggestion that the temple building was on a platform raised high enough to avoid including anything sited where the Aedicule is now; indeed Bahat notes that many temples to Aphrodite have a rotunda-like design, and argues that there is no archaeological reason to assume that the present rotunda was not based on a rotunda in the temple previously on the site.[^cite_note-BahatBAR-164]

## Location



1842 lithograph after [David Roberts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Roberts_\(painter\)), in *[The Holy Land, Syria, Idumea, Arabia, Egypt, and Nubia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holy_Land,_Syria,_Idumea,_Arabia,_Egypt,_and_Nubia)*

The New Testament describes Jesus's tomb as being outside the city wall,[^cite_note-165] as was normal for burials across the ancient world, which were regarded as unclean.[^cite_note-166][^cite_note-167] Today, the site of the Church is within the current walls of the old city of Jerusalem. It has been well documented by archaeologists that in the time of Jesus, the walled city was smaller and the wall then was to the east of the current site of the Church.[^cite_note-168] In other words, the city had been much narrower in Jesus's time, with the site then having been outside the walls; since [Herod Agrippa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_Agrippa) (41–44) is recorded by history as extending the city to the north (beyond the present northern walls), the required repositioning of the western wall is traditionally attributed to him as well.[^cite_note-169][^cite_note-170]

A recent archeological excavation conducted by Professor Francesca Romana Stasolla, found remains of olive trees and grapevines that are approximately 2,000 years old. These plant remnants, seeds and pollen, were discovered via [archaeobotanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoethnobotany) analysis performed on soil samples that had been extracted from beneath the stone floor of the church.[^cite_note-171]

The area immediately to the south and east of the sepulchre was a quarry and outside the city during the early first century as excavations under the Lutheran Church of the Redeemer across the street demonstrated.[^cite_note-\:1-75]

The church is a part of the [UNESCO World Heritage Site](44940.md) [Old City of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_City_\(Jerusalem\)).

The Christian Quarter and the [Armenian Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Quarter) are both located in the northwestern and western part of the Old City, due to the fact that the Holy Sepulchre is located close to the northwestern corner of the walled city. The adjacent neighbourhood within the Christian Quarter is called the [Muristan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muristan), a term derived from the Persian word for hospital, as Christian pilgrim hospices have been maintained in this area near the Holy Sepulchre since at least the time of [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne).

## Influence

From the ninth century onward, the construction of churches inspired by the Anastasis was extended across Europe.[^cite_note-SantoStefano-172] One example is [Santo Stefano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santo_Stefano_\(Bologna\)) in [Bologna](21069333.md), Italy, an agglomeration of seven churches recreating shrines of Jerusalem.[^cite_note-173]

Several churches and monasteries in Europe, for instance, in Germany and Russia, and at least one church in the United States have been wholly or partially modelled on the Church of the Resurrection, some even reproducing other [holy places](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_places) for the benefit of pilgrims who could not travel to the Holy Land. They include the [Heiliges Grab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heiliges_Grab_\(G%C3%B6rlitz\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) ("Holy Tomb") of [Görlitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6rlitz), constructed between 1481 and 1504, the [New Jerusalem Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jerusalem_Monastery) in [Moscow Oblast](372315.md), constructed by [Patriarch Nikon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_Nikon) between 1656 and 1666, and [Mount St. Sepulchre Franciscan Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_St._Sepulchre_Franciscan_Monastery) built by the Franciscans in [Washington, D.C.](108956.md) in 1898.[^cite_note-174]

Author Andrew Holt writes that the church was the most important in all [Christendom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christendom) at the time of the Crusades.[^cite_note-175]
