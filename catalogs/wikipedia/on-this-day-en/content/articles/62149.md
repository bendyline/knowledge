# Custard

**Custard** is a variety of culinary preparations based on sweetened [milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milk), [cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheese), or [cream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cream) cooked with [egg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eggs_as_food) or [egg yolk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egg_yolk) to thicken it, and sometimes also [flour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flour), [corn starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_starch), or [gelatin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelatin). Depending on the recipe, custard may vary in consistency from a thin pouring sauce (*[crème anglaise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cr%C3%A8me_anglaise)*) to the thick **pastry cream** (**_crème pâtissière_**) used to fill [éclairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89clair). The most common custards are used in [custard desserts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_custard_desserts) or [dessert sauces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dessert_sauce) and typically include sugar and [vanilla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanilla); however, [savory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umami) custards are also found, e.g., in [quiche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quiche).

## Preparation

Custard is usually cooked in a double boiler (*[bain-marie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bain-marie)*), or heated very gently in a [saucepan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saucepan) on a stove, though custard can also be [steamed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steaming), baked in the oven with or without a water bath, or even cooked in a [pressure cooker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_cooking). Custard preparation is a delicate operation because a temperature increase of 3–6 °C (5.4–10.8 °F) leads to overcooking and [curdling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curdling). Generally, a fully cooked custard should not exceed 80 °C (176 °F); it begins setting at 70 °C (158 °F).[^cite_note-1] A *bain marie* water bath slows heat transfer and makes it easier to remove the custard from the oven before it curdles.[^cite_note-2] Adding a small amount of cornflour (known in Canada and the US as '[corn starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_starch)') to the egg-sugar mixture stabilises the resulting custard, allowing it to be cooked in a single pan as well as in a double-boiler. A *[sous-vide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sous-vide)* water bath may be used to precisely control temperature.

A formal custard preparation, garnished with raspberries

While custard may refer to a wide variety of thickened dishes, technically (and in [French cookery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_cuisine)) the word *custard* (*crème* or more precisely *crème moulée*, [\[kʁɛm mule\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/French)) refers only to an egg-thickened custard.

&#x20;When [starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starch) is added, the result is called 'pastry cream' ([French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language): *crème pâtissière*, pronounced [\[kʁɛm pɑtisjɛːʁ\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/French)) or confectioners' custard, made with a combination of milk or cream, egg yolks, fine sugar, flour or some other starch, and usually a flavoring such as vanilla, chocolate, or lemon. *Crème pâtissière* is a key ingredient in many French desserts, including *[mille-feuille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mille-feuille)* (or Napoleons) and filed tarts. It is also used in Italian pastry and sometimes in [Boston cream pie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_cream_pie). The starch prevents over-cooking the eggs, and sets the cream (usually requiring it to be beaten or whipped before use).



Pastry cream



Layers of a [trifle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trifle) showing the custard in between [cake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cake), fruit and [whipped cream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whipped_cream)

When [gelatin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelatin) is added, it is known as *crème anglaise collée* ([\[kʁɛm ɑ̃ɡlɛz kɔle\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/French)).&#x20;

In the United Kingdom, custard has various traditional recipes some thickened principally with cornflour (cornstarch) rather than the egg component, others involving regular flour; see [custard powder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Custard_powder).

A quiche is a savoury custard tart. Some kinds of [timbale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbale_\(food\)) or vegetable loaf are made of a custard base mixed with chopped savoury ingredients. *Custard royale* is a thick custard cut into decorative shapes and used to garnish [soup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soup), [stew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stew) or [broth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broth). In German, it is known as *Eierstich* and is used as a garnish in German wedding soup (*Hochzeitssuppe*).[^cite_note-mcgavin_eierstich-3] *[Chawanmushi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chawanmushi)* is a [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_cuisine) savoury custard, steamed and served in a small bowl or on a saucer. [Chinese steamed egg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_steamed_egg) is a similar but larger savoury egg dish. *[Bougatsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bougatsa)* is a Greek breakfast [pastry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastry) whose sweet version consists of [semolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semolina) custard filling between layers of [phyllo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phyllo). In Peru, *leche asada* ('baked milk') is custard baked in individual molds.[^cite_note-Elichondo1997-4] It is considered a restaurant dish.[^cite_note-CuadraEscardo2013-5]

## History



Custard tarts

Custards baked in pastry ([custard tarts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Custard_tart)) were very popular in the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), and are the origin of the English word 'custard': the French term *[croustade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croustade)* originally referred to the crust of a tart,[^cite_note-6] and is derived from the Italian word *[crostata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crostata)*, and ultimately the Latin *crustāre*.[^cite_note-7]

Examples include *Crustardes of flessh* and *Crustade*, in the 14th century English collection *[The Forme of Cury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Forme_of_Cury)*. These recipes include solid ingredients such as meat, fish, and fruit bound by the custard.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-AustinT-9] Stirred custards cooked in pots are also found under the names *Creme Boylede* and *Creme boiled*.[^cite_note-AustinT-9] Some custards especially in the [Elizabethan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabethan) era used [marigold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendula) (*calendula*) to give the custard color.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-rodale-11]

In modern times, the name 'custard' is sometimes applied to starch-thickened preparations like [blancmange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blancmange) and [Bird's Custard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird's_Custard) powder.

## Chemistry

Stirred custard is thickened by [coagulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coagulation) of egg protein, while the same gives baked custard its gel structure. The type of milk used also impacts the result. Most important to a successfully stirred custard is to avoid excessive heat that will cause over-coagulation and [syneresis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syneresis_\(chemistry\)) that will result in a [curdled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curdled) custard.[^cite_note-12]

Eggs contain the proteins necessary for the gel structure to form, and emulsifiers to maintain the structure. Egg yolk also contains enzymes like amylase, which can break down added starch.[^cite_note-\:0-13] This enzyme activity contributes to the overall thinning of custard in the mouth. Egg yolk lecithin also helps to maintain the milk-egg interface. The proteins in egg whites are set at 60–80 °C (140–176 °F).[^cite_note-14]

Starch is sometimes added to custard to prevent premature curdling. The starch acts as a heat buffer in the mixture: as they hydrate, they absorb heat and help maintain a constant rate of heat transfer. Starches also make for a smoother texture and thicker mouth feel.[^cite_note-\:0-13]

If the mixture pH is 9 or higher, the gel is too hard; if it is below 5, the gel structure has difficulty forming because [protonation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protonation) prevents the formation of [covalent bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covalent_bond).[^cite_note-15]

## Physical-chemical properties

Cooked (set) custard is a weak [gel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gel), viscous, and [thixotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thixotropic); while it does become easier to stir the more it is manipulated, it does not, unlike many other thixotropic liquids, recover its lost viscosity over time.[^cite_note-16] On the other hand, a [suspension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspension_\(chemistry\)) of uncooked imitation custard powder ([starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starch)) in water, with the proper proportions, has the opposite [rheological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rheology) property: it is negative thixotropic, or [dilatant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilatant), allowing the demonstration of "walking on custard".[^cite_note-17]
