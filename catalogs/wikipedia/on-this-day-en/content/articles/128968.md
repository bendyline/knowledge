**Miami International Airport** ([IATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IATA_airport_code): **MIA**, [ICAO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ICAO_airport_code): **KMIA**, [FAA](11186.md) [LID](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Location_identifier): **MIA**), also known as **MIA** and historically as **Wilcox Field**, is the primary [international airport](1519705.md) serving [Miami](53846.md) and its [surrounding metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_metropolitan_area) in the U.S. state of [Florida](18933066.md). It hosts over 1,000 daily flights to 195 domestic and international destinations, including most countries in Central and South America and the Caribbean. The airport is in an [unincorporated area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unincorporated_area) in [Miami-Dade County, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_County,_Florida),[^cite_note-Censusmap-3] It is located approximately 6 miles (9.7 km) west-northwest of [downtown Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Downtown_Miami),[^cite_note-FAA-1] adjacent to the cities of Miami and [Miami Springs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Springs), and the village of [Virginia Gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_Gardens). Nearby cities include [Hialeah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hialeah), [Doral](109422.md), and the [census-designated place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census-designated_place) of [Fontainebleau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fontainebleau,_Florida).

In 2021, Miami International Airport became the [busiest international cargo airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_busiest_airports_in_the_United_States) in the U.S.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6] and the busiest U.S. gateway for international passengers, surpassing [John F. Kennedy International Airport](97218.md) in New York City.[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8] As of 2021, it is the [10th busiest airport in the U.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_busiest_airports_in_the_United_States) with 17,500,096 passengers for the year. It is [Florida](18933066.md)'s busiest airport by total aircraft operations and total cargo traffic.[^cite_note-9] The airport is [American Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Airlines)' third-largest hub and serves as its primary gateway to Latin America and the [Caribbean](18956035.md). Miami also serves as a focus city for [Avianca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avianca), [Frontier Airlines](163354.md), and [LATAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LATAM_Airlines_Group), both for passengers and cargo operations.

In 2024, MIA Airport served nearly 56 million passengers and saw 3 million tons of cargo passing through MIA, recording three consecutive record years for passenger volume and five straight years of cargo volume.[^cite_note-10]

Miami International Airport covers 3,300 acres (1,300 ha).[^cite_note-FAA-1][^cite_note-11] It is [South Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Florida)'s main airport for long-haul international flights and a hub for the [Southeastern United States](816925.md) with passenger and cargo flights to cities throughout the Americas, Europe, Africa, and Asia. It is the largest gateway between the U.S. and Central, South America and the Caribbean and one of the largest airline hubs in the nation.

## History

See also: [Miami Army Airfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Army_Airfield)

Crowds gather around [Pan American Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PanAm)' new international passenger terminal, the first U.S. mainland port of entry for visitors arriving in the U.S. via air, during the terminal's opening on January 9, 1929.

Aerial view of Pan American Airport and Pan American Field, Miami, Florida 1929.

Aerial View of Pan American Field and Pan American Airport on NW 36th Street in [Miami](53846.md) in 1929. The airport terminal with domed roof is located at center, flanked by two hangars to the east and one to the west. Only the west hangar, Pan Am Hangar 5, located at its original site off NW 36th Street, remains in existence.

In June 1928, [Pan American Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PanAm) acquired 116 acres of land on NW 36th Street for the purpose of building a privately owned and operated international airport in Miami, Florida. The establishment of a commercial airport and of regularly scheduled international passenger airline service by Pan Am was a transformative event for the City of Miami. By September 1928, Pan Am had begun to operate regularly scheduled Air Mail service between Miami and Havana. On January 9, 1929, Pan American Airport, also known as Pan American Field, was officially dedicated at a ceremony that included the inauguration of a newly expanded route system to Nassau, Santiago (Cuba), Haiti, the Dominican Republic and San Juan, Puerto Rico. The ceremony was attended by thousands of residents, government officials, and aviation celebrities, who saw in the new airport the promise of a bright new future in international aviation for Miami. It was the first mainland airport in the United States to have international [port of entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_entry) facilities. The passenger terminal building, designed by Delano & Aldrich of New York City, was the most advanced and luxurious in the country. Three hangars, two on the east and one on the west, provided housing and maintenance facilities for Pan Am's fleet of Sikorsky amphibian and Fokker aircraft.

During the first two years of its operation, it was from this busy airport that Pan American Airways pioneered U.S. international passenger aviation. By the end of 1930, for the first time in history, Pan Am had succeeded in establishing regularly scheduled Air Mail and passenger airline service from the U.S. to the West Indies, Caribbean, and Central and South America.&#x20;

On April 1, 1930, a collaboration between Pan American and Eastern Air Transport (later to become known as [Eastern Air Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Air_Lines)) was begun after Pan American Airport at NW 36th Street was designated as the first international field Post Office in the country. The announcement followed almost a year’s worth of efforts by the U.S. postal service to effect greater efficiencies in U.S. Air Mail delivery by coordinating the schedules of airlines involved in operating international and domestic Air Mail routes with railway connections to cities not served by airports.&#x20;

On January 1, 1931, Eastern Air Transport inaugurated regularly scheduled passenger service from Miami to New York from Pan Am’s NW 36th Street Airport. It was another historic event, a collaboration between the two airlines that made Miami the clearing point for a coordinated airline passenger system that extended from as far north as Canada to the tip of South America. No other U.S. city could boast such a distinction.&#x20;

[Eastern Air Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Air_Lines) officially took up primary residence at the 36th Street Airport in August 1935.

In 1940, Intercontinent Corporation, owned by William Pawley, built an aircraft manufacturing plant on land acquired immediately east of Pan American Field. The City and County, eager to encourage the growth of an aircraft manufacturing industry in Miami, agreed to finance and build runways and ground facilities at the Intercontinent plant, including an east–west runway that extended from Le Jeune Road as far west as Pan American Field, where it intersected with Pan Am's east–west runway. [National Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Airlines_\(1934%E2%80%931980\)), which had been operating in Miami at Miami's city-owned Municipal Airport since 1937, moved to the 36th Street Airport in 1942. National built a combined maintenance and terminal facility on LeJeune Road, across the street from the airport and would stop traffic on the road in order to taxi aircraft to and from its terminal. [Miami Army Airfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Army_Airfield) opened in 1943 on 1400 acres of land acquired during [World War II](32927.md) to the south of Pan American Field. The two airfields were listed in some directories as a single facility.[^cite_note-12]

Following [World War II](32927.md), the Dade County Port Authority embarked on a long-planned airport expansion in order to meet Miami's increasing commercial aviation needs. On December 31, 1945, a formal agreement between the County and Pan Am transferring ownership of the airport to the county was signed, becoming effective at 1\:00 AM on January 1, 1946. Thus ended any distinct identity of Pan American Field. The Port Authority also acquired Intercontinent's former holdings, which were subsequently leased to Eastern Air Lines, and changed the name of the newly expanded airport to Miami International Airport. Pan American's former NW 36th Street terminal building continued to serve as the hub for the new Miami International Airport. Between 1945 and 1950, the Port Authority cobbled together thousands more acres adjacent to and south of the airport, including the Army's former air base; the Seaboard Air Line Railroad property; and additional parcels, with the intention of meeting Miami's future aviation needs. The result was a County-owned, Miami International Airport based at NW 36th Street that by 1948 had grown to 2500 acres. The former domed-roofed Pan Am terminal building was extensively remodeled and enlarged, the words “Miami International Airport” now curving across its façade. The new airport was officially dedicated January 4, 1950. [United States Air Force Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Air_Force_Reserve) troop carrier and rescue squadrons also operated from the airport from 1949 through 1959, when the last unit relocated to nearby [Homestead Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestead_Air_Force_Base) (now [Homestead Air Reserve Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestead_Air_Reserve_Base)). In the late 1940s, Pan Am and Eastern also expanded their bases at MIA on NW 36th Street, which made the airport the world's largest commercial aircraft maintenance and overhaul facility at the time.[^cite_note-\:0-13]



20th Street Terminal in the 1950s

In the 1950s, a continuing boom in post-[World War II](32927.md) passenger aviation stretched the county's expanded airport to capacity. Scheduled airlines had outstripped ships, trains and buses to become the state's as well as the nation's largest carriers of interstate and international traffic. Delta had joined Pan Am, Eastern and National to become MIA's "Big Four" carriers and the airport also served a host of smaller scheduled and non-scheduled airlines. A new jet age loomed. Plans for an entirely new airport, to be built from scratch on land south and east of the existing facility were set in motion. On February 1, 1959, after years of planning and construction, Miami's brand-new Miami International Airport was formally inaugurated at 20th Street, on what was dubbed Wilcox Field in honor of Port Authority attorney [J. Mark Wilcox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Mark_Wilcox) who had been instrumental in bringing the project to completion. No longer needed, the former domed-roofed terminal building on NW 36th Street was torn down in November 1962. This part of MIA along NW 36th Street is known today as MIA's north field.

When it was dedicated in 1959, MIA's new 20th Street Terminal was the largest central airport terminal in the world, with five concourses (Concourses C-G) and a 270-room hotel. In 1961, the terminal was expanded with the addition of a sixth concourse (Concourse H) on the south side, which was the first concourse at the airport to include jetways. By 1965, the original five concourses were renovated with jetways added to them.[^cite_note-history1-14]

The 20th Street Terminal was expanded in the 1970s. Parking garages were added just east of the terminal and Concourse B opened on the north side of the terminal in 1973 to accommodate the expansion of Eastern Air Lines.[^cite_note-15] In 1977, Concourse E's satellite terminal opened. The satellite was originally connected with shuttle buses, though a [people mover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIA_e_Train) was built to connect the satellite in 1980.[^cite_note-history1-14]

Nonstop flights to [Chicago](6886.md) and [Newark](248492.md#Early_years) started in late 1946, but nonstops didn't reach west beyond [St. Louis](27687.md) and [New Orleans](53842.md) until January 1962. Nonstop transatlantic flights to Europe began in 1970. In the late 1970s and early 1980s, [Air Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Florida) had a hub at MIA, with a nonstop flight to [London](17867.md), England which it acquired from National upon the latter's merger with Pan Am. Air Florida ceased operations in 1982 after the crash of [Air Florida Flight 90](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Florida_Flight_90).[^cite_note-petzinger-16] [British Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Airways) flew a [Concorde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concorde) SST (supersonic transport) triweekly between Miami and London via [Dulles International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulles_International_Airport) in [Washington, D.C.](108956.md), from 1984 to 1991.[^cite_note-17]

The terminal was further expanded in the 1980s. The original Concourses D and E were rebuilt early in the decade and Concourses B and F were expanded.[^cite_note-history2-18] Pedestrian bridges with moving walkways were built in 1985 connecting the parking garages with the third level of the terminal. Within the next few years, the moving walkway system on the third level was expanded to run along the full length of the terminal.[^cite_note-19]

After former [Apollo 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_8) astronaut [Frank Borman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Borman) became president of [Eastern Air Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Air_Lines) in 1975, he moved Eastern's headquarters from [Rockefeller Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_Center) in [New York City](645042.md) to Building 16 in the northeast corner of MIA, Eastern's maintenance base. Eastern remained one of the largest employers in the Miami metropolitan area until ongoing labor union unrest, coupled with the airline's acquisition by Texas Air in 1986, ultimately forced the airline into bankruptcy in 1989.[^cite_note-petzinger-16] Eastern operated out of Concourses B through D on the north side of the terminal, where American's Concourse D stands today.[^cite_note-\:1-20][^cite_note-21] Concourse E was the home for most international carriers, while Pan Am operated out of Concourses E and F.[^cite_note-\:1-20][^cite_note-22]

Starting in 2026, MIA became a hub for soccer fans for two reasons: first, the nearby [Nu Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nu_Stadium) of [Major League Soccer](71802.md)'s [Inter Miami CF](41853326.md) has opened; connected to the airport via the [MIA Mover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIA_Mover). Secondly, it was a main transportation point for fans for events specific to the [2026 FIFA World Cup](36581929.md) in South Florida (and for connecting flights to other host cities), and the seven games that were hosted at [Hard Rock Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_Rock_Stadium), roughly 18 miles north of the airport.[^cite_note-23]

### American Airlines hub



[American Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Airlines) planes at Concourse D in January 2026

Amid Eastern's turmoil, [American Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Airlines) CEO [Robert Crandall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Crandall) sought a new hub in order to utilize new aircraft which AA had on order. AA studies indicated that [Delta Air Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Air_Lines) would provide strong competition on most routes from Eastern's hub at [Hartsfield–Jackson Atlanta International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartsfield%E2%80%93Jackson_Atlanta_International_Airport) in [Atlanta](3138.md), but that MIA had many key routes only served by Eastern. American Airlines announced that it would establish a base at MIA in August 1988. Lorenzo considered selling Eastern's profitable [Latin America](18524.md) routes to AA as part of a [Chapter 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapter_11) reorganization of Eastern in early 1989 but backed out in a last-ditch effort to rebuild the MIA hub. The effort quickly proved futile, and American Airlines purchased the routes (including the route authority between Miami and London then held by Eastern sister company [Continental Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Airlines)) in a liquidation of Eastern which was completed in 1990.[^cite_note-petzinger-16] Later in the 1990s, American transferred more employees and equipment to MIA from its failed domestic hubs at [Nashville, Tennessee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nashville_International_Airport), and [Raleigh–Durham, North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raleigh-Durham_International_Airport). The hub grew from 34 daily departures in 1989 to 157 in 1990, 190 in 1992, and a peak of 301 in 1995, including long-haul flights to Europe and [South America](26769.md).[^cite_note-24] Today Miami is American's largest air freight hub and is the main connecting point in the airline's north–south international route network.

In December 1992, [South African Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_African_Airways) launched flights to Johannesburg via Cape Town using a Boeing 747.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26] The company's [codeshare agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codeshare_agreement) with American Airlines supported the route. The carrier later decided to codeshare with Delta Air Lines instead, which operated a hub in Atlanta. Consequently, South African replaced its Miami service with a flight to Atlanta in January 2000.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28]

Concourse A was built on the northeast side of the terminal in 1995, and Concourse H was rebuilt in 1997. Concourse J was built in August 2007 along with an expansion of the terminal on the south side.[^cite_note-history2-18]

American began the development of the current North Terminal in the 1990s. Concourses B and C were demolished as part of the project with Concourse A becoming the eastern end of the expanded Concourse D. Although the terminal was originally scheduled to be completed in 2004, numerous delays arose in the construction process, and Miami-Dade County took over control of the project in 2005, at which time the project had a budget of \$2.85 billion.[^cite_note-29] The terminal was ultimately completed in 2011 and included [Skytrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skytrain_\(Miami_International_Airport\)), an automated people mover system, as well as a wing for [American Eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Eagle_\(airline_brand\)) commuter flights.[^cite_note-30]

### Other hub operations

[Pan Am](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_Am) was acquired by [Delta Air Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Air_Lines) in 1991, but filed for bankruptcy shortly thereafter. Its remaining international routes from Miami to Europe and Latin America were sold to [United Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Airlines) for \$135 million as part of Pan Am's emergency liquidation that December.[^cite_note-petzinger-16] United's Latin American hub offered 24 daily departures in the summer of 1992, growing to 36 daily departures to 21 destinations in the summer of 1994, but returned to 24 daily departures in the summer of 1995 and never expanded further.[^cite_note-\:2-31] United ended flights from Miami to South America, and shut down its Miami crew base, in May 2004, reallocating most Miami resources to its main hub in [O'Hare International Airport](248449.md) in Chicago.[^cite_note-32] United ceased all mainline service to Miami in 2005 with the introduction of its low-cost product [Ted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_\(airline\)).[^cite_note-\:2-31]

[Iberia](98613.md) also established a Miami hub in 1992, positioning a fleet of [DC-9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DC-9) aircraft at MIA to serve destinations in [Central America](6121.md) and the [Caribbean](18956035.md). The hub took advantage of rights granted under the 1991 bilateral aviation agreement between the United States and Spain.[^cite_note-33] During the 1990s, the airport had sterile international-to-international transit facilities in Concourse D (American, British, and Alitalia) and Concourse F (Iberia and four Central American carriers), and there were plans to establish a sterile corridor for international connecting passengers between six concourses.[^cite_note-34] However, the [September 11, 2001, attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_11,_2001,_attacks) made it necessary for many foreigners to obtain a visa in order to transit the United States, and as a result, Iberia closed its hub in 2004, around the same time as United.[^cite_note-35]

### Future

MIA is projected to process 77 million passengers and 4 million tons of freight annually by 2040.[^cite_note-36] To meet such a demand, the Miami-Dade Board of County Commissioners approved a \$5 billion improvement plan to take place over 15 years, concluding in 2035. The comprehensive plan includes concourse optimization, construction of two on-site luxury hotels, the demolition of Concourse G, and expansion of the airport's cargo capacity.[^cite_note-37]

### Terminals

Miami International Airport contains three terminals (North, Central, and South) and six concourses for a total of 131 gates.[^cite_note-MIAMap-38] With the exception of Concourse G, all concourses contain gates to access [U.S. Customs and Border Protection](757040.md) facilities.

- **Concourse D** contains 51 gates. Opened in 1959 as Concourse 5. Combined with the former Concourse A and newly built gates on the sites of former Concourses B & C as part of the North Terminal Development Program, with construction completed in March 2013.

  [^cite_note-MIAMap-38]

- **Concourse E** contains 18 gates. Opened in 1959 as Concourse 4 and rebuilt in the 1980s. The satellite terminal opened in 1977.

  [^cite_note-MIAMap-38]

- **Concourse F** contains 19 gates. Opened in 1959 as Concourse 3 and rebuilt in the 1980s.

  [^cite_note-MIAMap-38]

- **Concourse G** contains 14 gates. Opened in 1959 as Concourse 2.

  [^cite_note-MIAMap-38]

- **Concourse H** contains 13 gates. Opened in 1961 as Concourse 1. Partially rebuilt in the late 1990s with construction completed in March 1998

  [^cite_note-MIAMap-38]

- **Concourse J** contains 15 gates. Opened in August 2007.

  [^cite_note-MIAMap-38]



Concourse J in the South Terminal is MIA's newest passenger facility and has one gate that can accommodate the [Airbus A380](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A380).

American operates three Admirals Clubs and one Flagship Lounge across Concourses D & E.[^cite_note-MIAClub-39] Numerous other lounges exist across the airport as well, including an [American Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Express) Centurion Lounge located in Concourse D.[^cite_note-MIAClub-39][^cite_note-40] The North Terminal (Concourse D) is for the exclusive use of American Airlines. The Central Terminal (Concourses E, F, and G) has varied uses; Concourse E is mainly used by American and its [Oneworld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oneworld) partner airlines along with some Caribbean and Latin American airlines, and E's satellite terminal has a gate that can accommodate an [Airbus A380](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A380). Concourses F and G are used by non-AA domestic and Canadian carriers and flights. The South Terminal (Concourses H and J) is the main non-Oneworld international terminal. Concourse H is largely used by Delta and non-Oneworld international carriers that send narrowbody planes largely from Central and the northern parts of South America, and some widebody flights; and Concourse J is used by most non-Oneworld international carriers that send widebody planes and is the main terminal at MIA for non-Oneworld transcontinental flights. Concourse J also has one gate that can accommodate an A380.[^cite_note-41]

Miami International Airport offers the [MIA Mover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIA_Mover), a free people mover system to transfer passengers between MIA terminals and the [Miami Intermodal Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Intermodal_Center) (MIC) that opened to the public on September 9, 2011. The MIC provides direct access from the airport to ground transportation (shuttle/bus/rail) as well as the Rental Car Center. A [Metrorail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Metrorail) station opened at the MIC on July 28, 2012; a [Tri-Rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tri-Rail) station followed on April 5, 2015. Plans for [Amtrak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amtrak) to operate a station at the MIC were cancelled when it was discovered that the platform built for the service was too short for Amtrak trains. The platform now sits empty and closed, with no trains stopping at it.[^cite_note-42]

The [rental car center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Intermodal_Center#Rental_car_center) consolidates airport car rental operations at the MIC.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-43]

Miami International Airport has direct public transit service to [Miami-Dade Transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_Transit)'s [Metrorail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Metrorail) and [Metrobus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_Transit#Metrobus) networks; [Greyhound Bus Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greyhound_Bus_Lines) and to the [Tri-Rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tri-Rail) commuter rail system. [Metrorail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrorail_\(Miami\)) operates the Orange Line train from Miami International Airport to destinations such as [Downtown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Downtown_Miami), [Brickell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brickell), [Health District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_District_\(Miami\)), [Coconut Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coconut_Grove), [Coral Gables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral_Gables,_Florida), [Dadeland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dadeland), [Hialeah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hialeah), [South Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Miami), and [Wynwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wynwood). It takes approximately 15 minutes to get from the airport to Downtown.

[Miami-Dade Transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_Transit) operates an Airport Flyer bus that connects MIA directly to [South Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Beach).[^cite_note-44]

MIA is served directly by [Tri-Rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tri-Rail), Miami's [commuter rail](6286.md) system. The station opened on April 5, 2015. Tri-Rail connects MIA to northern Miami-Dade, [Broward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broward_County), and [Palm Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm_Beach_County) counties. Tri-Rail directly serves points north such as [Boca Raton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boca_Raton), [Deerfield Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deerfield_Beach), [Delray Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delray_Beach), [Fort Lauderdale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Lauderdale), [Hollywood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hollywood,_Florida), [Pompano Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompano_Beach) and [West Palm Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Palm_Beach).[^cite_note-45]

### Cargo yard

MIA has a number of air cargo facilities. The largest cargo complex is located on the west side of the airport, inside the triangle formed by Runways 12/30 and 9/27. Cargo carriers such as [LATAM Cargo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LATAM_Airlines_Group), [Atlas Air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_Air), [Amerijet International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amerijet_International), and [DHL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DHL_Aviation) operate from this area. The largest privately owned facility is the [Centurion Cargo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centurion_Cargo) complex in the northeast corner of the airport, with over 51,000 m<sup>2</sup> (550,000 sq ft) of warehouse space.[^cite_note-46] [FedEx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FedEx) and [UPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Parcel_Service) operate their own facilities in the northwest corner of the airport, off of 36th Street. In addition to its large passenger terminal in Concourse D, [American Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Airlines) operates a maintenance base to the east of Concourse D, centered around a semicircular hangar originally used by National Airlines which can accommodate three widebody aircraft.[^cite_note-47]

## Accidents and incidents



The aftermath of [RED Air Flight 203](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RED_Air_Flight_203) in June 2022

- On January 22, 1952, an Aerodex [Lockheed Model 18 Lodestar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Model_18_Lodestar) on a test flight crashed after takeoff due to engine failure; all 5 occupants were killed.

  [^cite_note-321]

- On August 4, 1952, a [Curtiss C-46 Commando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtiss_C-46_Commando) on a ferry flight crashed on approach to MIA because of the failure of the elevator control system; all 4 occupants died.

  [^cite_note-322]

- On March 25, 1958, [Braniff International Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braniff_International_Airways) Flight 971, a [Douglas DC-7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_DC-7) crashed 5 km (3.1 mi) WNW of MIA after attempting to return to the airport because of an engine fire crashing into an open marsh; 9 passengers out of 24 on board were killed.

  [^cite_note-323]

- On October 2, 1959, a [Vickers Viscount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vickers_Viscount) of [Cubana de Aviación](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubana_de_Aviaci%C3%B3n) was hijacked on a flight from Havana to [Antonio Maceo Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Maceo_Airport), [Santiago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santiago_de_Cuba) by three men demanding to be taken to the United States. The aircraft landed at Miami International Airport.

  [^cite_note-ASN021059-324]

- On February 12, 1963, [Northwest Airlines Flight 705](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Airlines_Flight_705), a [Boeing 720](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_720), crashed into the [Everglades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everglades) while en route from Miami to [Portland, Oregon](23503.md), via Chicago O'Hare, Spokane, and Seattle. All 43 passengers and crew died.

- On February 13, 1965, an [Aerolíneas de El Salvador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerol%C3%ADneas_de_El_Salvador) (AESA) [Curtiss C-46 Commando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtiss_C-46_Commando), a cargo flight, had an engine failure shortly after takeoff and crashed into an automobile junkyard, killing both occupants.

  [^cite_note-325]

- On March 5, 1965, a Fruehaf Inc. [Lockheed Model 18 Lodestar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Model_18_Lodestar) nosed down after takeoff due to elevator trim tab problems, and both occupants were killed.

  [^cite_note-326]

- On June 23, 1969, a [Dominicana de Aviación](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominicana_de_Aviaci%C3%B3n) [Aviation Traders Carvair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_Traders_Carvair), a modified DC-4, en route to Santo Domingo was circling back to Miami International Airport with an engine fire when it [crashed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominicana_de_Aviaci%C3%B3n_Flight_401) into buildings 1 mile (1.6 km) short of Runway 27. All 4 crewmembers aboard the Carvair and 6 on the ground were killed.

  [^cite_note-327]

- On April 14, 1970, an [Ecuatoriana de Aviacion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecuatoriana_de_Aviacion) [Douglas DC-7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_DC-7), a cargo flight, crashed after takeoff from MIA beyond the runway and slid 890 feet (270 m) before striking a concrete abutment; both occupants were killed.

  [^cite_note-328]

- On December 29, 1972, [Eastern Air Lines Flight 401](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Air_Lines_Flight_401), a [Lockheed L-1011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_L-1011), crashed into the [Everglades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everglades). The plane had left [JFK International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JFK_International_Airport) in New York City bound for Miami. There were 101 fatalities out of the 176 passengers and crew on board.

  [^cite_note-329]

  &#x20;(This accident is the subject of the movie *The Ghost of Flight 401*.)

- On June 21, 1973, a Warnaco Inc. [Douglas DC-7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_DC-7), a cargo flight, crashed into the Everglades six minutes after takeoff in heavy rain, wind, and lightning. All three occupants died.

  [^cite_note-330]

- On December 15, 1973, a [Lockheed L-1049 Super Constellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_L-1049_Super_Constellation) operated by Aircraft Pool Leasing Corp, a cargo flight, crashed 1.3 miles (2.1 km) E of MIA because of over-rotation of the aircraft causing a stall, crashing into a parking lot and several homes; all three occupants were killed, along with six on the ground.

  [^cite_note-331]

- On September 27, 1975, a [Canadair CL-44](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadair_CL-44) operated by Aerotransportes Entre Rios (AER), crashed after takeoff because of an external makeshift flight control lock on the right elevator, 4 crew and 2 passengers of the 10 on board died.

  [^cite_note-332]

- On January 15, 1977, a [Douglas DC-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_DC-3), registered as N73KW of [Air Sunshine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Sunshine) crashed shortly after take-off on a domestic scheduled passenger flight to [Key West International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_West_International_Airport), Florida. All 33 people on board survived.

  [^cite_note-ASN150177-333]

- On January 6, 1990, a Grecoair [Lockheed JetStar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_JetStar) crashed after aborting takeoff and exiting the runway, One occupant of the two on board died.

  [^cite_note-334]

- On May 11, 1996, [ValuJet Airlines Flight 592](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ValuJet_Airlines_Flight_592), a [McDonnell Douglas DC-9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonnell_Douglas_DC-9) crashed into the Everglades 10 minutes after taking off from MIA while en route to [Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartsfield-Jackson_Atlanta_International_Airport) after a fire broke out in the cargo hold, killing all 110 occupants on board.

- On August 7, 1997, [Fine Air Flight 101](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_Air_Flight_101), a [Douglas DC-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_DC-8) cargo plane, crashed onto NW 72nd Avenue less than a mile (1.6 km) from the airport. All four occupants on board and one person on the ground were killed.

- On November 20, 2000, [American Airlines Flight 1291](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Airlines_Flight_1291), an [Airbus A300](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A300) en route to [Port-au-Prince, Haiti](57391.md), returned to Miami following a [cabin depressurization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabin_depressurization). During the evacuation one of the emergency exit doors explosively opened, killing a flight attendant.

  [^cite_note-335]

- On July 1, 2002, two [America West Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/America_West_Airlines) pilots operating Flight 556 to Phoenix–Sky Harbor were ordered back to the gate by [air traffic control](48563.md) before takeoff after security agents notified supervisors that the men smelled of alcohol and became belligerent when they were told they were not allowed to take an open cup of coffee through the security checkpoint. [Breathalyzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breathalyzer) tests conducted after their removal from the aircraft revealed that both men had blood alcohol content in excess of the legal limit for operating a vehicle in Florida, and they were arrested by police. Investigators found that the men had been drinking at a local bar until 4\:40 am, roughly six hours before the flight was scheduled to depart. They were fired by the airline the next day, later stripped of their [pilot certificates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilot_licensing_and_certification), and convicted in 2005 of operating an aircraft while drunk.

  [^cite_note-336]

  [^cite_note-337]

- On December 7, 2005, passenger [Rigoberto Alpizar was killed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killing_of_Rigoberto_Alpizar) by [federal air marshals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Air_Marshal_Service) after frantically exiting an American Airlines flight to Orlando during boarding with a backpack strapped to his chest. The air marshals, who said they had heard Alpizar declare he had a bomb, confronted him in the [jetway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jetway) and shot him after he ignored their commands to stop moving and reached into the backpack. This was the first case of federal air marshals opening fire on a suspect after the [September 11 attacks](5058690.md).

  [^cite_note-338]

- On September 15, 2015, Qatar Airways Flight 778 to Doha overran Runway 9 during takeoff and collided with the [approach lights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approach_lighting_system) for Runway 27. The collision, which went unnoticed during the 13.5-hour flight, tore an 18-inch (46 cm) tear in the [pressure vessel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabin_pressurization#Mechanics) of the [Boeing 777-300ER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_777-300ER) aircraft just behind the rear cargo door. The crew had been confused by a printout from an onboard computer and erroneously began takeoff on Runway 9 at the intersection of Taxiway T1 rather than at the end of the runway, which trimmed roughly 1,370 m (4,490 ft) from the length of the runway available for takeoff.

  [^cite_note-339]

  [^cite_note-340]

- On June 21, 2022, [RED Air Flight 203](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RED_Air_Flight_203), a [McDonnell Douglas MD-82](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonnell_Douglas_MD-82) on a flight from [Las Américas International Airport](599919.md) with 130 passengers and 10 crew, skidded off Runway 9 on landing and collided with a small [glideslope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glideslope) equipment building, starting a fire in the right wing which was rapidly extinguished by firefighters. There were no fatalities, while 4 passengers sustained minor injuries. Crew and passengers reported the aircraft "shaking violently" after a seemingly routine landing, and skid marks on the runway showed that the left main [landing gear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_gear) had shimmied heavily before turning 90° outboard and collapsing. Airline records indicated that the left main landing gear shimmy damper had a history of hydraulic fluid leaks but had passed recent maintenance checks. The accident was attributed to ineffective shimmy dampening of the left main landing gear, but the reason for the poor dampening could not be verified, as damage from the crash "precluded evaluation of whether the damper was properly serviced."

  [^cite_note-341]

- On January 18, 2024, Atlas Air Flight 095, a cargo Boeing 747-87UF registered as N859GT, en route to [San Juan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Mu%C3%B1oz_Mar%C3%ADn_International_Airport), experienced an engine fire shortly after takeoff from Miami International Airport. The aircraft safely returned to the airport and made an emergency landing within 15 minutes of takeoff.

  [^cite_note-342]

- On September 6, 2026, [21 Air Flight 7598](84161986.md), an [Amazon Air](56149507.md) [Boeing 767-300ERF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_767-300ERF), operated by [21 Air](69248824.md), overran Runway 30 on landing. The plane struck multiple vehicles and over 60 units responded to the accident. 5 people died and another 5 were injured, including both pilots in the aircraft, though it is unclear who was onboard and who was around.

  [^cite_note-343]

  [^cite_note-344]
