An **explosion** is a rapid expansion in [volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume) of a given amount of matter associated with an extreme outward release of [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy), usually with the generation of high [temperatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperatures) and release of high-pressure [gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas). Explosions may also be generated by a slower expansion that would normally not be forceful, but is not allowed to expand, so that when whatever is containing the expansion is broken by the pressure that builds as the matter inside tries to expand, the matter expands forcefully. An example of this is a [volcanic eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcano) created by the expansion of magma in a magma chamber as it rises to the surface. [Supersonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersonic) explosions created by [high explosives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_explosive) are known as [detonations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detonation) and travel through [shock waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shock_wave). [Subsonic](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/subsonic) explosions are created by [low explosives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_explosive) through a slower [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion) process known as [deflagration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflagration).

## Causes

For an explosion to occur, there must be a rapid, forceful expansion of matter. There are numerous ways this can happen, both naturally and artificially, such as [volcanic eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcano), or two objects striking each other at very high speeds, as in an [impact event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_event).

Explosive volcanic eruptions occur when [magma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma) rises from below, it has dissolved gas in it. The reduction of [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) as the magma rises causes the gas to bubble out of solution, resulting in a rapid increase in volume, however the size of the magma chamber remains the same. This results in pressure buildup that eventually leads to an explosive eruption. Explosions can also occur outside of Earth in the universe in events such as [supernovae](27680.md), or, more commonly, stellar flares. Humans are also able to create explosions through the use of [explosives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explosives), or through [nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission) or [fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fusion), as in a [nuclear weapon](21785.md). Explosions frequently occur during [bushfires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bushfires) in [eucalyptus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucalyptus) forests where the volatile oils in the tree tops suddenly combust.[^cite_note-1]

### Astronomical



The nebula [M1-67](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M1-67) around [Wolf–Rayet star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf%E2%80%93Rayet_star) [WR 124](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WR_124) is the remnants of a [stellar explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_explosion), which is currently observed as six [light years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_years) across

[^cite_note-2]

Among the largest known explosions in the universe are [supernovae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernovae), which occur after the end of life of some types of [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star). [Solar flares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_flares) are an example of common, much less energetic, explosions on the Sun, and presumably on most other stars as well. The energy source for solar flare activity comes from the tangling of magnetic field lines resulting from the rotation of the Sun's conductive plasma. Another type of large astronomical explosion occurs when a meteoroid or an asteroid impacts the surface of another object, or [explodes in its atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airburst), such as a planet. This occurs because the two objects are moving at very high speed relative to each other (a minimum of 11.2 kilometres per second (7.0 mi/s) for an Earth impacting body[^cite_note-3]). For example, the [Tunguska event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunguska_event) of 1908 is believed to have resulted from a [meteor air burst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteor_air_burst).[^cite_note-4]

[Black hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_hole) mergers, likely involving [binary black hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_black_hole) systems, are capable of radiating many solar masses of energy into the universe in a fraction of a second, in the form of [gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_wave). This is capable of transmitting ordinary energy and destructive forces to nearby objects, but in the vastness of space, nearby objects are rare.[^cite_note-Forbes2020-5] The gravitational wave observed on 21 May 2019, known as [GW190521](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GW190521), produced a merger signal of about 100 ms duration, during which time is it estimated to have radiated away nine solar masses in the form of gravitational energy.

### Chemical

The [2025 Shandong factory explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Shandong_factory_explosion) is an example of a chemical explosive.

The most common artificial explosives are chemical explosives, usually involving a rapid and violent [oxidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation) reaction that produces large amounts of hot gas. Gunpowder was the first explosive to be invented and put to use. Other notable early developments in chemical explosive technology were [Frederick Augustus Abel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Augustus_Abel)'s development of nitrocellulose in 1865 and [Alfred Nobel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Nobel)'s invention of [dynamite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamite) in 1866. Chemical explosions (both intentional and accidental) are often initiated by an electric spark or flame in the presence of oxygen. Accidental explosions may occur in fuel tanks, rocket engines, etc.

Chemical reactions that cause explosions are typically either unimolecular decomposition reactions or multi-molecular reduction-oxidation (redox) reactions.[^cite_note-6]

### Electrical and magnetic



A [capacitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacitor) that has exploded

A high current electrical fault can create an "electrical explosion" by forming a high-energy [electrical arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_arc) which rapidly vaporizes metal and insulation material. This [arc flash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_flash) hazard is a danger to people working on energized [switchgear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switchgear). Excessive [magnetic pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_pressure) within an ultra-strong [electromagnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet) can cause a *magnetic explosion*.

### Mechanical and vapor

Strictly a physical process, as opposed to chemical or nuclear, e.g., the bursting of a sealed or partially sealed container under internal pressure is often referred to as an explosion. Examples include an overheated boiler or a simple tin can of beans tossed into a fire.

[Boiling liquid expanding vapor explosions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLEVE) are one type of mechanical explosion that can occur when a vessel containing a pressurized liquid is ruptured, causing a rapid increase in volume as the liquid evaporates. Note that the contents of the container may cause a subsequent chemical explosion, the effects of which can be dramatically more serious, such as a [propane](23643.md) tank in the midst of a fire. In such a case, to the effects of the mechanical explosion when the tank fails are added the effects from the explosion resulting from the released (initially liquid and then almost instantaneously gaseous) propane in the presence of an ignition source. For this reason, emergency workers often differentiate between the two events.

### Nuclear

Main articles: [Nuclear explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_explosion) and [Effects of nuclear explosions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_nuclear_explosions)

In addition to stellar [nuclear explosions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_explosion), a [nuclear weapon](21785.md) is a type of explosive weapon that derives its destructive force from [nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission) or from a combination of fission and fusion. As a result, even a nuclear weapon with a small yield is significantly more powerful than the largest conventional explosives available, with a single weapon capable of completely destroying an entire city.

## Properties

### Force



A breaching charge exploding against a test door during training



The effects of the [2020 Beirut explosion](64752725.md)

Explosive force is released in a direction perpendicular to the surface of the explosive. If a grenade is in mid air during the explosion, the direction of the blast will be 360°. In contrast, in a [shaped charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaped_charge) the explosive forces are focused to produce a greater local explosion; shaped charges are often used by military to breach doors or walls.

### Velocity

The speed of the reaction is what distinguishes an explosive reaction from an ordinary combustion reaction. Unless the reaction occurs very rapidly, the thermally expanding gases will be moderately dissipated in the medium, with no large differential in pressure and no explosion. As a wood fire burns in a fireplace, for example, there certainly is the evolution of heat and the formation of gases, but neither is liberated rapidly enough to build up a sudden substantial pressure differential and then cause an explosion. This can be likened to the difference between the energy discharge of a [battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battery_\(electricity\)), which is slow, and that of a flash [capacitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacitor) like that in a [camera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera) flash, which releases its energy all at once.

### Evolution of heat

The generation of heat in large quantities accompanies most explosive chemical reactions. The exceptions are called [entropic explosives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropic_explosion) and include organic peroxides such as [acetone peroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetone_peroxide).[^cite_note-7] It is the rapid liberation of heat that causes the gaseous products of most explosive reactions to expand and generate high [pressures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure). This rapid generation of high pressures of the released gas constitutes the explosion. The liberation of heat with insufficient rapidity will not cause an explosion. For example, although a unit mass of coal yields five times as much heat as a unit mass of [nitroglycerin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitroglycerin), the coal cannot be used as an explosive (except [in the form of coal dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_dust_explosion)) because the rate at which it yields this heat is quite slow. In fact, a substance that burns less rapidly (*i.e.* slow [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion)) may actually evolve more total heat than an explosive that detonates rapidly (*i.e.* fast [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion)). In the former, slow combustion converts more of the internal energy (*i.e.* [chemical potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_potential)) of the burning substance into heat released to the surroundings, while in the latter, fast combustion (*i.e.* [detonation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detonation)) instead converts more internal energy into work on the surroundings (*i.e.* less internal energy converted into heat); *cf.* [heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat) and [work (thermodynamics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_\(thermodynamics\)) are equivalent forms of energy. See [Heat of Combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_of_Combustion) for a more thorough treatment of this topic.

When a chemical compound is formed from its constituents, heat may either be absorbed or released. The quantity of heat absorbed or given off during transformation is called the [heat of formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_of_formation). Heats of formations for solids and gases found in explosive reactions have been determined for a temperature of 25 °C and atmospheric pressure, and are normally given in units of kilojoules per gram-molecule. A positive value indicates that heat is absorbed during the formation of the compound from its elements; such a reaction is called an endothermic reaction. In explosive technology only materials that are [exothermic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exothermic)—that have a net liberation of heat and have a negative heat of formation—are of interest. Reaction heat is measured under conditions either of constant pressure or constant volume. It is this heat of reaction that may be properly expressed as the "heat of explosion."

### Initiation of reaction

A chemical explosive is a compound or mixture which, upon the application of heat or shock, decomposes or rearranges with extreme rapidity, yielding much gas and heat. Many substances not ordinarily classed as explosives may do one, or even two, of these things.

A reaction must be capable of being initiated by the application of shock, heat, or a [catalyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalyst) (in the case of some explosive chemical reactions) to a small portion of the mass of the explosive material. A material in which the first three factors exist cannot be accepted as an explosive unless the reaction can be made to occur when needed.

### Fragmentation

Fragmentation is the accumulation and projection of particles as the result of a high explosives detonation. Fragments could originate from: parts of a structure (such as [glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass), bits of [structural material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_material), or roofing material), revealed [strata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strata) and/or various surface-level geologic features (such as loose [rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_\(geology\)), [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil), or [sand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sand)), the casing surrounding the explosive, and/or any other loose miscellaneous items not vaporized by the shock wave from the explosion. High velocity, low angle fragments can travel hundreds of metres with enough energy to initiate other surrounding high explosive items, injure or kill personnel, and/or damage vehicles or structures.

#### Rapid unscheduled disassembly

In [aerospace engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerospace_engineering), the term rapid unscheduled/unplanned disassembly is humorously used as a [euphemism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphemism) for the explosion of an aircraft or spacecraft. It was used by [Elon Musk](909036.md) in 2015 after a failure of its [Falcon 9 first-stage landing mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_first-stage_landing_tests) after it lost control just before landing.[^cite_note-8]

## Etymology

Classical Latin *explōdō* means "to hiss a bad actor off the stage", "to drive an actor off the stage by making noise", from *ex-* ("out") + *plaudō* ("to clap; to applaud"). The modern meaning developed later:[^cite_note-9]

- Classical Latin: "to drive an actor off the stage by making noise" hence meaning "to drive out" or "to reject"

In English:

- Around 1538: "drive out or off by clapping" (originally theatrical)

- Around 1660: "drive out with violence and sudden noise"

- Around 1790: "go off with a loud noise"

- Around 1882: first use as "bursting with destructive force"
