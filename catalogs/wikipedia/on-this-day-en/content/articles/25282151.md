The **economy of Argentina** is the second-largest in [South America](26769.md), behind [Brazil](3383.md). The [United Nations](31769.md)' [Human Development Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Development_Index) ranks Argentina "very high," reflecting a literate population, export-oriented [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_of_Argentina), and diversified industry. Under [President Javier Milei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_Javier_Milei), the country is a [market economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_economy), differing from the [Peronist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peronism) model earlier.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-\:2-26] The economy has rich [natural resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_resources); however, its performance has been volatile—growth spurts alternate with recessions—especially since the late 20th century, driving [income inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_distribution) and [poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty). Early in 2oth century, Argentina's [per capita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_capita) [GDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GDP) ranked among top 10 globally, at par with [Canada](5042916.md) and [Australia](4689264.md) and ahead of [France](5843419.md) and [Italy](14532.md).&#x20;

The [Argentine peso](407877.md) lost \~50% of its value in 2018 because of [hyperinflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation) (falling from \~18–20 to >38 pesos per [U.S. dollar](18717338.md)), prompting an [IMF stand-by program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IMF_Stand-By_Arrangement). It fell another 25% in 2019, 90% in 2020, 68% in 2021,[^cite_note-27] and 50% in 2022,[^cite_note-28] 54% in 2023, 30% in 2024, before rising in 2025.[^cite_note-29] The [FTSE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FTSE_Group) Global Equity Index (2018) listed Argentina as an [emerging market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emerging_market) and [G20 member](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-20_major_economies). Prolonged capital controls led [MSCI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MSCI_World) to reclassify it as a standalone market in 2021.[^cite_note-30]

Argentina's economy has swung from one of the world's richest in the early 20th century to repeated cycles of boom, bust, and hyperinflation, driven by commodity dependence, political instability, and policy shifts. Below is a summarized timeline based on historical data, with updates to 2025 reflecting recent reforms under Milei.

### Pre-20th Century Foundations (Before 1880s)

Before the 1880s, Argentina's economy relied on salted meat, wool, leather, and hides for exports and income, with limited industrialization.

### Golden age (1880–1905)

&#x20;Argentina was one of the wealthiest countries in the world:[^cite_note-34]

> Argentina began the 20th century as one of the wealthiest places on the planet. In 1913, it was richer than France or Germany, almost twice as prosperous as Spain, and its per capita GDP was almost as high as that of Canada.

— Edward L. Glaeser, Rafael Di Tella, Lucas Llach, Introduction to Argentine exceptionalism

From 1880–1905, British and French investments fueled livestock and grain exports, sparking rapid expansion and mass European immigration. GDP grew 7.5 times (8% annual average); per capita GDP rose from 35% to 80% of the U.S. level. A 2009 study noted GDP gains post-1870 but stagnant heights (proxy for welfare) until 1910, indicating uneven benefits.

During 1905–1941 growth slowed; although per capita GDP reached \~50% of U.S. levels by 1941, matching [Western Europe](33800.md)'s but with inequality.

### Great Depression (1929–1932)

The [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression) severely contracted the Argentine economy by sharply curtailing foreign trade, triggering a structural shift away from agro-export dependence.[^cite_note-35] In response, successive governments pursued [import substitution industrialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Import_substitution_industrialization), raising tariffs to shelter domestic manufacturers from foreign competition.[^cite_note-36] The [Roca–Runciman Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roca%E2%80%93Runciman_Treaty) (1933), negotiated to preserve Argentine beef access to British markets following the [Ottawa Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottawa_Conference), was widely criticized by Argentine nationalists as an asymmetrical concession to British commercial interests.[^cite_note-37]

### Perón era (1945–1955)

[Juan Perón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Per%C3%B3n) nationalized the [Central Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Bank_of_Argentina), railways, and industries. Inflation averaged 26% annually (1944–1974); GDP per capita peaked in the early 1950s, but stagnated due to commodity slumps and [nationalizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalization).[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40]

Argentine industry benefited from the inability of European countries to supply their products to the world market. Industrial manufactures were exported in considerable volumes, mainly to [Latin America](18524.md) and the [Caribbean](18956035.md). Some of the policies adopted at that time included rediscounts, the Export and Industrial and Commercial Stimulus Committee, the Industrial Promotion Laws, the creation of the State merchant fleet, credits from the [Banco Industrial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BIND_Group) (1944), and the nationalization of the Central Bank of Argentina (1946). Following [Keynesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keynesianism) theories, Perón sought to establish the [welfare state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_state), by increasing social security and improving income distribution. He did this through raising public spending and making simultaneous investments in different sectors such as defense, health, education, and housing. Production grew, and the increase in exports fed into an expansion of consumption.[^cite_note-41]

### Debt crisis and hyperinflation (1976–1990s)

From 1976–1983, the National Reorganization Process attempted [neoliberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoliberalism) policies under [José Alfredo Martínez de Hoz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Alfredo_Mart%C3%ADnez_de_Hoz) ballooned debt; inflation hit 344% in 1983, the currency devalued 10x, while growth stalled.[^cite_note-\:1-42]

[Stagflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagflation) ensued from 1975–1990, driven by increasing debt, tax evasion, and flight; hyperinflation peaked from 1989–1990.

In 1991 under [Domingo Cavallo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domingo_Cavallo), the government attempted reforms, pegging the peso to the dollar. With the money supply curbed, inflation fell to single digits. Liberalization/privatization boosted GDP 33% in four years. Erratic growth came after 1995. In 2001 a crisis shrank GDP by 20%, unemployment reached 25%, the peso declined by 70%.

### Commodity boom and Kirchner recovery (2002–2015)

From 2003–2011, expansionary policies and increased exports created >5M jobs. GDP nearly doubled (7.1% avg. annual, 9% 2003–2007). Real wages rose 72% (2003–2013). Postal, water, pensions, Aerolíneas Argentinas, YPF, railways were renationalized. A crisis stopped growth in 2009, although it rebounded to 9% (2010–2011).

During 2012–2015 capital controls, austerity, inflation, and trade slumps cut growth to 1.3% (2012–2014), edging up to 2.4% (2015).

### Debt restructuring (2000s–2010s)

From 2005 to 2010 Argentina restructured \$100B in loans. In 2001 it defaulted. Some bondholders rejected the restructuring, preventing it from going final. Debt service reached \~\$14B.[^cite_note-\:1-42]

In 2018 the IMF completed a bailout – \$30B as inflation hit 25%; interest rates reached 40% (world's highest). Inflation hit 54% in 2019 and 211% in 2023.

### Milei: shock therapy (2023–2025)

See also: [Shock therapy (economics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shock_therapy_\(economics\)) and [Economic reforms of Javier Milei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_reforms_of_Javier_Milei)

In 2023 inflation crossed 100% and then 200%. Milei (elected with a 55.7% majority) devalued the peso 50–54% to \~800/USD. He cut subsidies/public works (\$20B savings), achieved Argentina's first federal budget surplus since 2012.[^cite_note-BBC_News-43][^cite_note-44][^cite_note-reuters.com-45][^cite_note-46][^cite_note-thedailyeconomy.org-47]

In 2024, Milei cut 15,000 state jobs, sparking protests.[^cite_note-48] Layoffs grew to over 24,000 by mid-year. Poverty peaked at 57% in January, falling to 38.9–39% in Q3.[^cite_note-49] Inflation crashed to 2.4% in November, falling by almost half at 117.8%, down 93.6 percentage points from 2023's 211.4%. GDP fell between 3.8–4%.[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-51]

In 2026, the Central Bank of Argentina (BCRA) met its dollar accumulation goal of 10.1 billion in the first six months of the year. [^cite_note-52] Aggressive privatization and debt restructuring with the IMF has led to the S\&P Global Ratings agency to upgrade Argentina's sovereign credit rating to a B−. [^cite_note-53]

### Agriculture

Main article: [Agriculture in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_in_Argentina)

See also: [Argentine wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_wine)



Vineyard in [Mendoza Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendoza_Province). Argentina ranks as the fifth largest wine producer worldwide.

[^cite_note-Johnson_atlas_pg_300-301-57]

&#x20;Argentina ranks among major global agricultural producers. It leads in beef exports, [citrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citrus_fruit), grapes, [honey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honey), [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize), [sorghum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghum), [soybeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soybean), [squash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squash_\(plant\)), [sunflower seeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunflower_seed), wheat, and [yerba mate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yerba_mate).[^cite_note-58] The sector contributed 9% of GDP in 2010. It generated one fifth of exports, excluding processed food and feed, which amounted to another third. Farmers harvested 103 million tons that year. Oilseeds, mainly [soy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soy) and [sunflower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunflower), exceeded 54 million tons. Cereals, mainly maize, wheat, and sorghum, surpassed 46 million tons.[^cite_note-harvest-59] In recent years, maize output has increased significantly; between 2000 and 2023, production rose by a factor of 3.74, while the planting area expanded by 3.18 times. During this period, the average yield per unit increased by approximately 26%. The country led world production of yerba mate. It ranked among the top five for soy, maize, sunflower seed, [lemon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemon), and [pear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pear). It placed in the top ten for [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley), [grape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grape), [artichoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artichoke), [tobacco](30942.md), and [cotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton). #### Livestock

See also: [Argentine beef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_beef)

In 2019, producers raised 3 million tons of [beef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beef), fourth globally behind the US, Brazil, and China. They generated the fourth most [honey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honey), tenth most [wool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wool), 13th most [chicken meat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicken_meat), 23rd most [pork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pork), 18th most [cow's milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cow's_milk), and the 14th most [chicken eggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicken_egg).[^cite_note-auto-62] Soy and byproducts, mainly animal feed and vegetable oils, formed one fourth of export raw materials. Cereals added 10%. Cattle raising supports domestic needs. Beef, leather, and dairy reached 5% of exports.[^cite_note-indectrade-63] Sheep raising and wool thrive in [Patagonia](65433.md), though output halved since 1990. [Biodiesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiesel) exports exceeded US\$2 billion in 2011.[^cite_note-indectrade-63]

#### Crops by region

Apples and pears grow in the [Río Negro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%ADo_Negro_\(Argentina\)) valley. Rice, oranges, and citrus dominate the [northwest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_Northwest) and [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia,_Argentina). Grapes and [strawberries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strawberries) flourish in [Cuyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuyo_\(Argentina\)). [Berries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berries) appear in the far south. Cotton and tobacco lead in the [Gran Chaco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gran_Chaco). Sugarcane and [chile peppers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chile_pepper) prevail in the northwest. [Olives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olives) and garlic come from the west. Farmers cultivate [yerba mate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yerba_mate) tea in [Misiones](233567.md), tomatoes in [Salta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salta_Province), and peaches in Mendoza for local markets. Organic farming covers nearly 3 million hectares, second only to [Australia](4689264.md).[^cite_note-statesman-64] Fine wine production advanced in quality. Total [viticulture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viticulture) potential remains untapped. [Mendoza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendoza_Province) leads wine regions, followed by [San Juan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Juan_Province_\(Argentina\)).[^cite_note-65]

### Natural resources

See also: [Mining in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining_in_Argentina)

Mining and other extractive activities, such as gas and petroleum, are growing industries, increasing from 2% of GDP in 1980 to around 4% later.[^cite_note-mecon-68] The [northwest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_Northwest) and [San Juan Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Juan_Province_\(Argentina\)) are the main regions of activity. Coal is mined in [Santa Cruz Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Cruz_Province_\(Argentina\)). Minerals mined include [borate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borate), [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper), [lead](17747.md), [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium), [sulfur](27127.md), [tungsten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten), [uranium](31743.md), [zinc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc), [silver](27119.md), [titanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanium), and [gold](12240.md), whose production grew after 1997 via the [Bajo de la Alumbrera mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bajo_de_la_Alumbrera_mine) in [Catamarca Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catamarca_Province) and San Juan. Metal ore exports soared from US\$200 million in 1996 to US\$1.2 billion in 2004,[^cite_note-72] and to over US\$3 billion in 2010.[^cite_note-indectrade-63]

In 2019, Argentina was the 4th largest world producer of [lithium](17561.md),[^cite_note-73] the 9th largest world producer of [silver](27119.md),[^cite_note-74] the 17th largest world producer of [gold](12240.md)[^cite_note-75] and the 7th largest world producer of [boron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron).[^cite_note-76]

### Industry

Main article: [Industry in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industry_in_Argentina)

The [World Bank](36752.md) lists the top industrial countries each year, based on the value of production. According to the 2019 list, Argentina had the 31st most valuable industry (57.7 billion dollars), behind Mexico, Brazil and Venezuela, but ahead of Colombia, Peru and Chile.[^cite_note-77]

In 2019, Argentina was the 31st largest producer of [steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel), the 28th producer of [vehicles](163778.md), the 22nd brewer of [beer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beer), the 4th producer of [soybean oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soybean_oil) and the 3rd producer of [sunflower oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunflower_oil), among other industrial products.[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-79][^cite_note-80][^cite_note-auto-62]

[Manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing) is the largest single sector in the Argentine economy (15% of GDP), and is well-integrated with agriculture, such that half the nation's industrial exports were agricultural.[^cite_note-indectrade-63] Based on food processing and textiles during the first half of the 20th century, industrial production later diversified.[^cite_note-81] Leading sectors by production value were: [food processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_processing) and [beverages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beverage); [motor vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_vehicles) and [auto parts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auto_part); [refinery products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_refineries), and [biodiesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiesel); [chemicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemicals) and [pharmaceuticals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmaceutical); [steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel) and [aluminium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium); and [industrial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_machinery) and [farm machinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farm_machinery); [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics) and [home appliances](185982.md).[^cite_note-mecon-68] Other manufactured goods included [glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass) and [cement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cement); [plastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastics) and [tires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tires); [lumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumber) products; [textiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textile); [tobacco products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_product); [recording](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recording_media) and [print media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Print_media); [furniture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Furniture); [apparel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparel) and [leather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leather).[^cite_note-mecon-68]

Most manufacturing is organized in the 314 [industrial parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_park) operating nationwide as of 2012, a fourfold increase over the past decade.[^cite_note-82] Nearly half of Argentine industries are based in the [Greater Buenos Aires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Buenos_Aires) area, although [Córdoba](241269.md), [Rosario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosario), and [Ushuaia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ushuaia) are also significant industrial centers; the latter city became the nation's leading [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics) center during the 1980s.[^cite_note-83]

#### Automotive

Main article: [Automotive industry in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automotive_industry_in_Argentina)

Argentina produced 791,000 motor vehicles in 2013, and exported 433,000 (mainly to Brazil, which in turn exported a somewhat larger number to Argentina); Argentina's domestic auto market reached a record 964,000 in 2013.[^cite_note-84] This marked a peak in vehicle production; by 2021 production had fallen to 434,753 vehicles.[^cite_note-85] Vehicles remained Argentina's top export to Brazil, accounting for \$3.1bil in exports in 2021.[^cite_note-86]

Beverages became another significant sector, as Argentina continued among the top five wine producing countries; beer overtook wine production in 2000, and today leads by nearly two billion liters a year to one.[^cite_note-mecon-68]

#### Defense

Main article: [Defense industry of Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_industry_of_Argentina)

This section is an excerpt from [Defense industry of Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_industry_of_Argentina).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Defense_industry_of_Argentina\&action=edit)]



[I.Ae. 24 Calquin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I.Ae._24_Calquin) aircraft production, 1950

The Argentine [defense industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_industry) has developed, over the years, different programs to improve the [armed forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_Forces_of_the_Argentine_Republic) of [Argentina](18951905.md). The first major steps to establish a defense industry were made during the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War) and they received a boost during the 1970s after the [United States](3434750.md) imposed an arms embargo due to [human rights violations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirty_War). The politics of privatization carried out during the 1990s virtually eliminated domestic military production, but many factories were reopened during the last years.

#### Electronics and machinery

The production of [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer), [laptops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laptop), and [servers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Server_\(computing\)) grew by 160% in 2011, to nearly 3.4 million units, and covered two-thirds of local demand.[^cite_note-87] Argentina became an important manufacturer of [cell phones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_phone), providing about 80% of all devices sold in the country.[^cite_note-88] Production of cell phones, computers, and similar products is an assembly industry that imports the majority of the components, and uses designs originating from other countries. High labour costs for Argentina assembly work tend to limit penetration to only Latin America, protected by regional trade treaties.[^cite_note-89]

[Farm machinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farm_machinery), another important rubric historically dominated by imports, was similarly replaced by domestic production, which covered 60% of demand by 2013.[^cite_note-90]

#### Petroleum

Around 35 million m<sup>3</sup> each of petroleum and [petroleum fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_fuel) are produced, as well as 50 billion m<sup>3</sup> of natural gas, making the nation self-sufficient in these staples, and generating around 10% of exports.[^cite_note-mecon-68] The most important [oil fields](2910801.md) lie in [Patagonia](65433.md) and [Cuyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuyo_\(Argentina\)). A network of [pipelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport) send raw product to [Bahía Blanca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%ADa_Blanca), center of the [petrochemical industry](2293201.md), and to the [La Plata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Plata)-[Greater Buenos Aires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Buenos_Aires)-[Rosario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosario) industrial belt.

#### Construction

Construction permits nationwide covered over 15 million m<sup>2</sup> (160 million ft<sup>2</sup>) in 2013. The construction sector accounted for over 5% of GDP, and two-thirds of construction is for residential buildings.[^cite_note-91]

### Services

The [service sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_\(economics\)) is the largest contributor to GDP, accounting for over 60%. Argentina enjoys a diversified service sector, which includes well-developed social, corporate, financial, insurance, real estate, transport, communication services, and tourism.

[Trade in services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_in_services) remained in deficit, however, with US\$15 billion in service exports in 2013 against US\$19 billion in imports.[^cite_note-dsbb-20] [Business Process Outsourcing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_Process_Outsourcing) became the leading service export, and reached US\$3 billion.[^cite_note-92] Advertising revenues from contracts abroad were estimated at over US\$1.2 billion.[^cite_note-93]

#### Banking

Main article: [Banking in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banking_in_Argentina)

See also: [List of banks in the Americas § Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_banks_in_the_Americas#Argentina)



View of [Buenos Aires Central Business District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buenos_Aires_Central_Business_District) from the [Costanera Sur Ecological Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costanera_Sur_Ecological_Reserve)

Argentine banking deposits exceeded US\$120 billion in December 2012.[^cite_note-polmon-94] The financial sector developed via public sector banks, but became dominated by the private sector. Private sector banks accounted for most of the 80 active institutions (over 4,000 branches) and held nearly 60% of deposits and loans, and as many foreign-owned banks as local ones operated in the country.[^cite_note-aba-95] The long-largest bank in Argentina by far, is the public [Banco de la Nación Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banco_de_la_Naci%C3%B3n_Argentina). Not to be confused with the [Central Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Bank_of_Argentina), this institution accounts for 30% of deposits and a fifth of its loan portfolio.[^cite_note-aba-95]

During the 1990s, Argentina's financial system consolidated and strengthened. Deposits grew from less than US\$15 billion in 1991 to over US\$80 billion in 2000, while outstanding credit (70% of it to the private sector) tripled to nearly US\$100 billion.[^cite_note-aba1-96]

#### Education

Main article: [Education in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_Argentina)

This section is an excerpt from [Education in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_Argentina).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Education_in_Argentina\&action=edit)]

[Education in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_Argentina) is a responsibility shared by the national government, the provinces and federal district and private institutions. Education at all levels, including university, is free. President [Domingo Sarmiento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domingo_Sarmiento)'s assertion that "the sovereign should be educated" has been a keystone of Argentine Education since 1918. Education has been extended nearly universally and its maintenance remains central to political and cultural debate. There are a significant number of private schools and universities despite free schooling.

Education in state institutions is at the initial, primary, secondary and tertiary levels and in the undergraduate university level (not for graduate programs). Private education is paid, although in some cases (especially in primary and secondary schools) state subsidies support its costs.

According to studies by [UNESCO](21786641.md), guarantee equality to have institutional features that hinder the commercialization of education, as well as Finland has characteristics that favor multiethnic population education and special education, education favors Argentina equality. Illiteracy rates in Argentina are very low.[^cite_note-97] According to the last census, the [illiteracy rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illiteracy_rate) is 1.9%, the second lowest in Latin America. In the last decade, Argentina has created nine new universities, while the outflow of university students increased by 68%.

#### Tourism

Main article: [Tourism in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_Argentina)

[Tourism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_Argentina) is an increasingly important sector, providing 4% of direct economic output (over US\$17 billion) in 2012; around 70% of tourism sector activity by value is domestic.[^cite_note-weforum-105] According to [World Economic Forum](172185.md)'s 2017 Travel & Tourism Competitiveness Report, tourism generated over US\$22 billion, or 3.9% of GDP, and the industry employed more than 671,000 people, or approximately 3.7% of the workforce in 2016.[^cite_note-106] Around 6.7 million foreign visitors arrived in 2017, reflecting a doubling in visitors since 2002 despite a relative appreciation of the peso.[^cite_note-107]

Around 48% of visitors arrived by commercial flight, 40% by motor travel (mainly from Brazil), and 12% by sea.[^cite_note-111] [Cruise liner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruise_liner) arrivals were the fastest growing type of foreign tourism to Argentina; a of 160 liners carrying 510,000 passengers arrived at the [Port of Buenos Aires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Buenos_Aires) in 2013, an eightfold increase in a just a decade.[^cite_note-puerto-112]

## Infrastructure

### Energy

Main article: [Energy in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_in_Argentina)



Electricity generation in Argentina in terawatt-hours

Argentine electricity generation reached 133.3 billion [kWh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilowatt_hour) in 2013.[^cite_note-mecon-68] It was the third largest power market in Latin America. It mainly relies on centralised generation by natural gas (51%), [hydroelectricity](381399.md) (28%), and oil (12%).[^cite_note-iea-113] [Resource estimates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_&_gas_reserves_and_resource_quantification) of [unconventional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unconventional_\(oil_&_gas\)_reservoir) [shale gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shale_gas) and tight oil in the [Vaca Muerta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaca_Muerta) oil field and elsewhere were estimated to be the world's third-largest.[^cite_note-Fontevecchia-70] In 2017, Argentina was the 18th largest producer (and the largest producer in Latin America) of [natural gas](22131.md).[^cite_note-114] In 2020, the country was the 28th largest oil producer at 70,000 m<sup>3</sup> (440,300 bbl) per day.[^cite_note-115]

Argentines outputed over 133 billion [kWh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KWh) of electricity in 2013,[^cite_note-mecon-68] in large part through natural gas and [hydroelectric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroelectric) resources. [Nuclear energy](22153.md) is of high importance.[^cite_note-116] The country is one of the largest producers and exporters, alongside Canada and Russia of [cobalt-60](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobalt-60), a [radioactive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive) [isotope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope) widely used in cancer therapy. At the end of 2021 Argentina was 21st in terms of installed hydroelectric power (11.3 GW), the 26th at wind energy (3.2 GW) and the 43rd in solar energy (1.0 GW).[^cite_note-117]

The first of three [nuclear reactors](22151.md) was inaugurated in 1974. As of 2015 nuclear power generated 5% of the country's energy output.[^cite_note-iea-113]

The electricity sector was unbundled in [generation](9540.md), [transmission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity_transmission) and [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity_distribution) by early 1990s reforms. Generation is competitive and mostly liberalized; 75% of capacity is privately owned. In contrast, transmission and distribution is regulated with much less competitive.

[Transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport) in [Argentina](18951905.md) is mainly based on a complex network of routes, crossed by relatively inexpensive long-distance [buses](4146.md) and by [cargo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cargo) trucks. The country also has a number of national and international [airports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airport). The importance of the long-distance [train](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Train) is minor today, though in the past it was widely used and is now regaining momentum after the [re-nationalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Argentina#Re-nationalisation) of the country's commuter and freight networks.[^cite_note-118][^cite_note-119] [Fluvial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inland_navigation) transport is mostly used for cargo.

Within the urban areas, the main transportation system is by the bus or *[colectivo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colectivo)*; bus lines transport millions of people every day in the larger cities and their [metropolitan areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_area) as well as a [bus rapid transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus_rapid_transport) system known as [Metrobus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrobus_\(Buenos_Aires\)). [Buenos Aires](255919.md) additionally has an [underground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buenos_Aires_Underground), the only one in the country, and [Greater Buenos Aires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Buenos_Aires) is serviced by a system of [suburban trains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Argentina#Commuter_network).

### Water supply and sanitation

Main article: [Water supply and sanitation in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Argentina)

This section is an excerpt from [Water supply and sanitation in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Argentina).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Argentina\&action=edit)]

|  | This article needs to be **updated**. Please help update this article to reflect recent events or newly available information. *(June 2016)* |
| --- | --- |

[Drinking water supply and sanitation in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Argentina) is characterized by relatively low [tariffs](55551.md), mostly reasonable service quality, low levels of metering and high levels of consumption for those with access to services. At the same time, according to the [WHO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WHO), 21% of the total population remains without access to house connections and 52% of the [urban population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_area) do not have access to sewerage. The responsibility for operating and maintaining water and sanitation services rests with 19 provincial water and sewer companies, more than 100 municipalities and more than 950 cooperatives, the latter operating primarily in small towns. Among the largest water and sewer companies are [Agua y Saneamientos Argentinos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agua_y_Saneamientos_Argentinos) (AYSA) and Aguas Bonarenses S.A. (ABSA), both operating in [Greater Buenos Aires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Buenos_Aires), Aguas Provinciales de [Santa Fe](520560.md), and Aguas Cordobesas SA, all of them now publicly owned. In 2008 there were still a few private concessions, such as Aguas de [Salta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salta,_Argentina) SA, which is majority-owned by Argentine investors, and Obras Sanitarias de [Mendoza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendoza,_Argentina) (OSM).

Most service providers barely recover operation and maintenance costs and have no capacity to self-finance investments. While private operators were able to achieve higher levels of cost recovery, since the [Argentine financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_economic_crisis_\(1999-2002\)) in 2002 tariffs have been frozen and the self-financing capacity of utilities has disappeared. Roughly two-thirds of provincial water and sanitation spending since 2002 has come from general transfers from the federal government, the remainder coming from various national programs directed specifically to the sector.

Services are regulated by the 23 [Provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provinces_of_Argentina), in the case of 14 through regulatory agencies that have some limited autonomy from the government. Overall, however, responsibilities are not always clearly defined, and institutions are often weak, subject to political interference and lacking enforcement powers. The various national institutions with policy-setting responsibilities in the sector are not always well coordinated. There is no coherent national policy in terms of sector financing, subsidies, tariffs and service standards. The federal structure of the country and the dispersion of sector responsibilities between and within various levels of government make the development of a coherent sector policy all the more difficult.

Between 1991 and 1999, as part of one of the world's largest privatization programs covering a range of sectors, water and sanitation concessions with the private sector were signed covering 28% of the country's municipalities and 60% of the population.[^cite_note-Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Argentina_Galiani-120] The highest profile concession was signed in 1993 with a consortium led by the French firm [Suez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_\(company,_1997%E2%80%932008\)) for the central parts of [Greater Buenos Aires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Buenos_Aires). After the [2001 economic crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_economic_crisis_\(1999-2002\)), many concessions were renegotiated. Many were terminated, as it was the case in [Buenos Aires](255919.md) in 2006.[^cite_note-Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Argentina_argentina.gov.ar-121]

The impact of private sector participation in water and sanitation is a controversial topic. While the public perception of the mostly international concessionaires is overwhelmingly negative in Argentina, some studies show positive impacts. For example, a 2002 study assessed the impact of privatization on child mortality based on household survey data, finding that child mortality fell 5 to 7 percent more in areas that privatized compared to those that remained under public or cooperative management.[^cite_note-Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Argentina_iadb.org-122] The authors estimate that the main reason is the massive expansion of access to water. According to [Suez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_\(company,_1997%E2%80%932008\)), the private concession in Buenos Aires extended access to water to 2 million people and access to sanitation to 1 million people, despite a freeze in tariffs imposed by the government in 2001 in violation of the concession agreement. The government argues that the concessionaire did not fully comply with its obligations concerning expansion and quality, saying that the supplied water had high levels of nitrate, pressure obligations were not kept and scheduled works were not carried out.[^cite_note-Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Argentina_Solanes_2006_124–148-123]

In 2022, Argentina was the 44th largest exporter (by merchandise exports) totaling US\$88 billion, or 0.4%.[^cite_note-125]

Argentine exports are diversified. Agricultural raw materials are over 20% of the exports and agricultural goods account for over 50% of exports including processed foods. Soy products ([soybeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soybean), [vegetable oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetable_oil)) accounted for almost one fourth. Cereals, mostly maize and wheat, which were Argentina's leading export during much of the twentieth century, make up less than one tenth now.[^cite_note-autogenerated4-126]

Industrial goods comprise over one-third of Argentine exports. Motor vehicles and auto parts are the leading industrial export, at over 12% of merchandise exports. Chemicals, steel, aluminum, machinery, and plastics account for most of the remaining industrial exports. Trade in manufactures has historically been in deficit for Argentina, however, and despite the nation's overall trade surplus, its manufacturing trade deficit exceeded US\$30 billion in 2011.[^cite_note-rojo-127] Accordingly, the system of non-automatic import licensing was extended in 2011,[^cite_note-128] and regulations were enacted for the auto sector establishing a model by which a company's future imports would be determined by their exports.[^cite_note-129]

A net energy importer until 1987, Argentina's fuel exports began increasing in the early 1990s and account for about one-eighth of the total; refined fuels make up about half of that. As of 2009 exports of crude petroleum and natural gas were around US\$3 billion a year.[^cite_note-autogenerated4-126] Rapidly growing domestic energy demand and a gradual decline in oil production, resulted in a US\$3 billion energy trade deficit in 2011 (the first in 17 years)[^cite_note-130] and a US\$6 billion energy deficit in 2013.[^cite_note-131]

Argentine imports have historically been dominated by industrial and technological supplies, machinery, and parts, which have averaged US\$50 billion since 2011 (two-thirds of imports). Consumer goods including motor vehicles make up most of the rest.[^cite_note-autogenerated4-126] [Trade in services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_in_services) was in deficit. In 2013 this deficit widened to over US\$4 billion with a record US\$19 billion in service imports.[^cite_note-dsbb-20] The nation's chronic [current account deficit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_account_deficit) was reversed during the 2002 crisis, and an average current account surplus of US\$7 billion was logged between 2002 and 2009; this surplus later narrowed considerably, and was slightly negative in 2011.[^cite_note-132]

## Foreign investment

As of 2010 [foreign direct investment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_direct_investment) (FDI) in Argentina was divided nearly evenly between manufacturing (36%), natural resources (34%), and services (30%). The chemical and plastics sector (10%) and the automotive sector (6%) led foreign investment in local manufacturing; oil and gas (22%) and mining (5%), in natural resources; telecommunications (6%), finance (5%), and retail trade (4%), in services.[^cite_note-inv-137] Spain was the leading source of foreign direct investment in Argentina, accounting for US\$22 billion (28%) in 2009; the U.S. was second, with \$13 billion (17%);[^cite_note-inv-137] China grew to become the third-largest by 2011.[^cite_note-138] Investments from the Netherlands, Brazil, Chile, and Canada were significant; in 2012, foreign nationals held a of around US\$112 billion in [direct investment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_received_FDI).[^cite_note-dsbb-20]

Bilateral agreements played an important role in promoting U.S. private investment. Argentina inked an [Overseas Private Investment Corporation (OPIC)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_Private_Investment_Corporation) agreement and an active program with the [U.S. Export-Import Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Export-Import_Bank_of_the_United_States). Under the 1994 U.S.–Argentina Bilateral Investment Treaty, U.S. investors enjoyed national treatment in all sectors except shipbuilding, fishing, nuclear-power generation, and uranium production. The treaty allowed for international arbitration of investment disputes.

FDI averaged US\$5.7 billion from 1992 to 1998 and reached in US\$24 billion in 1999 (reflecting the purchase of 98% of [YPF](715706.md) stock by [Repsol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repsol)), fell to US\$1.6 billion in 2003.[^cite_note-unmdp-139] FDI then accelerated, reaching US\$8 billion in 2008.[^cite_note-140] The [financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_financial_crisis) cut this figure to US\$4 billion in 2009; but inflows recovered to US\$8.7 billion in 2011[^cite_note-141]

FDI volume remained below the regional average as a percent of GDP; Kirchner Administration policies and difficulty in enforcing contractual obligations were blamed.[^cite_note-142] Foreign investment in Argentina shifted after 2000, from [privatizations](24661.md) and [mergers and acquisitions](20769.md), to over half in the form of medium and high-tech investment (compared to 36% in Brazil and 3% in Chile).[^cite_note-143]

## Issues

The Fernández government has been accused of manipulating economic statistics.[^cite_note-144]

### Reliability of official CPI estimates

Official CPI inflation figures released monthly by [INDEC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INDEC) were a subject of political controversy from 2007 through 2015.[^cite_note-wsj-20110121-145][^cite_note-146][^cite_note-econ-16846418-147] Official inflation data were disregarded by leading union leaders, when negotiating pay rises.[^cite_note-ft-2011-02-03-148] Some private-sector estimates put inflation for 2010 at around 25%, much higher than the official 10.9% rate.[^cite_note-ft-2011-02-03-148] Inflation estimates from Argentina's provinces were higher than government figures.[^cite_note-ft-2011-02-03-148] The government backed up the validity of its data, but called in the IMF to help design a new nationwide index.[^cite_note-ft-2011-02-03-148]

The official CPI assessed 520 products, however the list was not made public.[^cite_note-149] Economic analysts were prosecuted for publishing estimates that disagreed with official statistics.[^cite_note-150] The government levied a fine of up to 500,000 pesos for providing what it called "fraudulent inflation figures".[^cite_note-ft-2011-02-03-148] Beginning in 2015, the government again called for competitive bids from the private sector to provide a weekly independent inflation index.[^cite_note-151]

### Inflation

See also: [1989 hyperinflation in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1989_hyperinflation_in_Argentina), [Década Perdida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A9cada_Perdida), [2012 fiscal austerity in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_fiscal_austerity_in_Argentina), and [2018–present Argentine monetary crisis](44816333.md)



Argentina money supply increases year over year. Daily data points.

[^cite_note-152]

High inflation was a weakness of the Argentine economy for decades, frequently above 25%,[^cite_note-nyt-2011-02-06-153] although it dramatically declined under Milei, reaching 1.5% in June 2025.[^cite_note-154]

The inflation rate in Argentina rose to 52.3 percent in February 2022.[^cite_note-155] In August the interest rate was hiked to 69.5% as inflation further deteriorated, hitting a 20-year high at 70%.[^cite_note-156] Inflation passed 100% in February 2023, for the first time since 1991.[^cite_note-157][^cite_note-158] On October 12 2023, Argentina's central bank again increased the benchmark interest rate from 118% to 133%, as inflation hit 138%.[^cite_note-159]

Argentine workers held funerals to mourn the "death of (their) wages".[^cite_note-160]

After Milei's victory in October 2023, efforts were made to curb the inflation rate. In January 2022, the monthly inflation rate kept increasing from 2.4% to a peak of 25% just before his inauguration. Soon after, Milei devalued the peso by 50% to cut the wide gap between the official exchange rate and the market exchange rate.[^cite_note-reuters.com-45] In a year he brought inflation down to a monthly 2.4%.[^cite_note-161] Milei then arranged a 2% monthly devaluation.[^cite_note-162][^cite_note-reuters.com-45]

In 2024, the peso appreciated by 44.2% against the dollar, outperforming all other currencies. These actions aimed to stabilize an economy teetering on the brink of [hyperinflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation). The peso's strengthening doubled average wages in dollar terms to \$990.[^cite_note-163]

### Income distribution

In relation to other Latin American countries, Argentina has a moderate to low level of income inequality. Its [Gini coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gini_coefficient) was about 0.407 (2022).[^cite_note-164] The gap was worst in the capital suburbs, where the economically successful live in [gated communities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gated_communities), while the poor live in slums known as *[villas miserias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villas_miserias)*.[^cite_note-nyt-165]

In the mid-1970s, the most affluent 10% of Argentina's population had an income 12 times that of the poorest 10%. That figure grew to 18 times by the mid-1990s, and by 2002, 43,[^cite_note-nyt-165] before declining to 16 in 2010.[^cite_note-voz-166]

Argentina had an [inequality-adjusted human development index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_inequality-adjusted_HDI) of 0.729, compared to 0.578 and 0.709 for neighboring Brazil and Chile, respectively.[^cite_note-167] The 2010 Census found that poverty still affected 1 in 8 inhabitants, however;[^cite_note-ambiente-168] and while the official, household survey poverty rate (based on U\$S 100 per person per month, net) was 4.7% in 2013,[^cite_note-grafpob-169] although the National Research Council estimated poverty in 2010 at 22.6%,[^cite_note-conicet-170] with private consulting firms estimating that in 2011 around 21% fell below the income poverty line.[^cite_note-171] The [World Bank](36752.md) estimated that, in 2013, 3.6% subsisted on less than US\$3.10 per person per day.[^cite_note-172]

### Informal Employment

As with many other Latin American countries, Argentina suffers from high levels of [informal employment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Informal_employment). As of January 2025, it is estimated that 42% of workers in Argentina were affected by informal unemployment.  The consequences of this are variable and widespread. An obvious issue is the government's inability to collect taxes on informal firms and workers. But the government is not the only group who is disadvantaged by informal work. Informal workers, on average, earn nearly 60% less than formal workers and are half as likely to benefit from social protection benefits.&#x20;
