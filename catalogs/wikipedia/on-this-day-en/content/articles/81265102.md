# Agriculture in Ukraine



[Ukraine's flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Ukraine) resembles the nation's farmlands. Pictured: [Kherson Oblast](1273370.md).



Percentage of population employed in agriculture and forestry, by 2008 data

**Agriculture in Ukraine** is one of the most important sectors of the [Ukrainian economy](31829.md). Although typically known as the [industrial base of the former Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrialization_in_the_Soviet_Union), Ukraine is one of the world's largest agricultural producers and exporters and is known as the "[breadbasket of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breadbasket_of_Europe)".[^cite_note-rferl.org-1] It encompasses about 30 to 40 millions of [hectares](24919513.md) of agricultural land, similar to [Germany](11867.md) and [France](5843419.md) combined.[^cite_note-Driebergen-2] In addition to providing the country's population with a stable supply of high-quality, safe and affordable food, Ukraine's agriculture sector is capable of making a significant contribution to solving the global problem of hunger. Its production potential significantly exceeds the needs of the domestic market.[^cite_note-й-3] As such, Ukraine is an important supplier of food (and livestock feed) to [North Africa](21714.md), [Asia](689.md), and [Western Europe](33800.md).

About 60% of [Ukraine's soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Ukraine) used for agriculture consists of [chornozem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernozem) ("black earth"), a humus-rich soil type that is very fertile.[^cite_note-Driebergen-2] [Winter wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_wheat), summer [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley), and [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize) are the main [cereal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cereal) crops, while [sunflowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunflower) and [sugar beet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_beet) are the main crops grown for industrial processing. The country has long been one of the world's largest agricultural exporters. For wheat, sugar beet, rapeseed, cucumber, rye, barley and walnuts, it was among the top 10 agricultural exporting countries in 2020, and for potatoes, buckwheat, cabbage, maize, pumpkin, carrots and sunflower oil, it was even among the top 5.[^cite_note-4]

Agriculture underwent several major changes in the 20th and 21st centuries. The [collectivisation of agriculture in the 1930s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectivization_in_the_Ukrainian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic) and privatisation at the end of the 20th century had particularly significant consequences. The [Russo-Ukrainian War](42085878.md) since 2014, and especially the full-scale [Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine) since 24 February 2022, also had a major impact, as production in parts of the country was greatly reduced. As of May 2024, about 30% of the total potential of the agricultural sector was destroyed, and almost 20% of agricultural land was occupied as a direct result of Russia's war against Ukraine.[^cite_note-May_2024_report-5] On the other hand, by 2024, Ukraine's agricultural exports had recovered to pre-war levels.[^cite_note-2024_statistics-6]

## Land use

Agricultural land covers 42 million hectares, or 70% of the country's total land area. 78.9% of agricultural land is [arable land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arable_land) and perennial plantations, 13.0% is [pastures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasture), 8.4% is [hayfields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hay). The highest proportion of arable land is in the Ukrainian regions of the [Pontic–Caspian steppe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontic%E2%80%93Caspian_steppe) (70–80%) and the [East European forest steppe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_European_forest_steppe) zones. Pastures are concentrated mainly in the [Carpathian Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpathian_Mountains), [Polissia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polissia) and the south-eastern steppe regions, while hayfields are located in the river valleys of the [Central European mixed forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_European_mixed_forests) and East European forest steppe zones.

### Crop production

Farms use various crop rotation schemes, sometimes with two, sometimes with four or more crops. An example of such a rotation scheme is fallow, winter wheat, winter wheat, sunflowers, spring barley and maize. [Fallow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallow) is used to maintain moisture levels. Sometimes the same crop is grown for several years in a row, particularly [alfalfa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfalfa) (lucerne).

#### Grains



Wheat field in [Rivne Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivne_Oblast) (2011)



Maize field behind a cow in a flowery meadow in [Luhansk Oblast](494904.md) (2011)

The main crops in agriculture are [cereals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cereals): winter and spring [wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat), [rye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rye), winter and spring [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley), [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize), [oats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oats), [buckwheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckwheat), [millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet) and [rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice). Grain farming plays an important role in Ukraine's agricultural sector, ensuring a stable supply of bread and bakery products to the population, as well as raw materials for industrial processing. The production, processing and export of grain in Ukraine generates significant cash flows to the budget and is an important area of employment for the country's population. In addition, the country's grain industry has significant development potential, primarily due to the availability of abundant land resources and a sufficient amount of skilled labour.

- Ukraine's main grain crop is winter wheat. The main areas of its cultivation are the forest steppe and the northern steppe regions. Spring wheat has lower yields compared to winter wheat and is mainly grown in the steppe regions of Ukraine with harsher winter conditions and almost no permanent snow cover. After a decline in wheat harvests in the 1990s, yields subsequently increased again.

- Barley is the second most important grain crop in Ukraine. Spring barley is grown in Polissia, and winter barley in the southern steppe and in the foothills of Crimea. Barley is the most important feed grain in Ukraine, with summer barley accounting for more than 90 per cent of the total.

- Maize is the third most important feed grain in Ukraine in terms of acreage. The best conditions for its cultivation are in the northern and central steppe and southern forest-steppe. More than half of the maize is used for silage. Most of the rest is used for poultry and pig feed.

- Winter rye is a valuable food crop. The main areas of its cultivation are Polissia and the western forest-steppe. Oats are common in the same areas as rye and serve as a complementary fodder crop.

- Sunflower is Ukraine's most important oilseed crop and has long been the most profitable crop. Originally, sunflowers were not grown on the same field more than once every seven years in order to prevent fungal diseases and maintain soil fertility. Later, cultivation became more intensive.

- Buckwheat has a large area of Ukraine devoted to its cultivation. The largest buckwheat crops are concentrated in Polissia and partly in the forest-steppe region.

- Millet is mainly grown in the forest-steppe and steppe regions.

- Rice is grown as a food crop on irrigated land in [Mykolaiv](494928.md), [Kherson](1273370.md) oblasts and in [Crimea](163045.md).

#### Potatoes and sugar beets

- [Potatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potatoes) are valuable, vitamin-rich food products that are raw materials for the food industry and valuable fodder. In terms of production, potatoes are second only to grain and are used as a food product, for technical processing into alcohol, starch, molasses and as animal feed. The main areas of potato production are Polissia and the forest-steppe of Ukraine, as well as the suburbs of large cities.

- Sugar beet production and acreage fell by half between 1994 and 2003. The acreage of sugar beet increased on privately owned plots. The main regions for growing sugar beets are the forest-steppe, northern steppe, and southern Polissia.

#### Vegetables

According to the 2024 report by the Center for Food and Land Use Research of the [Kyiv School of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyiv_School_of_Economics) (KSE),[^cite_note-KSE_2024-7] Ukraine produced more [cabbages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabbage) and [cucumbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cucumber) in total in 2021 than any of the top 9 vegetable-producing EU countries ([Spain](26667.md), [Poland](22936.md), the [Netherlands](21148.md), [Italy](14532.md), [Germany](11867.md), [France](5843419.md), [Greece](12108.md), [Romania](25445.md), and [Portugal](23033.md)).[^cite_note-KSE_2024-7]<sup>: 8–9 </sup> Amongst the other three major vegetable crops – [tomatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomato), [onions](51258.md), and [carrots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrot) – Ukrainian production volumes were also above the median values of these 9 top EU producer countries in 2021.[^cite_note-KSE_2024-7]<sup>: 8–9 </sup>

Nevertheless, the top EU producers had a higher yield per hectare (and thus higher productivity/effiency) for all five vegetables except cabbage (for which Ukrainian yields per hectare were very close to Italian yields).[^cite_note-KSE_2024-7]<sup>: 9 </sup> The gap in yields is highest in tomatoes and cucumbers, which might be explained by the fact that Ukraine has a much larger percentage of these plants grown in the open field rather than in protected [greenhouses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse) or [polytunnels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytunnel), as is common in the EU's top-producing countries such as the Netherlands.[^cite_note-KSE_2024-7]<sup>: 9 </sup>

### Livestock breeding

[Livestock breeding in Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livestock_breeding_in_Ukraine?action=edit\&redlink=1) has the place of a kind of ‘processing’ industry that transforms crop products into foodstuffs and technical raw materials. Livestock farming accounts for over 38% of gross agricultural output. Its main branches are cattle breeding, pig breeding, sheep breeding, and poultry farming. Livestock farming also includes [aquaculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquaculture_in_Ukraine?action=edit\&redlink=1), [beekeeping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beekeeping_in_Ukraine), and [silk farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_industry). In general, the objectives of animal husbandry are to produce high-quality food products and valuable raw materials for the food industry and light industry. The production process in animal husbandry is more mechanised and contributes to the transition of the industry to an industrial basis.

## History

[Geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Ukraine) has long influenced the [economy of the Ukrainian lands](31829.md). Rich fertile soils (such as [chornozem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernozem) areas) made the area a "[breadbasket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breadbasket)": for [ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece),[^cite_note-9] as well as for [early modern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_Europe),[^cite_note-10] later the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union),[^cite_note-11] and again Europe in the 21st century.[^cite_note-Driebergen-2]

### Prehistoric agriculture



3D model of the [Cucuteni–Trypillia culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cucuteni%E2%80%93Trypillia_culture) settlement of [Maidanetske](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maidanetske), c. 3700 BCE.

[^cite_note-12]

Main article: [Economy of the Cucuteni–Trypillia culture § Subsistence economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_Cucuteni%E2%80%93Trypillia_culture#Subsistence_economy)

The oldest evidence of large-scale agriculture on the modern territory of Ukraine (as well as [Moldova](19260.md) and [Romania](25445.md)) comes from the [Cucuteni–Trypillia culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cucuteni%E2%80%93Trypillia_culture) (abbreviated CT), a [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_Europe)–[Chalcolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcolithic) [archaeological culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological_culture) (c. 5050 to 2950 BCE). There is still much uncertainty and disagreement amongst scholars of this CT group, ranging from questions about the relative size of these settlements (the standard view being that these may have been home to thousands of people, with maximalists claiming tens of thousands, and minimalists only small-sized villages), their permanence versus their seasonality, and their degree of urbanisation versus agrarian rurality.[^cite_note-c580-13]

### Kievan Rus' (c. 880–1240)

This section is an excerpt from [Economy of Kievan Rus' § Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Kievan_Rus'#Agriculture).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Economy_of_Kievan_Rus%27\&action=edit)]



Spring [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley) in the field (pictured in 2015, modern [Ukraine](31750.md)). Archaeological evidence suggests northern communities were planting both winter and spring crops as early as the 11th and 12th centuries, sowing [winter rye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_rye) late in the year that sprouted once snows melted in spring.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200766-18]

&#x20;The southerners had longer growing seasons, and fertile [black soils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chornozem), and typically cultivated [millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet) instead of rye.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200766-18]

The main [cereal grain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cereal_grain) crops in Kievan Rus' were [rye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rye) in the north, [millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet) in the south, supplemented by [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley), [wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat), [buckwheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckwheat), and [oats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oats).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200766-18] [Peas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peas), [lentils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lentils), [flax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flax), and [hemp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemp) were also grown.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200766-18]

The lands of Kievan Rus' were primarily located in two climatic zones: the [Eurasian Steppe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_Steppe) (including parts of the [East European forest steppe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_European_forest_steppe) and [Ponto-Caspian steppe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponto-Caspian_steppe)) with the highly fertile [black soils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chornozem) south and west of Kiev[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200765-19] (encompassing most of present-day [Ukraine](31750.md)), and the forest zone[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200765-19] in the northwest (including the [Novgorod Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novgorod_Republic) and present-day [Belarus](3457.md)) and the northeast (including the region known as [Zalesye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zalesye), or "area beyond the forest", which became the core of [Vladimir-Suzdal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir-Suzdal)). Unlike the forest-steppes in the southwest, the northern forestlands (covered with [cedar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cedrus) and [birch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birch) trees, and more to the south [oak](39696.md) trees) were generally well-watered, with adequate precipitation and extensive river systems to sustain agriculture.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200765-19] However, the lands [north of the 50th parallel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/50th_parallel_north) (above Kiev) had short growing seasons, and lacked the highly fertile black soils of the southwest.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200765-19]

The northern climate always made agriculture precarious, never allowed for abundant production, and sometimes the farming system broke down.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200767-20] Several famines have been recorded in written sources, such as the 1024 famine in Suzdalia caused by a drought, prompting the inhabitants to buy grain from [Volga Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volga_Bulgaria).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200767-20] Another famine took place in the Rostov region and [Yaroslavl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaroslavl) in 1071.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200767-20] Throughout the year 1092, there was a drought in southern Kievan Rus', after which a late frost destroyed young crops and ruined the harvest, causing another famine.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200767-20]

In the forest-steppe zone, the steam farming system with two- and three-land [crop rotations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_rotation) was used, in the forest zone the [slash-and-burn agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash-and-burn_agriculture) system[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200765-19] and [shifting cultivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shifting_cultivation) (long [fallowing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallow)) system.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200765–66-21] Woods were cut down and burnt, thus freeing up arable land and fertilising the soil at the same time. In forest-steppe and steppe regions, the most common farming system was fallowing (crop rotation), in which the fertility of the land was restored naturally.[^cite_note-Economy_of_Kievan_Rus'_Osvita-22]

High labour productivity made it possible to produce more grain than was necessary to meet the biological needs of the population. This, as well as the availability of excellent pastures and hayfields, allowed feudal households to keep large numbers of livestock. For tilling the soil and growing crops, the most advanced tools of the time were used: [scythes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythes) (for mowing hay),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200766-18] [sickles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sickle) (for reaping),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200766-18] [hoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoe_\(tool\)), [spades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spade), [ards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ard_\(plough\)), [ploughs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plough), *[sokhas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sokha)*,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200766-18] [harrows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harrow_\(tool\)), [mattocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mattock),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200766-18] and others for digging or ploughing, which were sophisticated enough to ensure high yields – on average, slightly less than 8 centners of grain per hectare. A [flail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flail_\(tool\)) was used to [thresh the grain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threshing).[^cite_note-Economy_of_Kievan_Rus'_Osvita-22][^cite_note-Economy_of_Kievan_Rus'_\:2-23] Sometimes peasants formed collectives to share equipment and help each other with farm work.[^cite_note-Economy_of_Kievan_Rus'_\:2-23]

### Galicia–Volhynia (1199–1349)

This section is an excerpt from [Kingdom of Galicia–Volhynia § Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Galicia%E2%80%93Volhynia#Agriculture).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kingdom_of_Galicia%E2%80%93Volhynia\&action=edit)]

The economy of Galicia–Volhynia was predominantly a [subsistence economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsistence_economy). It was based on [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture), which was based on self-sufficient lands: the *[dvoryshche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dvoryshche?action=edit\&redlink=1)* ([Ukrainian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_language): дворище). These were rural agricultural communities, owned and run by a group of related families (remnants of the earlier [clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clan) system), comparable to the *[zadrugas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zadruga)* amongst the [South Slavs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Slavs). These economic units had their own arable land, hayfields, meadows, forests, foraging grounds and fish lakes. The main agricultural crops were [oats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oats) and [rye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rye), less so [wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat) and [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley). In addition, [animal husbandry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_husbandry) was developed, primarily [horse breeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse_breeding), as well as [sheep breeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheep_breeding) and [pig breeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig_breeding). Important components of the economy were [foraging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foraging): animal [hunting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunting), [honey hunting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honey_hunting), and [fishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing).

### Cossack Hetmanate (1649–1764)

This section is an excerpt from [Cossack Hetmanate § Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cossack_Hetmanate#Agriculture).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Cossack_Hetmanate\&action=edit)]

> "Ukraine is a [very fertile country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chornozem), as are [Ruthenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruthenian_Voivodeship) and [Podolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podolian_Voivodeship), and once the soil is cultivated, it yields abundant grains (...).[^cite_note-26] The large number of \[rivers and streams] indicates good soil quality. (...) Ukraine is also very rich in various livestock, game and fish; there is a lot of honey and wax, wood, which, in addition to ordinary consumption, is used for building houses. The inhabitants of Ukraine lack only wine and salt; the former they get from [Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hungary), [Transylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality_of_Transylvania_\(1570%E2%80%931711\)), [Wallachia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallachia) and [Moldavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moldavia); beer, honey and vodka are distilled from grain; those are very popular here. Salt they import from the [Wieliczka Salt Mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wieliczka_Salt_Mine) near [Kraków](16815.md), or [Pokuttia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pokuttia)."

— Pierre Chevalier, French officer (1663)

[^cite_note-27]

As the Cossack Hetmanate emerged out of the [Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth), agriculture remained the main branch of the economy. One of the main causes of the [Khmelnytsky Uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmelnytsky_Uprising) was the anti-feudal struggle of the domestic peasantry. Therefore, immediately after the formation of their own state, all the property of the old Polish magnate nobility was expropriated by the people. Magnates, nobility, and tenants were driven out, and their lands, livestock, and property were transferred to Cossacks, peasants, burghers, and the state administration. The legislation of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth lost its force and the peasants became free. The temporary partial return to the old feudal norms after the defeat in the [Battle of Berestechko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Berestechko) and the [Treaty of Bila Tserkva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Bila_Tserkva) only strengthened the resistance of the peasantry to the "hereditary lords". Finally, on the territory of the Ukrainian state, the *[folwark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folwark)* (Ukrainian: *filvarka*) manor system of management, the land ownership of the crown land, Polish and Ukrainian magnates and nobility, and the [Catholic Church](606848.md) were eliminated after the victory in the [Battle of Batih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Batih) (1652).

The main part of the liberated territories (and it was a significant land fund: the kingdom owned about 150 cities and towns, magnates and nobles owned about 1,500, and the Catholic Church – 50 estates), as well as uninhabited lands, passed to the state fund, which was owned by the *Military Treasury* is a component of the apparatus of the Hetman-Starshyna administration. The supreme administrator of the land was the hetman, locally it was managed by colonels and centurions. The lands of Orthodox monasteries and the higher clergy, small nobility, Cossacks and burghers remained in private ownership.

Personally, free peasants had to pay a tax to the *Military Treasury* in the form of a monetary rent. Peasants of free military villages considered the land they cultivated to be their property. In the second half of the 17th – at the beginning of the 18th century, it was freely inherited, given, sold, bought. In privately owned, temporary-conditional possessions, the peasants' right to use the land was limited, and when buying and selling land, only the right to its possession was transferred with existing coercions in favor of the land owners.

After the [Khmelnytsky Uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmelnytsky_Uprising), 80-90% of peasants owned land. According to the materials of the Russian general [Rumyantsev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyotr_Rumyantsev)'s description of "Little Russia", the peasants of the Starshyna, monastery, and government were divided into those who owned land and those who were landless. Allotment owners bequeathed land, leased it, bought and sold it, organized farms. The number of wealthy peasants, who concentrated a large part of allotment land and livestock, increased. The landless commoners either engaged in agriculture on senior, monastic, state land allocated to them for temporary use, or lived off the sale of labor.[^cite_note-28] Some landless peasants kept a lot of cattle, beehives, were engaged in crafts and trades. Some of them had up to 30 to 40 heads of cattle, 20 to 30 pigs, 30 to 40 horses, and up to 300 sheep. Some peasants, the so-called servants, did not have any farms and constantly lived in the foreman's estates either "for subsistence" or for an annual fee (2 to 10 karbovanets).

### Agriculture in Interwar Polish Ukraine (1918–1939)

This section is an excerpt from [Second Polish Republic § Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Polish_Republic#Agriculture).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_Polish_Republic\&action=edit)]

Statistically, the majority of citizens of the Second Polish Republic lived in the countryside (75% in 1921). Farmers made up 65% of the population. In 1929, agricultural production made up 65% of Poland's GNP.[^cite_note-Second_Polish_Republic_lex-29] After 123 years of partitions, regions of the country were very unevenly developed. The lands of the former [German Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire) were the most advanced; in [Greater Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Poland), [Upper Silesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Silesia) and [Pomerelia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pomerelia), farming and crops were on a Western European level.[^cite_note-Second_Polish_Republic_onet1-30] The situation was much worse in parts of [Congress Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_Poland), the [Eastern Borderlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Borderlands), and what was formerly [Galicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galicia_\(Eastern_Europe\)), where agriculture was quite backward and primitive, with a large number of small farms, unable to succeed in either the domestic or international market. Another problem was the overpopulation of the countryside, which resulted in chronic unemployment. Living conditions were so bad in several eastern regions, such as the counties inhabited by the [Hutsul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutsul) minority, that there was permanent starvation.[^cite_note-Second_Polish_Republic_przeglad-tygodnik-31] Farmers rebelled against the government (see: [1937 peasant strike in Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1937_peasant_strike_in_Poland)), and the situation began to change in the late 1930s, due to the construction of several factories for the [Central Industrial Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Industrial_Region_\(Poland\)), which gave employment to thousands of rural and small town residents.

### Interwar Soviet Ukraine (1919–1945)

Further information: [Collectivization in the Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectivization_in_the_Ukrainian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic)

In the 1930s, under pressure from Joseph Stalin, agriculture became collective in nature, despite resistance from Ukrainian farmers.[^cite_note-Driebergen-2] This reform led to the creation of large state farms and collective farms.&#x20;

This section is an excerpt from [Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic § Before 1945](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic#Before_1945).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ukrainian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic\&action=edit)]

At the onset of Soviet Ukraine, having largely inherited conditions from the Tsarist Empire, one of the biggest exporters of wheat in the world, the Ukrainian economy was still centered around [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture), with over 90% of the workforce being peasants.[^cite_note-32] In the 1920s, Soviet policies in Ukraine attached importance to developing the economy. The initial agenda, [War Communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_Communism), had prescribed total communisation and appropriation per quota of food from the people by force[^cite_note-33] - further economic damage and a [1921–1923 famine in Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1921%E2%80%931923_famine_in_Ukraine) claiming up to one million lives ensued. With the [New Economic Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Economic_Policy) and the partial introduction of free markets, an economic recovery followed.&#x20;

During the 1920s, the majority of Ukraine's economic enterprises was subordinated to the union centre.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEКульчицький2013156_(volume_2)-34] In 1921 Soviet Ukraine's authorities introduced the system of [khozraschet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khozraschet), which turned enterprises into autonomous subjects of the economy. Such enterprises were united into [trusts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trust_\(business\)), which represented separate branches of industry.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEКульчицький2013158-160_(volume_2)-35] At the same time, trusts were barred from paying taxes from their [fixed assets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_assets), and were instead obliged to deliver a specially introduced "industrial tax", which increased their dependence on the state budget. The economic system was distorted due to the prioritization of non-consumer production by the regime.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEКульчицький2013164-165_(volume_2)-36] The period of NEP saw the revival of credit and financial institutions, a currency reform and the organization of state trade and cooperation. Starting from 1925 the economic policies experienced a turn towards [industrialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrialization) and [collectivization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectivization).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEКульчицький2013169-170_(volume_2)-37]



Construction of the [Dnieper Hydroelectric Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dnieper_Hydroelectric_Station) in [Zaporizhzhia](1873579.md), 1931

After the death of Lenin and the consolidation of his power, Stalin was determined to accelerate industrialisation and reversed policy again. As heavy industry and wheat exports boomed, common people in rural areas were bearing a cost. Gradually escalating measures, from raised taxes, dispossession of property, and [forced deportations into Siberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_settlements_in_the_Soviet_Union#Deportations_of_1928%E2%80%931939) culminated in extremely high grain delivery quotas. Even though there is no evidence that agricultural yield could not feed the population at the time, four million Ukrainians were starved to death during the 1932–1933 [Holodomor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holodomor), while Moscow exported over a million tonnes of grain to the West,[^cite_note-38] decimating the population.[^cite_note-39]

In the aftermath of collectivization, the Soviet regime achieved a mode of coexistence with Ukraine's peasants through the employment of women in order to compensate for demographic losses during the famine, as well as [mechanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanization) of agriculture and the introduction of a [passport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passport) system.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEКульчицький2013543-552_(volume_2)-40] In the industrial sphere, Soviet Ukraine during the 1930s was dominated by [metallurgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallurgy) and [food industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_industry). Between 1928 and 1937 the industrial growth in Ukrainian SSR achieved record numbers worldwide. The number of workers grew almost threefold.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEКульчицький2013563_(volume_2)-41] For the first time in history, by the late 1930s, less than half of Soviet Ukraine's population was employed in agriculture. During that time the republic was Europe's second biggest producer of [pig iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig_iron) and took the fourth place worldwide in [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) production. At the same time, the development of Ukraine's industry repeated pre-revolutionary patterns, with most of its centers being concentrated in the [Donbas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donbas)-[Dnieper](380138.md) region.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEКульчицький2013574-575_(volume_2)-42]

### Cold War Soviet Ukraine (1945–1991)

This section is an excerpt from [Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic § Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic#Agriculture).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ukrainian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic\&action=edit)]

In 1945, agricultural production stood at only 40 percent of the 1940 level, even though the republic's territorial expansion had "increased the amount of [arable land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arable_land)".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMagocsi1996692-43] In contrast to the remarkable growth in the industrial sector,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMagocsi1996692–693-44] agriculture continued in Ukraine, as in the rest of the Soviet Union, to function as the economy's [Achilles heel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achilles_heel). Despite the human toll of [collectivisation of agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectivization) in the Soviet Union, especially in Ukraine, Soviet planners still believed in the effectiveness of collective farming. The old system was reestablished; the numbers of [collective farms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolkhoz) in Ukraine increased from 28 thousand in 1940 to 33 thousand in 1949, comprising 45 million hectares; the numbers of [state farms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovkhoz) barely increased, standing at 935 in 1950, comprising 12.1 million hectares. By the end of the [Fourth Five-Year Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Five-Year_Plan_\(Soviet_Union\)) (in 1950) and the [Fifth Five-Year Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_Five-Year_Plan_\(Soviet_Union\)) (in 1955), agricultural output still stood far lower than the 1940 level. The slow changes in agriculture can be explained by the low productivity in collective farms, and by bad weather-conditions, which the Soviet planning system could not effectively respond to. Grain for human consumption in the post-war years decreased, this in turn led to frequent and severe food shortages.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMagocsi1996693-45]

The increase of [Soviet agricultural production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_of_the_Soviet_Union) was tremendous, however, the Soviet Ukrainians still experienced food shortages due to the inefficiencies of a [highly centralised economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planned_economy). During the peak of Soviet-Ukrainian agriculture output in the 1950s and early-to-mid-1960s, human consumption in Ukraine, and in the rest of the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union), actually experienced short intervals of decrease. There are many reasons for this inefficiency, but its origins can be traced back to the single-purchaser and -producer [market system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_system) set up by [Joseph Stalin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMagocsi1996706-46] Khrushchev tried to improve the agricultural situation in the Soviet Union by expanding the total crop size – for instance, in the Ukrainian SSR alone "the amount of land planted with [corn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize) grew by 600 percent". At the height of this policy, between 1959 and 1963, one-third of Ukrainian [arable land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arable_land) grew this crop. This policy decreased the total production of wheat and [rye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rye); Khrushchev had anticipated this, and the production of wheat and rye moved to [Soviet Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Central_Asia) as part of the [Virgin Lands Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_Lands_Campaign). Khrushchev's agricultural policy failed, and in 1963 the Soviet Union had to import food from abroad. The total level of agricultural productivity in Ukraine decreased sharply during this period, but recovered in the 1970s and 1980s during [Leonid Brezhnev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Brezhnev)'s rule.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMagocsi1996708-47] A major role in Ukraine's agriculture during Soviet times was played by personal land plots, which were confiscated from peasant during Khrushchev's rule. Following their restoration to villagers by the late 1960s, they produced one-quarter of all vegetables and one-third of the total meat, lard, milk and fruit production, as well as more than half of all potatoes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEКульчицький2013258_(volume_3)-48] The presence of such plots in Ukraine ameliorated the [deficit of goods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortage_economy) in the republic during the later years of Soviet rule, when rapid [urbanisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanisation) produced an additional stress on agricultural producers.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEКульчицький2013267_(volume_3)-49]

In 1986 an attempt to reform Soviet agriculture was made through the introduction of Agroproms, which were tasked with controlling the production of agricultural goods. This system failed to bring results, and supply difficulties continued to increase. In March 1988 collective farmers were allowed to rent land plots for a period of up to 50 years and received control over goods produced in this way. However, local administration hindered the introduction of that reform, being supported by the majority of peasants themselves.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEКульчицький2013304_(volume_3)-50]

### 1990s in independent Ukraine

At the end of the 1990s, a modernisation process took place. After the [dissolution of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union) and the [independence of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_of_Ukraine) in 1991, former [kolkhoz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolkhoz) (kolhosp) farming collectives were abolished, and every Ukrainian citizen was granted 2 hectares of resellable land according to the [Constitution of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Ukraine).[^cite_note-Driebergen-2] To prevent speculators and mafia-like businessmen from snapping up the land at a bargain price – as had happened with heavy industry – a moratorium was imposed (2002): a ban on sales of former kolhosp farmlands.[^cite_note-Driebergen-2] As a result, agricultural lands ended up divided among 7 million Ukrainians, resulting in highly fragmented ownership.[^cite_note-Driebergen-2] However, much of the land remained in the hands of the state. The Ukrainians mostly leased the land to new private agricultural organisations. With the loss of state support for private farmers, agriculture faced declining production figures. The already shrinking livestock population declined further, the area of agricultural land decreased and grain production fell by 50%. There was no money to invest in modern machinery. Only the cultivation of sunflowers remained stable. At the same time, farmers began to grow other crops and efficiency was increased in certain sectors.

### Land reform after 2000

Main article: [Land reform in Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reform_in_Ukraine)



Farmlands in [Odesa Oblast](61438266.md) (2006)

After 2000, [land reform in Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reform_in_Ukraine) was initiated. Due to undesirable effects of the first agricultural reform after the collapse of the Soviet Union, a moratorium on the sale of agricultural land was imposed in 2002 to put a stop to the large-scale purchase of agricultural land by oligarchs. Since at least 2011, farmland was remaining as the only major asset in Ukraine that was not [privatised](24661.md).[^cite_note-UkrAgroConsult-51]

An October 2013 report by the [Cabinet of Ministers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_of_Ministers) of Ukraine identified the following problems in the agricultural sector, that were to be addressed in the 2013–2020 period:[^cite_note-806-2013-52]

- uneven development of various forms of economic activity, accompanied by a weakening of the position of medium-sized agricultural producers as a result of the creation of formally identical but unequal economic conditions for agricultural producers of different sizes and social burdens;

- lack of motivation for cooperation and consolidation of small agricultural producers within rural communities, weakening of the economic basis for the development of rural communities;

- instability of the competitive position of domestic agricultural products on foreign markets due to the incomplete process of adaptation to European requirements for quality and food safety;

- slow pace of technical and technological modernisation of production;

- risks of increased production costs due to the growing wear and tear of equipment, the prevalence of outdated technologies, and the increasing cost of non-renewable natural resources in the production cost structure of domestic agricultural products;

- losses of such products due to imperfections in the logistics system for their storage and the infrastructure of the agricultural market as a whole;

- lack of motivation among agricultural producers to comply with agro-ecological requirements;

- limited capacity of the domestic market for agricultural products, due to low purchasing power of the population;



[Flood-meadows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flood-meadow) with grazing cattle and haystacks in [Kutyshche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kutyshche), [Lviv Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lviv_Oblast) (2018)

- insufficient effectiveness of self-organisation and self-regulation of the agricultural products market, difficulty for agricultural producers in developing a consolidated position on the protection of their interests;

- lack of information among a significant proportion of agricultural producers about market conditions and the conditions for doing business in the sector;

- incompleteness of [land reform in Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reform_in_Ukraine).

  [^cite_note-806-2013-52]

In March 2020, the Ukrainian parliament lifted a ban on the sale of farmland.[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54] The land market was fully opened for the first time since independence on 1 July 2021.[^cite_note-55][^cite_note-rferl.org-1] In 2020, partly under pressure from the IMF, which would only provide funding if agriculture was reformed, it became possible again to sell agricultural land, albeit temporarily (until 1 January 2024) and subject to conditions such as a maximum size of 100 hectares. However, the 2020 reform is controversial.[^cite_note-rfe22aug21-56] Meanwhile, the production of grains and oilseeds has increasingly come under the control of large agricultural companies established after 2000. The transition to a more market-oriented environment seems to have been relatively successful, but many smaller companies have disappeared or are projected to disappear. Existing smaller companies often face financial problems, which are frequently resolved by attracting an investor with market knowledge and capital. The general effect has been that farmers lost control over their businesses.[^cite_note-Driebergen-2]

According to some researchers, by 2024 approximately 28% of agricultural land was managed by foreign investors such as [NCH Capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCH_Capital), [Vanguard Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanguard_Group), Kopernik Global Investors, [BNP Asset Management Holding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BNP_Paribas#BNP_Asset_Management_Holding), NN Investment Partners Holdings (subsidiary of [Goldman Sachs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldman_Sachs)), and [Norges Bank Investment Management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norges_Bank).[^cite_note-Oak2023-57]

Since 2014, agricultural activities in Ukraine have been severely disrupted by the illegal [Russian annexation of Crimea](42228673.md), and the subsequent outbreak of the [War in Donbas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_Donbas), starting the [Russo-Ukrainian War](42085878.md). Around the same time, in May 2014, the [Colorado potato beetle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_potato_beetle) was becoming something of a plague for farmers in eastern Ukraine. During the [2014 pro-Russian unrest in Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_pro-Russian_unrest_in_Ukraine), the word *kolorady*, from the Ukrainian and Russian term for Colorado beetle ([Ukrainian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_language): жук колорадський, [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): колорадский жук), gained popularity among Ukrainians as a derogatory term to describe pro-Russian separatists in the [Donetsk](494889.md) and [Luhansk](494904.md) Oblasts (provinces) of [Eastern Ukraine](13065940.md). The nickname reflects the similarity of black and orange stripes on [St. George's ribbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribbon_of_Saint_George) worn by many of the separatists.[^cite_note-59]

## Statistical overviews



A Ukrainian T-150K tractor built by the [Kharkiv Tractor Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Tractor_Plant) (2011)

### 2000s

In 2008, agriculture accounted for 8.29% of Ukraine's GDP and by 2012 had grown to 10.43% of the GDP. Agriculture accounted for \$13.98 billion of value added to the economy of Ukraine in 2012. Despite being a top 10 world producer of several crops such as wheat and corn Ukraine still only ranks 24 out of 112 nations measured in terms of overall agricultural production.[^cite_note-61][^cite_note-62]

Professionalised, scientific [breeding of barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley_breeding) began in 1910 and as of 2003 supplied improved [cultivars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley_cultivar) to the country.[^cite_note-Bothmer-et-al-2003-63]<sup>: 34 </sup>

### 2010s

In 2011, Ukraine was the world's largest producer of [sunflower oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunflower_oil),[^cite_note-UkrInform-64] a major global producer of grain and sugar, and a potential global player on meat and dairy markets. It was one of the largest producers of nuts. Ukraine produced more natural [honey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honey)[^cite_note-65] than any other European country and was one of the world's largest honey producers. An estimated 1.5% of its population was involved in honey production, therefore Ukraine had the highest honey per capita production rate in the world.[^cite_note-66] Because Ukraine possesses 30% of the world's richest [black soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernozem), makes its agricultural industry have huge potential.

At the beginning of the 21st century, Ukraine's agricultural industry was highly profitable, generating profit margins of 40–60%.[^cite_note-UkrAgroConsult-51] Analysts indicated that the sector had the potential to increase its output by up to four times.[^cite_note-Kra-67] Ukraine was the world's 6th largest, 5th if not including the EU as a separate state, producer of corn in the world and the 3rd largest corn exporter in the world. In 2012, Ukraine signed a contract with China, the world's largest importer of corn, to supply China with 3 million tonnes of corn annually at market price. The deal included a \$3 billion line of credit extension from China to Ukraine.[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69]

In 2014, Ukraine's total grain crop was estimated to be a record 64 million metric tons. In 2014, Ukraine lost control over portions of several regions to Russia, followed by the start of [the war](42085878.md) in Donbas and the [annexation of Crimea by the Russian Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annexation_of_Crimea_by_the_Russian_Federation),[^cite_note-71] hence the actual available crop yield was closer to 60.5 million metric tons. Due to the decline of the metallurgy industry, which was previously Ukraine's top export category, agricultural products have become Ukraine's largest export category as a result of the war in Donbas.[^cite_note-72]

- 21 million 218 thousand tonnes of corn were harvested;

### 2024

As of the end of May 2024, according to a statement by the State Secretary of the Ministry of Agrarian Policy and Food of Ukraine, as a result of [full-scale war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine), about 30% of the total potential of the agricultural sector was destroyed, and almost 20% of agricultural land was occupied.[^cite_note-May_2024_report-5] In general, in the two years since the start of the full-scale invasion, Ukraine's agricultural sector has suffered direct losses of more than US\$10 billion.[^cite_note-s781-78]

In 2024, Ukraine reached its pre-war export levels of \$24.5 billion, which accounted for 59% of total exports, according to a January 2025 Ministry of Agrarian Policy report.[^cite_note-2024_statistics-6] In total, Ukraine exported 78.3 million tonnes of agricultural products in 2024.[^cite_note-2024_statistics-6] For comparison: in 2021, a historic record was set for agricultural exports, reaching \$27.7 billion.[^cite_note-2024_statistics-6]
