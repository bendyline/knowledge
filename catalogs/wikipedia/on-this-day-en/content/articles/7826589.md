**Jihadism** is the ideology of modern, armed [militant](310790.md) [Islamic movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_aspects_of_Islam) that seek to [establish states based on Islamic principles](2646438.md).[^cite_note-Ahmad_2024-1][^cite_note-CTC-Sentinel_2024-2] In a narrower sense, it refers to the belief that armed confrontation is an efficient and theologically legitimate method of sociopolitical change towards an [Islamic system of governance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_governance).[^cite_note-Sedgwick_2015-3][^cite_note-Ashour_2011-4] The term "jihadism" has been applied to various [Islamic extremist](8734632.md) and/or [Islamist](15012.md) individuals and organizations with [militant](310790.md) ideologies based on the classical [Islamic](6037917.md) notion of *[lesser jihad](16203.md)*.[^cite_note-9]

Jihadism has its roots in the late 19th- and early 20th-century ideological developments of [Islamic revivalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_revival), which further developed into [Qutbism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qutbism) and [Salafi jihadism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism) related ideologies during the 20th and 21st centuries.[^cite_note-Poljarevic_2021-6][^cite_note-Aydinli2018-1-10][^cite_note-Jalal_2009-11][^cite_note-French_2020-12] Jihadist ideologues envision *[jihad](16203.md)* as a "revolutionary struggle" against the [international order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_order) to unite the [Muslim world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world) under [Islamic law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_law).[^cite_note-auto-13]

The Islamist organizations that participated in the [Soviet–Afghan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet%E2%80%93Afghan_War) of 1979 to 1989 reinforced the rise of jihadism, which has since propagated during various [armed conflicts](2346678.md).[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15] Jihadism rose in prominence after the 1990s; by one estimate, 5 percent of civil wars involved jihadist groups in 1990, but this grew to more than 40 percent by 2014.[^cite_note-16] Conflicts involving jihadist groups tend to be particularly intractable.[^cite_note-17] With the rise of the [Islamic State](9087364.md) (IS) militant group in 2014—which a large contingent of Jihadist groups have opposed—large numbers of [foreign Muslim volunteers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_fighters_in_the_Syrian_and_Iraqi_Civil_Wars) came from abroad to join the militant cause in Syria and Iraq.[^cite_note-23]

French political scientist and professor [Gilles Kepel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilles_Kepel) also identified a specific [Salafist version of jihadism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism) in the 1990s.[^cite_note-29] Jihadism with an international, [pan-Islamist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Islamism) scope is also known as global jihadism.[^cite_note-32] The term has also been invoked to retroactively characterise the military campaigns of historic [Islamic empires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_empires),[^cite_note-The_End_of_the_Jihad_State-33][^cite_note-books.google.co.uk-34] and the later [Fula jihads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fula_jihads) in West Africa in the 18th and 19th centuries.[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36]

## Terminology

Main article: [Jihad](16203.md)



[Jihadist variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jihadist_flag) of the [Black Standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Standard) as used by various [Islamist organizations](15012.md) since the late 1990s, which consists of the *[Shahada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahada)* in white script centered on a black background

The concept of *[jihad](16203.md)* ("exerting"/"striving"/"struggling") is fundamental to Islam and has multiple uses, with *[greater jihad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_Islam#Jihad)* (internal *jihad*), meaning [internal struggle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introspection) against evil in oneself, and *[lesser jihad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_Islam#Jihad)* (external *jihad*), which is further subdivided into *[jihad of the pen/tongue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_missionary_activity)* (debate or persuasion) and *[jihad of the sword](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_war)* (warfare). The latter form of *jihad* has meant conquest and conversion in the classical Islamic interpretation, usually excepting [followers of other monotheistic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People_of_the_Book).[^cite_note-DeLong-Bas_2018-37][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBonner200613-38][^cite_note-ER-39] [Modernist Islamic scholars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_modernism) generally equate military *jihad* with defensive warfare.[^cite_note-40][^cite_note-hallaq334-41] Much of contemporary Muslim opinion considers internal *jihad* to have primacy over external *jihad* in the Islamic tradition, while many Western writers favor the opposite view.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBonner200613-38] Today, the word *jihad* is often used without religious connotations, like the English term *[crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusade)*.

The term "jihadism" has been in use since the 1990s, more widely in the aftermath of the [9/11 attacks](5058690.md) in the United States.[^cite_note-BBC-What-2014-42] It was first used by the Indian and Pakistani [mass media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_media), and by French academics, who used the more exact term "[jihadist-Salafist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism)".[^cite_note-43] Historian David A. Charters defines "jihadism" as "a revolutionary program whose ideology promises radical social change in the [Muslim world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world)... \[with] a central role to *[jihad](16203.md)* as an armed political struggle to overthrow "[apostate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murtadd)" regimes, to expel their [infidel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kafir) allies, and thus to restore [Muslim lands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dar_al-islam) to governance by Islamic principles."[^cite_note-auto-13] According to [Reuven Firestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reuven_Firestone), the term "jihadism" as commonly used in the [Western world](21208200.md) describes "militant Islamic movements that are perceived as existentially threatening to the West."[^cite_note-ccnrm-263-44]

David Romano, researcher of political science at [McGill University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McGill_University) in [Montreal, Quebec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreal,_Quebec), has defined his use of the term as referring to "an individual or political movement that primarily focuses its attention, discourse, and activities on the conduct of a violent, uncompromising campaign that they term a *jihad*".[^cite_note-romano-45] Following Daniel Kimmage, he distinguishes the jihadist discourse of jihad as a global project to remake the world from the resistance discourse of groups such as [Hezbollah](13919.md), which is framed as a regional project against a specific enemy.[^cite_note-romano-45]

"Jihadism" has been defined otherwise as a [neologism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neologism) for [militant](310790.md), predominantly [Sunnī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) [Islamic movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_aspects_of_Islam) that use [ideologically motivated violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violent_extremism) to defend the *[Ummah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ummah)* (the collective [Muslim world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world)) from foreign [non-Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kafir) and those that they perceive as [domestic infidels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_other_religions).[^cite_note-CTC-Sentinel_2024-2][^cite_note-46] The term "jihadist globalism" is also often used in relation to [Islamic terrorism](566821.md) as a [globalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globalism) ideology, and more broadly to the [war on terror](13425800.md).[^cite_note-47] Austrian-American academic [Manfred B. Steger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manfred_B._Steger), Professor of [Sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology) at [University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Hawai%CA%BBi_at_M%C4%81noa), proposed an extension of the term "jihadist globalism" to apply to all extremely violent strains of religiously influenced ideologies that articulate the global imaginary into concrete political agendas and terrorist strategies; these include [al-Qaeda](1921.md), [Jemaah Islamiyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jemaah_Islamiyah), [Hamas](13913.md), and [Hezbollah](13919.md), which he finds "today's most spectacular manifestation of religious globalism".[^cite_note-48]

According to Jewish-American [political scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science) [Barak Mendelsohn](https://www.haverford.edu/users/bmendels), "the overwhelming majority of [Muslims](19541.md) reject jihadi views of Islam. Furthermore, as the cases of [Saudi](349303.md) and [other Gulf regimes](3088555.md) show, states may gain domestic legitimacy through economic development and social change, rather than based on religion and piety".[^cite_note-CTC-Sentinel_2024-2] Many Muslims do not use the terms "jihadism" or "jihadist", disliking the association of illegitimate violence with a noble religious concept, and instead prefer the use of delegitimising terms such as "deviants".[^cite_note-BBC-What-2014-42][^cite_note-50] [Maajid Nawaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maajid_Nawaz), founder and chairman of the anti-extremism think tank [Quilliam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quilliam_\(think_tank\)), defines jihadism as a violent subset of [Islamism](15012.md): "Islamism \[is] the desire to impose any version of Islam over any society. Jihadism is the attempt to do so by force."[^cite_note-dailybeast-51]

"Jihad Cool" is a term for the [rebranding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebranding) of militant jihadism as fashionable, or "[cool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cool_\(aesthetic\))", to younger people through [consumer culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_culture), social media, magazines,[^cite_note-52] [Rap videos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jihadism_and_rap),[^cite_note-53] toys, [propaganda videos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda_through_media),[^cite_note-54] and other means.[^cite_note-npr-55][^cite_note-56] It is a [subculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subculture) mainly applied to individuals in [developed nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developed_country) who are recruited to travel to conflict zones on jihad. For example, jihadi rap videos make participants look "more [MTV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MTV) than Mosque", according to [NPR](29697232.md), which was the first to report on the phenomenon in 2010.[^cite_note-npr-55] To justify their acts of [religious violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_violence), jihadist individuals and networks resort to the nonbinding genre of Islamic legal literature (*[fatwa](11259.md)*) developed by Salafi-jihadist legal authorities, whose legal writings are shared and spread via the Internet.[^cite_note-57]

[Islamic extremism](8734632.md) dates back to the [early history of Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_history_of_Islam) with the emergence of the [Kharijites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharijites) in the 7th century CE.[^cite_note-Izutsu_2006-58] The original schism between [Kharijites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharijites) and [Shīʿas](26961.md) among Muslims was disputed over the [political and religious succession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Succession_to_Muhammad) to the guidance of the Muslim community (*[Ummah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ummah)*) after the death of the [Islamic prophet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_prophet) [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad).[^cite_note-Izutsu_2006-58] From their essentially political position, the Kharijites developed extreme doctrines that set them apart from both mainstream Sunnī and Shīʿa Muslims.[^cite_note-Izutsu_2006-58] Shīʿas believe [ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali) is the true successor to Muhammad, while Sunnīs consider [Abu Bakr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Bakr) to hold that position. The Kharijites broke away from both the Shīʿas and the Sunnīs during the [First Fitna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Fitna) (the first Islamic Civil War);[^cite_note-Izutsu_2006-58] they were particularly noted for adopting a radical approach to *[takfīr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takfir)* ([excommunication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excommunication)), whereby they declared both Sunnī and Shīʿa Muslims to be either [infidels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kafir) (*kuffār*) or [false Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munafiq) (*munāfiḳūn*), and therefore deemed them [worthy of death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_punishment_in_Islam) for their perceived [apostasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostasy_in_Islam) (*ridda*).[^cite_note-Izutsu_2006-58][^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60]

[Sayyid Qutb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyid_Qutb), an Egyptian Islamist ideologue and a prominent leader of the [Muslim Brotherhood](20742.md) in Egypt, was an influential promoter of the [Pan-Islamist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Islamism) [ideology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology) during the 1960s.[^cite_note-Polk_2018-64] When he was executed by the [Egyptian government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_government) under the [regime of Gamal Abdel Nasser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Egypt_under_Gamal_Abdel_Nasser), [Ayman al-Zawahiri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayman_al-Zawahiri) formed [Egyptian Islamic Jihad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_Islamic_Jihad), an organization which seeks to replace the government with an Islamic state that would reflect Qutb's ideas about the [Islamic revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_revival) that he yearned for.[^cite_note-65] The [Qutbist ideology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qutbism) has been influential among jihadist movements and [Islamic terrorists](566821.md) who seek to overthrow secular governments, most notably [Osama bin Laden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osama_bin_Laden) and Ayman al-Zawahiri of [al-Qaeda](1921.md),[^cite_note-Gallagher_2021-61][^cite_note-Aydinli2018-2-62][^cite_note-Moussalli_2012-63] as well as the [Salafi-jihadist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism) terrorist group [ISIL/ISIS/IS/Daesh](9087364.md).[^cite_note-Baele_2019-66] Moreover, Qutb's books have been frequently been cited by Osama bin Laden and [Anwar al-Awlaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anwar_al-Awlaki).[^cite_note-NYT:_path_to_terror-67][^cite_note-Irwin-68][^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71][^cite_note-72]

[Sayyid Qutb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyid_Qutb) could be said to have founded the actual movement of [radical Islam](8734632.md).[^cite_note-Cook_2015-8][^cite_note-Moussalli_2012-63][^cite_note-Polk_2018-64] Unlike the other Islamic thinkers who have been mentioned above, Qutb was not an [apologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_apologetics).[^cite_note-Cook_2015-8] He was a prominent leader of the Muslim Brotherhood and a highly influential Islamist ideologue,[^cite_note-Cook_2015-8][^cite_note-Moussalli_2012-63] and the first to articulate these anathemizing principles in his magnum opus *Fī ẓilāl al-Qurʾān* (*In the shade of the Qurʾān*) and his 1966 manifesto *[Maʿālim fīl-ṭarīq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milestones_\(book\))* (*Milestones*), which lead to his execution by the [Egyptian government of Gamal Abdel Nasser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Egypt_under_Gamal_Abdel_Nasser) in 1966.[^cite_note-Cook_2015-8][^cite_note-73] Other [Salafi movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_movement) in the [MENA region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East_and_North_Africa) and across the [Muslim world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world) adopted many of his Islamist principles.[^cite_note-Cook_2015-8][^cite_note-Moussalli_2012-63]

According to Qutb, the [Muslim community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ummah) (*Ummah*) has been extinct for several centuries and it has also reverted to *[jahiliyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahiliyah)* (the pre-Islamic age of ignorance) because those who call themselves "[Muslims](19541.md)" have failed to follow the [Islamic law](28840.md) (*sharīʿa*).[^cite_note-Cook_2015-8][^cite_note-Moussalli_2012-63] In order to restore Islam, bring back its days of glory, and free the Muslims from the clasps of ignorance, Qutb proposed the [rejection and shunning of modern society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactionary), establishing a [vanguard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanguard) which was modeled after the [early Muslim generations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salaf) (*Salaf*), [preaching Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dawah), and bracing oneself for [poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty) or even bracing oneself for death in preparation for *[jihad](16203.md)* against what he perceived was a *jahili* government/society, and the overthrow of them.[^cite_note-Cook_2015-8][^cite_note-Moussalli_2012-63] [Qutbism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qutbism), the radical Islamist ideology which is derived from the ideas of Qutb,[^cite_note-Moussalli_2012-63] was denounced by many prominent [Muslim scholars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulama) as well as by other members of the Egyptian [Muslim Brotherhood](20742.md), like [Yusuf al-Qaradawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yusuf_al-Qaradawi).

## Sunni jihadism

Main articles: [Islamic revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_revival) and [Salafi jihadism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism)



[Jihadist variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jihadist_flag) of the [Black Standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Standard) as used by [Caucasian jihadists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Mujahideen_in_Chechnya) in 2002 displays the phrase *[al-jihād](16203.md) [fī sabīlillāh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fi_sabilillah)* above the *[takbīr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takbir)* and two crossed [swords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scimitar)

According to [Rudolph F. Peters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolph_F._Peters), contemporary traditionalist Muslims "copy phrases of the classical works on [fiqh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiqh)" in their writings on jihad; [Islamic modernists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_modernism) "emphasize the defensive aspect of jihad, regarding it as tantamount to *[bellum justum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_war_theory)* in modern international law; and the contemporary fundamentalists ([Abul A'la Maududi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abul_A'la_Maududi), [Sayyid Qutb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyid_Qutb), [Abdullah Azzam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdullah_Azzam), etc.) view it as a struggle for the expansion of Islam and the realization of Islamic ideals."[^cite_note-Petersjihad150-74]

Some of the earlier [Muslim scholars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulama) and [theologians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_theology) who had profound influence on [Islamic fundamentalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_fundamentalism) and the ideology of contemporary jihadism include the medieval Muslim thinkers [Ibn Taymiyyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Taymiyyah), [Ibn Kathir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Kathir), and [Muhammad ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Abd_al-Wahhab), alongside the modern Islamist ideologues [Muhammad Rashid Rida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashid_Rida), Sayyid Qutb, and Abul A'la Maududi.[^cite_note-Badara_2017-7][^cite_note-Jalal_2009-11][^cite_note-Homegrown_2021-30][^cite_note-75][^cite_note-76] Jihad has been propagated in modern fundamentalism beginning in the late 19th century, an ideology that arose in the context of struggles against [colonial powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_colonialism) in [North Africa](21714.md) at that time, as in the [Mahdist War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahdist_War) in Sudan, and notably in the mid-20th century by [Islamic revivalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_revival) authors such as Sayyid Qutb and Abul Ala Maududi.[^cite_note-77]



Egyptian Muslim scholar [Sayyid Qutb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyid_Qutb) through his prison-writings constituted the ideological basis of the [Salafi-jihadist movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism).

[^cite_note-78]

The term "jihadism" has arisen in the 2000s to refer to the contemporary jihadist movements, the development of which was in retrospect traced to [developments of Salafism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_movement) paired with the origins of [al-Qaeda](1921.md) in the [Soviet–Afghan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet%E2%80%93Afghan_War) during the 1980s. Forerunners of [Salafi jihadism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism) principally include Egyptian militant scholar and theoretician [Sayyid Qutb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyid_Qutb), who developed "the intellectual underpinnings" in the 1950s, for what would later become the doctrine of most Salafi-jihadist terrorist organizations around the world, including [al-Qaeda](1921.md) and [Islamic State](9087364.md).[^cite_note-NYT:_path_to_terror-67][^cite_note-Irwin2-79][^cite_note-80][^cite_note-81][^cite_note-82] Going radically further than his predecessors, Qutb called upon Muslims to form an ideologically committed vanguard that would wage armed *[jihad](16203.md)* against the [secular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secular_state), [democratic states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_democracy) and Western-allied governments in the [Arab world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world), until the restoration of [Islamic rule](28840.md).[^cite_note-auto3-83][^cite_note-Tran2-84] [Ayman al-Zawahiri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayman_al-Zawahiri), the Egyptian-born [pan-Islamist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Islamism) militant and physician who was second in command and co-founder of [al-Qaeda](1921.md), called Qutb "the most prominent theoretician of the fundamentalist movements".[^cite_note-85]

The [Soviet–Afghan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet%E2%80%93Afghan_War) (1979–1989) is said to have "amplified the jihadist tendency from a fringe phenomenon to a major force in the [Muslim world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world)."[^cite_note-86] It served to produce foot soldiers, leadership, and organization. [Abdullah Yusuf Azzam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdullah_Yusuf_Azzam) provided propaganda for the Afghan cause. After the war, veteran jihadists returned to their home countries, and from there would disperse to other sites of conflict involving Muslim populations, such as [Algeria](358.md), [Bosnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnia), and [Chechnya](6095.md), creating a "transnational jihadist stream."[^cite_note-87]

An explanation for jihadist willingness to kill civilians and self-professed Muslims on the grounds that they were actually [apostates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostasy_in_Islam) (*[takfīr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takfir)*) is the vastly reduced influence of the traditional diverse class of *[ulama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulama)*, often highly educated [Islamic jurists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faq%C4%ABh). In "the vast majority" of Muslim countries during the post-colonial world of the 1950s and 1960s, the private religious endowments (*[awqāf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waqf)*) that had supported the independence of Islamic scholars and jurists for centuries were taken over by the state. The jurists were made salaried employees and the nationalist rulers naturally encouraged their employees (and their employees' interpretations of Islam) to serve the rulers' interests. Inevitably, the jurists came to be seen by the Muslim public as doing this.[^cite_note-ptiI-aef-6-88]

Into this vacuum of religious authority came [aggressive proselytizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_propagation_of_conservative_Sunni_Islam), funded by tens of billions of dollars of [petroleum-export money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petro-Islam) from [Saudi Arabia](349303.md).[^cite_note-Kepel51-89] The version of Islam being propagated (Saudi doctrine of [Wahhabism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wahhabism)) billed itself as a return to pristine, simple, straightforward Islam,[^cite_note-ptiI-aef-8-90] not one [school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhhab) among many, and not interpreting [Islamic law](28840.md) historically or contextually, but as the one, orthodox "straight path" of Islam.[^cite_note-ptiI-aef-8-90] Unlike the traditional teachings of the jurists, who tolerated and even celebrated divergent opinions and schools of thought and kept extremism marginalized, Wahhabism had "extreme hostility" to "[any sectarian divisions within Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_schools_and_branches)".[^cite_note-ptiI-aef-8-90]

### Salafi jihadism

Further information: [Afghanistan conflict (1978–present)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghanistan_conflict_\(1978%E2%80%93present\)), [Arab Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Cold_War), [Arab–Iranian conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab%E2%80%93Iranian_conflict), [Arab–Israeli conflict](7960202.md), [Arab Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Spring), [Arab Winter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Winter), [International propagation of Salafism and Wahhabism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_propagation_of_Salafism_and_Wahhabism), and [War on terror](13425800.md)

The [Egyptian Islamist movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_extremism_in_20th-century_Egypt) of the 1950s are generally considered to be the precursors of contemporary [Salafi-jihadist groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism).[^cite_note-farid-misnomer-91] The theological doctrines of the Syrian-Egyptian Islamic scholar [Rashid Rida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashid_Rida) (1865–1935) greatly influenced these movements. Amongst his notable ideas included reviving the traditions of the [early Muslim generations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salaf) (*Salaf*), as well ridding the [Muslim world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world) of Western influences and *[jahiliyyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahiliyyah)* (pre-Islamic ignorance) by specifically looking up to the model of *[Khulafa Rashidun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_Caliphate)*. Rida's ideas would set the foundations of future Salafi-Jihadist movements and greatly influence Islamists like [Hassan al-Banna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hassan_al-Banna), [Sayyid Qutb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyid_Qutb), and other [Muslim fundamentalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_fundamentalism) figures.[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94][^cite_note-95] Rida's treatises laid the theological framework of future militants who would eventually establish the [Salafi-jihadist movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism).[^cite_note-auto3-83][^cite_note-Tran2-84]

In 2003, [CIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIA) officer [Marc Sageman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc_Sageman) described [Salafi jihadism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism) as a "Muslim [revivalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revivalist_\(person\)) social movement" with "roots in Egypt". According to Sageman, Salafi jihadists are influenced by the strategy of prominent Egyptian Islamist ideologues such as [Sayyid Qutb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyid_Qutb) and [Muhammad 'Abd al-Salam Faraj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Abd_al-Salam_Faraj), who advocated the revolutionary overthrow of secular regimes and the [establishment of Islamic states](2646438.md) through armed [jihad](16203.md).[^cite_note-911commision2-96] According to French political scientist and professor [Gilles Kepel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilles_Kepel), the [Salafist version of jihadism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism) combined "respect for the sacred texts in their most literal form, ... with an absolute commitment to jihad, whose number-one target had to be America, perceived as the greatest enemy of the faith."[^cite_note-KepelJihad2-97] Kepel wrote that the [Salafis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_movement) whom he encountered in Europe in the 1980s, were "totally apolitical".[^cite_note-kepel-orig-98][^cite_note-BLivesey-99] However, by the mid-1990s, he met some who felt jihad in the form of "violence and terrorism" was "justified to realize their political objectives". The mingling of many Salafists who were alienated from mainstream European society with violent jihadists created "a volatile mixture".[^cite_note-BLivesey-99]

In the 1990s, militant Islamists of the [al-Jama'a al-Islamiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Jama'a_al-Islamiyya) were active in the [terrorist attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrorism_in_Egypt#Attacks_during_the_1990s) on police, government officials, and foreign tourists in Egypt, while the [Armed Islamic Group of Algeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_Islamic_Group_of_Algeria) was one of the prominent [Islamic extremist groups](8734632.md) active during the [Algerian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algerian_Civil_War).[^cite_note-kepel-orig-98] In [Afghanistan](737.md), the [Taliban](30635.md) are adherents of the [Deobandi movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deobandi_movement), not the Salafi school of Islam, but they closely cooperated with bin Laden and various Salafi-jihadist leaders.[^cite_note-kepel-orig-98]

&#x20;In Iraq, resentment amongst Sunnis over their marginalization after the [fall of the Ba'athist regime in 2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Baghdad_\(2003\)) led to the rise of jihadist networks in the region, which resulted in the [al-Qaeda led insurgency in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_insurgency_\(2003%E2%80%932006\)).[^cite_note-100] [De-Ba'athification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De-Ba'athification) policy initiated by the [new government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coalition_Provisional_Authority) led to rise in support of jihadists and remnants of [Iraqi Ba'athists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba'ath_Party_\(Iraqi-dominated_faction\)) started allying with al-Qaeda in their common fight against the United States.[^cite_note-101] Iraq War journalist [George Packer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Packer) writes in *[The Assassins' Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Assassins'_Gate:_America_in_Iraq)*:

> "The Iraq War proved some of the [Bush administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_George_W._Bush)'s assertions false, and it made others self-fulfilling. One of these was the insistence on an operational link between Iraq and al-Qaeda... after the fall of the regime, the most potent ideological force behind the insurgency was Islam and its hostility to non-Islamic intruders. Some former Baathist officials even stopped drinking and took to prayer. The insurgency was called *[mukawama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muqawamah)*, or resistance, with overtones of religious legitimacy; its fighters became *[mujahideen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mujahideen)* (holy warriors) and proclaimed their mission to be *[jihad](16203.md)*."[^cite_note-102][^cite_note-103]



[Abu Mus'ab al-Zarqawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Mus'ab_al-Zarqawi), former leader of [al-Qaeda in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Qaeda_in_Iraq), is widely regarded as one of the influential [Salafi jihadists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism).

The [2021 re-establishment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Taliban_offensive) of the [Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan](737.md) and the 2024 establishment of the [post-Assad Syrian Arab Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_caretaker_government) grew out of the Salafi-jihadist groups [Taliban](30635.md) and [Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham](53015755.md), respectively.[^cite_note-104][^cite_note-105]

### Ideologists of Salafi jihadism

"Theoreticians" of Salafi jihadism include Afghan jihadist veterans such as the Palestinian [Abu Qatada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Qatada_al-Filistini), the Syrian [Mustafa Setmariam Nasar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustafa_Setmariam_Nasar), the Egyptian Mustapha Kamel, known as [Abu Hamza al-Masri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Hamza_al-Masri).[^cite_note-kepel-theory-106] [Al-Qaeda](1921.md)'s second leader and co-founder [Ayman al-Zawahiri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayman_al-Zawahiri) would praise [Sayyid Qutb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyid_Qutb) and his writings, stating that Qutb's call formed the ideological inspiration for the contemporary Salafi-jihadist movement.[^cite_note-107] Other leading figures in the movement include [Anwar al-Awlaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anwar_al-Awlaki), former leader of [Al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula (AQAP)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Qaeda_in_the_Arabian_Peninsula);[^cite_note-108] [Abu Bakar Bashir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Bakar_Bashir), leader of the banned Indonesian militant Islamist group [Jema'ah Islamiyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jema'ah_Islamiyah); [Nasir al-Fahd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasir_al-Fahd), Saudi Arabian Salafi-jihadist scholar who opposes the [Kingdom of Saudi Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Saudi_Arabia) and reportedly pledged allegiance to [Islamic State](9087364.md);[^cite_note-109] [Mohammed Yusuf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammed_Yusuf_\(Boko_Haram\)), founder of the Islamic terrorist organization [Boko Haram](23769768.md);[^cite_note-110] [Omar Bakri Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omar_Bakri_Muhammad),[^cite_note-111] [Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Bakr_al-Baghdadi), first leader of the Islamic terrorist organization [Islamic State](9087364.md);[^cite_note-112][^cite_note-113] etc.

### Salafi-jihadist groups

Salafist-jihadist groups include [al-Qaeda](1921.md),[^cite_note-911commision2-96][^cite_note-Aydinli2018-3-114][^cite_note-Jalal_2009-11] [Salafia Jihadia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafia_Jihadia),[^cite_note-Stanford2-115] the now defunct Algerian [Armed Islamic Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_Islamic_Group) (GIA),[^cite_note-KepelJihad2-97] and the Egyptian group [Al-Gama'a al-Islamiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Gama'a_al-Islamiyya) which still exists.

#### Salafia Jihadia



Mohamed Fizazi, ideologist of [Salafia Jihadia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafia_Jihadia)

[Salafia Jihadia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafia_Jihadia) is a [Salafi-jihadist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism) terrorist organization based in [Morocco](19291.md) and [Spain](26667.md).[^cite_note-Stanford2-115] The group was allied with [al-Qaeda](1921.md) and [Moroccan Islamic Combatant Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moroccan_Islamic_Combatant_Group) (GICM).

The group was known for its participation in the [2003 Casablanca bombings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_Casablanca_bombings), in which 12 suicide bombers killed 33 people and injured over 100. Salafia Jihadia has variously been described as a movement or loose network of [Salafi-jihadist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism) groups and [cells](14558092.md), or as a generic term applied by Moroccan authorities for militant Salafi activists.[^cite_note-JF-116][^cite_note-117]

[Salafia Jihadia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafia_Jihadia) is said to function as a network of several loosely affiliated [Salafi-jhadist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism) groups and [cells](14558092.md), including groups such as al Hijra Wattakfir, Attakfir Bidum Hijra, Assirat al Mustaqim, Ansar al Islam and Moroccan Afghans.[^cite_note-ISS-118][^cite_note-TRAC-119] The spiritual leader and founder of the group is [Mohammed Fizazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammed_Fizazi?action=edit\&redlink=1), former imam of the [al-Quds Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Quds_Mosque) (which was shut down by German authorities in 2010).[^cite_note-ISS-118] Fizazi was arrested in 2003 and sentenced to 30 years imprisonment for his radical statements and connection to the [Casablanca bombings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_Casablanca_bombings).[^cite_note-120] [Salafia Jihadia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafia_Jihadia) has since spawned a wider ideological movement out of [Saudi Arabia](349303.md) and the [Gulf states](3088555.md).[^cite_note-Stanford2-115]

#### Al-Gama'a al-Islamiyya

[Al-Gama'a al-Islamiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Gama'a_al-Islamiyya), another Salafi-jihadist movement,[^cite_note-jamestown-9-27-12-121] fought an insurgency against the Egyptian government from 1992 to 1998 during which at least 800 Egyptian policemen and soldiers, jihadists, and civilians were killed. Outside of Egypt, it is best known for a [November 1997 attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luxor_massacre) at the Temple of Hatshepsut in [Luxor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luxor) where fifty-eight foreign tourists trapped inside the temple were hunted down and hacked and shot to death. The group declared a ceasefire in March 1999,[^cite_note-FAS-IG-122] although as of 2012, it is still active in jihad against the [Ba'athist Syrian](57134800.md) regime.[^cite_note-jamestown-9-27-12-121]

#### Al-Qaeda



[Jihadist variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jihadist_flag) of the [Black Standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Standard) as used by [al-Qaeda](1921.md) and its factions since the late 1980s, which consists of the *[Shahada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahada)* in white script centered on a black background

Perhaps the most famous and effective [Salafi-jihadist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism) group is [al-Qaeda](1921.md).[^cite_note-911commision2-96][^cite_note-Aydinli2018-3-114][^cite_note-Jalal_2009-11][^cite_note-jones-title-123] Al-Qaeda evolved from the [Maktab al-Khidamat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maktab_al-Khidamat) (MAK), or the "Services Office", a Muslim organization founded in 1984 to raise and channel funds and recruit foreign *[mujahideen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mujahideen)* for the war against the Soviets in Afghanistan. It was established in [Peshawar](66230.md), Pakistan, by [Osama bin Laden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osama_bin_Laden) and [Abdullah Yusuf Azzam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdullah_Yusuf_Azzam).[^cite_note-Aydinli2018-3-114]

#### Al-Salafiya al-Jihadiya in the Sinai

[Al-Salafiya al-Jihadiya in the Sinai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Salafiya_al-Jihadiya_in_the_Sinai) was established in 2012 by [Muhammad al-Zawahiri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_al-Zawahiri),[^cite_note-124] it was created in order to fight [Egyptian Security Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_Armed_Forces) and [Israel Defense Forces](39237.md) in the [Sinai Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinai_Peninsula) and [Gaza Strip](12047.md).[^cite_note-125]

The group, and many other groups in the Sinai Peninsula, has ties with [al-Qaeda](1921.md),[^cite_note-126] and was one of the many groups who committed terrorist attacks on civilians and Egyptian Armed Forces during many periods of [terrorist attacks in the Sinai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinai_insurgency) in 2012 through 2013.[^cite_note-127]

#### Islamic State (IS)

In Syria and Iraq, both [Jabhat al-Nusra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabhat_al-Nusra) and [Islamic State](9087364.md) have been described as [Salafi-jihadist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism) terrorist organizations.[^cite_note-hassan-2014-128] Originating in the [Jaish al-Ta'ifa al-Mansurah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaish_al-Ta'ifa_al-Mansurah) founded by [Abu Omar al-Baghdadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Omar_al-Baghdadi) in 2004, the organization (primarily under the [Islamic State of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_State_of_Iraq) name) affiliated itself with [al-Qaeda in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Qaeda_in_Iraq) and fought alongside them during the [2003–2006 phase of the Iraqi insurgency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003%E2%80%932006_phase_of_the_Iraqi_insurgency). The group later changed their name to Islamic State of Iraq and Levant for about a year,[^cite_note-129][^cite_note-130] before declaring itself to be a [worldwide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Islamism) [caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate),[^cite_note-131][^cite_note-newname-132] called simply the "[Islamic State](9087364.md)".[^cite_note-BBCSep2014-133] They are a transnational [Salafi jihadist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadist) group and an [unrecognised](524626.md) [quasi-state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-state). IS gained global prominence in 2014, when their militants conquered large territories in northwestern Iraq and eastern Syria, taking advantage of the ongoing [civil war in Syria](30741795.md) and the disintegrating local military forces of Iraq. By the end of 2015, their self-declared [caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate) ruled an area with a population of about 12 million,[^cite_note-134][^cite_note-135] where they enforced their extremist interpretation of [Islamic law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_law), managed an annual budget exceeding US\$1 billion, and commanded more than 30,000 fighters.[^cite_note-136] After a grinding conflict with American, Iraqi, and Kurdish forces, IS lost control of all their Middle Eastern territories by 2019, subsequently reverting to [insurgency](589841.md) from remote hideouts while continuing their [propaganda efforts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_media_use_by_the_Islamic_State). These efforts have garnered a significant following in northern and [Sahelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahelian) Africa,[^cite_note-137][^cite_note-138] where IS still controls a significant territory, and the [war against the Islamic State](43517689.md) continues.[^cite_note-C-T-K-139][^cite_note-140]

#### Jabhat al-Nusra

[Jabhat al-Nusra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabhat_al-Nusra) has been described as possessing "a hard-line Salafi-Jihadist ideology" and being one of "the most effective" groups fighting the regime.[^cite_note-briefing-141] Writing after ISIS victories in Iraq, Hassan Hassan believes that ISIS is a reflection of "ideological shakeup of Sunni Islam's traditional Salafism" since the [Arab Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Spring), where [Salafism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_movement), "traditionally inward-looking and loyal to the political establishment", has "steadily, if slowly", been eroded by Salafism-jihadism.[^cite_note-hassan-2014-128]

#### Boko Haram

[Boko Haram](23769768.md) in Nigeria is a Salafi-jihadist terrorist organization[^cite_note-Thurston-2019-18-142] that has killed tens of thousands of people, displacing 2.3 million from their homes.[^cite_note-AP-18-11-15-143]

#### Jund Ansar-Allah

[Jund Ansar Allah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jund_Ansar_Allah) is, or was, an armed Salafi-jihadist organization based in the [Gaza Strip](12047.md). On August 14, 2009, the group's spiritual leader, Sheikh [Abdel Latif Moussa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdel_Latif_Moussa), announced during Friday sermon the establishment of an Islamic [emirate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate) in the Palestinian territories attacking the ruling authority, the [Islamist](15012.md) group [Hamas](13913.md), for failing to enforce [Sharia](28840.md) law. Hamas forces responded to his sermon by surrounding his [Ibn Taymiyyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Taymiyyah) mosque complex and attacking it. In the fighting that ensued, 24 people (including Sheikh Abdel Latif Moussa himself) were killed and over 130 were wounded.[^cite_note-144]

### Salafi jihadism in Europe

#### Sweden

In 2017, [Swedish Security Police](368897.md) reported that the number of jihadists in Sweden had risen to thousands from about 200 in 2010.[^cite_note-153] Based on social media analysis, an increase was noted in 2013.[^cite_note-154] According to police in Sweden, Salafist-jihadists affect the communities where they are active.[^cite_note-\:0-155]

According to Swedish researcher [Magnus Ranstorp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnus_Ranstorp), Salafi-jihadism is [antidemocratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criticism_of_democracy), [homophobic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homophobia) and aims to [subjugate women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_apartheid) and is therefore opposed to a societal order founded on democracy.[^cite_note-\:0-155]

#### United Kingdom

The report found that [Middle Eastern](19323.md) nations are providing financial support to mosques and Islamic educational institutions, which have been linked to the spread of Salafi-jihadist materials which espoused "an illiberal, bigoted" ideology.[^cite_note-156][^cite_note-157]

#### Germany

According to [Deutsche Welle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Welle), Salafism is a growing movement in [Germany](11867.md) whose aim of a [caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate) is incompatible with a [Western democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_democracy).[^cite_note-\:6-158] According to the German [Federal Agency for Civic Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Agency_for_Civic_Education), nearly all jihadist terrorists are Salafists, but not all Salafists are terrorists. The dualistic view on "true believers" and "false believers" in practice means people being treated unequally on religious grounds. The call for a religious state in the form of a caliphate means that Salafists reject the [rule of law](25166191.md) and the [sovereignty of the people's rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_sovereignty). The Salafist view on gender and society leads to discrimination and the subjugation of women.[^cite_note-159]

Estimates by German [interior intelligence service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Office_for_the_Protection_of_the_Constitution) show that it grew from 3,800 members in 2011 to 7,500 members in 2015.[^cite_note-bfv-160] In Germany, most of the recruitment to the movement is done on the Internet and also on the streets,[^cite_note-bfv-160] a propaganda drive which mostly attracts youth.[^cite_note-bfv-160] There are two ideological camps: one advocates [Salafi-Activism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_movement#Salafi_activists) and directs its recruitment efforts towards non-Muslims and non-Salafist Muslims to gain influence in society;[^cite_note-bfv-160] the other and minority movement, the jihadist Salafism, advocates gaining influence by the use of violence and nearly all identified terrorist cells in Germany came from Salafist circles.[^cite_note-bfv-160]

#### France

In December 2017, a Salafi-jihadist mosque in [Marseille](40888948.md) was closed by authorities for preaching about violent jihad.[^cite_note-161] In August 2018, after the [European Court of Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Court_of_Human_Rights) approved the decision, French authorities deported the [Salafi-jihadist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism) preacher Elhadi Doudi to his home country [Algeria](358.md) because of his radical messages he spread in Marseille.[^cite_note-162]

### Deobandi jihadism

Main articles: [Deobandi movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deobandi_movement) and [Deobandi jihadism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deobandi_jihadism)



Sami-ul Haq, [Deobandi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deobandi_movement) scholar and ideologist of the [Taliban](30635.md)

[Deobandi jihadism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deobandi_jihadism) is a [militant](310790.md) interpretation of Islam that draws upon the teachings of the [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) [Deobandi movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deobandi_movement), which originated in the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent) in the 19th century. The [Deobandism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deobandi_movement) underwent three waves of armed jihad. The first wave involved the establishment of an Islamic territory centered on [Thana Bhawan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thana_Bhawan) by the movement's elders during the [Indian Rebellion of 1857](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Rebellion_of_1857), before the founding of [Darul Uloom Deoband](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darul_Uloom_Deoband). [Imdadullah Muhajir Makki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imdadullah_Muhajir_Makki) was the Amir al-Mu'minin of this Islamic territory; however, after the British defeated the Deobandi forces in the [Battle of Shamli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shamli), the territory fell. Following the establishment of Darul Uloom Deoband, [Mahmud Hasan Deobandi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahmud_Hasan_Deobandi) led the initiation of the second wave. He mobilized an armed resistance against the British through various initiatives, including the formation of the Samratut Tarbiat. When the British uncovered his [Silk Letter Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Letter_Movement), they arrested [Hasan Deobandi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahmud_Hasan_Deobandi) and held him captive in [Malta](19137.md). After his release, he and his disciples entered into mainstream politics and actively participated in the democratic process.&#x20;

In late 1979, the [Pakistan–Afghan border](203570.md) became the center of the Deobandi jihadist movement's third wave, which was fueled by the [Soviet–Afghan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet%E2%80%93Afghan_War). Under the patronage of President [Zia-ul-Haq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Zia-ul-Haq), its expansion took place through various madrasas such as [Darul Uloom Haqqania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darul_Uloom_Haqqania) and [Jamia Uloom-ul-Islamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamia_Uloom-ul-Islamia). [Jamiat Ulema-e-Islam (S)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamiat_Ulema-e-Islam_\(S\)) provided political support for it. Trained militants from the Pakistan–Afghan border participated in the [Afghan jihad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghan_mujahideen), and later went on to form various organizations, including the [Taliban](30635.md). The most successful example of Deobandi jihadism is the Taliban, who established [Islamic rule](28840.md) in Afghanistan. The head of the Jamiat Ulema-e-Islam (S), [Sami-ul-Haq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sami-ul-Haq), is referred to as the "father of the Taliban". The [Deobandi jihadist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deobandi_jihadism) group [Taliban](30635.md) was formed in Afghanistan in the mid-1990s, the founder of the Taliban was [Mullah Omar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mullah_Omar), a former mujahideen fighter who had lost an eye during the war against the Soviet Union. In 1994, he gathered a group of students and religious scholars, many of whom had received their education in [Deobandi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deobandi_movement) [madrasahs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrasa) located in [Khyber Pakhtunkhwa](21950.md) and [Balochistan](8431743.md), and established the [Taliban](30635.md) as a political and military movement.[^cite_note-163]

## Shia Islamism

Main articles: [Hezbollah military activities](7635103.md), [Iranian Revolution](347268.md), and [Shia Islamism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shia_Islamism)

Further information: [Iran–Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War), [Iran–Israel proxy conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Israel_proxy_conflict), [Iran–Saudi Arabia proxy conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Saudi_Arabia_proxy_conflict), and [Sectarian violence among Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sectarian_violence_among_Muslims)

According to the [Iranian-American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian-American) academic [Vali Nasr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vali_Nasr), which serves as Majid Khaddouri Professor of International Affairs and Middle East Studies at the [Johns Hopkins School of Advanced International Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_School_of_Advanced_International_Studies) (SAIS), political tendencies of [Shīʿa](26961.md) and [Sunnī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) Islamic ideologies differ, with [Sunnī fundamentalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_fundamentalism) "in [Pakistan](23235.md) and much of the [Arab world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world)" being "far from politically revolutionary", primarily focused on attempting to [Islamicize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamization) the political establishment rather than trying to change it through revolutionary struggle, whereas the Shīʿīte conception of [political Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_Islam) is strongly influenced by [Ruhollah Khomeini](26232.md) and his talk of the oppression of the poor and [class war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_struggle), which characterized the success of the [Islamic Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Revolution) in [Iran](14653.md) (1978–1979):[^cite_note-Nasr_2007-164]

> With the [Shia awakening of Iran](347268.md), the years of sectarian tolerance were over. What followed was a Sunni-versus-Shia contest for dominance, and it grew intense. \[...] The revolution even moved leftists in [Muslim-majority countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim-majority_countries) such as [Indonesia](14579.md), [Turkey](11125639.md), and [Lebanon](17771.md) to look at Islam with renewed interest. After all, in Iran, Islam had succeeded where leftist ideologies had failed. \[...] But admiration for what had happened in Iran did not equal acceptance of Iranian leadership. Indeed, Islamic activists outside of Iran quickly found Iranian revolutionaries to be arrogant, offputting, and drunk on their own success. Moreover, [Sunni fundamentalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_fundamentalism) in [Pakistan](23235.md) and much of the [Arab world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world) was far from politically revolutionary. It was rooted in conservative religious impulses and the bazaars, mixing mercantile interests with religious values. As the French scholar of contemporary Islam [Gilles Kepel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilles_Kepel) puts it, it was less to tear down the existing system than to give it a fresh, thick coat of "Islamic green" paint. [Khomeini](26232.md)'s [fundamentalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_fundamentalism_in_Iran), by contrast, was "red"—that is, genuinely revolutionary.[^cite_note-Nasr_2007-164]

The term "jihadism" is almost exclusively used to describe [Sunnī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) [extremist groups](8734632.md).[^cite_note-econ-unsavory-165] One example is [Syria](7515849.md), where there have been thousands of [Muslim foreign fighters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_fighters_in_the_Syrian_civil_war) engaged in the [Syrian civil war](30741795.md), for example, non-Syrian [Shīʿa Islamist groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shia_Islamism) are often referred to as "militia", while Sunnī foreign fighters are referred to as "jihadists" (or "would-be jihadists").[^cite_note-167][^cite_note-169] One who does use the term "Shia jihad" is Danny Postel, who complains that "this Shia jihad is largely left out of the dominant narrative."[^cite_note-postel-2016-170][^cite_note-Smyth-2-10-2016-171] Other authors see the ideology of "resistance" (*muqawama*) as more dominant, even among [Shīʿa Islamist groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shia_Islamism). For clarity, they suggest use of the term *muqawamist* instead.[^cite_note-172] [Houthi rebels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houthi_insurgency) have often called for *jihad* to resist [Saudi Arabia's intervention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Arabian%E2%80%93led_intervention_in_Yemen) in [Yemen](350939.md), even though the [Houthi movement](17240332.md) stems from [Zaydī Shīʿism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaydism), a [subsect of Shīʿa Islam](26961.md#Major_denominations_or_branches) which is closer to Sunnī theology in comparison to other Shīʿa denominations.[^cite_note-173][^cite_note-174]

## Beliefs

See also: [Muslim attitudes toward terrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_attitudes_toward_terrorism)

According to Shadi Hamid and Rashid Dar, jihadism is driven by the idea that *[jihad](16203.md)* is an "[individual obligation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fard)" (*fard ‘ayn*) incumbent upon all [Muslims](19541.md). This is in contrast with the belief of Muslims up until now (and by contemporary non-jihadists) that *jihad* is a "[collective obligation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fard)" (*farḍ al-kifāya*) carried out according to orders of legitimate representatives of the [Muslim community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ummah) (*Ummah*). Jihadists insist that all Muslims should participate because (they believe) today's Muslim leaders in the world are illegitimate and do not command the [authority to ordain justified violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_violence).[^cite_note-Hamid-15-7-16-175]

### Evolution of jihad



The [Sarkha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarkha), slogan of the [Houthi](17240332.md), with the top saying "*[God is the greatest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takbir)*", the next line saying "*[Death to America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_to_America)*", followed by "*[Death to Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_to_Israel)*", followed by "*[A curse upon the Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisemitism_in_Islam)*", and the bottom saying "*Victory to Islam*"

Some observers[^cite_note-Poljarevic_2021-6][^cite_note-1Kadri-2012-176][^cite_note-Gorka-2009-177] have noted the evolution in the rules of jihad—from the original "classical" doctrine to that of 21st-century [Salafi jihadism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism).[^cite_note-Ajjoub_2021-178] According to the [legal historian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_historian) Sadarat Kadri,[^cite_note-1Kadri-2012-176] during the last couple of centuries, incremental changes in Islamic legal doctrine (developed by Islamists who otherwise condemn any *[bid‘ah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bid%E2%80%98ah)* (innovation) in religion), have "normalized" what was once "unthinkable".[^cite_note-1Kadri-2012-176] "The very idea that Muslims might blow themselves up for God was unheard of before 1983, and it was not until the early 1990s that anyone anywhere had tried to justify killing innocent Muslims who were not on a battlefield."[^cite_note-1Kadri-2012-176]

The first or the "classical" doctrine of jihad which was developed towards the end of the 8th century, emphasized the "jihad of the sword" (*jihad bil-saif*) rather than the "jihad of the heart",[^cite_note-179] but it contained many legal restrictions which were developed from interpretations of both the [Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran) and the [Hadith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith), such as detailed rules involving "the initiation, the conduct, the termination" of jihad, the treatment of prisoners, the distribution of [booty](209811.md), etc. Unless there was a sudden attack on the Muslim community, jihad was not a "personal obligation" (*fard ‘ayn*); instead it was a "collective one" (*[fard al-kifaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fard_al-kifaya)*),[^cite_note-Khadduri-1955-60-180] which had to be discharged "in the way of God" (*fi sabil Allah*),[^cite_note-2Kadri-2012-181] and it could only be directed by the [caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliph), "whose discretion over its conduct was all but absolute."[^cite_note-2Kadri-2012-181] (This was designed in part to avoid incidents like the [Kharijia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khawarij)'s jihad against and killing of [Caliph Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_as_Caliph), since they deemed that he was no longer a Muslim).[^cite_note-Poljarevic_2021-6] [Martyrdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahid) resulting from an attack on the enemy with no concern for one's own safety was considered praiseworthy, but dying by one's own hand (as opposed to the enemy's) merited a special place in [Hell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahannam).[^cite_note-ARSI-BL-xii-182] The category of jihad which is considered to be a collective obligation is sometimes simplified as "offensive jihad" in Western texts.[^cite_note-183]

Scholars like [Abul Ala Maududi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abul_Ala_Maududi), [Abdullah Azzam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdullah_Azzam), [Ruhollah Khomeini](26232.md), leaders of al-Qaeda and others, believe that defensive global jihad is a personal obligation, which means that no caliph or Muslim head of state needs to declare it. Killing oneself in the process of killing the enemy is an act of *[Shuhada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuhada)* (martyrdom) and it brings one a special place in [Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jannah), not a special place in [Hell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahannam); and the killing of Muslim bystanders (nevermind [Non-Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kafir)), should not impede acts of jihad. Military and intelligent analyst [Sebastian Gorka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebastian_Gorka) described the new interpretation of jihad as the "willful targeting of civilians by a non-state actor through unconventional means."[^cite_note-R._Habeck_2006_42-184][^cite_note-Gorka-2009-177] Al-Qaeda's splinter groups and competitors, [Jama'at al-Tawhid wal-Jihad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jama'at_al-Tawhid_wal-Jihad) and the [Islamic State of Iraq and Syria](9087364.md), are thought to have been heavily influenced[^cite_note-Ajjoub_2021-178][^cite_note-McCoy-12-8-2004-185][^cite_note-Crooke-30-8-2014-186][^cite_note-187][^cite_note-188] by a 2004 work on jihad entitled *[Management of Savagery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Management_of_Savagery)* (*Idarat at-Tawahhush*),[^cite_note-Ajjoub_2021-178] written by Abu Bakr Naji[^cite_note-Ajjoub_2021-178] and intended to provide a strategy to create a new Islamic [caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate) by first destroying "vital economic and strategic targets" and [terrifying the enemy](30636.md) with cruelty to break its will.[^cite_note-nyJune14-189]

Islamic theologian Abu Abdullah al-Muhajir has been identified as one of the key theorists and [ideologues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideologue) behind modern jihadist violence.[^cite_note-Ajjoub_2021-178][^cite_note-CP265-190][^cite_note-Atlantic_2016-191][^cite_note-Guardian-192] His theological and legal justifications influenced [Abu Musab al-Zarqawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Musab_al-Zarqawi), al-Qaeda member and former leader of [al-Qaeda in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Qaeda_in_Iraq), as well as several other jihadi terrorist groups, including [ISIL](9087364.md) and [Boko Haram](23769768.md).[^cite_note-Ajjoub_2021-178][^cite_note-CP265-190][^cite_note-Atlantic_2016-191][^cite_note-Guardian-192] Zarqawi used a 579-page manuscript of al-Muhajir's ideas at AQI training camps that were later deployed by ISIL, known in Arabic as *Fiqh al-Dima* and referred to in English as *The Jurisprudence of Jihad* or *The Jurisprudence of Blood*.[^cite_note-Ajjoub_2021-178][^cite_note-CP265-190][^cite_note-Atlantic_2016-191][^cite_note-Guardian-192][^cite_note-ChrisStout-193] The book has been described by [counterterrorism](83392.md) scholar Orwa Ajjoub as rationalizing and justifying "[suicide operations](18835454.md), the mutilation of corpses, [beheading](70802.md), and the killing of children and non-combatants".[^cite_note-Ajjoub_2021-178] *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)*'s journalist Mark Towsend, citing Salah al-Ansari of [Quilliam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quilliam_\(think_tank\)), notes: "There is a startling lack of study and concern regarding this abhorrent and dangerous text \[*The Jurisprudence of Blood*] in almost all Western and Arab scholarship".[^cite_note-Guardian-192] Charlie Winter of *[The Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Atlantic)* describes it as a "theological playbook used to justify the group's abhorrent acts".[^cite_note-Atlantic_2016-191] He states:

> Ranging from ruminations on the merits of beheading, torturing, or burning prisoners to thoughts on assassination, siege warfare, and the use of biological weapons, Muhajir's intellectual legacy is a crucial component of the [literary corpus of ISIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology_of_the_Islamic_State_of_Iraq_and_the_Levant)—and, indeed, whatever comes after it—a way to render practically anything permissible, provided, that is, it can be spun as beneficial to the jihad. \[...] According to Muhajir, [committing suicide to kill people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_suicide_bombers) is not only a theologically sound act, but a commendable one, too, something to be cherished and celebrated regardless of its outcome. \[...] neither Zarqawi nor his inheritors have looked back, liberally using Muhajir's work to normalize the use of suicide tactics in the time since, such that they have become the single most important military and terrorist method—defensive or offensive—used by ISIS today. The way that Muhajir theorized it was simple—he offered up a theological fix that allows any who desire it to sidestep the Koranic injunctions against suicide.[^cite_note-Atlantic_2016-191]

Clinical psychologist [Chris E. Stout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Stout_\(psychologist\)) also discusses the al Muhajir-inspired text in his essay, "Terrorism, Political Violence, and Extremism" (2017). He assesses that jihadists regard their actions as being "for the greater good"; that they are in a "weakened in the earth" situation that renders [Islamic terrorism](566821.md) a valid means of solution.[^cite_note-ChrisStout-193]
