The **Daytona 500** is a 500-mile-long (805 km) [NASCAR Cup Series](56571945.md) motor race held annually at [Daytona International Speedway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytona_International_Speedway) in [Daytona Beach, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytona_Beach,_Florida). It is the first of two Cup races held every year at Daytona, the second being the [Coke Zero Sugar 400](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_Zero_Sugar_400), and one of three held in [Florida](18933066.md), with the annual [Straight Talk Wireless 400](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASCAR_Cup_Series_at_Homestead%E2%80%93Miami_Speedway) being held at [Homestead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestead%E2%80%93Miami_Speedway) south of [Miami](53846.md). From 1988 to 2019, it was one of the four [restrictor plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restrictor_plate) races on the Cup schedule. The inaugural Daytona 500 was held in [1959](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1959_Daytona_500) coinciding with the opening of the speedway and since [1982](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_Daytona_500), it has been the season-opening race of the Cup Series.[^cite_note-1]

The Daytona 500 is regarded as the most important and prestigious race on the NASCAR calendar, carrying by far the largest purse.[^cite_note-2] Championship points awarded are equal to that of any other NASCAR Cup Series race. It is also the series' first race of the year; this phenomenon is unique in sports, which tend to have championships or other major events at the end of the season rather than the start. From [1995](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1995_Daytona_500)–[2020](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Daytona_500), U.S. [television ratings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nielsen_Media_Research) for the Daytona 500 were the highest for any auto race of the year, surpassing the traditional leader, the [Indianapolis 500](159018.md) which in turn greatly surpasses the Daytona 500 in gate attendance and international viewing. In 2021 the Indianapolis 500 surpassed the Daytona 500 in TV ratings and viewership.[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4] The [2006 Daytona 500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Daytona_500) attracted the sixth largest average live global TV audience of any sporting event that year with 20 million viewers.[^cite_note-5] As of 2026, the capacity is 102,000.[^cite_note-6]

The race serves as the final event of [Speedweeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytona_Speedweek) and is also known as "The Great American Race" or the "[Super Bowl](27718.md) of Stock Car Racing".[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9] Since its inception, the race has been held in mid-to-late February. From [1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_Daytona_500) to [2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Daytona_500), and again since [2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Daytona_500), the event has been as associated with [Presidents Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidents'_Day) weekend,[^cite_note-10] falling usually on the third Sunday of February. Since 2022, it has been held one week after the [Super Bowl](27718.md), the same day as the [NBA All-Star Game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NBA_All-Star_Game), and on eight occasions, it has been run on [Valentine's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentine's_Day).

Since 1997, the winner of the Daytona 500 has been presented with the [Harley J. Earl Trophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harley_J._Earl_Trophy) in Victory Lane, and the winning car is displayed in race-winning condition for one year at [Daytona 500 Experience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytona_500_Experience), a museum and gallery adjacent to Daytona International Speedway.

## Origins



Aerial view of Daytona International Speedway

The race is the direct successor of shorter races held on the [Daytona Beach Road Course](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytona_Beach_and_Road_Course). This long square was partially on the sand and also on the highway near the beach. Earlier events featured 200-mile (320 km) races with stock cars. A 500-mile (805 km) [stock car race](28349.md) was held at [Daytona International Speedway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytona_International_Speedway) in [1959](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1959_Daytona_500). It was the second 500-mile NASCAR race, following the annual [Southern 500](22298383.md), and has been held every year since. By [1961](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1961_Daytona_500), it began to be referred to as the **Daytona 500**,[^cite_note-programs-11] by which it is still commonly known.

Daytona International Speedway is 2.5 miles (4 km) long and a 500-mile race[^cite_note-12] requires 200 laps to complete. However, the race was considered official after halfway (100 laps/250 miles) had been completed from 1959 to 2016. From 2017 to 2019, the race was considered official after the conclusion of Stage 2 (120 laps/300 miles) when stage-racing was introduced. In 2020, they revised the rule in which a race is considered official at either halfway or the conclusion of Stage 2 (whichever comes first, in this case halfway). The race has been shortened four times due to rain (in [1965](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1965_Daytona_500), [1966](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1966_Daytona_500), [2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_Daytona_500), and [2009](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Daytona_500)) and once in response to the energy crisis of [1974](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1974_Daytona_500). Since the adaptation of the [green–white–checkered finish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green%E2%80%93white%E2%80%93checkered_finish) rule in 2004, the race has gone past 500 miles on ten occasions ([2005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_Daytona_500), [2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Daytona_500), [2007](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_Daytona_500), [2010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Daytona_500), [2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Daytona_500), [2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Daytona_500), [2015](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Daytona_500), [2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Daytona_500), [2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Daytona_500), [2020](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Daytona_500) and 2023). It took two attempts to finish the race in 2010, 2011, and 2020. The 2023 running is the longest Daytona 500 contested, lasting 212 laps/530 miles.

[Tyler Reddick](41108702.md) is the defending winner.

Main article: [Daytona 500 history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytona_500_history)

- **[1959](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1959_Daytona_500)**: [Lee Petty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Petty), patriarch of the racing family, won the inaugural 500 Mile NASCAR International Sweepstakes at Daytona on February 22, 1959, defeating [Johnny Beauchamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_Beauchamp).

  [^cite_note-13]

- **[1960](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1960_Daytona_500)**: [Junior Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junior_Johnson) made use of the [draft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drafting_\(aerodynamics\)), then a little-understood phenomenon, to win while running a slower, year-old car in a field of 68 cars, the most in the history of the Daytona 500.

- **[1961](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1961_Daytona_500)**: First race to be called Daytona 500.

  [^cite_note-14]

- **[1965](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1965_Daytona_500)**: The first rain-shortened Daytona 500. [Fred Lorenzen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Lorenzen) was in the lead when the race was called on lap 133 of 200.

  [^cite_note-15]

- **[1966](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1966_Daytona_500)**: Richard Petty becomes the first two-time winner, having previously won the [1964 race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_Daytona_500). Through 2024, only 12 drivers have won 2 or more Daytona 500s.

- **[1967](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1967_Daytona_500)**: [Mario Andretti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Andretti) led 112 of the 200 laps including the last 33 to capture his first and only win in the Cup Series.

- **[1968](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Daytona_500)**: For much of this race, both [Cale Yarborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cale_Yarborough) and (unrelated) [LeeRoy Yarbrough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LeeRoy_Yarbrough) traded the lead. With 5 laps to go, Cale made a successful slingshot pass on the third turn to take the lead from LeeRoy and never looked back as he won his first Daytona 500 by 1.3 seconds.

- **[1969](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1969_Daytona_500)**: Having learned from the previous year, LeeRoy Yarbrough would use the same slingshot treatment out of turn 3 on [Charlie Glotzbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Glotzbach), to score the victory on the final lap.

- **[1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_Daytona_500)**: Richard Petty becomes the first three-time winner, including the [1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_Daytona_500) and [1966 races](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1966_Daytona_500). Through 2015, only 5 drivers have won 3 or more Daytona 500s.

- **[1972](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1972_Daytona_500)**: A. J. Foyt cruised into the lead on lap 80 and stayed there through the 200 lap race, lapping the entire field. Foyt beat second-place Charlie Glotzbach by nearly two laps, with Jim Vandiver finishing 6 laps down in third.

- **[1973](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_Daytona_500)**: Richard Petty becomes the first four-time winner, including the [1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_Daytona_500), [1966](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1966_Daytona_500), and [1971 races](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_Daytona_500). Through 2015, only Petty (7 total) and Cale Yarborough have won at least 4 Daytona 500s.

- **[1974](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1974_Daytona_500)**: During the start of the 1974 NASCAR season, many races had their distance cut ten percent in response to the [1973 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis). As a result, the 1974 Daytona 500 was shortened to 180 laps (450 miles), as symbolically, the race "started" on lap 21. Richard Petty became the first of only 4 drivers (as of 2021) to win consecutive Daytona 500s, while also setting a mark of 5 total wins.

- **[1976](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976_Daytona_500)**: In the 1976 race, Richard Petty was leading on the last lap when he was passed on the backstretch by David Pearson. Petty tried to turn under Pearson coming off the final corner but didn't clear Pearson. The contact caused the drivers to spin into the grass in the infield just short of the finish line. Petty's car didn't start, but Pearson was able to keep his car running and limp over the finish line for the win.

- **[1979](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_Daytona_500)**: The 1979 race was the first Daytona 500 to be broadcast live on national television,

  [^cite_note-nascar.com-16]

  [^cite_note-amazon-17]

  &#x20;airing on [CBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBS_Sports), whose audience was increased in much of the Eastern and Midwestern USA due to a blizzard. (The [Indianapolis 500](159018.md) was only broadcast on tape delay that evening in this era; most races were broadcast only through the final quarter to half of the race, as was the procedure for ABC's Championship Auto Racing broadcasts; with the new CBS contract, the network and NASCAR agreed to a full live broadcast.) That telecast introduced in-car and low-level track-side cameras, which has now become standard in all sorts of automotive racing broadcasts. A final lap crash and subsequent fight between leaders Cale Yarborough and [Donnie Allison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donnie_Allison) (along with Donnie's brother [Bobby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Allison)) brought national (if unwelcome) publicity to NASCAR, with the added emphasis of a snowstorm that bogged down much of the northeastern part of the United States. Donnie Allison was leading the race on the final lap with Yarborough drafting him tightly. As Yarborough attempted a slingshot pass at the end of the backstretch, Allison attempted to block him. Yarborough refused to give ground and as he pulled alongside Allison, his left side tires left the pavement and went into the wet and muddy infield grass. Yarborough lost control of his car and contacted Allison's car halfway down the backstretch. As both drivers tried to regain control, their cars made contact several more times before finally locking together and crashing into the outside wall in turn three. After the cars settled in the grass, Donnie Allison and Yarborough began to argue. After they had talked it out, Bobby Allison, who was lapped at that point, pulled over, began defending his brother, and a fight broke out. Richard Petty, who was over half a lap behind at the time, went on to win; with the brawl in the infield, the television audience scarcely noticed. The story was the talk of the water cooler the next day, even making the front page of *[The New York Times](30680.md)* Sports section.

- **[1980](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_Daytona_500)**: [Buddy Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddy_Baker) won the fastest Daytona 500 in history before the introduction of mandatory stage cautions in 2017, at 177.602 mph (285.809 km/h).

- **[1981](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1981_Daytona_500)**: Richard Petty becomes the first seven-time winner, three wins more than the second-highest multiple winner, Cale Yarborough. With wins in [1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_Daytona_500), [1966](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1966_Daytona_500), [1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_Daytona_500), [1973](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_Daytona_500), [1974](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1974_Daytona_500), and [1979](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_Daytona_500), Petty is the only driver to win in three different decades.

- **[1982](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_Daytona_500)**: The Daytona 500 becomes the opening race in the NASCAR season, a position held since. Bobby Allison wins his second Daytona 500 but many people consider this a controversial win because on lap 3 Bobby Allison's rear bumper broke away from the car (later it was discovered that it was welded on purpose by a wire welder) and caused a pileup further behind the leaders. Without a rear bumper, Allison's car gained an aerodynamic advantage and won the race by just over twenty-two seconds.

- **[1983](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1983_Daytona_500)**: Cale Yarborough was the first driver to run a qualifying lap over 200 mph (320 km/h) in his [Chevrolet Monte Carlo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevrolet_Monte_Carlo); only to crash out during the second qualifying lap. Yarborough raced in a backup [Pontiac LeMans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontiac_LeMans) displayed at a nearby [Hardee's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardee's) (Yarborough's sponsor) and took the backup car to his 3rd Daytona 500 victory. This would also be the final time a driver won a Daytona 500 in a Pontiac.

- **[1984](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984_Daytona_500)**: Cale Yarborough completed a lap of 201.848 mph (324.843 km/h), officially breaking the 200 mph (320 km/h) barrier at Daytona. He joined Richard Petty as the only drivers to win the race in consecutive years and to win the race four times overall.

- **[1985](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1985_Daytona_500)**: [Bill Elliott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Elliott) dominated the race, and by lap 140, was close to lapping the entire field. During a pit stop, NASCAR officials held him in the pit area in order to repair a supposed broken headlight assembly. The two-minute pit stop dropped him to third, barely clinging to the lead lap. Elliott made up the deficit and survived a late-race caution and a final lap restart to win his first Daytona 500. Elliott would go on to win the first [Winston Million](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_Jewel_\(NASCAR\)#Winston_Million).

- **[1986](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1986_Daytona_500)**: The race that came down to a two-car duel between [Dale Earnhardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dale_Earnhardt) and [Geoff Bodine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoff_Bodine). With 3 laps to go, Earnhardt was forced to make a pit stop for a "splash 'n go". However, as Earnhardt left the pits, he burned a piston, allowing Bodine to cruise to victory.

- **[1987](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1987_Daytona_500)**: Winner Bill Elliott qualified for the pole position at an all-time Daytona record of 210.364 mph (338.532 km/h). Bill Elliott dominated much of the race, leading 104 of the 200 laps. During two different points in the race, he pulled away from the other leaders and was all by himself on the track, leading the first 35 laps, 29 in a row at another point, and the last three.

- **[1988](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Daytona_500)**: [Restrictor plates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restrictor_plate) were mandated to reduce dangerously high speeds at Daytona. This race was remembered for two things. First, Richard Petty's rollover crash in the tri-oval on lap 106, initiated when he was tagged from behind by Phil Barkdoll. Petty rolled over about eight times and was then hit by [Brett Bodine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brett_Bodine). The wreck also collected 1972 race winner A. J. Foyt, Eddie Bierschwale, and [Alan Kulwicki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Kulwicki). All of the drivers, including Petty, walked away. Second, [Bobby Allison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Allison) and his son [Davey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davey_Allison) finished one-two and celebrated together in Victory Lane, making Bobby Allison the oldest driver to win the Daytona 500.

- **[1989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1989_Daytona_500)**: [Darrell Waltrip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darrell_Waltrip) stretches his final tank of fuel for 53 laps to win in his 17th try.

- **[1990](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1990_Daytona_500)**: Dale Earnhardt appeared headed for certain victory until the closing laps. On lap 193, Geoff Bodine spun in the first turn, causing the third and final caution of the race. All of the leaders pitted except [Derrike Cope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derrike_Cope), who stayed out to gain track position. On the lap 195 restart, Earnhardt re-took the lead. On the final lap, going into turn three, he ran over a [bell housing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_housing) from the blown engine of [Rick Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rick_Wilson_\(racing_driver\))'s car. He blew a tire, allowing the relatively unknown Cope to slip by and take his first career win in a major upset.

  [^cite_note-18]

- **[1991](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1991_Daytona_500)**: Dale Earnhardt's Daytona 500 frustrations continued as [Ernie Irvan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernie_Irvan) passed Earnhardt with six laps to go to. Ultimately, Earnhardt - who was battling issues with the car temperature after striking a seagull early in the race - spun with two laps remaining and collected [Davey Allison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davey_Allison) and [Kyle Petty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyle_Petty). Irvan took the win as the race ended under the caution flag. The race was dominated by complex pit stop rules, implemented to improve safety in the pit area.

- **[1992](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Daytona_500)**: Davey Allison dominated the second half en route to his lone Daytona 500 victory. He avoided a major wreck on lap 92 and went on to lead the final 102 laps.

- **[1993](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1993_Daytona_500)**: In a frightening wreck on lap 170, [Rusty Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rusty_Wallace) flipped over multiple times on the back straightaway. With two laps to go, [Dale Earnhardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dale_Earnhardt) was leading Jeff Gordon and [Dale Jarrett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dale_Jarrett). Jarrett battled into the lead with one lap to go. It was the fourth time Earnhardt had been leading the Daytona 500 with less than ten laps to go but failed to win.

- **[1994](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1994_Daytona_500)**: [Sterling Marlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sterling_Marlin) gambled on fuel and was able to complete the final 59 laps without stopping, to win his first career Cup victory. During Speedweeks, two drivers, Neil Bonnett and Rodney Orr, died during separate practice accidents. Also, [Loy Allen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loy_Allen_Jr.) became the first rookie to start the Daytona 500 on the pole.

- **[1995](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1995_Daytona_500)**: Sterling Marlin became the first driver since Cale Yarborough, and only third overall, to win back-to-back Daytona 500s. It was the third win in five years for [Morgan–McClure Motorsports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morgan%E2%80%93McClure_Motorsports) (1991, 1994, 1995).

- **[1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Daytona_500)**: Dale Jarrett won his second Daytona 500 in four years, again holding off Dale Earnhardt,

  [^cite_note-19]

  &#x20;who finished second for the third time in four years.

- **[1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Daytona_500)**: [Jeff Gordon](18600829.md) became the youngest winner of the event to that point at the age of 25 in a three-way finish under caution with [Hendrick Motorsports](2089777.md) teammates [Terry Labonte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_Labonte) and [Ricky Craven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricky_Craven) finishing second and third. Dale Earnhardt's Daytona "curse" continued as, while contending for the lead with 12 laps to go, he was involved in a rollover crash on the backstretch, though after observing from an ambulance that his car was still relatively intact, got back in and finished the race in 31st.

- **[1998](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_Daytona_500)**: Dale Earnhardt finally won the Daytona 500 after "20 years of trying, 20 years of frustration." Though Earnhardt had usually been a strong competitor in the Daytona 500, mechanical problems, crashes, or other misfortunes had prevented him from winning.

- **[1999](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_Daytona_500)**: Jeff Gordon accomplished the feat of winning the pole and the race marking the first time since [1987](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1987_Daytona_500) when [Bill Elliott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Elliott) did this.

- **[2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_Daytona_500)**: Dale Jarrett avenged his previous year's rollover accident by winning the 1999 season championship & 2000 500 which was the final 500 broadcast for CBS.

- **[2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_Daytona_500)**: Also known as "Black Sunday" or the "darkest day in NASCAR", as Dale Earnhardt [died in a crash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_Dale_Earnhardt) on the final lap. [Michael Waltrip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Waltrip) and [Dale Earnhardt Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dale_Earnhardt_Jr.) were running first and second on the final lap, while Earnhardt Sr. was third. In turn 4, Earnhardt lost control after making contact from Sterling Marlin and crashed into the outside wall, taking [Ken Schrader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Schrader) with him. Earnhardt suffered a fatal [basilar skull fracture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilar_skull_fracture). The death overshadowed Waltrip's first win, which came in his 463rd Cup Series race.

  [^cite_note-20]

- **[2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_Daytona_500)**: Michael Waltrip became a two-time winner in the shortest ever Daytona 500 after the race was shortened to 109 laps due to rain.

  [^cite_note-21]

- **[2004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Daytona_500)**: Dale Earnhardt Jr. won his first Daytona 500 in his fifth attempt, six years after his father accomplished the feat after 20 attempts. Defending winner Michael Waltrip was eliminated early when his car flipped over during the Big One.

  [^cite_note-22]

- **[2005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_Daytona_500)**: The start time was changed, allowing the race to finish under the lights at dusk. In the first use of the green-white-checkered finish rule in the Daytona 500, Gordon held off Kurt Busch, and Earnhardt Jr. to win his third Daytona 500. The race went 203 laps/507.5 miles.

- **[2007](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_Daytona_500)**: Running fifth with half a lap to go, [Kevin Harvick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Harvick) picked up a push and surged to the front to nip [Mark Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Martin) by 0.02 seconds at the line. Most of the rest of the field crashed across the line as [The Big One](21509243.md) erupted behind them.

- **[2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Daytona_500)**: The celebrated 50th running of the Daytona 500 was the first using NASCAR's [Car of Tomorrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Car_of_Tomorrow). ## Qualifying procedure

Main article: [The Duel at Daytona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Duel_at_Daytona)

The qualifying procedure is unique for the Daytona 500. Some teams must race their way into the Daytona 500 field. The first row is set by a timed round of qualifying. Prior to 2021, the session was held one week before the race (prior to 2003, this was two rounds; prior to 2001, it was three) but the session is now held on the Wednesday evening leading up to the race. The remainder of the field is set on the Thursday before the race by two separate qualifying races (these were 100 miles (160 km) from 1959 to 1967; 125 miles (201 km) from 1969 to 2004; and 150 miles (240 km) with a two-lap overtime, if necessary, beginning in 2005 (these races were not held in 1968 due to rain)). The top two drivers from the qualifying races who were not in the top 35 in owner points were given spots on the field, and the rest of the field was set by the finishing order of the duels, with guaranteed spots to those in the top 35. The remaining spots, 40 to 43, were filled by top qualifying times of those not already in the field from the qualifying race. If there was a previous NASCAR champion without a spot, he would get one of those four spots, otherwise, the fourth-fastest car was added to the field.

Before 2005 – and beginning again in 2013 – after the top two cars were set, the top fourteen cars in the qualifying races advance to the field, and then between six (1998–2003), eight (1995–97, 2004) or 10 (until 1994) fastest cars which do not advance from the qualifying race are added, then cars in the top 35 in owner points not locked into the race, and then the driver with the championship provisional, except for 1985 when no such car was eligible for a provisional starting spot, the only time that happened in the Daytona 500 from when the provisional was added in 1976 through 2004.

## Television

See also: [List of Daytona 500 broadcasters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Daytona_500_broadcasters)

The Daytona 500 was the first 500-mile (800 km) auto race to be televised live flag-to-flag on network television when [CBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBS) aired it in 1979, continuing to air until 2000.

From 2001 to 2006, the race alternated between [Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASCAR_on_Fox) and [NBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASCAR_on_NBC) under the terms of a six–year, \$2.48 billion NASCAR television contract, with Fox broadcasting the Daytona 500 in odd-numbered years (2001, 2003, 2005) and the [Pepsi 400](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_Zero_Sugar_400) in even-numbered years (2002, 2004, 2006) and NBC broadcasting the opposite race in that year.

In 2005, a new television contract was signed, which made Fox the sole broadcaster of the Daytona 500 for eight years, from 2007 to 2014. In 2013, 10 more years were added to the contract, giving Fox every Daytona 500 from 2015 to 2024 as well, for a total of at least 20 Daytona 500s in a row. The installation of the [lighting system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Night_game#Motorsports) at Daytona International Speedway in 1998, as well as the implementations of the television packages in 2001 and 2007 respectively, have resulted in the race starting and ending much later than it did in the race's early years. The race started at 12\:15 p.m. [EST](496028.md) from 1979 until 2000. The start time was moved to 1\:00 p.m [EST](496028.md) from 2001 to 2004, 2\:30 p.m. in 2005 and 2006, and 3\:30 p.m. from 2007 to 2009, all for the convenience of west coast viewers. The 2005 race ended at sunset for the first time in its history, and the 2006 race ended well after sunset.

Every Daytona 500 between 2006 and 2010, as well as the 2012 and 2014 races, ended under the lights. The changing track conditions caused by the onset of darkness in the closing laps in these years forced the crew chiefs to predict the critical car setup adjustments needed for their final two pit stops. The 2007 race was the first Daytona 500 to go into prime-time, ending at 7\:07 p.m. Eastern time. In 2010, the race moved back to a 1\:00 p.m. start time, which should have resulted in it ending in daylight; however, two red flags caused by track surface issues led to long delays that pushed the race to 7\:34 p.m. EST, pushing the race into prime-time for the second time. The 2012 race was also scheduled to start at 1\:00 p.m. EST on Sunday, February 26, but heavy rain in the area caused the race to be postponed to 7\:00 p.m. EST on Monday, February 27, making it the first Daytona 500 to be postponed to a Monday, as well as the first (and only) Daytona 500 to be run as a night race. Due to a two–hour red flag period after a jet dryer fire on the track with 40 laps remaining, the race did not end until about 12\:40 a.m. on Tuesday, February 28. The 2013 race marked a return to the race's past tradition of ending in the late afternoon, as it ended at about 4\:40 p.m., the race's earliest ending time since 2004. Although the 2014 race started around 1\:30 p.m. EST, heavy rain and a [tornado warning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tornado_warning) red–flagged the race after 38 laps and it was delayed for a record six hours and 22 minutes; the race finished the entire 500–mile distance around after 11\:00 p.m. the same day, which effectively competed with the time-delayed East Coast broadcast of [NBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympics_on_NBC)'s coverage of the [2014 Winter Olympics closing ceremony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Winter_Olympics_closing_ceremony), scheduled between 7\:00 and 10\:30 p.m. The 2015 race started on time around 1\:00 p.m., and ended after 203 laps due to a Green–white–checkered finish.

Rain delays forced the 2025 race to go on longer as well, with television coverage cutting into numerous Fox [Animation Domination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animation_Domination) shows.

The television ratings for the Daytona 500 have surpassed those of the larger [Indianapolis 500](159018.md) (which has much larger physical attendance and international attendance) since 1995, even though the 1995 race was available in far fewer homes than the year before. Then-broadcaster CBS had lost well-established [VHF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Very_high_frequency) (channels 2–13) affiliates in major markets as a result of the [Fox affiliate switches of 1994](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1994%E2%80%931996_United_States_broadcast_television_realignment). As an example, new affiliates [WDJT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WDJT-TV) in [Milwaukee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milwaukee) and [WGNX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WANF) in [Atlanta](3138.md) — both cities that are home to NASCAR races — and [WWJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WWJ-TV) in [Detroit](8687.md), close to [Michigan International Speedway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_International_Speedway), were on the [UHF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultra_high_frequency) band (channels 14–69), meaning that they had a significantly reduced broadcast area compared to former affiliates [WITI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WITI_\(TV\)), [WAGA-TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WAGA-TV), and [WJBK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WJBK), respectively. WDJT was not available in many Wisconsin markets by the time the Daytona 500 took place.

- **2005–07, 2010–12, 2015, 2018–20, 2022–23 and 2025:** Races extended due to [NASCAR overtime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green%E2%80%93white%E2%80%93checkered_finish).

- **2012:** Race was postponed from Sunday afternoon to Monday night due to rain and finished after midnight on Tuesday.

- **2020:** Race began on Sunday but finished on Monday evening due to rain.

- **2021:** Race ran on Sunday, but finished after midnight on Monday.

- **2024:** Race was postponed from Sunday afternoon to Monday afternoon at 4 PM Eastern Standard Time due to rain.
