# Regime change

Not to be confused with [Regime shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regime_shift), [Government formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_formation), [Election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election), or [Peaceful transition of power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peaceful_transition_of_power).

For the book, see [*Regime Change* (book)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regime_Change_\(book\)).

**Regime change** is the partly forcible or coercive replacement of one government [regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regime) with another. It is typically understood as a violation of the [sovereignty](28791.md) of the target state. Regime change may replace all or part of the [state's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_\(polity\)) most critical leadership system, administrative apparatus, or [bureaucracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureaucracy). Regime change may occur through domestic processes, such as [revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution), [coup](25914154.md), or reconstruction of government following [state failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Failed_state) or [civil war](7085.md).[^cite_note-1] It can also be imposed on a country by foreign actors through invasion, [overt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interventionism_\(politics\)) or [covert interventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covert_interventions), or [coercive diplomacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coercive_diplomacy).[^cite_note-\:0-2][^cite_note-3] Regime change may entail the construction of new institutions, the restoration of old institutions, and the promotion of new [ideologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology).[^cite_note-\:0-2]

## Types

### Internal regime change

Regime change can be precipitated by revolution or a [coup d'état](25914154.md). For example, the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution), the [Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution), and the [Iranian Revolution](347268.md).[^cite_note-4]

### Foreign-imposed regime change

Commonly referred to as *regime change wars*, foreign-imposed regime change is the deposing of a regime by a foreign state, which can be achieved through covert means or by direct military action. Interstate war can also culminate into a foreign-imposed regime change for the losers, as occurred for the [Axis powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_powers) during [World War II](32927.md) ([Nazi Germany](21212.md), [Fascist Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascist_Italy), and the [Empire of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Japan)).[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6] Foreign-imposed regime change is sometimes used by states as a foreign policy tool.[^cite_note-7] According to a dataset by Alexander Downes, 120 leaders have been removed through foreign-imposed regime change between 1816 and 2011.[^cite_note-\:0-2]

During the [Cold War](325329.md), the [United States](3434750.md) and the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) frequently intervened in elections and engaged in attempts at regime change, both covertly and overtly.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10][^cite_note-wapodov-11] According to [Michael Poznansky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Poznansky?action=edit\&redlink=1), covert regime change became more common when non-intervention was codified into international law, leading states that wanted to engage in regime change to do so covertly and conceal their violations of international law.[^cite_note-12]

Modern examples of regime-change include the [2001 invasion of Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_invasion_of_Afghanistan) and the [2003 invasion of Iraq](201936.md).

### Regime promotion

According to [John Owen IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Owen_IV?action=edit\&redlink=1), there are four historical waves of forcible regime promotion:[^cite_note-13]

1. [Catholicism](606848.md) vs [Protestantism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism): From the 1520s to the early 18th century

2. [Republicanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republicanism) vs [Constitutional monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_monarchy) vs [Absolute monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_monarchy): From the 1770s to the late 19th century

3. [Communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communism) vs [Socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism) vs [Capitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism): From the late 1910s to the 1980s

4. [Islamism](15012.md) vs [Secularism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secularism): post-1990

## Impact

&#x20;Studies by Alexander Downes, [Lindsey O'Rourke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindsey_O'Rourke?action=edit\&redlink=1) and Jonathan Monten indicate that foreign-imposed regime change seldom reduces the likelihood of civil war,[^cite_note-\:0-2] violent removal of the newly imposed leader,[^cite_note-\:0-2] and the probability of conflict between the intervening state and its adversaries,[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-\:0-2] as well as does not increase the likelihood of [democratization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratization) (unless regime change comes with pro-democratic institutional changes in countries with favorable conditions for democracy).[^cite_note-15] Downes argues,[^cite_note-\:0-2]

> The strategic impulse to forcibly oust antagonistic or non-compliant regimes overlooks two key facts. First, the act of overthrowing a foreign government sometimes causes its military to disintegrate, sending thousands of armed men into the countryside where they often wage an insurgency against the intervener. Second, externally-imposed leaders face a domestic audience in addition to an external one, and the two typically want different things. These divergent preferences place imposed leaders in a quandary: taking actions that please one invariably alienates the other. Regime change thus drives a wedge between external patrons and their domestic protégés or between protégés and their people.&#x20;

Research by Nigel Lo, Barry Hashimoto, and [Dan Reiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Reiter) has contrasting findings, as they find that interstate "peace following wars last longer when the war ends in foreign-imposed regime change."[^cite_note-16] However, research by Reiter and Goran Peic finds that foreign-imposed regime change can raise the probability of civil war.[^cite_note-17]
