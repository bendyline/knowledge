***The New England Journal of Medicine*** (***NEJM***) is a weekly [medical journal](29500858.md) published by the [Massachusetts Medical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Medical_Society). Founded in 1812, the journal is among the most prestigious [peer-reviewed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer_review) medical journals.[^cite_note-NYT_200_years-1] Its 2024 impact factor was 78.5, ranking it 2nd out of 168 journals in the category "Medicine, General & Internal".[^cite_note-2]

## History

In September 1811, [Boston](24437894.md) physician [John Collins Warren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Collins_Warren_\(surgeon,_born_1778\)),[^cite_note-3] along with [James Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Jackson_\(physician\)), submitted a formal prospectus to establish the *New England Journal of Medicine and Surgery and Collateral Branches of Science* as a medical and philosophical journal.[^cite_note-4] Subsequently, the first issue of the *New England Journal of Medicine and Surgery and the Collateral Branches of Medical Science* was published in January 1812.[^cite_note-5] The journal was published quarterly.[^cite_note-6]

In 1823, another publication, the *Boston Medical Intelligencer*, appeared under the editorship of [Jerome V. C. Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome_V._C._Smith).[^cite_note-7]

In 1828, the editors of the *New England Journal of Medicine and Surgery and the Collateral Branches of Medical Science* purchased the weekly *Intelligencer* for \$600,[^cite_note-8] merging the two publications to form the *Boston Medical and Surgical Journal*, and shifting from quarterly to weekly publication.[^cite_note-9]

In 1921, the Massachusetts Medical Society purchased the *Journal* for US\$1 and, the publication was renamed to *The New England Journal of Medicine* in 1928.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11]

## Logo

The journal's logo depicts the snake-wrapped [Rod of Asclepius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rod_of_Asclepius) crossed over a [quill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quill) pen. The dates on the logo represent the founding of the components of *The New England Journal of Medicine*: 1812 for the *New England Journal of Medicine and Surgery and Collateral Branches of Medical Science*, 1823 for the *Boston Medical Intelligencer*, 1828 for the *Boston Medical and Surgical Journal*, and 1928 for the *New England Journal of Medicine*.[^cite_note-12]

- In November 1846, the New England Journal of Medicine published a report by [Henry Jacob Bigelow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Jacob_Bigelow) on the first public demonstration of a generalized anesthetic. The agent was inhaled ether, the first modern anesthetic.

  [^cite_note-13]

  &#x20;This allowed patients to remain sedated during operations ranging from [dental extraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dental_extraction) to [amputation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amputation).

  [^cite_note-Bigelow1846-14]

  &#x20;"A patient has been rendered completely insensible during an amputation of the thigh, regain consciousness after a short interval," Bigelow wrote. "Other severe operations have been performed without the knowledge of the patients."

- In June 1906, [James Homer Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Homer_Wright) published an article that described how he stained and studied [bone marrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone_marrow) with descriptions of what are now known as [megakaryocytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megakaryocytes) and [platelets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platelets).

  [^cite_note-Wright1906-15]

- In October 1872, a lecture by [Charles-Édouard Brown-Séquard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles-%C3%89douard_Brown-S%C3%A9quard) was published that proposed the then-revolutionary idea that one [cerebral hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerebral_hemisphere) can influence both sides of the body. The neurologist would go on to describe what is now known as the [Brown-Séquard syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown-S%C3%A9quard_syndrome).

  [^cite_note-Brown-SequardWebber1872-16]

- In June 1948, [Sidney Farber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Farber) reported promising results in treatment of early childhood [leukemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leukemia). Based on anecdotal evidence that children with acute leukemia worsened if they were given [folic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folic_acid), he worked on blocking folic acid metabolism. His team gave 16 infants and children with [acute lymphoblastic leukemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_lymphoblastic_leukemia) a folic acid inhibitor, [aminopterin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aminopterin)—10 showed improvement by clinical and hematologic parameters after three months.

  [^cite_note-FarberDiamond1948-17]

  &#x20;In his article, Farber advised receiving the results cautiously: "It is again emphasized that these remissions are temporary in character and that the substance is toxic and may be productive of even greater disturbances than have been encountered so far in our studies," he wrote. "No evidence has been mentioned in this report that would justify the suggestion of the term 'cure' of acute leukemia in children."

- In November 1952, cardiologist [Paul Zoll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Zoll) published an early report on resuscitation of the heart. "The purpose of this report is to describe the successful use in 2 patients of a quick, simple, effective and safe method of arousing the heart from ventricular standstill by an artificial, external, electric pacemaker", he wrote. "For the first time it was possible to keep a patient alive during [ventricular asystole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ventricular_asystole) lasting for hours to days. This procedure may prove valuable in many clinical situations."

  [^cite_note-18]

- In February 1973, *NEJM* published the first report of polyp removal using a [colonoscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonoscope) and introduced a procedure during screening to reduce cancer risk. The authors reported on 218 patients, from whom they removed 303 polyps (at one or more procedures per patient).

  [^cite_note-WolffShinya1973-19]

Based on the knowledge that BCR-ABL, a constitutively activated [tyrosine kinase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrosine_kinase), causes CML, the authors tested with success an inhibitor of this tyrosine kinase in patients who had failed first-line therapy. ## Social media

On April 25, 1996, the *NEJM* announced a new web site, which published each week the abstracts for research articles and the full text of editorials, cases, and letters to the editor. After print publishing for 184 years this was the *NEJM*'s first use of the Internet for electronic publication.[^cite_note-Campion1996-30]

The site was launched several months earlier in 1996, but the editors wanted proof that weekly electronic publication would work. Only then was an announcement approved for publication on the editorial page. In 1997, the website included prepublication releases of certain articles prior to their print publication.[^cite_note-prepub-online-31] In 1998, online publication extended to include the full text of all its articles from 1993 forward.[^cite_note-32]

Since its launch, *NEJM* has added to its site:[^cite_note-nejm200-33]

- Videos in Clinical Medicine, peer-reviewed educational videos to teach procedures requiring skilled techniques and specialized physical examination.

  [^cite_note-McMahonIngelfinger2006-34]

- Interactive Medical Cases, which mimic a clinical encounter by presenting the patient's history with results of the physical examination and laboratory and radiographic tests. Multiple-choice questions throughout test the taker's knowledge.

  [^cite_note-McMahonSolomon2009-35]

- NEJM Archive, the entire collection of the journal's published material.

  [^cite_note-nejm200-33]

## Influence

The [George Polk Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Polk_Awards) site noted that its 1977 award to *The New England Journal of Medicine*: "...provided the first significant mainstream visibility for a publication that would achieve enormous attention and prestige in the ensuing decades."[^cite_note-36]

The journal usually has the highest [impact factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_factor) of the journals of [internal medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_medicine). According to the [Journal Citation Reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_Citation_Reports), *NEJM* had a 2017 impact factor of 79.258,[^cite_note-NEJM_Fact_Sheet-37] ranking it first of 153 journals in the category "General & Internal Medicine".[^cite_note-38] It was the only journal in the category with an impact factor of more than 70. By comparison, the second and third ranked journals in the category (*[The Lancet](463899.md)* and [*JAMA*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JAMA_\(journal\))) had impact factors of 53.254 and 47.661 respectively.[^cite_note-39]

[Theodore Dalrymple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Dalrymple) feels that this influence is unwarranted. In *False Positive: A Year of Error, Omission, and Political Correctness in the New England Journal of Medicine*, he examines various articles on medical and social issues that the *NEJM* published over the course of a year. He found that many arrived at conclusions which were not supported by the evidence presented, or ignored easily available evidence that contradicted their conclusions.[^cite_note-40]

## Ingelfinger rule

*The New England Journal of Medicine* requires that articles it publishes have not been published or released elsewhere. Referred to as the [Ingelfinger rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingelfinger_rule), the policy is intended to protect newsworthiness, and to subject research to peer review "before it is touted to the public or the profession".[^cite_note-ingelfinger-41] By 1991, four types of exceptions were recognized, including when "prepublication release of research conclusions is warranted because of immediate implications for the public health".[^cite_note-42]

The rule was first described in a 1969 [editorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Editorial), "Definition of Sole Contribution", by [Franz Ingelfinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Ingelfinger), the [editor-in-chief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Editor-in-chief) at that time.[^cite_note-ingelfinger-41][^cite_note-43] A number of [medical journals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_journals) have similar rules in place.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45]

## Vioxx correction controversy

See also: [VIGOR study and publishing controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vioxx#VIGOR_study_and_publishing_controversy)

In the early 2000s, *The New England Journal of Medicine* was involved in a controversy around problems with research on the drug [Vioxx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rofecoxib). A study was published in the journal in November 2000 which noted an increase in [myocardial infarction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myocardial_infarction) amongst those taking Vioxx.[^cite_note-46] According to [Richard Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Smith_\(editor\)), the former editor of the *[British Medical Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BMJ)*, concerns about the correctness of that study were raised with the journal's editor, [Jeff Drazen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_M._Drazen), as early as August 2001. That year, both the US [Food and Drug Administration](11632.md) and the *Journal of the American Medical Association* also cast doubt on the validity of the data interpretation that had been published in the *NEJM*.[^cite_note-BMJ-47]

[Merck](30874304.md) withdrew the drug from market in September 2004. In December 2005, *NEJM* published an expression of concern about the original study following discovery that the authors knew more about certain adverse events than they disclosed at the time of publication. From the Expression of Concern: "Until the end of November 2005, we believed that these were late events that were not known to the authors in time to be included in the article published in the Journal on November 23, 2000. It now appears, however, from a memorandum dated July 5, 2000, that was obtained by subpoena in the Vioxx litigation and made available to the Journal, that at least two of the authors knew about the three additional myocardial infarctions at least two weeks before the authors submitted the first of two revisions and 4 1/2 months before publication of the article."[^cite_note-NEJM-48] Physician [John Abramson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Abramson) has noted that those authors' financial ties to Merck violated *NEJM*'s [conflict of interest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conflict_of_interest) policy, which the journal failed to enforce.[^cite_note-49]

During the five-year period between publication and Expression of Concern, it has been estimated that Merck paid *NEJM* as much as US\$836,000 for article reprints that Merck used for promotional purposes.[^cite_note-50] The journal was publicly rebuked for its response to the research issues in editorials appearing in publications including the *British Medical Journal*[^cite_note-BMJ-47] and the *[Journal of the Royal Society of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_the_Royal_Society_of_Medicine)*.[^cite_note-JRS-51]

## Open access policy

NEJM makes articles that meet the criteria for global and public health importance freely available to all readers upon publication at NEJM.org. NEJM also partners with Research4Life in their Access to Research in Health (Hinari) program to grant to low-income countries immediate free access to NEJM.org.

NEJM does not charge authors any submission or publication fees. NEJM also works with authors whose articles report research supported by funding bodies with open access mandates, including (but not limited to) Plan S funders and the U.S. government, including NIH, to ensure that authors are able to meet their funders' requirements for public access to research results.

For research articles submitted before February 1, 2024, NEJM makes the full-text Version of Record available at NEJM.org six months after publication. For research articles submitted on or after February 1, 2024, NEJM will provide authors with a PDF file of the Author Accepted Manuscript that may be deposited in a noncommercial repository after publication.[^cite_note-52]

*NEJM* also has two [podcast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podcast) features, one with interviews of doctors and researchers that are publishing in the journal, and another summarizing the content of each issue. Other offerings include Continuing Medical Education, Videos in Clinical Medicine (showing videos of medical procedures), and the weekly Image Challenge.
