**State media** are typically understood as media outlets that are owned, operated, or significantly influenced by the government.[^cite_note-pf0000380618-1] They are sometimes distinguished from [public service media](178808.md), which are designed to serve the public interest, operate independently of government control, and are financed through a combination of public funding, licensing fees, and sometimes advertising. The crucial difference lies in the level of independence from government influence and the commitment to building trust and serving a broad public interest rather than the interests of a specific political party or government agenda.[^cite_note-pf0000380618-1][^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3]

State media serve as tools for public diplomacy and narrative shaping. These media outlets can broadcast via television, radio, print, and increasingly on social media, to convey government viewpoints to domestic and international audiences. The approach to using state media can vary, focusing on positive narratives, adjusting narratives retroactively, or spreading misinformation through sophisticated social media campaigns.[^cite_note-4] State media are an important tool of regime control for [authoritarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authoritarianism) governments.[^cite_note-5]

[Public](178808.md) or state media may be financed through different sources, such as direct [government funding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_funding), a [licence fee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Licence_fee) paid by television viewers, [donations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donation), and [commercial advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_advertising).

## Other definitions

State media is also referred to media entities that are administered, funded, managed, or directly controlled by the [government](12229.md) of a country.[^cite_note-DragomirSöderström2022-6] Three factors that can affect the independence of state media over time are: funding, ownership/governance, and editorial autonomy.[^cite_note-DragomirSöderström2022-6] These entities can range from being completely state-controlled, where the government has full control over their funding, management, and editorial content, to being independent [public service media](178808.md), which, despite receiving government funding, operate with editorial autonomy and are governed by structures designed to protect them from direct political interference.[^cite_note-DragomirSöderström2022-6]

State media is often associated with authoritarian governments that use state media to control, influence, and limit information.[^cite_note-StockmannGallagher2011-7]

## Overview

Its content, according to some sources, is usually more prescriptive, telling the audience what to think, particularly as it is under no pressure to attract high ratings or generate advertising revenue[^cite_note-silver-8] and therefore may cater to the forces in control of the state as opposed to the forces in control of the corporation, as described in the [propaganda model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda_model) of the mass media. In more controlled regions, the state may [censor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship) content which it deems illegal, immoral or unfavorable to the government and likewise regulate any programming related to the media; therefore, it is not independent of the governing party.[^cite_note-9] In this type of environment, journalists may be required to be members or affiliated with the ruling party, such as in the [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc) former Socialist States the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union), [China](5405.md) or [North Korea](21255.md).[^cite_note-silver-8] Within countries that have high levels of [government interference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_interference) in the media, it may use the state press for [propaganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda) purposes:

- To promote the state in a favorable light,

- Vilify opposition to the government by launching smear campaigns

- Giving skewed coverage to opposition views, or

- Act as a [mouthpiece](338047.md) to advocate a state's [ideology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology).

Additionally, the state-controlled media may only report on legislation after it has already become law to stifle any debate.[^cite_note-10] The media legitimizes its presence by emphasizing "national unity" against domestic or foreign "aggressors".[^cite_note-11] In more open and competitive contexts, the state may control or fund its own outlet and is in competition with opposition-controlled and/or [independent media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_media). The state media usually have less government control in more open societies and can provide more balanced coverage than media outside of state control.[^cite_note-12]

State media outlets usually enjoy increased funding and subsidies compared to private media counterparts, but this can create inefficiency in the state media.[^cite_note-13] However, in the [People's Republic of China](5405.md), where state control of the media is high, levels of funding have been reduced for state outlets, which have forced [Chinese Communist Party](7175.md) media to sidestep official restrictions on content or publish "soft" editions, such as weekend editions, to generate income.[^cite_note-14]

State media can be classified based on their relationship to the state, including factors such as ownership, editorial independence, funding, and political alignment. This framework is commonly used by media watchdogs, and international organizations to assess press freedom, transparency, and the role of media in democratic or authoritarian regimes.[^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16][^cite_note-pf0000380618-1]

## Theories of state ownership

Two contrasting theories of state control of the media exist; the public interest or Pigouvian theory states that government ownership is beneficial, whereas the public choice theory suggests that state control undermines [economic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_freedom) and [political freedoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_freedom).

### Public interest theory

The [public interest theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_interest_theory) states that government ownership of media is desirable.[^cite_note-17] Three reasons are offered. Firstly, the dissemination of information is a public good, and to withhold it would be costly even if it is not paid for. Secondly, the cost of the provision and dissemination of information is high, but once costs are incurred, marginal costs for providing the information are low and so are subject to increasing returns.[^cite_note-p342-18] Thirdly, state media ownership can be less biased, more complete and accurate if consumers are ignorant and in addition to private media that would serve the governing classes.[^cite_note-p342-18] However, Pigouvian economists, who advocate regulation and [nationalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalization), are supportive of free and private media.[^cite_note-19] Public interest theory holds that when operated correctly, government ownership of media is a public good that benefits the nation in question.[^cite_note-DjankovMcLieshNenovaShleifer2003-20] It contradicts the belief that all state media is propaganda and argues that most states require an unbiased, easily accessible, and reliable stream of information.[^cite_note-DjankovMcLieshNenovaShleifer2003-20] State media can be established as a mean for the state to provide a consistent news outlet while private news companies operate as well. The benefits and detriments of this approach often depend on the editorial independence of the media organization from the government.[^cite_note-21]

Many criticisms of public interest theory center on the possibility of true editorial independence from the state.[^cite_note-DjankovMcLieshNenovaShleifer2003-20] While there is little profit motive, the media organization must be funded by the government instead which can create a dependency on the government's willingness to fund an entity may often be critical of their work.[^cite_note-StockmannGallagher2011-7] The reliability of a state-run media outlet is often heavily dependent on the reliability of the state to promote a free press, many state-run media outlets in western democracies are capable of providing independent journalism while others in authoritarian regimes become mouthpieces for the state to legitimize their actions.[^cite_note-DjankovMcLieshNenovaShleifer2003-20]

### Public choice theory

The [public choice theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_choice) asserts that state-owned media would manipulate and distort information in favor of the ruling party and entrench its rule and prevent the public from making informed decisions, which undermines democratic institutions.[^cite_note-p342-18] That would prevent private and independent media, which provide alternate voices allowing individuals to choose politicians, goods, services, etc. without fear from functioning. Additionally, that would inhibit competition among media firms that would ensure that consumers usually acquire unbiased, accurate information.[^cite_note-p342-18] Moreover, this competition is part of a checks-and-balances system of a [democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy), known as the [Fourth Estate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Estate), along with the [judiciary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judiciary), [executive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_\(government\)) and [legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislature).[^cite_note-p342-18] States are dependent on the public for their legitimacy that allows them to operate.[^cite_note-22] The flow of information becomes critical to their survival, and public choice theory argues that states cannot be expected to ignore their own interests, and instead the sources of information must remain as independent from the state as possible.[^cite_note-DjankovMcLieshNenovaShleifer2003-20] Public choice theory argues that the only way to retain independence in a media organization is to allow the public to seek the best sources of information themselves.[^cite_note-GrosecloseMilyo2005-23] This approach is effective at creating a free press that is capable of criticizing government institutions and investigating incidents of government corruption.[^cite_note-DjankovMcLieshNenovaShleifer2003-20]

Those critical of the public choice theory argue that the economic incentives involved in a public business force media organizations to stray from unbiased journalism and towards sensationalist editorials in order to capture public interest.[^cite_note-Dragomir2017-24] This has become a debate over the effectiveness of media organizations that are reliant on the attention of the public.[^cite_note-Dragomir2017-24] Sensationalism becomes the key focus and turns away from stories in the public interest in favor of stories that capture the attention of the most people.[^cite_note-GrosecloseMilyo2005-23] The focus on sensationalism and public attention can lead to the dissemination of misinformation to appease their consumer base.[^cite_note-GrosecloseMilyo2005-23] In these instances, the goal of providing accurate information to the public collapses and instead becomes biased toward a dominant ideology.[^cite_note-GrosecloseMilyo2005-23]

Both theories have implications regarding the determinants and consequences of ownership of the media.[^cite_note-25] The public interest theory suggests that more benign governments should have higher levels of control of the media which would in turn increase [press freedom](105323.md) as well as economic and political freedoms. Conversely, the public choice theory affirms that the opposite is true - "public spirited", benevolent governments should have less control which would increase these freedoms.[^cite_note-26]

Generally, state ownership of the media is found in poor, [autocratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocratic) non-democratic countries with highly interventionist governments that have some interest in controlling the flow of information.[^cite_note-27] Countries with "weak" governments do not possess the political will to break up state media monopolies.[^cite_note-28] Media control is also usually consistent with state ownership in the [economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy).[^cite_note-29]

As of 2002, the press in most of [Europe](9239.md) (with the exception of [Belarus](3457.md), [Russia](25391.md) and [Ukraine](31750.md)) is mostly private and free of state control and ownership, along with [North](21139.md) and South America (with the exception of [Cuba](5042481.md) and [Venezuela](32374.md))[^cite_note-p19-30] The press "role" in the national and societal dynamics of the [United States](3434750.md) and [Australia](4689264.md) has virtually always been the responsibility of the private commercial sector since these countries' earliest days.[^cite_note-31] Levels of state ownership are higher in some [African](5334607.md) countries, the [Middle East](19323.md) and some [Asian](689.md) countries (with the exception of [Japan](15573.md), [India](14533.md), [Indonesia](14579.md), [Mongolia](19271.md), [Nepal](171166.md), the [Philippines](23440.md), [South Korea](27019.md) and [Thailand](30128.md) where large areas of private press exist.) Full state monopolies exist in [China](5405.md), [Myanmar](19457.md), and [North Korea](21255.md).[^cite_note-p19-30]

## Consequences of state ownership

Issues with state media include complications with [press freedom](105323.md) and [journalistic objectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journalistic_objectivity). In general, state media typically has lower levels of trust than public media.[^cite_note-\:0-32] According to Christopher Walker in the *[Journal of Democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Democracy)*, "[authoritarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authoritarian) or [totalitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totalitarian) media outlets" take advantage of both domestic and foreign media due to state censorship in their native countries and the openness of democratic nations to which they broadcast. He cites China's [CCTV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_Central_Television), Russia's [RT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia_Today), and Venezuela's [TeleSUR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TeleSUR) as examples.[^cite_note-33]

### Civil and political rights

The public interest theory claims state ownership of the press enhances [civil and political rights](37071.md); whilst under the public choice theory, it curtails them by suppressing public oversight of the government and facilitating [political corruption](66220.md). High to absolute government control of the media is primarily associated with lower levels of political and civil rights, higher levels of corruption, quality of regulation, security of property and [media bias](18932.md).[^cite_note-p367-37][^cite_note-38] State ownership of the press can compromise election monitoring efforts and obscure the integrity of electoral processes.[^cite_note-39] Independent media sees higher oversight by the media of the government. For example, reporting of corruption increased in [Mexico](3966054.md), [Ghana](12067.md) and [Kenya](188171.md) after restrictions were lifted in the 1990s, but government-controlled media defended officials.[^cite_note-40][^cite_note-p25-41] Heavily influenced state media can provide corrupt regimes with a method to combat efforts by protestors.[^cite_note-StockmannGallagher2011-7] Propaganda spread by state-media organizations can detract from accurate reporting and provide an opportunity for a regime to influence public sentiment.[^cite_note-DjankovMcLieshNenovaShleifer2003-20] Mass protests against governments considered to be authoritarian, such as those in China, Russia, Egypt, and Iran are often distorted by state-run media organizations in order to defame protesters and provide a positive light on the government's actions.[^cite_note-StockmannGallagher2011-7][^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44]

### Economic freedom

It is common for countries with strict control of newspapers to have fewer firms listed per capita on their markets[^cite_note-45] and less developed banking systems.[^cite_note-46] These findings support the public choice theory, which suggests higher levels of state ownership of the press would be detrimental to economic and financial development.[^cite_note-p367-37] This is due to state media being commonly associated with autocratic regimes where economic freedom is severely restricted and there is a large amount of corruption within the economic and political system.[^cite_note-Dragomir2017-24]

### Trust

In countries like Hungary (as of 2024) and Poland (as of 2022), state media has suffered from low levels of trust.[^cite_note-\:0-32] Surveys have found that state-owned [television in Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_in_Russia) is viewed by the Russian public as one of the country's most authoritative and trusted institutions.[^cite_note-\:Wang-47][^cite_note-48]

### Large language models

According to a 2026 *Nature* study, state media significantly shape the outputs of large language models (LLMs), as LLMs are trained on state media content.[^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50]
