# Parasitism

"Parasite" redirects here. For other uses, see [Parasite (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasite_\(disambiguation\)).



A [fish parasite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_parasite), the isopod *[Cymothoa exigua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cymothoa_exigua)*, replacing the tongue of a *[Lithognathus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithognathus)*

**Parasitism** is a [close relationship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbiosis) between [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species), where one organism, the **parasite**, lives (at least some of the time) [on](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ectoparasitic_infestation) or inside another organism, the [host](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_\(biology\)), causing it some harm,[^cite_note-ParasiteDefinition-1] and is [adapted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptation) structurally to this way of life.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPoulin20074–5-2] Parasites include single-celled [protozoans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protozoa) such as the agents of [malaria](20423.md), [sleeping sickness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleeping_sickness), and [amoebic dysentery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amoebic_dysentery); animals such as [hookworms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hookworm), [lice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lice), [mosquitoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosquito), and [vampire bats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vampire_bat); [fungi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungi) such as [honey fungus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armillaria_mellea) and the agents of [ringworm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ringworm); and plants such as [mistletoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mistletoe), [dodder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodder), and the [broomrapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orobanchaceae).

There are six major parasitic [strategies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_ecology#Evolutionarily_stable_strategy) of exploitation of animal hosts, namely [parasitic castration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitic_castration), directly transmitted parasitism (by contact), [trophically-transmitted](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/trophic) parasitism (by being eaten), [vector-transmitted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disease_vector) parasitism, [parasitoidism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitoid), and micropredation. One major axis of classification concerns invasiveness: an endoparasite lives inside the host's body; an ectoparasite lives outside, on the host's surface.

Like [predation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predation), parasitism is a type of [consumer–resource interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer%E2%80%93resource_interactions),[^cite_note-pmid21199247-3] but unlike predators, parasites, with the exception of parasitoids, are much smaller than their hosts, do not kill them, and often live in or on their hosts for an extended period. Parasites of animals are highly [specialised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalist_and_specialist_species), each parasite species living on one given animal species, and [reproduce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproduction) at a faster rate than their hosts. Classic examples include interactions between [vertebrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertebrate) hosts and [tapeworms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapeworms), [flukes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trematoda), and those between the malaria-causing *[Plasmodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium)* species, and [fleas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flea).

Parasites reduce host [fitness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitness_\(biology\)) by general or specialised [pathology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathology), that ranges from [parasitic castration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitic_castration) to [modification of host behaviour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavior-altering_parasites). Parasites increase their own fitness by exploiting hosts for resources necessary for their survival, in particular by feeding on them and by using intermediate (secondary) hosts to assist in their [transmission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_\(medicine\)) from one definitive (primary) host to another. Although parasitism is often unambiguous, it is part of a spectrum of interactions between [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species), grading via parasitoidism into predation, through evolution into [mutualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutualism_\(biology\)), and in some fungi, shading into being [saprophytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saprophytic).

Human knowledge of parasites such as [roundworms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundworm) and [tapeworms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapeworm) dates back to [ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt), [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece), and [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome). In [early modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern) times, [Antonie van Leeuwenhoek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek) observed *[Giardia lamblia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giardia_lamblia)* with his microscope in 1681, while [Francesco Redi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Redi) described internal and external parasites including [sheep liver fluke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheep_liver_fluke) and [ticks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tick). Modern [parasitology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitology) developed in the 19th century. In human culture, parasitism has negative connotations. These were exploited to [satirical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satire) effect in [Jonathan Swift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Swift)'s 1733 poem "On Poetry: A Rhapsody", comparing poets to [hyperparasitical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperparasitical) "vermin". In fiction, [Bram Stoker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bram_Stoker)'s 1897 [Gothic horror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_fiction) novel *[Dracula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dracula)* and its many later adaptations featured a [blood-drinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematophagy) parasite. [Ridley Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ridley_Scott)'s 1979 film *[Alien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alien_\(film\))* was one of many works of [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction) to feature a parasitic alien species.[^cite_note-Guardian2009-4]

## Etymology

First used in English in 1539, the word *parasite* comes from the [Medieval French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_French) *parasite*, from the [Latinised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latinisation_\(literature\)) form *parasitus*, from [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language)&#x20;*&#x20;*&#x3C0;αράσιτο&#x3C2;*[^cite_note-5] (parasitos)* 'one who eats at the table of another', &#x20;*&#x20;*&#x69;n turn fro&#x6D;*&#x20;*&#x3C0;αρ&#x3AC;*[^cite_note-6] (para)* 'beside, by' and&#x20;*&#x20;*&#x3C3;ῖτο&#x3C2;*&#x20;(sitos)* 'wheat, food'.[^cite_note-7] The related term *parasitism* appears in English from 1611.[^cite_note-8]

## Evolutionary strategies

See also: [Parasitology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitology)

### Basic concepts

Further information: [Parasitic life cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitic_life_cycle)



Head (scolex) of tapeworm *[Taenia solium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taenia_solium)*, an [intestinal parasite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intestinal_parasite), has hooks and [suckers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucker_\(zoology\)) to attach to its [host](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_\(biology\))

Parasitism is a kind of [symbiosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbiosis), a close and persistent long-term biological interaction between a parasite and its host. Unlike [saprotrophs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saprotroph), parasites feed on living hosts, though some parasitic fungi, for instance, may continue to feed on hosts they have killed. Unlike [commensalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commensalism) and [mutualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutualism_\(biology\)), the parasitic relationship harms the host, either feeding on it or, as in the case of intestinal parasites, consuming some of its food. Because parasites interact with other species, they can readily act as [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disease_vector) of pathogens, causing [disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitic_disease).[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-SuzukiSasaki2019-10][^cite_note-pmid37458178-11] [Predation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predation) is by definition not a symbiosis, as the interaction is brief, but the entomologist [E. O. Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._O._Wilson) has characterised parasites as "predators that eat prey in units of less than one".[^cite_note-Wilson2014-12]

Within that scope are many possible strategies. [Taxonomists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomist) classify parasites in a variety of overlapping schemes, based on their interactions with their hosts and on their [life cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_life_cycle), which can be complex. An [obligate parasite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obligate_parasite) depends completely on the host to complete its life cycle, while a [facultative parasite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facultative_parasite) does not. Parasite life cycles involving only one host are called "direct"; those with a definitive host (where the parasite reproduces sexually) and at least one intermediate host are called "indirect".[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14] An endoparasite lives inside the host's body; an ectoparasite lives outside, on the host's surface.[^cite_note-Para-Site-15] Mesoparasites—like some [copepods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copepod), for example—enter an opening in the host's body and remain partly embedded there.[^cite_note-16] Some parasites can be generalists, feeding on a wide range of hosts, but many parasites, and the majority of protozoans and [helminths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helminth) that parasitise animals, are specialists and extremely host-specific.[^cite_note-Para-Site-15] An early basic, functional division of parasites distinguished microparasites and macroparasites. These each had a [mathematical model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_model) assigned in order to analyse the population movements of the host–parasite groupings.[^cite_note-Rollinson-17] The microorganisms and viruses that can reproduce and complete their life cycle within the host are known as microparasites. Macroparasites are the multicellular organisms that reproduce and complete their life cycle outside of the host or on the host's body.[^cite_note-Rollinson-17][^cite_note-18]

Much of the thinking on types of parasitism has focused on terrestrial animal parasites of animals, such as helminths. Those in other environments and with other hosts often have analogous strategies. For example, the [snubnosed eel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snubnosed_eel) is probably a facultative endoparasite (i.e., it is semiparasitic) that opportunistically burrows into and eats sick and dying fish.[^cite_note-Caira1997-19] [Plant-eating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phytophagous) insects such as [scale insects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_insect), [aphids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphid), and [caterpillars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caterpillar) closely resemble ectoparasites, attacking much larger plants; they serve as vectors of bacteria, fungi and viruses which cause [plant diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_pathology). As female scale insects cannot move, they are obligate parasites, permanently attached to their hosts.[^cite_note-Rollinson-17]

The sensory inputs that a parasite employs to identify and approach a potential host are known as "host cues". Such cues can include, for example, vibration,[^cite_note-20] exhaled [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide), skin odours, visual and heat signatures, and moisture.[^cite_note-21] [Parasitic plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitic_plant) can use, for example, light, host physiochemistry, and volatiles to recognize potential hosts.[^cite_note-22]

### Major strategies

There are six major parasitic [strategies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_ecology#Evolutionarily_stable_strategy), namely [parasitic castration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitic_castrator); directly transmitted parasitism; [trophically](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/trophic)-transmitted parasitism; [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_\(epidemiology\))-transmitted parasitism; [parasitoidism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitoid); and micropredation. These apply to parasites whose hosts are plants as well as animals.[^cite_note-Rollinson-17][^cite_note-PoulinRandhawa2015-23] These strategies represent [adaptive peaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptation); intermediate strategies are possible, but organisms in many different groups have consistently [converged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_evolution) on these six, which are evolutionarily stable.[^cite_note-PoulinRandhawa2015-23]

A perspective on the evolutionary options can be gained by considering four key questions: the effect on the [fitness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitness_\(biology\)) of a parasite's hosts; the number of hosts they have per life stage; whether the host is prevented from reproducing; and whether the effect depends on intensity (number of parasites per host). From this analysis, the major evolutionary strategies of parasitism emerge, alongside predation.[^cite_note-LaffertyKuris2002-24]

| Host [fitness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitness_\(biology\)) | Single host, stays alive | Single host, dies | Multiple hosts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Able to reproduce (fitness > 0)** | Conventional parasite    *Pathogen* | Trophically-transmitted parasite[^cite_note-25]    *Trophically-transmitted pathogen* | Micropredator    *Micropredator* |
| **Unable to reproduce (fitness = 0)** | -----    *Parasitic castrator* | Trophically-transmitted parasitic castrator    *Parasitoid* | Social predator[^cite_note-26]    *Solitary predator* |

#### Parasitic castrators

Main article: [Parasitic castration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitic_castration)



The parasitic castrator *[Sacculina carcini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacculina_carcini)* (highlighted) attached to [its crab host](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liocarcinus_holsatus)

[Parasitic castrators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitic_castrator) partly or completely destroy their host's ability to reproduce, diverting the energy that would have gone into reproduction into host and parasite growth, sometimes causing gigantism in the host. The host's other systems remain intact, allowing it to survive and to sustain the parasite.[^cite_note-PoulinRandhawa2015-23][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPoulin2007111-27] Parasitic crustaceans such as those in the specialised [barnacle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnacle) genus *[Sacculina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacculina)* specifically cause damage to the gonads of their many species[^cite_note-Elumalai_Viswanathan_Pravinkumar_Raffi-28] of host [crabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crab). In the case of *Sacculina*, the testes of over two-thirds of their crab hosts degenerate sufficiently for these male crabs to develop female [secondary sex characteristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_sex_characteristic) such as broader abdomens, smaller [claws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chela_\(organ\)) and egg-grasping appendages. Various species of helminth castrate their hosts (such as insects and snails). This may happen directly, whether mechanically by feeding on their gonads, or by secreting a chemical that destroys reproductive cells; or indirectly, whether by secreting a hormone or by diverting nutrients. For example, the [trematode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trematoda) *[Zoogonus lasius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoogonus_lasius?action=edit\&redlink=1)*, whose [sporocysts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trematode_life_cycle_stages) lack mouths, castrates the intertidal marine snail *[Tritia obsoleta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tritia_obsoleta)* chemically, developing in its gonad and killing its reproductive cells.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPoulin2007111-27][^cite_note-Cheng13-29]

#### Directly transmitted



[Human head-lice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pediculus_humanus_capitis) are directly transmitted [obligate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obligate_parasite) ectoparasites

Directly transmitted parasites, not requiring a vector to reach their hosts, include such parasites of terrestrial vertebrates as lice and mites; marine parasites such as [copepods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copepod) and [cyamid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyamidae) amphipods; [monogeneans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monogenea); and many species of nematodes, fungi, protozoans, bacteria, and viruses. Whether endoparasites or ectoparasites, each has a single host-species. Within that species, most individuals are free or almost free of parasites, while a minority carry a large number of parasites; this is known as an [aggregated distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aggregated_distribution).[^cite_note-PoulinRandhawa2015-23]

#### Trophically transmitted



*[Clonorchis sinensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clonorchis_sinensis)*, the Chinese liver fluke, is trophically transmitted

[Trophically](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/trophic)-transmitted parasites are transmitted by being eaten by a host. They include trematodes (all except [schistosomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schistosomes)), [cestodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cestodes), [acanthocephalans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acanthocephala), [pentastomids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentastomida), many [roundworms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundworms), and many protozoa such as *[Toxoplasma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxoplasma)*.[^cite_note-PoulinRandhawa2015-23] They have complex life cycles involving hosts of two or more species. In their juvenile stages they infect and often [encyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyst) in the intermediate host. When the intermediate-host animal is eaten by a predator, the definitive host, the parasite survives the digestion process and matures into an adult; some live as [intestinal parasites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intestinal_parasite). Many trophically transmitted parasites [modify the behaviour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasite_increased_trophic_transmission) of their intermediate hosts, increasing their chances of being eaten by a predator. As with directly transmitted parasites, the distribution of trophically transmitted parasites among host individuals is aggregated.[^cite_note-PoulinRandhawa2015-23] [Coinfection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinfection) by multiple parasites is common.[^cite_note-30] [Autoinfection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strongyloides_stercoralis#Autoinfection), where (by exception) the whole of the parasite's [life cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_life_cycle) takes place in a single primary host, can sometimes occur in helminths such as *[Strongyloides stercoralis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strongyloides_stercoralis)*.[^cite_note-ASP-31]

#### Vector-transmitted

Further information: [Disease vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disease_vector)



The [vector-transmitted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_\(epidemiology\)) protozoan endoparasite *[Trypanosoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trypanosoma)* among human [red blood cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_blood_cell)

[Vector-transmitted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disease_vector) parasites rely on a third party, an intermediate host, where the parasite does not reproduce sexually,[^cite_note-Para-Site-15] to carry them from one definitive host to another.[^cite_note-PoulinRandhawa2015-23] These parasites are microorganisms, namely [protozoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protozoa), [bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteria), or [viruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus), often intracellular [pathogens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathogen) (disease-causers).[^cite_note-PoulinRandhawa2015-23] Their vectors are mostly [hematophagic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematophagy) arthropods such as fleas, lice, ticks, and mosquitoes.[^cite_note-PoulinRandhawa2015-23][^cite_note-32] For example, the deer tick *[Ixodes scapularis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ixodes_scapularis)* acts as a vector for diseases including [Lyme disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyme_disease), [babesiosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babesiosis), and [anaplasmosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_granulocytic_anaplasmosis).[^cite_note-pmid11450660-33] Protozoan endoparasites, such as the [malarial](20423.md) parasites in the genus *[Plasmodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium)* and sleeping-sickness parasites in the genus *[Trypanosoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trypanosoma)*, have infective stages in the host's blood which are transported to new hosts by biting insects.[^cite_note-PollittMacGregor2011-34]

#### Parasitoids

Main articles: [Parasitoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitoid) and [Parasitoid wasp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitoid_wasp)

[Parasitoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitoid) are insects which sooner or later kill their hosts, placing their relationship close to predation.[^cite_note-35] Most parasitoids are [parasitoid wasps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitoid_wasp) or other [hymenopterans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hymenoptera); others include [dipterans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipterans) such as [phorid flies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoridae). They can be divided into two groups, idiobionts and koinobionts, differing in their treatment of their hosts.[^cite_note-GullanCranston2010-36]

[Idiobiont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idiobiont) parasitoids sting their often-large prey on capture, either killing them outright or paralysing them immediately. The immobilised prey is then carried to a nest, sometimes alongside other prey if it is not large enough to support a parasitoid throughout its development. An [egg is laid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oviposition) on top of the prey and the nest is then sealed. The parasitoid develops rapidly through its larval and pupal stages, [feeding on the provisions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_provisioning) left for it.[^cite_note-GullanCranston2010-36]

[Koinobiont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koinobiont) parasitoids, which include [flies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diptera) as well as wasps, lay their eggs inside young hosts, usually larvae. These are allowed to go on growing, so the host and parasitoid develop together for an extended period, ending when the parasitoids emerge as adults, leaving the prey dead, eaten from inside. Some koinobionts regulate their host's development, for example preventing it from [pupating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pupa) or making it [moult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecdysis) whenever the parasitoid is ready to moult. They may do this by producing hormones that mimic the host's moulting hormones ([ecdysteroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecdysteroid)), or by regulating the host's endocrine system.[^cite_note-GullanCranston2010-36]

#### Micropredators



[Mosquitoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosquito) are micropredators and important vectors of disease

A micropredator attacks more than one host, reducing each host's fitness by at least a small amount, and is only in contact with any one host intermittently. This behavior makes micropredators suitable as vectors, as they can pass smaller parasites from one host to another.[^cite_note-PoulinRandhawa2015-23][^cite_note-LaffertyKuris2002-24][^cite_note-Wilson2017-37] Most micropredators are [hematophagic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematophagy), feeding on blood. They include annelids such as [leeches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leech), crustaceans such as [branchiurans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branchiura) and [gnathiid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnathiidae) isopods, various [dipterans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diptera) such as mosquitoes and [tsetse flies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsetse_fly), other arthropods such as fleas and ticks, vertebrates such as [lampreys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamprey), and mammals such as [vampire bats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vampire_bat).[^cite_note-PoulinRandhawa2015-23]

### Transmission strategies

Parasites use a variety of methods to infect animal hosts, including physical contact, the [fecal–oral route](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fecal%E2%80%93oral_route), free-living infectious stages, and vectors, suiting their differing hosts, life cycles, and ecological contexts.[^cite_note-Godfrey2013-38] Examples to illustrate some of the many possible combinations are given in the table.

### Variations

Among the many variations on parasitic strategies are hyperparasitism,[^cite_note-Dissanaike1957-40] social parasitism,[^cite_note-Thomas2010-41] brood parasitism,[^cite_note-Payne1997-42] kleptoparasitism,[^cite_note-SlaterRosenblatt2005-43] sexual parasitism,[^cite_note-Pietsch2005-44] and adelphoparasitism.[^cite_note-Rochat2001-45]

#### Hyperparasitism

Main article: [Hyperparasite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperparasite)

[Hyperparasites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperparasite) feed on another parasite, as exemplified by protozoa living in helminth parasites,[^cite_note-Dissanaike1957-40] or facultative or obligate parasitoids whose hosts are either conventional parasites or parasitoids.[^cite_note-PoulinRandhawa2015-23][^cite_note-GullanCranston2010-36] Levels of parasitism beyond secondary also occur, especially among facultative parasitoids. In [oak gall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oak_gall) systems, there can be up to four levels of parasitism.[^cite_note-46]

Hyperparasites can control their hosts' populations, and are used for this purpose [in agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_pest_control) and to some extent in [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine). The controlling effects can be seen in the way that the [CHV1 virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypovirus#CHV1%E2%80%93Chestnut_blight_hypovirulence) helps to control the damage that [chestnut blight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chestnut_blight), *Cryphonectria parasitica*, does to [American chestnut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_chestnut) trees, and in the way that [bacteriophages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriophage) can limit bacterial infections. It is likely, though little researched, that most pathogenic microparasites have hyperparasites which may prove widely useful in both agriculture and medicine.[^cite_note-ParrattLaine2016-47]

#### Social parasitism

Further information: [Ant mimicry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ant_mimicry), [Cuckoo bee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuckoo_bee), and [Emery's rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emery's_rule)

Social parasites take advantage of interspecific interactions between members of [eusocial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eusocial) animals such as [ants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ant), [termites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Termite), and [bumblebees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bumblebee). Examples include the large blue butterfly, *[Phengaris arion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phengaris_arion)*, its larvae employing [ant mimicry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ant_mimicry) to parasitise certain ants,[^cite_note-Thomas2010-41] *[Bombus bohemicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombus_bohemicus)*, a bumblebee which invades the hives of other bees and takes over reproduction while their young are raised by host workers, and *[Melipona scutellaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melipona_scutellaris)*, a eusocial bee whose virgin queens escape killer workers and invade another colony without a queen.[^cite_note-48] An extreme example of interspecific social parasitism is found in the ant *[Tetramorium inquilinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetramorium_inquilinum)*, an obligate parasite which lives exclusively on the backs of other *Tetramorium* ants.[^cite_note-49] A mechanism for the evolution of social parasitism was first proposed by Carlo Emery in 1909.[^cite_note-50] Now known as "[Emery's rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emery's_rule)", it states that social parasites tend to be closely related to their hosts, often being in the same genus.[^cite_note-RD-51][^cite_note-Emery_1909-52][^cite_note-53]

Intraspecific social parasitism occurs in parasitic nursing, where some individual young take milk from unrelated females. In [wedge-capped capuchins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedge-capped_capuchin), higher ranking females sometimes take milk from low ranking females without any reciprocation.[^cite_note-obrien5-54]

#### Brood parasitism

Further information: [Brood parasitism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brood_parasitism)

In [brood parasitism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brood_parasitism), the hosts suffer increased parental investment and energy expenditure to feed parasitic young, which are commonly larger than host young. The growth rate of host nestlings is slowed, reducing the host's fitness. Brood parasites include birds in different families such as [cowbirds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cowbird), [whydahs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viduidae), [cuckoos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuckoo), and [black-headed ducks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black-headed_duck). These do not build nests of their own, but leave their eggs in nests of other [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species). In the family *[Cuculidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuckoo)*, over 40% of cuckoo species are obligate brood parasites, while others are either facultative brood parasites or provide parental care.[^cite_note-55] The eggs of some brood parasites [mimic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimicry) those of their hosts, while some cowbird eggs have tough shells, making them hard for the hosts to kill by piercing, both mechanisms implying selection by the hosts against parasitic eggs.[^cite_note-Payne1997-42][^cite_note-56][^cite_note-DeMarsico2013-57] The adult female [European cuckoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_cuckoo) further mimics a predator, the [European sparrowhawk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_sparrowhawk), giving her time to lay her eggs in the host's nest unobserved.[^cite_note-Welbergen2011-58] Host species often combat parasitic egg mimicry through egg [polymorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymorphism_\(biology\)), having two or more egg phenotypes within a single population of a species. Multiple phenotypes in host eggs decrease the probability of a parasitic species accurately "matching" their eggs to host eggs.[^cite_note-59]

#### Kleptoparasitism

Further information: [Kleptoparasitism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleptoparasitism)

In [kleptoparasitism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleptoparasitism) (from Greek κλέπτης (*kleptēs*), "thief"), parasites steal food gathered by the host. The parasitism is often on close relatives, whether within the same species or between species in the same genus or family. For instance, the many lineages of [cuckoo bees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuckoo_bee) lay their eggs in the nest cells of other [bees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bee) in the same family.[^cite_note-SlaterRosenblatt2005-43] Kleptoparasitism is uncommon generally but conspicuous in birds; some such as [skuas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skuas) are specialised in pirating food from other seabirds, relentlessly chasing them down until they disgorge their catch.[^cite_note-Furness1978-60]

#### Sexual parasitism

Main article: [Sexual parasitism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_parasitism)

A unique approach is seen in some species of [anglerfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglerfish), such as *[Ceratias holboelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceratias_holboelli)*, where the males are reduced to tiny [sexual parasites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_parasite), wholly dependent on females of their own species for survival, permanently attached below the female's body, and unable to fend for themselves. The female nourishes the male and protects him from predators, while the male gives nothing back except the sperm that the female needs to produce the next generation.[^cite_note-Pietsch2005-44]

#### Adelphoparasitism

Adelphoparasitism, (from Greek [ἀδελφός](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E1%BC%80%CE%B4%CE%B5%CE%BB%CF%86%CF%8C%CF%82) (*adelphós*), brother[^cite_note-61]), also known as sibling-parasitism, occurs where the host species is closely related to the parasite, often in the same family or genus.[^cite_note-Rochat2001-45] In the citrus blackfly parasitoid, *[Encarsia perplexa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encarsia_perplexa)*, unmated females may lay [haploid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ploidy#haploid) eggs in the fully developed larvae of their own species, producing male offspring,[^cite_note-62] while the marine worm *[Bonellia viridis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonellia_viridis)* has a similar reproductive strategy, although the larvae are planktonic.[^cite_note-BerecSchembri2005-63]

### Plants

Main article: [Parasitic plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitic_plant)



*[Cuscuta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuscuta)* (a dodder), a stem [holoparasite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holoparasite), on an [acacia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acacia) tree

A [hemiparasite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemiparasite) or *partial parasite* such as [mistletoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mistletoe) derives some of its nutrients from another living plant, whereas a [holoparasite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obligate_parasite) such as *[Cuscuta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuscuta)* derives all of its nutrients from another plant.[^cite_note-Rastogi-69] [Parasitic plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitic_plant) make up about one per cent of [angiosperms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angiosperms) and are in almost every [biome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biome) in the world.[^cite_note-Kokla_Melnyk_2010-70][^cite_note-Heide-Jørgensen,_H._2008-71][^cite_note-Nickrent2002-72] All these plants have modified roots, [haustoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haustorium), which penetrate the host plants, connecting them to the conductive system—either the [xylem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylem), the [phloem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phloem), or both. This provides them with the ability to extract water and nutrients from the host. A parasitic plant is classified depending on where it latches onto the host, either the stem or the root, and the amount of nutrients it requires. Since holoparasites have no [chlorophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll) and therefore cannot make food for themselves by [photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis), they are always obligate parasites, deriving all their food from their hosts.[^cite_note-Heide-Jørgensen,_H._2008-71] Some parasitic plants can locate their [host](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_\(biology\)) plants by detecting [chemicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_substance) in the air or soil given off by host [shoots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoot_\(botany\)) or [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root), respectively. About 4,500 [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species) of parasitic plant in approximately 20 [families](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_\(taxonomy\)) of [flowering plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowering_plant) are known.[^cite_note-Heide-Jørgensen,_H._2008-71][^cite_note-NickrentMusselman2004-73]

Species within the *[Orobanchaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orobanchaceae)* (broomrapes) are among the most economically destructive of all plants. Species of *[Striga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Striga)* (witchweeds) are estimated to cost billions of dollars a year in crop yield loss, infesting over 50 million hectares of cultivated land within Sub-Saharan Africa alone. *Striga* infects both grasses and grains, including [corn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zea_mays), [rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oryza_sativa), and [sorghum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghum), which are among the world's most important food crops. *[Orobanche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orobanche)* also threatens a wide range of other important crops, including [peas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peas), [chickpeas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicer_arietinum), [tomatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solanum_lycopersicum), [carrots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrot), and varieties of [cabbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabbage). Yield loss from *Orobanche* can be total; despite extensive research, no method of control has been entirely successful.[^cite_note-WestwoodYoder2010-74]

Many [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plants) and [fungi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungi) exchange carbon and nutrients in mutualistic [mycorrhizal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycorrhizal) relationships. Some 400 species of [myco-heterotrophic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myco-heterotrophy) plants, mostly in the tropics, however effectively [cheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheating_\(biology\)) by taking carbon from a fungus rather than exchanging it for minerals. They have much reduced roots, as they do not need to absorb water from the soil; their stems are slender with few [vascular bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vascular_bundle), and their leaves are reduced to small scales, as they do not photosynthesize. Their seeds are small and numerous, so they appear to rely on being infected by a suitable fungus soon after germinating.[^cite_note-75]



The honey fungus, *[Armillaria mellea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armillaria_mellea)*, is a parasite of trees, and a [saprophyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saprophyte) feeding on the trees it has killed.

### Fungi

Further information: [Pathogenic fungus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathogenic_fungus) and [Plant pathology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_pathology)

Parasitic [fungi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungi) derive some or all of their nutritional requirements from plants, other fungi, or animals.

Plant pathogenic fungi are classified into three categories depending on their mode of nutrition: biotrophs, [hemibiotrophs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemibiotrophs) and necrotrophs. Biotrophic fungi derive nutrients from living plant cells, and during the course of infection they colonise their plant host in such a way as to keep it alive for a maximally long time.[^cite_note-76] One well-known example of a biotrophic pathogen is [*Ustilago maydis*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_smut), causative agent of the corn smut disease. Necrotrophic pathogens on the other hand, kill host cells and feed [saprophytically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saprophytic), an example being the root-colonising honey fungi in the genus *[Armillaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armillaria).*[^cite_note-RHS-77] Hemibiotrophic pathogens begin their colonising their hosts as biotrophs, and subsequently killing off host cells and feeding as necrotrophs, a phenomenon termed the [biotrophy-necrotrophy switch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biotrophy-necrotrophy_switch?action=edit\&redlink=1).[^cite_note-78]

Pathogenic fungi are well-known causative agents of diseases on animals as well as humans. Fungal infections ([mycosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungal_infection)) are estimated to kill 1.6 million people each year.[^cite_note-79] One example of a potent fungal animal pathogen are [Microsporidia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsporidia) - obligate [intracellular parasitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intracellular_parasite) fungi that largely affect insects, but may also affect vertebrates including humans, causing the intestinal infection [microsporidiosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsporidiosis).[^cite_note-Didier-80]



*[Borrelia burgdorferi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borrelia_burgdorferi)*, the bacterium that causes [Lyme disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyme_disease), is transmitted by *[Ixodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ixodes)* ticks.

### Protozoa

Further information: [Human parasite § Protozoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_parasite#Protozoa)

Protozoa such as *[Plasmodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium)*, *[Trypanosoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trypanosoma)*, and *[Entamoeba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entamoeba)*[^cite_note-Esch2013-81] are endoparasitic. They cause serious diseases in vertebrates including humans—in these examples, malaria, sleeping sickness, and [amoebic dysentery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amoebiasis)—and have complex life cycles.[^cite_note-PollittMacGregor2011-34]

### Bacteria

Main article: [Pathogenic bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathogenic_bacteria)

Many bacteria are parasitic, though they are more generally thought of as [pathogens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathogenic_bacteria) causing disease.[^cite_note-McFall-Ngai2007-82] Parasitic bacteria are extremely diverse, and infect their hosts by a variety of routes. To give a few examples, *[Bacillus anthracis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacillus_anthracis)*, the cause of [anthrax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthrax), is spread by contact with infected [domestic animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestic_animal); its [spores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spore), which can survive for years outside the body, can enter a host through an abrasion or may be inhaled. *[Borrelia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borrelia)*, the cause of [Lyme disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyme_disease) and [relapsing fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relapsing_fever), is transmitted by vectors, ticks of the genus *[Ixodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ixodes)*, from the diseases' reservoirs in animals such as [deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deer). *[Campylobacter jejuni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campylobacter_jejuni)*, a cause of [gastroenteritis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastroenteritis), is spread by the fecal–oral route from animals, or by eating insufficiently cooked [poultry](23197.md), or by contaminated water. *[Haemophilus influenzae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haemophilus_influenzae)*, an agent of [bacterial meningitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacterial_meningitis) and respiratory tract infections such as [influenza](19572217.md) and [bronchitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronchitis), is transmitted by droplet contact. *[Treponema pallidum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treponema_pallidum)*, the cause of [syphilis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syphilis), is [spread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexually_transmitted_disease) by [sexual activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_activity).[^cite_note-Lippincott-83]



[Enterobacteria phage T4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enterobacteria_phage_T4) is a [bacteriophage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriophage) virus. It infects its host, *[Escherichia coli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escherichia_coli)*, by injecting its DNA through its tail, which attaches to the bacterium's surface.

### Viruses

Main articles: [Virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus) and [Bacteriophage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriophage)

[Viruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus) are obligate [intracellular parasites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intracellular_parasite), characterised by extremely limited biological function, to the point where, while they are evidently able to infect all other organisms from bacteria and [archaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaea) to animals, plants and fungi, it is unclear whether they can themselves be described as living. They can be either [RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthornavirae) or [DNA viruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_virus) consisting of a single or double strand of [genetic material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_material) ([RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA) or [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA), respectively), covered in a [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein) coat and sometimes a [lipid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipid) envelope. They thus lack all the usual machinery of the [cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_\(biology\)) such as [enzymes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme), relying entirely on the host cell's ability to replicate DNA and synthesise proteins. Most viruses are [bacteriophages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriophage), infecting bacteria.[^cite_note-Koonin2006-84][^cite_note-Breitbart_M,_Rohwer_F_2005_278–84-85][^cite_note-Lawrence-86][^cite_note-87]

## Evolutionary ecology



Restoration of a *[Tyrannosaurus](30467.md)* with holes possibly caused by a *[Trichomonas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichomonas)*-like parasite

Further information: [Evolutionary ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_ecology)

Parasitism is a major aspect of evolutionary ecology; for example, almost all free-living animals are host to at least one species of parasite. Vertebrates, the best-studied group, are hosts to between 75,000 and 300,000 species of helminths and an uncounted number of parasitic microorganisms. On average, a mammal species hosts four species of nematode, two of trematodes, and two of cestodes.[^cite_note-Dobson2008-88] Humans have 342 species of helminth parasites, and 70 species of protozoan parasites.[^cite_note-Sukhdeo2012-89] Some three-quarters of the links in [food webs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_web) include a parasite, important in regulating host numbers. Perhaps 40 per cent of described species are parasitic.[^cite_note-Dobson2008-88]

### Fossil record

Further information: [Paleoparasitology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoparasitology)

Parasitism is hard to demonstrate from the [fossil record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_record), but holes in the [mandibles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandible) of several specimens of *[Tyrannosaurus](30467.md)* may have been caused by *[Trichomonas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichomonas)*-like parasites.[^cite_note-90] *[Saurophthirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saurophthirus)*, the Early Cretaceous [flea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flea), parasitized [pterosaurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pterosauria).[^cite_note-91][^cite_note-92] Eggs that belonged to [nematode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nematode) worms and probably [protozoan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protozoa) [cysts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyst) were found in the Late Triassic [coprolite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprolite) of [phytosaur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phytosaur). This rare find in Thailand reveals more about the ecology of prehistoric parasites.[^cite_note-93]

### Coevolution

Further information: [Host–parasite coevolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host%E2%80%93parasite_coevolution)

As hosts and parasites evolve together, their relationships often change. When a parasite is in a sole relationship with a host, selection drives the relationship to become more benign, even mutualistic, as the parasite can reproduce for longer if its host lives longer.[^cite_note-Rook2007-94] But where parasites are competing, selection favours the parasite that reproduces fastest, leading to increased virulence. There are thus varied possibilities in [host–parasite coevolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host%E2%80%93parasite_coevolution).[^cite_note-MasseyBuckling2004-95]

[Evolutionary epidemiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_epidemiology) analyses how parasites spread and evolve, whereas [Darwinian medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darwinian_medicine) applies similar evolutionary thinking to non-parasitic diseases like [cancer](105219.md) and [autoimmune conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoimmune_disease).[^cite_note-Ewald1994-96]

#### Long-term partnerships favouring mutualism



*[Wolbachia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolbachia)* bacteria within an insect cell

Long-term partnerships can lead to a relatively stable relationship tending to [commensalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commensalism) or [mutualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutualism_\(biology\)), as, all else being equal, it is in the evolutionary interest of the parasite that its host thrives. A parasite may evolve to become less harmful for its host or a host may evolve to cope with the unavoidable presence of a parasite—to the point that the parasite's absence causes the host harm. For example, although animals parasitised by [worms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helminth) are often clearly harmed, such infections may also reduce the prevalence and effects of [autoimmune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoimmunity) disorders in animal hosts, including humans.[^cite_note-Rook2007-94] In a more extreme example, some [nematode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nematode) worms cannot reproduce, or even survive, without infection by *[Wolbachia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolbachia)* bacteria.[^cite_note-97]

[Lynn Margulis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynn_Margulis) and others have argued, following [Peter Kropotkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Kropotkin)'s 1902 *[Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_Aid:_A_Factor_of_Evolution)*, that natural selection drives relationships from parasitism to mutualism when resources are limited. This process may have been involved in the [symbiogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbiogenesis) which formed the [eukaryotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eukaryote) from an intracellular relationship between [archaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaea) and bacteria, though the sequence of events remains largely undefined.[^cite_note-98][^cite_note-99]

#### Competition favouring virulence

Competition between parasites can be expected to favour faster reproducing and therefore more [virulent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virulence) parasites, by [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection).[^cite_note-MasseyBuckling2004-95][^cite_note-Rigaud2010-100]



Biologists long suspected [cospeciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cospeciation) of [flamingos](165927.md) and [ducks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duck) with their parasitic [lice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louse), which were similar in the two families. Cospeciation did occur, but it led to flamingos and [grebes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grebe), with a later [host switch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_switch) of flamingo lice to ducks.

Among competing parasitic insect-killing bacteria of the genera *[Photorhabdus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photorhabdus)* and *[Xenorhabdus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenorhabdus)*, virulence depended on the relative potency of the antimicrobial [toxins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxin) ([bacteriocins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriocins)) produced by the two strains involved. When only one bacterium could kill the other, the other strain was excluded by the competition. But when [caterpillars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caterpillar) were infected with bacteria both of which had toxins able to kill the other strain, neither strain was excluded, and their virulence was less than when the insect was infected by a single strain.[^cite_note-MasseyBuckling2004-95]

#### Cospeciation

A parasite sometimes undergoes [cospeciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cospeciation) with its host, resulting in the pattern described in [Fahrenholz's rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fahrenholz's_rule), that the phylogenies of the host and parasite come to mirror each other.[^cite_note-Page-101]

An example is between the [simian foamy virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simian_foamy_virus) (SFV) and its primate hosts. The phylogenies of SFV polymerase and the mitochondrial [cytochrome c oxidase subunit II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cytochrome_c_oxidase_subunit_II) from African and Asian primates were found to be closely congruent in branching order and divergence times, implying that the simian foamy viruses cospeciated with Old World primates for at least 30 million years.[^cite_note-SwitzerSalemi2005-102]

The presumption of a shared evolutionary history between parasites and hosts can help elucidate how host taxa are related. For instance, there has been a dispute about whether [flamingos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicopteriformes) are more closely related to [storks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciconiiformes) or [ducks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anseriformes). The fact that flamingos share parasites with ducks and geese was initially taken as evidence that these groups were more closely related to each other than either is to storks. However, evolutionary events such as the duplication, or the extinction of parasite species (without similar events on the host phylogeny) often erode similarities between host and parasite phylogenies. In the case of flamingos, they have similar lice to those of [grebes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grebe). Flamingos and grebes do have a common ancestor, implying cospeciation of birds and lice in these groups. Flamingo lice then [switched hosts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_switch) to ducks, creating the situation which had confused biologists.[^cite_note-JohnsonKennedy2006-103]



The protozoan *[Toxoplasma gondii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxoplasma_gondii)* facilitates its transmission by [inducing behavioral changes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavior-altering_parasites) in rats through infection of neurons in their [central nervous system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_nervous_system).

Parasites infect [sympatric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sympatry) hosts (those within their same geographical area) more effectively, as has been shown with [digenetic trematodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digenea) infecting lake snails.[^cite_note-Lively00-104] This is in line with the [Red Queen hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Queen_hypothesis), which states that interactions between species lead to constant natural selection for coadaptation. Parasites track the locally common hosts' phenotypes, so the parasites are less infective to [allopatric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allopatric_speciation) hosts, those from different geographical regions.[^cite_note-Lively00-104]

### Modifying host behaviour

Further information: [Behavior-altering parasites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavior-altering_parasites)

Some parasites [modify host behaviour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavior-altering_parasites) in order to increase their transmission between hosts, often in relation to predator and prey ([parasite increased trophic transmission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasite_increased_trophic_transmission)). For example, in the [California coastal salt marsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_coastal_salt_marsh), the fluke *[Euhaplorchis californiensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euhaplorchis_californiensis)* reduces the ability of its [killifish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killifish) host to avoid predators.[^cite_note-105] This parasite matures in [egrets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egret), which are more likely to feed on infected killifish than on uninfected fish. Another example is the protozoan *[Toxoplasma gondii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxoplasma_gondii)*, a parasite that matures in [cats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felis_silvestris_catus) but can be carried by many other [mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammalia). Uninfected [rats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rattus_rattus) avoid cat odors, but rats infected with *T. gondii* are drawn to this scent, which may increase transmission to feline hosts.[^cite_note-106] The malaria parasite modifies the skin odour of its human hosts, increasing their attractiveness to mosquitoes and hence improving the chance for the parasite to be transmitted.[^cite_note-de_Boer2017-39] The spider *[Cyclosa argenteoalba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclosa_argenteoalba)* often have parasitoid wasp larvae attached to them which alter their web-building behavior. Instead of producing their normal sticky spiral shaped webs, they made simplified webs when the parasites were attached. This manipulated behavior lasted longer and was more prominent the longer the parasites were left on the spiders.[^cite_note-107]



Trait loss: bedbug *[Cimex lectularius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimex_lectularius)* is flightless, like many insect ectoparasites.

### Trait loss

Parasites can exploit their hosts to carry out a number of functions that they would otherwise have to carry out for themselves. Parasites which lose those functions then have a selective advantage, as they can divert resources to reproduction. Many insect ectoparasites including [bedbugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimex), [batbugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyctenidae), [lice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lice) and [fleas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flea) have lost their [ability to fly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insect_flight), relying instead on their hosts for transport.[^cite_note-108] Trait loss more generally is widespread among parasites.[^cite_note-109] An extreme example is the [myxosporean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myxosporea) *[Henneguya zschokkei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henneguya_zschokkei)*, an ectoparasite of fish and the only animal known to have lost the ability to respire aerobically: its cells lack [mitochondria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondria).[^cite_note-PNAS-110]

### Host defences

Hosts have evolved a variety of defensive measures against their parasites, including physical barriers like the skin of vertebrates,[^cite_note-Colorado-111] the immune system of mammals,[^cite_note-Maizels2009-112] insects actively removing parasites,[^cite_note-Jeanne1979-113] and defensive chemicals in plants.[^cite_note-Runyon2010-114]

The evolutionary biologist [W. D. Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._D._Hamilton) suggested that [sexual reproduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_reproduction) could have evolved to help to defeat multiple parasites by enabling [genetic recombination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_recombination), the shuffling of genes to create varied combinations. Hamilton showed by mathematical modelling that sexual reproduction would be evolutionarily stable in different situations, and that the theory's predictions matched the actual ecology of sexual reproduction.[^cite_note-Hamilton1990-115][^cite_note-EbertHamilton1996-116] However, there may be a trade-off between [immunocompetence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunocompetence) and breeding male vertebrate hosts' [secondary sex characteristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_sex_characteristic), such as the plumage of [peacocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peacock) and the manes of [lions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lion). This is because the male hormone [testosterone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testosterone) encourages the growth of secondary sex characteristics, favouring such males in [sexual selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_selection), at the price of reducing their immune defences.[^cite_note-FolstadKarter1992-117]

#### Vertebrates



The dry [skin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin) of vertebrates such as the [short-horned lizard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short-horned_lizard) prevents the entry of many parasites.

The physical barrier of the tough and often dry and waterproof [skin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin) of reptiles, birds and mammals keeps invading microorganisms from entering the body. [Human skin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_skin) also secretes [sebum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebum), which is toxic to most microorganisms.[^cite_note-Colorado-111] On the other hand, larger parasites such as [trematodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trematode) detect chemicals produced by the skin to locate their hosts when they enter the water. Vertebrate [saliva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saliva) and tears contain [lysozyme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysozyme), an enzyme that breaks down the [cell walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacterial_cell_structure#Cell_wall) of invading bacteria.[^cite_note-Colorado-111] Should the organism pass the mouth, the [stomach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stomach) with its [hydrochloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid), toxic to most microorganisms, is the next line of defence.[^cite_note-Colorado-111] Some intestinal parasites have a thick, tough outer coating which is digested slowly or not at all, allowing the parasite to pass through the stomach alive, at which point they enter the intestine and begin the next stage of their life. Once inside the body, parasites must overcome the [immune system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immune_system)'s [serum proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serum_proteins) and [pattern recognition receptors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pattern_recognition_receptor), intracellular and cellular, that trigger the adaptive immune system's [lymphocytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lymphocyte) such as [T cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T_cell) and antibody-producing [B cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B_cell). These have receptors that recognise parasites.[^cite_note-Maizels2009-112]

#### Insects



[Leaf spot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf_spot) on [oak](39696.md). The spread of the parasitic fungus is limited by defensive chemicals produced by the tree, resulting in circular patches of damaged tissue.

Insects often adapt their nests to reduce parasitism. For example, one of the key reasons why the wasp *[Polistes canadensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polistes_canadensis)* nests across multiple [combs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeycomb), rather than building a single comb like much of the rest of its genus, is to avoid infestation by [tineid moths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tineid_moth). The tineid moth lays its eggs within the wasps' nests and then these eggs hatch into larvae that can burrow from cell to cell and prey on wasp pupae. Adult wasps attempt to remove and kill moth eggs and larvae by chewing down the edges of cells, coating the cells with an oral secretion that gives the nest a dark brownish appearance.[^cite_note-Jeanne1979-113]

#### Plants

Plants respond to parasite attack with a series of chemical defences, such as [polyphenol oxidase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyphenol_oxidase), under the control of the [jasmonic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jasmonic_acid)-insensitive (JA) and [salicylic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salicylic_acid) (SA) signalling pathways.[^cite_note-Runyon2010-114][^cite_note-Thaler2002-118] The different biochemical pathways are activated by different attacks, and the two pathways can interact positively or negatively. In general, plants can either initiate a specific or a non-specific response.[^cite_note-Thaler2002-118][^cite_note-Frank_2000-119] Specific responses involve recognition of a parasite by the plant's cellular receptors, leading to a strong but localised response: defensive chemicals are produced around the area where the parasite was detected, blocking its spread, and avoiding wasting defensive production where it is not needed.[^cite_note-Frank_2000-119] Non-specific defensive responses are systemic, meaning that the responses are not confined to an area of the plant, but spread throughout the plant, making them costly in energy. These are effective against a wide range of parasites.[^cite_note-Frank_2000-119] When damaged, such as by [lepidopteran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepidoptera) [caterpillars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caterpillar), leaves of plants including [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize) and [cotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton) release increased amounts of volatile chemicals such as [terpenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terpene) that signal they are being attacked; one effect of this is to attract parasitoid wasps, which in turn attack the caterpillars.[^cite_note-Paré_Tumlinson_pp._325–332-120]&#x20;

## Biology and conservation

### Ecology and parasitology



The rescuing from extinction of the [California condor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_condor) was a successful and expensive project, but its ectoparasite, the louse *[Colpocephalum californici](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colpocephalum_californici)*, was made extinct.

Parasitism and parasite evolution were until the twenty-first century studied by [parasitologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitologist), in a science dominated by medicine, rather than by [ecologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecologist) or [evolutionary biologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_biologists). Even though parasite-host interactions were plainly ecological and important in evolution, the history of parasitology caused what the evolutionary ecologist Robert Poulin called a "takeover of parasitism by parasitologists", leading ecologists to ignore the area. This was in his opinion "unfortunate", as parasites are "omnipresent agents of natural selection" and significant forces in evolution and ecology.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPoulin2007x,_1–2-121] In his view, the long-standing split between the sciences limited the exchange of ideas, with separate conferences and separate journals. The technical languages of ecology and parasitology sometimes involved different meanings for the same words. There were philosophical differences, too: Poulin notes that, influenced by medicine, "many parasitologists accepted that evolution led to a decrease in parasite virulence, whereas modern evolutionary theory would have predicted a greater range of outcomes".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPoulin2007x,_1–2-121]

Their complex relationships make parasites difficult to place in food webs: a trematode with multiple hosts for its various life cycle stages would occupy many positions in a [food web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_web) simultaneously, and would set up loops of energy flow, confusing the analysis. Further, since nearly every animal has (multiple) parasites, parasites would occupy the top levels of every food web.[^cite_note-Sukhdeo2012-89]

Parasites can play a role in the proliferation of non-native species. For example, invasive [green crabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcinus_maenas) are minimally affected by native [trematodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trematoda) on the Eastern Atlantic coast. This helps them outcompete native crabs such as the [Atlantic Rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancer_irroratus) and [Jonah crabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonah_crab).[^cite_note-122]

Ecological parasitology can be important to attempts at control, like during the [campaign for eradicating the Guinea worm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eradication_of_dracunculiasis). Even though the parasite was eradicated in all but four countries, the worm began using frogs as an intermediary host before infecting dogs, making control more difficult than it would have been if the relationships had been better understood.[^cite_note-123]

### Rationale for conservation

Further information: [Conservation biology of parasites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_biology_of_parasites)

| External videos |
| --- |
| video icon ["Why you should care about parasites"](https://knowablemagazine.org/article/living-world/2018/why-you-should-care-about-parasites), 14 December 2018, *[Knowable Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowable_Magazine)* |

Although parasites are widely considered to be harmful, the eradication of all parasites would not be beneficial. Parasites account for at least half of life's diversity; they perform important ecological roles; and without parasites, organisms might tend to asexual reproduction, diminishing the diversity of traits brought about by sexual reproduction.[^cite_note-Holt2010-124] Parasites provide an opportunity for the transfer of genetic material between species, facilitating evolutionary change.[^cite_note-Combes-125] Many parasites require multiple hosts of different species to complete their life cycles and rely on predator-prey or other stable ecological interactions to get from one host to another. The presence of parasites thus indicates that an ecosystem is healthy.[^cite_note-HudsonDobson2006-126]

An ectoparasite, the California condor louse, *[Colpocephalum californici](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colpocephalum_californici)*, became a well-known conservation issue. A large and costly captive breeding program was run in the United States to rescue the [California condor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_condor). It was host to a louse, which lived only on it. Any lice found were "deliberately killed" during the program, to keep the condors in the best possible health. The result was that one species, the condor, was saved and returned to the wild, while another species, the parasite, became extinct.[^cite_note-Stringer2014-127]

Although parasites are often omitted in depictions of [food webs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_web), they usually occupy the top position. Parasites can function like [keystone species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keystone_species), reducing the dominance of superior competitors and allowing [competing species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Competition_\(biology\)) to co-exist.[^cite_note-Sukhdeo2012-89][^cite_note-LaffertyAllesina2008-128][^cite_note-Chase2013-129]



Parasites are [distributed unevenly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aggregated_distribution) among their hosts, most hosts having no parasites, and a few hosts harbouring most of the parasite population. This distribution makes sampling difficult and requires careful use of statistics.

### Quantitative ecology

Further information: [Aggregated distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aggregated_distribution)

A single parasite species usually has an aggregated distribution across host animals, which means that most hosts carry few parasites, while a few hosts carry the vast majority of parasite individuals. This poses considerable problems for students of parasite ecology, as it renders [parametric statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_statistics) as commonly used by biologists invalid. [Log-transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_transformation_\(statistics\)) of data before the application of parametric test, or the use of [non-parametric statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-parametric_statistics) is recommended by several authors, but this can give rise to further problems, so quantitative parasitology is based on more advanced biostatistical methods.[^cite_note-pmid10780537-130]

## History

### Ancient

Further information: [Human parasite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_parasite)

[Human parasites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_parasite) including roundworms, the [Guinea worm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinea_worm), [threadworms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinworm_\(parasite\)) and tapeworms are mentioned in Egyptian papyrus records from 3000 BC onwards; the [Ebers Papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebers_Papyrus) describes [hookworm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hookworm). In [ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece), parasites including the [bladder worm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bladder_worm) are described in the [Hippocratic Corpus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocratic_Corpus), while the comic playwright [Aristophanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristophanes) called tapeworms "hailstones". The Roman physicians Celsus and [Galen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galen) documented the roundworms *[Ascaris lumbricoides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascaris_lumbricoides)* and *[Enterobius vermicularis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enterobius_vermicularis)*.[^cite_note-Cox2004-131]

### Medieval



A plate from [Francesco Redi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Redi)'s *Osservazioni intorno agli animali viventi che si trovano negli animali viventi* (Observations on living animals found inside living animals), 1684

In his *[Canon of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Canon_of_Medicine)*, completed in 1025, the Persian physician [Avicenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avicenna) recorded human and animal parasites including roundworms, threadworms, the Guinea worm and tapeworms.[^cite_note-Cox2004-131]

In his 1397 book *Traité de l'état, science et pratique de l'art de la Bergerie* (Account of the state, science and practice of the art of shepherding), [Jehan de Brie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jehan_de_Brie?action=edit\&redlink=1) wrote the first description of a trematode endoparasite, the sheep liver fluke *[Fasciola hepatica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasciola_hepatica)*.[^cite_note-ChengHistory-132][^cite_note-133]

### Early modern

In the [early modern period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_period), [Francesco Redi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Redi)'s 1668 book *Esperienze Intorno alla Generazione degl'Insetti* (*Experiences of the Generation of Insects*), explicitly described ecto- and endoparasites, illustrating [ticks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ticks), the larvae of [nasal flies of deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cephenemyiinae), and [sheep liver fluke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasciola_hepatica).[^cite_note-Ioli1997-134] Redi noted that parasites develop from eggs, contradicting the theory of [spontaneous generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_generation).[^cite_note-Bush2001-135] In his 1684 book *Osservazioni intorno agli animali viventi che si trovano negli animali viventi* (*Observations on Living Animals found in Living Animals*), Redi described and illustrated over 100 parasites including the [large roundworm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascaris_lumbricoides) in humans that causes [ascariasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascariasis).[^cite_note-Ioli1997-134] Redi was the first to name the cysts of *[Echinococcus granulosus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echinococcus_granulosus)* seen in dogs and sheep as parasitic; a century later, in 1760, [Peter Simon Pallas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Simon_Pallas) correctly suggested that these were the larvae of tapeworms.[^cite_note-Cox2004-131]

In 1681, [Antonie van Leeuwenhoek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek) observed and illustrated the protozoan parasite *[Giardia lamblia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giardia_lamblia)*, and linked it to "his own loose stools". This was the first protozoan parasite of humans to be seen under a microscope.[^cite_note-Cox2004-131] A few years later, in 1687, the Italian biologists [Giovanni Cosimo Bonomo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Cosimo_Bonomo) and [Diacinto Cestoni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diacinto_Cestoni) described [scabies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scabies) as caused by the parasitic mite *[Sarcoptes scabiei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarcoptes_scabiei)*, marking it as the first disease of humans with a known microscopic causative agent.[^cite_note-136]



[Ronald Ross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Ross) won the 1902 [Nobel Prize](21201.md) for showing that the [malaria parasite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria_parasite) is transmitted by mosquitoes. This 1897 notebook page records his first observations of the parasite in mosquitoes.

### Parasitology

Main article: [Parasitology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitology)

Modern [parasitology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitology) developed in the 19th century with accurate observations and experiments by many researchers and clinicians;[^cite_note-ChengHistory-132] the term was first used in 1870.[^cite_note-137] In 1828, James Annersley described [amoebiasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amoebiasis), protozoal infections of the intestines and the liver, though the pathogen, *[Entamoeba histolytica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entamoeba_histolytica)*, was not discovered until 1873 by Friedrich Lösch. [James Paget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Paget) discovered the intestinal nematode *[Trichinella spiralis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichinella_spiralis)* in humans in 1835. James McConnell described the human liver fluke, *[Clonorchis sinensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clonorchis_sinensis)*, in 1875.[^cite_note-Cox2004-131] [Algernon Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algernon_Thomas) and [Rudolf Leuckart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Leuckart) independently made the first discovery of the life cycle of a trematode, the sheep liver fluke, by experiment in 1881–1883.[^cite_note-ChengHistory-132] In 1877 [Patrick Manson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Manson) discovered the life cycle of the [filarial worms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filarioidea) that cause [elephantiasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lymphatic_filariasis) transmitted by mosquitoes. Manson further predicted that the [malaria](20423.md) parasite, *[Plasmodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium)*, had a mosquito vector, and persuaded [Ronald Ross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Ross) to investigate. Ross confirmed that the prediction was correct in 1897–1898. At the same time, [Giovanni Battista Grassi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Battista_Grassi) and others described the malaria parasite's life cycle stages in *[Anopheles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anopheles)* mosquitoes. Ross was [controversially awarded the 1902 Nobel prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_controversies#Physiology_or_medicine) for his work, while Grassi was not.[^cite_note-Cox2004-131] In 1903, [David Bruce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bruce_\(microbiologist\)) identified the protozoan parasite and the [tsetse fly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsetse_fly) vector of [African trypanosomiasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_trypanosomiasis).[^cite_note-138]

### Vaccine

Further information: [Malaria vaccine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria_vaccine)

Given the importance of malaria, with some 220 million people infected annually, many attempts have been made to interrupt its transmission. Various methods of [malaria prophylaxis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria_prophylaxis) have been tried including the use of [antimalarial drugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimalarial_drug) to kill off the parasites in the blood, the eradication of its mosquito vectors with [organochlorine and other insecticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insecticide), and the development of a [malaria vaccine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria_vaccine). All of these have proven problematic, with [drug resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_resistance), [insecticide resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insecticide_resistance) among mosquitoes, and repeated failure of vaccines as the parasite mutates.[^cite_note-139] The first and as of 2015 the only licensed vaccine for any parasitic disease of humans is [RTS,S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RTS,S) for *[Plasmodium falciparum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_falciparum)* malaria.[^cite_note-BBC2015-140]

### Biological control

Further information: [Biological control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_control)



*[Encarsia formosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encarsia_formosa)*, widely used in [greenhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse) horticulture, was one of the first [biological control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_control) agents developed.

[^cite_note-Hoddle_1998-141]

Several groups of parasites, including microbial pathogens and parasitoidal wasps have been used as [biological control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_control) agents in [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture) and [horticulture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horticulture).[^cite_note-142][^cite_note-143]

### Resistance

Further information: [Drug resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_resistance)

Poulin observes that the widespread [prophylactic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophylactic) use of [anthelmintic drugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthelmintic) in domestic sheep and cattle constitutes a worldwide un[controlled experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_experiment) in the life-history evolution of their parasites. The outcomes depend on whether the drugs decrease the chance of a helminth larva reaching adulthood. If so, natural selection can be expected to favour the production of eggs at an earlier age. If on the other hand the drugs mainly affects adult [parasitic worms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitic_worm), selection could cause delayed maturity and increased [virulence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virulence). Such changes appear to be underway: the nematode *[Teladorsagia circumcincta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teladorsagia_circumcincta)* is changing its adult size and [reproductive rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fecundity) in response to drugs.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPoulin2007265–266-144]

## Cultural significance



"An Old Parasite in a New Form": an 1881 *[Punch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punch_\(magazine\))* cartoon by [Edward Linley Sambourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Linley_Sambourne) compares a [crinoletta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crinoline) bustle to a parasitic insect's [exoskeleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exoskeleton)

### Classical times

In the [classical era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_era), the concept of the parasite was not strictly pejorative: the *parasitus* was an [accepted role in Roman society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patronage_in_ancient_Rome), in which a person could live off the hospitality of others, in return for "flattery, simple services, and a willingness to endure humiliation".[^cite_note-145][^cite_note-146]

### Society

Further information: [Parasitism (social offense)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitism_\(social_offense\))

Parasitism has [a derogatory sense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitism_\(social_offense\)) in popular usage. According to the immunologist John Playfair,[^cite_note-Playfair2007-147]

> In everyday speech, the term 'parasite' is loaded with derogatory meaning. A parasite is a sponger, a lazy profiteer, a drain on society.[^cite_note-Playfair2007-147]

The [satirical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satirical) cleric [Jonathan Swift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Swift) alludes to hyperparasitism in his 1733 poem "On Poetry: A Rhapsody", comparing poets to "vermin" who "teaze and pinch their foes":[^cite_note-148][^cite_note-Swift1733-149]

> The vermin only teaze and pinch\
> &#x20;Their foes superior by an inch.\
> &#x20;So nat'ralists observe, a flea\
> &#x20;Hath smaller fleas that on him prey;\
> &#x20;\
> &#x20;And these have smaller fleas to bite 'em.\
> &#x20;And so proceeds *[ad infinitum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_tag#Ad_infinitum)*.\
> &#x20;Thus every poet, in his kind,\
> &#x20;Is bit by him that comes behind:

A 2022 study examined the naming of some 3000 parasite species discovered in the previous two decades. Of those named after scientists, over 80% were named for men, whereas about a third of authors of papers on parasites were women. The study found that the percentage of parasite species named for relatives or friends of the author has risen sharply in the same period.[^cite_note-Poulin_McDougall_Presswell_2022-150]

### Fiction

Further information: [List of fictional parasites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fictional_parasites) and [Parasites in fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasites_in_fiction)



Fictional parasitism: oil painting *Parasites* by Katrin Alvarez, 2011

In [Bram Stoker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bram_Stoker)'s 1897 [Gothic horror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_fiction) novel *[Dracula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dracula)*, and [its many film adaptations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dracula_in_popular_culture), the eponymous [Count Dracula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count_Dracula) is a [blood-drinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematophagy) parasite (a vampire). The critic [Laura Otis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laura_Otis) argues that as a "thief, seducer, creator, and mimic, Dracula is the ultimate parasite. The whole point of vampirism is sucking other people's blood—living at other people's expense."[^cite_note-151]

Disgusting and terrifying [parasitic alien species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasites_in_fiction) are widespread in [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction),[^cite_note-152][^cite_note-Dove2011-153] as for instance in [Ridley Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ridley_Scott)'s 1979 film *[Alien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alien_\(film\))*.[^cite_note-Pappas2012-154][^cite_note-Sercel2017-155] In one scene, a [Xenomorph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alien_\(creature_in_Alien_franchise\)) bursts out of the chest of a man, with blood squirting out under high pressure assisted by [explosive squibs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullet_hit_squib). [Animal organs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organ_\(anatomy\)) were used to reinforce the shock effect. The scene was filmed in a single take, and the startled reactions of the actors were genuine.[^cite_note-Guardian2009-4][^cite_note-Nordine2017-156]

The [entomopathogenic fungus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entomopathogenic_fungus) *[Cordyceps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cordyceps)* is represented culturally as a deadly threat to the human race. The video game series *[The Last of Us](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_of_Us)* (2013–present) and its [television adaptation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_of_Us_\(TV_series\)) present *Cordyceps* as a parasite of humans, causing a [zombie apocalypse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zombie_apocalypse).[^cite_note-Hill_2013_SciAm-157] Its human hosts initially become violent "infected" beings, before turning into blind zombie "clickers", complete with fruiting bodies growing out from their faces.[^cite_note-Hill_2013_SciAm-157]
