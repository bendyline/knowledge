**Bromobenzyl cyanide** (**BBC**), also known in the military idiom as **camite**, is an obsolete [lachrymatory agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lachrymatory_agent)[^cite_note-2] introduced in [World War I](4764461.md) by the [Allied Powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_I), being a standard agent, along with [chloroacetophenone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenacyl_chloride), adopted by the [Chemical Warfare Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_Warfare_Service).[^cite_note-3] It was thought to have been phased out in the 1930s, but has allegedly seen use in 2024, when authorities in the Republic of [Georgia](48768.md) are said to have used it to quell anti-government protests. [^cite_note-HudsonBBC2025-4]

When implemented in World War I, it revolutionized the use of tear agents due to its extreme potency and great persistence.[^cite_note-5] Bromobenzyl cyanide has been described as too toxic for use as a non-lethal crowd control agent.[^cite_note-6]

## Use against protestors in 2024

Main article: [Use of camite during the 2024–2026 Georgian protests](81732552.md)

Despite it being described as obsolete, [Georgian](48768.md) authorities appeared to have used camite in November 2024 against civil protestors in [Tbilisi](42298.md), adding it to the water in a water cannon aimed at the people, according to a *[BBC News](1139893.md)* investigation.[^cite_note-HudsonBBC2025-4]

## Applications

An application for bromobenzyl cyanide is in Hoch's synthesis of [diphenylacetonitrile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diphenylacetonitrile).[^cite_note-7]
