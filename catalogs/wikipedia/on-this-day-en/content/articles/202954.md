The **Tōhoku region** (東北地方, *Tōhoku-chihō*; IPA: [\[toːhokɯ̥ tɕiꜜhoː\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Japanese)), **Northeast region**, **Ōu region** (奥羽地方, *Ōu-chihō*), or **Northeast Japan** (東北日本, *Tōhoku Nihon*) consists of the northeastern portion of [Honshu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honshu), the largest [island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_archipelago) of [Japan](15573.md). It consists of six [prefectures](74517.md) (*ken*): [Akita](61729.md), [Aomori](61732.md), [Fukushima](211914.md), [Iwate](183477.md), [Miyagi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miyagi_Prefecture), and [Yamagata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamagata_Prefecture).[^cite_note-3]

Tōhoku retains a reputation as a remote, scenic region with a harsh [climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate). In the 20th century, [tourism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism) became a major industry in the Tōhoku region.

## History

Main articles: [Mutsu Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutsu_Province) and [Dewa Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dewa_Province)

### Ancient and classical period

See also: [Ethnic groups of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_groups_of_Japan)



[Mōtsū-ji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C5%8Dts%C5%AB-ji) portrait of three generations of the [Northern Fujiwara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Fujiwara). The top is [Fujiwara no Kiyohira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujiwara_no_Kiyohira), the right is [Fujiwara no Motohira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujiwara_no_Motohira), the left figure is [Fujiwara no Hidehira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujiwara_no_Hidehira).

In mythological times, this area, along with [Kantō region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kant%C5%8D_region), was called Azuma (吾妻, あづま) and was the region of [Honshu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honshu) occupied by the [indigenous peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples) known as the [Emishi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emishi), and, later, the [Ainu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ainu_people). The area was historically the [Dewa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dewa_Province) and the [Mutsu or Michinoku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutsu_Province) regions,[^cite_note-4] a term first recorded in the *Hitachi no kuni [Fudoki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fudoki)* (常陸国風土記) (654). There is some variation in modern usage of the term "Michinoku".[^cite_note-5] The combination of the [kanji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanji) used to write Dewa and Mutsu/Michi was read as Ōu (奥羽), and sometimes is used in place of the name Tōhoku in texts.

[Yamato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamato_people) (ethnic Japanese) settlement in the Tōhoku occurred between the seventh and ninth centuries, well after the [Yamato culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Japan) had become firmly established in central and southwestern Japan. As the last stronghold of the Emishi, the [indigenous peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples) of Honshu, and the site of [many battles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sengoku_period), the region has at various times throughout history maintained a degree of autonomy from Kyoto.

The [Northern Fujiwara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Fujiwara) (奥州藤原氏, *Ōshū Fujiwara-shi*) were a [kuge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuge) ([Yamato noble clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_clans) of the Heian) that ruled the Tōhoku in the 12th century as their own realm. They were independent from the [Imperial Court in Kyoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Court_in_Kyoto) by the strength of their [warrior bands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samurai). This ended when [Minamoto no Yoritomo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minamoto_no_Yoritomo), head of the [Minamoto clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minamoto_clan) and founder of the [Kamakura shogunate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamakura_shogunate), defeated them in 1189 a few years after the [Genpei War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genpei_War).[^cite_note-aaa-6] [Fujiwara no Kiyohira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujiwara_no_Kiyohira) (藤原清衡), who was of mixed Yamato and Emishi parentage, was its first ruler. He ruled from what is now [Hiraizumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiraizumi) in [Iwate Prefecture](183477.md) beginning about 1100. Initially, he used the clan name [Kiyohara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiyohara_clan), but later adopted his father's clan name. He was married to a wide variety of wives, including two Emishi women and a [Taira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taira_clan) noblewoman, to establish strong alliances for his stake.

### Feudal period

#### Christianity in Tōhoku



[Cast iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cast_iron) [teapots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teapot) like this one sit atop [stoves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stove) during the long [winters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter) in Tōhoku.

[Date Masamune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Date_Masamune) (1567–1636), feudal lord of [Date clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Date_clan), expanded trade in the Tōhoku region.[^cite_note-\:0-7][^cite_note-\:1-8] Although initially faced with attacks by hostile clans, he managed to overcome them after a few defeats and eventually ruled [one of the largest fiefdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sendai_Domain) of the later [Tokugawa shogunate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_shogunate). He built many palaces and worked on many projects to beautify the region.[^cite_note-\:0-7] He is also known to have encouraged foreigners to come to his land.[^cite_note-\:1-8]

Even though he funded and promoted an envoy to establish relations with the Pope in Rome, he was likely motivated at least in part by a desire for foreign technology, similar to that of other lords, such as [Oda Nobunaga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oda_Nobunaga).[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-\:2-10] He showed sympathy for Christian missionaries and traders in Japan. In addition to allowing them to come and preach in his province, he released the prisoner and missionary [Padre Sotelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Sotelo) from the hands of Tokugawa Ieyasu. Date Masamune allowed Sotelo as well as other missionaries to practice their religion and win converts in Tōhoku.[^cite_note-\:2-10]

Once [Tokugawa Ieyasu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_Ieyasu) (1543–1616) outlawed Christianity, Masamune reversed his position, and though disliking it, let Ieyasu persecute Christians in his domain.[^cite_note-\:2-10] For 270 years, Tōhoku remained a place of tourism, trade and prosperity. [Matsushima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matsushima), for instance, a series of tiny islands, was praised for its beauty and serenity by the wandering [haiku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haiku) poet [Matsuo Bashō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matsuo_Bash%C5%8D).[^cite_note-\:0-7][^cite_note-\:1-8]

The [haiku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haiku) [poet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poet) [Matsuo Bashō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matsuo_Bash%C5%8D) (1644–1694) wrote *[Oku no Hosomichi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oku_no_Hosomichi)* (*The Narrow Road to the Deep North*) during his travels through Tōhoku.

### Contemporary period

In the 1960s, [ironworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironworks), [steelmaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steelmaking), [cement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cement), [chemical industry](58721.md), [pulp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulp_and_paper_industry), and [petroleum refining](195137.md) industries began developing. The region is traditionally known as a less developed area of Japan.[^cite_note-11]

The catastrophic [9.0-magnitude earthquake and tsunami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_T%C5%8Dhoku_earthquake_and_tsunami) on March 11, 2011, inflicted massive damage along the east coast of this region, causing 19,759 deaths,[^cite_note-12] and was the costliest natural disaster ever which left 500,000 people homeless along with a radiation [accident](31162817.md) at the [Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant](11986472.md).

## Geography



The Tōhoku region and Hokkaido seen from space



A geofeatures map of Tōhoku

Tōhoku, like most of Japan, is hilly or mountainous, with the [Ōu Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Cu_Mountains) running north–south. The inland location of many of the region's lowlands has led to a concentration of much of the population there. Coupled with coastlines that do not favor seaport development, this settlement pattern resulted in a much greater than usual dependence on land and rail transportation. Low points in the central mountain range make communications between lowlands on either side of the range moderately easy.

Tōhoku was traditionally considered the [granary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granary) of Japan because it supplied [Sendai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sendai) and the [Tokyo](30057.md)-[Yokohama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yokohama) market with rice and other farming commodities. Tōhoku provided 20 percent of the nation's rice crop.

### Subdivision

The most often used subdivision of the region is dividing it to "North Tōhoku" (北東北, *Kita-Tōhoku*) consisting of Aomori, Akita, and Iwate Prefectures and "South Tōhoku" (南東北, *Minami-Tōhoku*) consisting of Yamagata, Miyagi, and Fukushima Prefectures.

## Climate

The climate is colder than in other parts of Honshū due to the stronger effect of the [Siberian High](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siberian_High), and permits only one crop a year on [paddy fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paddy_field). The [Pacific coast](23070.md) of Tōhoku, however, is generally much less snowy than the region's popular image and has among the smallest seasonal temperature variation in Japan. The city of Iwaki, for instance, has daily mean temperatures ranging from 3.0 °C (37.4 °F) in January to 23.9 °C (75.0 °F) in August.

The population decline of Tōhoku, which began before the year 2000, has accelerated, now including previously dynamic [Miyagi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miyagi_Prefecture). Despite this, [Sendai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sendai) City has grown, in part due to relocations of people affected by the 2011 disaster. The population decline of Aomori, Iwate and Akita Prefectures, Honshu's three northernmost, began in the early 1980s after an initial loss of population in the late 1950s. Fukushima Prefecture, prior to 1980, had traditionally been the most populated, but today Miyagi is the most populated and urban by far.
