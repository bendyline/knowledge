The **Doomsday Clock** is a symbol that represents the estimated likelihood of a human-made [global catastrophe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_catastrophic_risk), in the opinion of the [nonprofit organization](72487.md) *[Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists](100091.md)*.[^cite_note-1]

Maintained since 1947, the Clock is a proxy mechanism for threats to humanity from unchecked scientific and technological advances: A hypothetical global catastrophe is represented by midnight on the Clock, with the *Bulletin's* opinion on how close the world is to "zero" represented by a certain number of minutes or seconds to midnight. This is assessed in January of each year. The main factors influencing the Clock are [nuclear warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_warfare), [climate change](5042951.md), and [artificial intelligence](1164.md).[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3] The *Bulletin*'s Science and Security Board monitors new developments in the [life sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_sciences) and technology that could inflict irrevocable harm to humanity.[^cite_note-2007PressRelease-4]

The Clock's original setting in 1947 was seven minutes to midnight. It has since been set backward eight times and forward 19 times. The farthest time from midnight was 17 minutes in 1991, and the closest is 85 seconds in 2026.[^cite_note-Lukiv2025-5]

The Clock was moved to 150 seconds (2 minutes, 30 seconds) in 2017, then forward to two minutes to midnight in 2018, and left unchanged in 2019.[^cite_note-bulletin2019-6] It was moved forward to 100 seconds (1 minute, 40 seconds) in 2020,[^cite_note-james-7] 90 seconds (1 minute, 30 seconds) in 2023,[^cite_note-bulletin2023-8] 89 seconds (1 minute, 29 seconds) in 2025,[^cite_note-\:1-9] and 85 seconds (1 minute, 25 seconds) in 2026.[^cite_note-\:2-10]

## History



Cover of the 1947 *Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists* issue, featuring the Doomsday Clock at "seven minutes to midnight"

The Doomsday Clock's origin can be traced to the international group of researchers called the Chicago Atomic Scientists, who had participated in the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project).[^cite_note-The_Spokesman-Review-11] After the [atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bombings_of_Hiroshima_and_Nagasaki), they began publishing a [mimeographed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimeograph) newsletter and then the magazine, *Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists*, which, since its inception, has depicted the Clock on every cover. The Clock was first represented in 1947, when the *Bulletin* co-founder Hyman Goldsmith asked artist [Martyl Langsdorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martyl_Langsdorf) (wife of Manhattan Project research associate and [Szilárd petition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szil%C3%A1rd_petition) signatory [Alexander Langsdorf Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Langsdorf_Jr.)) to design a cover for the magazine's June 1947 issue. As [Eugene Rabinowitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Rabinowitch), another co-founder of the *Bulletin*, explained later:

> The *Bulletin*'s Clock is not a gauge to register the ups and downs of the international power struggle; it is intended to reflect basic changes in the level of continuous danger in which mankind lives in the nuclear age...[^cite_note-12]

&#x20;Langsdorf chose a clock to reflect the urgency of the problem: like a countdown, the Clock suggests that destruction will naturally occur unless someone takes action to stop it.[^cite_note-13]

In January 2007, designer [Michael Bierut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Bierut), who was on the *Bulletin*'s Governing Board, redesigned the Doomsday Clock to give it a more modern feel. In 2009, the *Bulletin* ceased its print edition and became one of the first print publications in the U.S. to become entirely digital; the Clock is now found as part of the logo on the *Bulletin*'s website. Information about the Doomsday Clock Symposium,[^cite_note-Doomsday_Clock_Symposium-14] a timeline of the Clock's settings,[^cite_note-\:0-15] and multimedia shows about the Clock's history and culture[^cite_note-16] can also be found on the *Bulletin*'s website.

The 5th Doomsday Clock Symposium[^cite_note-Doomsday_Clock_Symposium-14] was held on November 14, 2013, in [Washington, D.C.](108956.md); it was a day-long event that was open to the public and featured panelists discussing various issues on the topic "Communicating Catastrophe". There was also an evening event at the [Hirshhorn Museum and Sculpture Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hirshhorn_Museum_and_Sculpture_Garden) in conjunction with the Hirshhorn's current exhibit, "Damage Control: Art and Destruction Since 1950".[^cite_note-November_15,_2013-17] The panel discussions, held at the [American Association for the Advancement of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Science), were streamed live from the *Bulletin*'s website and can still be viewed there.[^cite_note-18] Reflecting international events dangerous to humankind, the Clock has been adjusted 27 times since its inception in 1947, when it was set to "seven minutes to midnight".[^cite_note-19]

The Doomsday Clock has become a universally recognized metaphor according to *The Two-Way*, an [NPR](29697232.md) blog.[^cite_note-20] According to the *Bulletin*, the Clock attracts more daily visitors to the *Bulletin*'s site than any other feature.[^cite_note-slate-21]

## Basis for settings

"Midnight" has a deeper meaning besides the constant threat of war. There are various elements taken into consideration when the scientists from the *Bulletin* decide what Midnight and "global catastrophe" really mean in a particular year. They might include "politics, energy, weapons, diplomacy, and climate science";[^cite_note-NPR.org-22] potential sources of threat include [nuclear threats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_threat), climate change, [bioterrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioterrorism), and artificial intelligence.[^cite_note-23] Members of the board judge Midnight by discussing how close they think humanity is to the end of civilization. In 1947, at the [beginning of the Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War_\(1947%E2%80%931953\)), the Clock was started at seven minutes to midnight.[^cite_note-\:0-15]

## Fluctuations and threats

Before January 2020, the two tied-for-lowest points for the Doomsday Clock were in 1953 (when the Clock was set to two minutes until midnight, after the U.S. and the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) began testing [hydrogen bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_bombs)) and in 2018, following the failure of world leaders to address tensions relating to nuclear weapons and climate change issues. In other years, the Clock's time has fluctuated from 17 minutes in 1991 to 2 minutes, 30 seconds in 2017.[^cite_note-\:0-15][^cite_note-Closer-24] Discussing the change in 2017, [Lawrence Krauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Krauss), one of the scientists from the *Bulletin*, warned that political leaders must make decisions based on facts, and those facts "must be taken into account if the future of humanity is to be preserved".[^cite_note-NPR.org-22] In an announcement from the *Bulletin* about the status of the Clock, they went as far to call for action from "wise" public officials and "wise" citizens to make an attempt to steer human life away from catastrophe while humans still can.[^cite_note-\:0-15]

On January 24, 2018, scientists moved the clock to two minutes to midnight, based on threats greatest in the nuclear realm. The scientists said, of recent moves by [North Korea](21255.md) under [Kim Jong-un](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Jong-un) and the administration of [Donald Trump](4848272.md) in the U.S.: "Hyperbolic rhetoric and provocative actions by both sides have increased the possibility of nuclear war by accident or miscalculation".[^cite_note-Closer-24]

The clock was left unchanged in 2019 due to the twin threats of nuclear weapons and climate change, and the problem of those threats being "exacerbated this past year by the increased use of information warfare to undermine democracy around the world, amplifying risk from these and other threats and putting the future of civilization in extraordinary danger".[^cite_note-bulletin2019-6]

On January 23, 2020, the Clock was moved to 100 seconds (1 minute, 40 seconds) before midnight. The *Bulletin*'s executive chairman, [Jerry Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_Brown), said "the dangerous rivalry and hostility among the superpowers increases the likelihood of nuclear blunder... Climate change just compounds the crisis".[^cite_note-james-7] The "100 seconds to midnight" setting remained unchanged in 2021 and 2022.[^cite_note-25]

On January 24, 2023, the Clock was moved to 90 seconds (1 minute, 30 seconds) before midnight, which was largely attributed to the [risk of nuclear escalation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_risk_during_the_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine) that arose from the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine). Other reasons cited included climate change, biological threats such as [COVID-19](63030231.md), and risks associated with disinformation and [disruptive technologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disruptive_technologies).[^cite_note-bulletin2023-8]

On January 28, 2025, the Clock was moved to 89 seconds (1 minute, 29 seconds) before midnight. In addition to 2024's concerns, the increased usage of artificial intelligence in both the battlefield and social media was noted as a new factor.[^cite_note-26]

On January 27, 2026, the Clock was moved to 85 seconds (1 minute, 25 seconds) before midnight, the closest it has ever been set to midnight since its inception in 1947. According to the bulletin, it is all caused by a "failure of leadership". From Alexandra Bell, CEO and president of the scientists, "It is a hard truth, but this is our reality," as no significant action was taken to push the clock back.[^cite_note-\:2-10]

## Criticism

In 2016, [Anders Sandberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anders_Sandberg) of the [Future of Humanity Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_of_Humanity_Institute) has stated that the "grab bag of threats" currently mixed together by the Clock can induce paralysis.[^cite_note-27] People may be more likely to succeed at smaller, incremental challenges; for example, taking steps to prevent the accidental detonation of nuclear weapons was a small but significant step towards avoiding nuclear war.[^cite_note-nyt-28] Alex Barasch in *[Slate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slate_\(magazine\))* argued that "putting humanity on a permanent, blanket high-alert isn't helpful when it comes to policy or science" and criticized the *Bulletin* for neither explaining nor attempting to quantify their methodology.[^cite_note-slate-21]

[Cognitive psychologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_psychology) [Steven Pinker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Pinker) harshly criticized the Doomsday Clock as a political stunt, pointing to the words of its founder that its purpose was "to preserve civilization by scaring men into rationality". He stated that it is inconsistent and not based on any [objective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objectivity_\(science\)) indicators of security, using as an example its being farther from midnight in 1962 during the [Cuban Missile Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Missile_Crisis) than in the "far calmer 2007". He argued it was another example of humanity's tendency toward historical pessimism, and compared it to other predictions of self-destruction that went unfulfilled.[^cite_note-29]

Writing for the *[New Statesman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Statesman)*, British journalist [James Ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Ball_\(journalist\)) questioned the Clock's purpose and noted the Bulletin's lack of objective methodology for setting the Clock. Ball then observes that the organization is no different to other doomsday cults preaching the end of the world except that the Bulletin is secular instead of religious.[^cite_note-30]

Conservative media outlets have often criticized the *Bulletin* and the Doomsday Clock. Keith Payne wrote 2010 in the *[National Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Review)* that the Clock overestimated the effects of "developments in the areas of nuclear testing and formal arms control".[^cite_note-31] In 2018, Tristin Hopper in the *[National Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Post)* acknowledged that "there are plenty of things to worry about regarding climate change", but states that climate change is not in the same league as total nuclear destruction.[^cite_note-national_post-32] In addition, critics accuse the *Bulletin* of pushing a political agenda.[^cite_note-nyt-28][^cite_note-national_post-32][^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34]

## In popular culture

- Australian rock band [Midnight Oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midnight_Oil)'s 1984 LP *[Red Sails in the Sunset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sails_in_the_Sunset_\(album\))* features a song called "[Minutes to Midnight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minutes_to_Midnight_\(song\))", and the album's cover shows an aerial-view rendering of [Sydney](27862.md) after a nuclear strike.

- The title of [Iron Maiden](159172.md)'s 1984 song "[2 Minutes to Midnight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2_Minutes_to_Midnight)" is a reference to the Doomsday Clock.

- The 1986 short story "[The End of the Whole Mess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_End_of_the_Whole_Mess)" by [Stephen King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_King) refers to the Doomsday Clock being set at fifteen seconds before midnight due to elevated geopolitical tension.

- In the [Flobots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flobots)' song "[The Circle in the Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Circle_in_the_Square)", the lyrics say "the clock is now 11\:55 on the big hand", which was the Doomsday Clock's setting in 2012 when the song was released.

  [^cite_note-64]

- The title of the 1982 *[Doctor Who](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Who)* episode "[Four to Doomsday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_to_Doomsday)" references the Doomsday Clock. In the 2017 episode "[The Pyramid at the End of the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pyramid_at_the_End_of_the_World)", the Monks changed every clock in the world to three minutes to midnight as a warning about what will happen if humanity does not accept their help. Representatives of the three most powerful armies on Earth agreed not to fight each other, believing a potential war is the catastrophe. However, the clock remained displaying two minutes to midnight. After the Doctor averted the true catastrophe – an [accidental bacteriological disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biotechnology_risk) –, the clock began moving backwards.

  [^cite_note-66]

- One minute to midnight on the Doomsday Clock is heavily referenced in the [grime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grime_\(music_genre\))/punk crossover song "Effed" by [Nottingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nottingham) rapper Snowy and [Jason Williamson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jason_Williamson) of [Sleaford Mods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleaford_Mods). Because of the track's political content, there was an initial reluctance from mainstream radio stations to play the track before the [2019 United Kingdom general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_United_Kingdom_general_election). However, the track was later championed by a number of [BBC Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio) DJs, including punk innovator [Iggy Pop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iggy_Pop).
