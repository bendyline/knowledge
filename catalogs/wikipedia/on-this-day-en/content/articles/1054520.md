A Hatzalah ambulance in the [Crown Heights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_Heights,_Brooklyn) neighborhood of [Brooklyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn) in [New York City](645042.md)

**Hatzalah**, also spelled **Hatzolah** or **Hatzola** ([/hətˈsʌlə/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English); [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language): הַצָּלָה, lit. 'rescue'  or 'relief', [\[hat͡saˈla\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Hebrew)), is the title used by many Jewish volunteer [emergency medical service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_medical_services) (EMS) organizations serving mostly areas with [Jewish](25955086.md) communities around the world. Most local branches operate independently of each other, but use the common name.

## History

The original Hatzalah [emergency medical services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_medical_services) (EMS) was founded in [Williamsburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Williamsburg,_Brooklyn), a neighborhood of [Brooklyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn), New York, by Hershel Weber in the late 1960s.[^cite_note-Willie-1] His aim was to improve rapid emergency medical response in the community, and to mitigate cultural concerns of a [Yiddish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yiddish)-speaking, [Hasidic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasidic) community.[^cite_note-nyt-women-2] The idea spread to other [Orthodox Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodox_Judaism) neighborhoods in the [New York City area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_metropolitan_area), and eventually to other regions, countries, and continents.[^cite_note-3] Hatzalah is believed to be the largest volunteer ambulance service in the United States.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5]

Hatzalah members were present at several operations such as the [September 11 attacks](5058690.md)[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7] and [Surfside condominium collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surfside_condominium_collapse),[^cite_note-Hope-8] and provided medical equipment to hospitals during the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md).[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10] In March 2026, four Hatzola ambulances were [set on fire](82760663.md) in London.[^cite_note-11]

## Operations



Two Hatzolah ambulances on stand-by on the [Upper East Side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_East_Side)&#x20;

Each volunteer is called a *unit* (as in, a crew of one) and is assigned a unit number that starts with a neighborhood code, followed by a [serial number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_number) for that neighborhood (e.g., "Q-120" means "Queens unit number 120"[^cite_note-Madrich-12]).&#x20;

In some areas there may be periods where coverage is not strong enough, for example on a summer weekend. When this happens, coordinators may assign an on-call rotation. The rotation may still respond from their houses, or they may stay at the garage through their shift. In such periods, Hatzalah functions closer to a typical EMS crew setup, though the dispatchers may still seek non-on-call members to respond, and there will still often be a non-ambulance responder as first dispatched, even if that responder starts from the base.[^cite_note-Madrich-12]

In [Israel](9282173.md), [United Hatzalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Hatzalah) relies upon mobile phone technologies which include an SOS [app](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_app) and a special emergency phone number, 1221, with messages to news organizations distributed by [WhatsApp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WhatsApp).[^cite_note-13]

### Response times

Hatzalah's model provides for rapid [first responder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Certified_first_responder) response times. Each Hatzalah neighborhood's response time varies. For example, in [Borough Park, Brooklyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_Park,_Brooklyn), daytime response in life-threatening emergency are between 1–2 minutes, and nighttime response times are 5–6 minutes. However, following implementation of Truck-1 (night on-call crews) the nighttime response in Borough Park was shortened significantly.[^cite_note-Bloomberg-14] In the Beverly-La Brea neighborhood of [Los Angeles](18110.md), response times average at 60–90 seconds.[^cite_note-15] In Israel, the response time is under 3 minutes.[^cite_note-16]

### Organization

Hatzalah is not a single organization. Each chapter operates autonomously, or, in some cases, with varying levels of affiliation with neighboring Hatzalah chapters and under a central association.[^cite_note-Affiliates-17]

In New York City, there are usually two or three members who are "coordinators",[^cite_note-NYC_Struct-18] managing all operational aspects of the chapter. The New York City-area Hatzalah is formally called Chevra Hatzalah of New York. It combines dispatch and some other functions for over a dozen neighborhood chapters, which each have their own fund-raising, management, garages, ambulances, and assigned members. Together, the combined [New York State](8210131.md) branches have grown to become the largest all-volunteer ambulance system in the United States.[^cite_note-Bloomberg-14]

## Legal status

In New York, Hatzalah usually uses red and white lights and sirens, like normal unmarked emergency vehicles. In New Jersey, Hatzalah usually use blue lights.[^cite_note-19] Since 2021, [Florida law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_law) has permitted faith-based volunteer ambulance services to operate, allowing Hatzalah response vehicles to use emergency lights and sirens.[^cite_note-DeSantis-20] In Toronto, Hatzalah uses green lights, having the same legal status as volunteer firefighters who also use green lights in their vehicles.[^cite_note-21] In the United Kingdom, Hatzola organisations cannot legally use or install [blue lights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_vehicle_equipment_in_the_United_Kingdom) and sirens on their responders' private vehicles.[^cite_note-22]

On 20 February 2013, the [Federal Communications Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Communications_Commission) granted Chevrah Hatzalah's request for a waiver to obtain calling party numbers (CPN), even when callers have [caller ID blocking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caller_ID_blocking#Blocking).[^cite_note-23] In the United States, this type of CPN blocking waiver is normally only given to [911](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/911_\(emergency_telephone_number\)) call centers, but Chevrah Hatzalah does not receive calls through 911. Other Hatzalah dispatch numbers, including other New York State Hatzalah groups, do not have this waiver.

## Interaction with other agencies

### Magen David Adom

United Hatzalah's relationship with [Magen David Adom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magen_David_Adom) is strained, and MDA has banned its members and volunteers from also volunteering in other rescue organizations, including Hatzalah.[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25]

### Ezras Nashim

Hatzalah was criticized by the *[New York Post](102227.md)*[^cite_note-26][^cite_note-27] and *JEMS Magazine*[^cite_note-28] for its practice of not allowing women to join. A group of Orthodox women founded an organization called [Ezras Nashim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezras_Nashim), an all-female Orthodox Jewish volunteer EMT ambulance service,[^cite_note-29] citing the need for modesty and sensitivity to the needs of fellow Orthodox women, with the goal of preserving women's modesty in emergency medical situations, especially [childbirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Childbirth).[^cite_note-30]
