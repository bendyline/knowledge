The **2025 Hong Kong Legislative Council election** was a general election held on 7 December 2025 to elect all 90 members of the 8th [Legislative Council of Hong Kong](273946.md).[^cite_note-4] Following the [2021 Hong Kong electoral changes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Hong_Kong_electoral_changes), 90 seats were up for election, including [40 seats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_Committee_\(constituency\)) elected by the 1,500-member [Election Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_Committee), 30 trade-based indirectly elected [functional constituency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_constituency_\(Hong_Kong\)) seats, and 20 seats directly elected through [geographical constituencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographical_constituency). The election, in which only [pro-government "patriots"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro-Beijing_camp_\(Hong_Kong\)) were allowed to run and only 20 seats were popularly elected, was internationally considered to be neither [free nor fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_and_fair_election).

The past legislative session, the [7th Legislative Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7th_Legislative_Council_of_Hong_Kong), was criticised for its lack of meaningful discourse, with many bills being approved without a quorum. The introduction of smart ballot boxes also prompted controversy, while the future of democrats and moderates was called into question due to the effective dissolution of the [pro-democracy camp](3291227.md). Ultimately, there were only two "non-core pro-establishment candidates" from the [PoD Research Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PoD_Research_Institute), both of whom did not declare their affiliation and came last.

Following multiple reports that indicated the central Chinese government established an age limit on lawmakers, all 12 of them aged over 70,[^cite_note-yky-5] including [council president](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Legislative_Council_of_Hong_Kong) [Andrew Leung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Leung) and the leaders of five parties – [Lo Wai-kwok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lo_Wai-kwok) of the [Business and Professionals Alliance for Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_and_Professionals_Alliance_for_Hong_Kong) (BPA), [Regina Ip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regina_Ip) of the [New People's Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_People's_Party) (NPP), [Tommy Cheung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tommy_Cheung) of the [Liberal Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Party_\(Hong_Kong\)), [Michael Tien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Tien) of [Roundtable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundtable_\(Hong_Kong\)), and [Tik Chi-yuen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tik_Chi-yuen) of [Third Side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Side) – chose not to seek another term for various reasons, contributing to a retirement wave and an abundance of newcomer candidates. Third Side and [New Prospect for Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Prospect_for_Hong_Kong) did not field any candidates after their incumbents retired.

Election-related activities were suspended due to the [Wang Fuk Court fire](81694683.md), but the election went ahead as scheduled despite calls for postponement. Only government-organised election forums were held, which were criticised for lacking debate. The government ran a massive turnout campaign, set up dedicated polling stations, and extended voting hours to raise the historically low turnout from the last election. Many people were arrested for damaging election posters and inciting others not to vote or cast [invalid votes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invalid_vote). The election resulted in a 31.9% voter turnout—a slight increase from the 2021 election but still lower than the 52.3% turnout [in 2016](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Hong_Kong_legislative_election). The total votes cast in the [geographical constituencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographical_constituency) also decreased from 2021 by 33,000, and registration decreased by 7.64%.

## Background

This is the second "patriots governing Hong Kong" election under the [new electoral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Hong_Kong_electoral_changes) introduced in 2021, after which the Legislative Council was expanded from 70 to 90 seats, but with only 20 seats allocated for geographical constituencies (GCs) which are directly elected by constituents. The remaining seventy seats include 40 [Election Committee Constituency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_Committee_\(constituency\)) (ECC) seats selected by the 1,500-member [body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_Committee), and 30 seats of trade-based indirectly elected [functional constituencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_constituency_\(Hong_Kong\)) (FCs) voted by selected groups of professionals and corporate representatives.

Only pro-Beijing "[patriots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriots_administering_Hong_Kong)" are allowed to run; the majority of opposition pro-democracy members were banned, jailed, or forced into exile.[^cite_note-\:15-6] In addition, the election is considered a disproportionate two-tier democracy and very limited popular representation, as only 20 of the Council's 90 seats are directly elected.[^cite_note-\:15-6] The current election laws are considered by the foreign ministries of various countries,[^cite_note-7] [Inter-Parliamentary Alliance on China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inter-Parliamentary_Alliance_on_China), an inter-parliamentary group of lawmakers in several countries, and some [NGOs](46539.md) to be neither free nor fair.[^cite_note-usa_statement-8][^cite_note-ipac_statement-9][^cite_note-fh_statement-10]

### Past legislative session

The [7th Legislative Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7th_Legislative_Council_of_Hong_Kong) elected in [2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Hong_Kong_legislative_election) was the first after the [national security law](64033197.md) and electoral changes were imposed by the government of China. Without any legislator explicitly from the [pro-democracy camp](3291227.md), the legislature packed with [pro-Beijing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro-Beijing_camp_\(Hong_Kong\)) members was criticised to lack meaningful discussions and debates, while newcomers of the Legislative Council resorted to politically correct measures only, such as repeating speeches by Chinese officials.[^cite_note-11] *[Ming Pao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_Pao)* reported in August 2023 that amongst the 24 government bills passed since the beginning of the legislative term, including the [domestic security law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safeguarding_National_Security_Ordinance) which was fast-tracked,[^cite_note-12] 16 of those were approved without a quorum,[^cite_note-13] prompting concerns of LegCo members' performance.[^cite_note-14] [Andrew Leung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Leung), [President of the Legislative Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Legislative_Council_of_Hong_Kong), denied claims that the chamber rubber-stamped bills and defended it as highly efficient.[^cite_note-15] However, an opinion poll by pro-government party and think tank [Path of Democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_of_Democracy) said 48.1% of the interviewed expressed dissatisfaction with legislators' performance and only 29.4% were content.[^cite_note-\:1-16]

Apart from evaluation from the own party, it was also reported that government officials were involved in assessing the performance of the legislators in the run-up to the election, an act seen as unprecedented before the "patriot-only" principle was implemented.[^cite_note-17]

### Smart ballot box

In December 2024, the government of Hong Kong announced a series of new measures to be implemented in this election. The Improving Electoral Arrangements (Consolidated Amendments) Bill contained a plan to introduce "smart ballot boxes" to ensure ballots were validly marked.[^cite_note-18] While the [Constitutional and Mainland Affairs Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_and_Mainland_Affairs_Bureau) said the scanners would "streamline and enhance" procedures while ensuring that "cardinal election principles" such as voting confidentiality would be upheld, some lawmakers expressed concerns over the implications of compromising vote secrecy.[^cite_note-19] Although insisting the voter rights would be protected as voters would not be identified, the government withdrew the proposal less than a week later after "considering the views of legislators".[^cite_note-20] The bureau said it attaches great importance to the views and is willing to "accept well-intentioned advice", while the smart ballot boxes will still be made available for voters who choose to use them.[^cite_note-21]

### Future of democrats and moderates

The implementation of the "[Patriots administering Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriots_administering_Hong_Kong)" principle barred "unpatriotic" opposition from being elected to public offices. The flagship pro-democracy group, the [Democratic Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Party_\(Hong_Kong\)), once the largest party in the Legislative Council, is planning to dissolve in 2025.[^cite_note-22] Media sources indicated Chinese government has pressed for the closure of the "worthless" party, which was unable to run in the 2021 and 2023 "patriot-only" elections.[^cite_note-23] Reportedly also under pressure, the [League of Social Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Social_Democrats) disbanded in late June. The pro-democracy camp is considered to have come to an end without any active political groups.

The new rules also reduced the portion of directly elected seats to around 22%, which was considered by democratic watchdogs as a decrease in democratic values.[^cite_note-ipac_statement-9][^cite_note-fh_statement-10] *[Hong Kong Economic Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong_Economic_Journal)* said some members from the business sector recommended Beijing authorities to draw up a timeline to gradually reintroduce more elected seats in order to improve Hong Kong's international image and align with the Basic Law article. [Third Side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Side), the only self-claimed moderate group in the Legislative Council, and [Path of Democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_of_Democracy), an extra-parliamentary moderate group, expressed the hope that an electoral reform would be initiated by the government in due course.[^cite_note-24] However, it was speculated by media that the moderates would also be barred from legislative races as they are "no longer needed in a patriotic administration".[^cite_note-25]

## Retirement wave

### Announcements

On 29 September, [Andrew Leung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Leung), who served as [president of the legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Legislative_Council_of_Hong_Kong) for nine years, made a surprise announcement that he would stand down from the legislature, citing his age and his family.[^cite_note-26] He said he hoped this would give capable candidates time to prepare sufficiently for the election. His decision further raised speculation about an age limit of 70 for lawmakers, although Leung said it was solely his personal decision. While recalling his nine-year leadership, Leung said the Legislative Council successfully ended the opposition's disruption.[^cite_note-\:5-27] Possible successors for the presidency include [Starry Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starry_Lee) and [Chan Chun-ying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chan_Chun-ying).[^cite_note-\:3-28] [Ma Fung-kwok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma_Fung-kwok), 70, announced his retirement on the same day as Leung, also citing his age.[^cite_note-\:3-28]

Two veteran legislators, 76-year-old [Liberal Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Party_\(Hong_Kong\)) leader [Tommy Cheung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tommy_Cheung) and [Chan Kin-por](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chan_Kin-por), 71, confirmed their intention not to seek another term on 11 October. Cheung said he had carefully considered his decision with the development of the [One Country, Two Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Country,_Two_Systems), while Chan said he would be passing the baton to younger rank to run for the election.[^cite_note-\:6-29] The sole self-branded "non-establishment" moderate member [Tik Chi-yuen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tik_Chi-yuen) was also stepping down, saying a younger colleague from his party [Third Side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Side) will run only as the party did not gather enough funding to support his re-election.[^cite_note-\:4-30] More lawmakers dropped out in the next few days, including 72-year-old [Business and Professionals Alliance for Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_and_Professionals_Alliance_for_Hong_Kong) chairman [Lo Wai-kwok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lo_Wai-kwok),[^cite_note-\:9-31] pro-Beijing camp's unofficial convenor [Martin Liao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Liao), aged 68,[^cite_note-\:10-32] and [Roundtable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundtable_\(Hong_Kong\)) convener [Michael Tien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Tien).[^cite_note-round-33] Outspoken lawmakers such as [Doreen Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doreen_Kong) opted to stand down as well, and dismissed claims that she was forced to do so.[^cite_note-34] The two remaining legislators over 70, [Regina Ip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regina_Ip) and [Lai Tung-kwok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lai_Tung-kwok) of the [New People's Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_People's_Party) (NPP), confirmed their retirement on 25 October.[^cite_note-\:11-35] Another outspoken member [Paul Tse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Tse) did not submit nomination for the election, saying that "silence speaks louder than words",[^cite_note-36] and thereby ending his 17-year career in the assembly.[^cite_note-35no-37]

As a result, 35 incumbent legislators did not seek re-election, more than half having served only one term.[^cite_note-38] All 12 incumbents who were over 70 chose not to seek re-election, while the other 23 decided against staying in the legislature likely because of their unsatisfactory performance, or vocal criticisms on some government policies, or to pave way for succession within the party.[^cite_note-yky-5]

### Reactions

The unprecedentedly large[^cite_note-\:2-39] wave of retirements sparked claims that Beijing imposed an age limit for lawmakers and demanded a major upheaval of the composition, although the officials denied "blessing" or giving "special treatment" for candidates.[^cite_note-40] [John Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lee_Ka-chiu), the Chief Executive, said the departures are normal and personal decisions by the lawmakers and the Executive Council will not be reshuffled after ExCo members left the LegCo.[^cite_note-\:10-32] Lee added the generational succession is a positive sign for the Legislative Council.[^cite_note-41] However, the farewell motion, which is tabled by convention at the last meeting to mark the end of the term, was withdrawn in light of possible "sensitive remarks" and giving undue advantages to legislators before the election.[^cite_note-42] A farewell banquet for LegCo members and John Lee was also cancelled.[^cite_note-43]

Both Chinese government and John Lee strongly rebutted claims that Beijing has "blessed" any hopefuls in the election and condemned "anti-China" forces for ongoing attempts to sabotage, disrupt, and discredit the patriots-only election through spreading rumours of Chinese interference. They also praised the retiring incumbents for their nobility and upright characters.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46]

According to John Burns, emeritus professor of politics at the [University of Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Hong_Kong), China has sought to strengthen its influence in the legislature after this retirement wave, with younger candidates linked to the mainland emerging in their place. He claimed that China’s representative office "carefully curates" electoral contests to ensure preferred candidates win.[^cite_note-\:16-47]

## Contesting parties and candidates



161 nominations were received after the massive wave of retirement

A total of 161 nominations were received to run, seven more than the previous election, with 107 of them being newcomers. Nearly half, 13 of the 28 functional constituencies, were contested by new faces.[^cite_note-\:12-53] *[South China Morning Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_China_Morning_Post)* noted that 30% of the 161 candidates had links to mainland Chinese firms, a 50% increase from 2021, which indicated the prominence of mainland capital in Hong Kong's economy. Up to 16 of the Hong Kong's 36 [National People's Congress](38878740.md) (NPC) deputies also registered to run in the election, more than double the seven in the 2021 election, alongside at least 12 members of the [Chinese People’s Political Consultative Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_People%E2%80%99s_Political_Consultative_Conference) (CPPCC).[^cite_note-\:13-54] All 161 competitors were cleared to contest by the vetting panel [Candidate Eligibility Review Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candidate_Eligibility_Review_Committee), as announced on 10 November.[^cite_note-hkfp_20251111-55]

## Campaign

All election-related activities were suspended following the [Wang Fuk Court fire](81694683.md) on 26 November for the government to be fully engaged in handling the disaster. Election forums and electioneering by all major parties were cancelled.[^cite_note-74] Despite calls to postpone the election, John Lee confirmed on 2 December, after the end of the three-day mourning period, that the election would go ahead as planned.[^cite_note-75]

### Election forums



Election forum at [Hong Kong Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong_Park) under preparation

The Hong Kong government announced that it would hold 39 election forums starting from 11 November.[^cite_note-hkfp_20251111-55] Local media reported that several media organisations, including three television stations, had planned to hold "around eight to nine" election forums, but were called off suddenly, as candidates decided to only attend government forums due to time and stress. Government officials declined to comment whether the candidates were required to attend only government-organised forums.[^cite_note-76][^cite_note-77][^cite_note-78] However, the forums were criticised for lacking debate between the candidates, and the authorities reportedly encouraged more interaction in the following forums. Chief Secretary for Administration [Eric Chan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Chan_Kwok-ki) said the government is hoping for a "higher-level" debate without insults, foul language, or other tactics seen in past debates.[^cite_note-79]

The content of an [i-CABLE News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I-CABLE_News_Channel_\(Hong_Kong\)) interview which showed campaign workers who attended the forum unable to recall the candidates' names and admitting that they were arranged to participate without knowing its details was taken down a few hours after airing, but was not reported by mainstream Hong Kong media.[^cite_note-80]

### Turnout campaign



Election publicity outside the [government complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Government_Complex_\(Hong_Kong\))

The previous Legislative Council election recorded a historic low turnout of 30.2% despite widespread campaigning. In this election, the government allocated 1.28 billion dollars for advertising, marking an increase of over 50%. Aiming for at least a turnout of 30%, they believed a costly advertising campaign, similar to the [2023 local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Hong_Kong_local_elections), could be criticized as a waste of public money when the government's deficit remains troublesome.[^cite_note-\:0-81][^cite_note-82][^cite_note-83] [Erick Tsang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erick_Tsang), [Secretary for Constitutional and Mainland Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_for_Constitutional_and_Mainland_Affairs), said the government does not have a benchmark for the turnout.[^cite_note-84]

Four major business chambers – the [Chinese General Chamber of Commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_General_Chamber_of_Commerce) (CGCC), the [Chinese Manufacturers' Association of Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Manufacturers'_Association_of_Hong_Kong) (CMA), the [Federation of Hong Kong Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federation_of_Hong_Kong_Industries) (FHKI) and the [Hong Kong General Chamber of Commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong_General_Chamber_of_Commerce) (HKGCC) – issued a joint statement in early November to incentivise their staff to vote, by pledging to give their workers half-day paid leave.[^cite_note-85] The Travel Industry Council called upon local travel agencies to delay the departure time of their group tours to give travellers enough time to cast their votes.[^cite_note-86]

On 11 November, the [Labour and Welfare Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_and_Welfare_Bureau) announced that it would provide a one-off subsidy of HK\$20,000 to elderly centres and district support centres for persons with disabilities for arranging transportation and staff to take elderly people with disabilities and their caregivers to polling stations to vote, and to provide care services to the elderly and persons with disabilities while the caregivers were at polling station.[^cite_note-87]

The [Electoral Affairs Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_Affairs_Commission) announced on 14 November that it would, for the first time, set up dedicated polling stations for elderly, disabled, medical staff, ethnic minorities and civil servants and disciplined services personnel on duty on polling day. Three polling stations would also be set up near the boundary at [Sheung Shui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheung_Shui), the Hong Kong Port of [Hong Kong-Zhuhai-Macao Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong-Zhuhai-Macao_Bridge) and the [Hong Kong International Airport](248457.md). The voting hours would last 16 hours, extending two more hours from the 2021 election. [Secretary for the Civil Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_for_the_Civil_Service) [Ingrid Yeung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingrid_Yeung) said the bureau had sent instructions to all bureau and department heads to establish a support centre at their respective departments on polling day in order to mobilise over 50,000 civil servants to help out and take all necessary measures to facilitate their voting, with compensation leave or overtime allowance granted for those who were on duty.[^cite_note-88]

## Arrests

Hong Kong police arrested over 29 people for alleged criminal damage to posters, in what he said were believed to be separate cases.[^cite_note-89] The [National Security Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Department) also arrested a 68-year-old local man for allegedly posting comments on social media encouraging people not to vote or cast invalid ballots in the election.[^cite_note-90] The [Independent Commission Against Corruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Commission_Against_Corruption_\(Hong_Kong\)) (ICAC) arrested 11 on suspicion of inciting others not to vote or to cast invalid votes by reposting online posts,[^cite_note-\:17-91] and issued arrest warrants for exiled activists Alan Keung Ka-wai, who participated in the exiled [pro-independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong_independence_movement) group [Hong Kong Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong_Parliament_\(exiled_organisation\)), and Tong Wai-hung, protester who was acquitted of rioting charges in the [2019 Sheung Wan clashes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_the_2019%E2%80%932020_Hong_Kong_protests_\(July_2019\)#28_July_protest), for allegedly inciting others not to vote in the election.[^cite_note-92] Another was placed on the wanted list on the day of election.[^cite_note-\:17-91]

Chief Executive John Lee vowed to take a hard line against any disruptions to the election. Beijing's [Hong Kong and Macau Affairs Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong_and_Macau_Affairs_Office) also shared a strongly worded commentary urging vigilance against attempts to jeopardise the election, saying that local law enforcement would not "sit back and do nothing".[^cite_note-93] The [Office for Safeguarding National Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_for_Safeguarding_National_Security) stated some individuals both inside and outside had launched "various attacks, smears and slanders" against the election, "inciting people not to vote or to cast blank votes" and "deliberately damaging election campaign materials". It criticised "some anti-China and pro-chaos forces" with "evil nature" for attempting to weaken the legitimacy of the new electoral system and democratic development process, endanger Hong Kong's good governance and national security, adding that it firmly supported the Hong Kong government in cracking down on any interference and disruption.[^cite_note-ICAC1114-94] It published another statement later accusing the "anti-China and pro-chaos" elements and external forces for their "unrepentant evil intentions" and "unyielding malice", vowing to defend election security and order with a "zero-tolerance" attitude.[^cite_note-95]

## Results

&#x20;\
&#x20;Despite massive publicity across the city,[^cite_note-\:8-96] The election resulted only in a 31.9% voter turnout—a slight increase from the 2021 election but still lower than the 52.3% turnout [in 2016](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Hong_Kong_legislative_election).[^cite_note-Turnout-2] The total votes cast in the [geographical constituencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographical_constituency) also decreased from 2021 by 33,000, and registration decreased by 7.64%.[^cite_note-\:14-97] The Chinese authorities nonetheless claimed the voter participation "all far surpassed the previous term", adding that the election successfully demonstrated "high-quality democracy" and the results "fully represent the will of Hong Kong people".[^cite_note-98]

DAB emerged as the largest party again, holding 20 seats in the Legislative Council, one more than the last term.[^cite_note-rthk1208-99] The party faced criticism after one of their members was involved in the renovation works at Wang Fuk Court, forcing the party chair to rebut "slander and false accusations".[^cite_note-\:8-96] FTU retained their seven seats after their defeat in Election Committee constituency and in direct elections, while BPA continued to rank second with eight seats, one less than last session. NPP's seats halved from 6 to 3, making them the fourth largest and the Liberal Party the third with their four legislators. Professional Power's [Christine Fong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christine_Fong) finally entered Legislative Council on her sixth attempt and topped the geographical constituencies with 58,828 votes.[^cite_note-rthk1208-99]

## Analysis

The sweeping change in the composition of the Legislative Council, with at least 40% set to be first-time members,[^cite_note-\:12-53] paved way for Beijing to replace the traditional establishment nurtured during the colonial era with younger and more disciplined loyalists, as well as [mainland Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland_Chinese) who would coordinate more closely.[^cite_note-\:2-39] The election also signaled a defeat for [Hong Kong drifters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong_drifter) (*gang piao*, young, educated migrants from mainland China) and moderates, after three legislators from the former camp ([Gary Zhang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Zhang), [Shang Hailong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shang_Hailong), and [Wendy Hong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendy_Hong)) chose to serve only one term, and the moderate Third Side failed to gather enough nominations.[^cite_note-108] The new session would also be composed of more "national team" members, meaning managers from Chinese enterprises or state-run companies.[^cite_note-\:2-39] Separately, more than two dozen NPC members and two dozen CPPCC members joining the race at a time when more local elites were getting involved in national politics has resulted in a "revolving door" phenomenon.[^cite_note-\:13-54]On the other hand, the Chinese government praised the candidates as "outstanding talent", and stressed that the candidates will provide voters with choice and enable them to pick "the best from the best".[^cite_note-109]
