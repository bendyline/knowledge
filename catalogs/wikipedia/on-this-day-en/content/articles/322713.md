**Croke Park** ([Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language): *Páirc an Chrócaigh*, IPA: [\[ˈpˠaːɾʲc ə ˈxɾˠoːkəj\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Irish)) is a [Gaelic games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_games) stadium in [Dublin](8504.md), Ireland. Named after Archbishop [Thomas Croke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Croke), it is referred to as **Croker** by [GAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_Athletics_Association) fans and locals.[^cite_note-slipups-2] It is the headquarters of the [Gaelic Athletic Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_Athletic_Association) (GAA). Since 1891[^cite_note-3] the site has been used by the GAA to host Gaelic sports, including the [annual All-Ireland finals in Gaelic football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_All-Ireland_Senior_Football_Championship_finals) and [hurling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_All-Ireland_Senior_Hurling_Championship_finals).

A major expansion and redevelopment of the stadium ran from 1991 to 2005, raising capacity to its current 82,300 spectators.[^cite_note-4] This makes Croke Park the [fourth-largest stadium in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_European_stadiums_by_capacity), and the largest not usually used for [association football](10568.md) in Europe.

Other events held at the stadium include the opening and closing ceremonies of the [2003 Special Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_Special_Olympics_World_Summer_Games), and numerous musical concerts. In 2012, Irish pop group [Westlife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westlife) sold out the stadium in a record-breaking time: less than 5 minutes.[^cite_note-music-news.com-5] From 2007 to 2010, Croke Park hosted home matches of the [Ireland national rugby union team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland_national_rugby_union_team) and the [Republic of Ireland national football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Ireland_national_football_team), while their new [Aviva Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviva_Stadium) was constructed. This use of Croke Park for non-Gaelic sports [was controversial](#Non-Gaelic_games) and required temporary changes to GAA rules. In June 2012, the stadium hosted the closing ceremony of the [50th International Eucharistic Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucharistic_Congress) during which [Pope Benedict XVI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI) gave an address over a video link.[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7]

## City and Suburban Racecourse



A fireworks and light display was held in Croke Park in front of 79,161 fans on Saturday 31 January 2009 to mark the GAA's 125th anniversary

The area now known as Croke Park was owned in the 1880s by Maurice Butterly and known as the City and Suburban Racecourse, or Jones' Road sports ground. From 1893 it was also used by the [Bohemian Football Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemian_F.C.).[^cite_note-8] In 1891 *Butterly's field* hosted Athletics meetings organised by the GAA.

## History

Recognising the potential of the Jones' Road sports ground, a journalist and GAA member, Frank Dineen, borrowed much of the £3,250 asking price and bought the ground in 1908. In 1913 the GAA came into exclusive ownership of the plot when they purchased it from Dineen for £3,500. The ground was then renamed Croke Park in honour of Archbishop [Thomas Croke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Croke), one of the GAA's first patrons.

In 1913, Croke Park only had one stand on what is now known as the Hogan Stand side and grassy banks all round. In 1917, a grassy hill was constructed on the railway end of Croke Park to afford patrons a better view of the pitch. This terrace was known originally as Hill 60, after a battle in the [Gallipoli Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallipoli_campaign), in which [The Royal Dublin Fusiliers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Royal_Dublin_Fusiliers), [Munster Fusiliers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munster_Fusiliers) and [Connaught Rangers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connaught_Rangers) fought. It was later renamed Hill 16 in memory of the 1916 [Easter Rising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Rising). It is erroneously said to have been built from the ruins of the [GPO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Post_Office,_Dublin) but was constructed the previous year in 1915.

In 1918, the GAA set out to create a high-capacity stadium at Croke Park. Following the Hogan Stand, the Cusack Stand, named after [Michael Cusack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Cusack_\(Gaelic_Athletic_Association\)) from Clare (who founded the GAA and served as its first secretary), was built in 1927. 1936 saw the first double-deck Cusack Stand open with 5,000 seats, and concrete terracing being constructed on Hill 16. In 1952 the Nally Stand was built in memorial of [Pat Nally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pat_Nally), another of the GAA founders. Seven years later, to celebrate the 75th anniversary of the GAA, the first cantilevered "New Hogan Stand" was opened.

The highest attendance ever recorded at an All-Ireland Senior Football Championship Final was 90,556 for [Offaly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offaly_county_football_team) v [Down](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Down_county_football_team) in [1961](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1961_All-Ireland_Senior_Football_Championship_Final). Since the introduction of seating to the Cusack Stand in 1966, the largest crowd recorded has been 84,516.

### Bloody Sunday



Bloody Sunday remembrance plaque

Main article: [Bloody Sunday (1920)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloody_Sunday_\(1920\))

On 21 November 1920, during the [Irish War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_War_of_Independence), Croke Park was the scene of a massacre perpetrated by the [Auxiliary Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auxiliary_Division) of the [Royal Irish Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Irish_Constabulary) (RIC). The RIC entered the ground and began shooting into the crowd, killing or fatally wounding 14 civilians during a [Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_county_football_team)-[Tipperary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tipperary_county_football_team) Gaelic football match. The dead included 13 spectators and Tipperary player [Michael Hogan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Hogan_\(sportsman\)). Posthumously, the Hogan Stand, built in 1924, was named in his honour. Those shootings, on the day which became known as [Bloody Sunday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloody_Sunday_\(1920\)), were a reprisal for the killing of 15 people associated with the [Cairo Gang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo_Gang), a group of [British intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_intelligence_agencies) officers, by [Michael Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Collins_\(Irish_leader\))' "[Squad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Squad_\(Irish_Republican_Army_unit\))" earlier that day.[^cite_note-9]

### Dublin Rodeo

In 1924, American [rodeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodeo) promoter, [Tex Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tex_Austin), staged the Dublin Rodeo,[^cite_note-10] Ireland's first professional rodeo at Croke Park Stadium.[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12] For seven days, with two shows each day from 18 to 24 August, sell-out crowds saw cowboys and cowgirls from Canada, the United States, Mexico, Argentina and Australia compete for rodeo championship titles.[^cite_note-13] Canadian bronc riders such as Andy Lund and his brother Art Lund, trick riders such as Ted Elder[^cite_note-14] and Vera McGinnis were among the contestants.[^cite_note-15] British Pathe filmed some of the rodeo events.[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18]

## Stadium redesign

In 1984 the organisation decided to investigate ways to increase the capacity of the old stadium. The design for an 80,000-capacity stadium was completed in 1991. Gaelic sports have special requirements as they take place on a large field. A specific requirement was to ensure the spectators were not too far from the field of play. This resulted in the three-tier design from which viewing games is possible: the main concourse, a premium level incorporating hospitality facilities and an upper concourse. The premium level contains restaurants, bars and conference areas. The project was split into four phases over a 14-year period. Such was the importance of Croke Park to the GAA for hosting big games, that the stadium did not close during redevelopment. During each phase, different parts of the ground were redeveloped, while leaving the rest of the stadium open. Big games, including the annual All-Ireland Hurling and Football finals, were played in the stadium throughout the development.



The outside of the Cusack Stand

### Phase one – New Cusack Stand



The stadium viewed from [Phibsborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phibsborough)

The first phase of construction was to build a replacement for Croke Park's Cusack Stand. A lower deck opened for use in 1994. The upper deck opened in 1995. Completed at a cost of £35 million, the new stand is 180 metres long, 35 metres high, has a capacity for 27,000 people and contains 46 hospitality suites. The new Cusack Stand contains three tiers from which viewing games is possible: the main concourse, a premium level incorporating hospitality facilities and finally an upper concourse. One end of the pitch was closer to the stand after this phase, as the process of slightly re-aligning the pitch during the redevelopment of the stadium began. The works were carried out by [Sisk Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sisk_Group).[^cite_note-19]

### Phase two – Davin Stand

Phase Two of the development started in late 1998 and involved extending the new Cusack Stand to replace the existing Canal End terrace. It involved reacquiring a rugby pitch that had been sold to [Belvedere College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belvedere_College) in 1910 by Frank Dineen. In payment and part exchange, the college was given the nearby Distillery Road sports grounds.[^cite_note-20]

It is now known as The Davin Stand (Irish: *Ardán Dáimhím*), after [Maurice Davin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Davin), the first president of the GAA. This phase also saw the creation of a tunnel which was later named the Ali tunnel in honour of [Muhammad Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ali) and [his fight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_non-Gaelic_games_played_in_Croke_Park#Boxing) against Al Lewis in July 1972 in Croke Park.[^cite_note-21]

### Phase three – Hogan Stand

Phase Three saw the building of the new Hogan Stand. This required a greater variety of spectator categories to be accommodated including general spectators, corporate patrons, VIPs, broadcast and media services and operation staff. Extras included a fitted-out mezzanine level for VIP and Ard Comhairle (Where the dignitaries sit) along with a top-level press media facility. The end of Phase Three took the total spectator capacity of Croke Park to 82,000.

The [1999 All-Ireland Senior Football Championship Final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_All-Ireland_Senior_Football_Championship_Final) was the last to be held with the old Hogan Stand in place.[^cite_note-22]

### Phase four – Nally Stand & Nally End/Dineen Hill 16 terrace

After the 2003 Special Olympics, construction began in September 2003 on the final phase, Phase Four. This involved the redevelopment of the Nally Stand, named after the athlete [Pat Nally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pat_Nally), and Hill 16 into a new Nally End/Dineen Hill 16 terrace. While the name Nally had been used for the stand it replaced, the use of the name Dineen was new, and was in honour of [Frank Dineen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Dineen), who bought the original stadium for the GAA in 1908, giving it to them in 1913. The old Nally Stand was taken away and reassembled in Pairc Colmcille, home of Carrickmore GAA in [County Tyrone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Tyrone).[^cite_note-23]

The phase four development was officially opened by the then GAA President [Seán Kelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Se%C3%A1n_Kelly_\(politician\)) on 14 March 2005. For logistical reasons (and, to a degree, historical reasons), and also to provide cheaper high-capacity space, the area is a terrace rather than a seated stand, the only remaining standing room in Croke Park. Unlike the previous Hill, the new terrace was divided into separate sections – Hill A (Cusack Stand side), Hill B (behind the goals) and the Nally terrace (on the site of the old Nally Stand). The fully redeveloped Hill has a capacity of around 13,200, bringing the overall capacity of the stadium to 82,300. This made the stadium the second biggest in Europe at the time after the [Camp Nou](222488.md), Barcelona. The presence of terracing meant that for the brief period when Croke Park hosted international [association football](10568.md) during 2007–2009, the capacity was reduced to approximately 73,500, due to FIFA's statutes stating that competitive games must be played in all-seater stadiums.

## Pitch

Panorama of Croke Park.

Panorama of Croke Park.

The pitch in Croke Park is a soil pitch that replaced the [Desso GrassMaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desso_GrassMaster) pitch laid in 2002.[^cite_note-slipups-2] This replacement was made after several complaints by players and managers that the pitch was excessively hard and far too slippery.[^cite_note-slipups-2]

Since January 2006, a special growth and lighting system called the SGL Concept has been used to assist grass growing conditions, even in the winter months. The system, created by Dutch company SGL (Stadium Grow Lighting), helps in controlling and managing all pitch growth factors, such as light, temperature, CO<sub>2</sub>, water, air and nutrients.[^cite_note-24]

### Floodlighting



Croke Park floodlights in use during Six Nations Championship match

With the [2007 Six Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_Six_Nations_Championship) clash with [France](955656.md) and possibly other matches in subsequent years requiring lighting the GAA installed [floodlights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floodlights_\(sport\)) in the stadium (after planning permission was granted). Indeed, many other GAA grounds around the country have started to erect floodlights as the organisation starts to hold games in the evenings, whereas traditionally major matches were played almost exclusively on Sunday afternoons. The first game to be played under these lights at Croke Park was a [National Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Football_League_\(Ireland\)) [Division One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Football_League_\(Ireland\)#Performance_by_County) match between [Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_county_football_team) and [Tyrone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrone_county_football_team) on 3 February 2007 with Tyrone winning in front of a capacity crowd of over 81,000 – which remains a record attendance for a National League game, with Ireland's Six Nations match with France following on 11 February.[^cite_note-25] Temporary floodlights were installed for the [American Bowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Bowl) game between [Chicago Bears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Bears) and [Pittsburgh Steelers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_Steelers) on the pitch in 1997, and again for the [2003 Special Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_Special_Olympics).

## Non-Gaelic games

Further information: [List of non-Gaelic games played in Croke Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_non-Gaelic_games_played_in_Croke_Park)

There was great debate in Ireland regarding the use of Croke Park for sports other than those of the GAA. As the GAA was founded as a [nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_nationalist) organisation to maintain and promote indigenous Irish sport, it has felt honour-bound throughout its history to oppose other, foreign (in practice, British) sports. In turn, nationalist groups supported the GAA as the prime example of purely Irish sporting culture.[^cite_note-31]

Until its abolition in 1971, rule 27 of the GAA constitution stated that a member of the GAA could be banned from playing its games if found to be also playing association football, rugby or [cricket](25675557.md). That rule was abolished but [rule 42](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_42) still prohibited the use of GAA property for games with interests in conflict with the interests of the GAA. The belief was that rugby and association football were in competition with Gaelic football and hurling, and that if the GAA allowed these sports to use their ground it might be harmful to Gaelic games, while other sports, not seen as direct competitors with Gaelic football and hurling, were permitted, such as the two games of [American football](18951490.md), the [Croke Park Classic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croke_Park_Classic) [college football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_football) game between the [University of Central Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Central_Florida) and [Penn State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania_State_University) and an [American Bowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Bowl) [NFL](21211.md) preseason game between the [Chicago Bears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Bears) and the [Pittsburgh Steelers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_Steelers), on the Croke Park pitch during the 1990s.[^cite_note-32]

In June 2003, Croke Park served as the main venue for the [2003 Special Olympics World Summer Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_Special_Olympics_World_Summer_Games) with a noted highlight of the Special Olympics being [Muhammad Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ali) attending the opening ceremony.[^cite_note-O'Connor6Jun2016-33] They were the first edition of the [Special Olympics World Summer Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Olympics_World_Games) not to be held in the United States.[^cite_note-Watterson1Apr1999-34]

On 16 April 2005, a motion to temporarily relax rule No. 42 was passed at the GAA Annual Congress. The motion gives the GAA Central Council the power to authorise the renting or leasing of Croke Park for events other than those controlled by the Association, during a period when [Lansdowne Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lansdowne_Road) – the venue for international football and rugby matches – was closed for redevelopment. The final result was 227 in favour of the motion to 97 against, 11 votes more than the required two-thirds majority.

In January 2006, it was announced that the GAA had reached an agreement with the [Football Association of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_Association_of_Ireland) (FAI) and [Irish Rugby Football Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Rugby_Football_Union) (IRFU) to stage two Six Nations games and four football internationals at Croke Park in 2007 and in February 2007, use of the pitch by the FAI and the IRFU in 2008 was also agreed.[^cite_note-RTE-35] These agreements were within the temporary relaxation terms, as Lansdowne Road was still under redevelopment until 2010. Although the GAA had said that hosted use of Croke Park would not extend beyond 2008, irrespective of the redevelopment progress,[^cite_note-RTE-35] fixtures[^cite_note-36] for the [2009 Six Nations rugby tournament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Six_Nations_Championship) saw the Irish rugby team using Croke park for a third season. 11 February 2007 saw the first rugby union international to be played there. Ireland were leading France in a Six Nations clash, but lost 17–20 after conceding a last minute (converted) try. [Raphaël Ibañez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapha%C3%ABl_Iba%C3%B1ez) scored the first try in that match; [Ronan O'Gara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronan_O'Gara) scored Ireland's first-ever try at Croke Park.

A second match between Ireland and [England](691024.md) on 24 February 2007 was politically symbolic because of the events of [Bloody Sunday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloody_Sunday_\(1920\)) in 1920.[^cite_note-37] There was considerable concern as to what reaction there would be to the singing of the British [national anthem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_anthem) "[God Save the Queen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_Save_the_Queen)". Ultimately the anthem was sung without interruption or incident, and applauded by both sets of supporters at the match, which Ireland won by 43–13 (their largest-ever win over England in rugby).

On 2 March 2010, Ireland played their final international rugby match against a Scotland team that was playing to avoid the wooden spoon and hadn't won a championship match against Ireland since 2001. Outside half, Dan Parks inspired the Scots to a 3-point victory and ended Irish Hopes of a triple crown.[^cite_note-38]

On 24 March 2007, the first association football match took place at Croke Park. The [Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Ireland_national_football_team) took on [Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_national_football_team) in [UEFA Euro 2008 qualifying Group D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UEFA_Euro_2008_qualifying_Group_D), with a [Stephen Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Ireland) goal securing a [1–0 victory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UEFA_Euro_2008_qualifying_Group_D#Republic_of_Ireland_v_Wales) for the Irish in front of a crowd of 72,500. Prior to this, the [IFA Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_Association_of_Ireland#History) had been played at the then Jones' Road in 1901, but this was 12 years before the GAA took ownership.

Croke Park was included as one of the stadiums in the [Euro 2028](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_2028) bid shortlist by England, the Republic of Ireland, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, but did not make the final list.[^cite_note-BBC-39][^cite_note-TA-40]

### World record rugby union attendance

On 2 May 2009, Croke Park was the venue for a [Heineken Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heineken_Cup) rugby [semi-final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008%E2%80%9309_Heineken_Cup#Semi-finals), in which [Leinster](985961.md) defeated [Munster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munster_Rugby) 25–6. The attendance of 82,208 set a new world record attendance for a club rugby union game.[^cite_note-bbc_Leinster_25–6_Munster-46] This record stood until 31 March 2012 when it was surpassed by an [English Premiership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011%E2%80%9312_Premiership_Rugby) game between [Harlequins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlequin_F.C.) and [Saracens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saracens_F.C.) at [Wembley Stadium](8913012.md) which hosted a crowd of 83,761.[^cite_note-47] This was beaten again in 2016 in the [Top 14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015%E2%80%9316_Top_14_season) final at the [Nou Camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nou_Camp) which hosted a crowd of 99,124.

### Katie Taylor's final fight

When Irish boxing icon [Katie Taylor](12426364.md) announced she would retire in 2026, her longtime promoter of [Matchroom Boxing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matchroom_Sport) decided to stage the event at Croke Park, a venue where she had always wanted to compete. More than 80,000 tickets were sold for her farewell card in the first 17 minutes after going on sale, and the attendance on 5 September was reported to be at least 83,000. In the main event, Taylor defeated previously unbeaten [Flora Pili](83915503.md) by unanimous decision, retiring as the undisputed super lightweight champion.[^cite_note-48] This was the first boxing event at Croke Park since July 1972, when 18,725 attended a card headlined by [Muhammad Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ali) [defeating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_vs._Al_Lewis) [Alvin Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvin_Lewis_\(boxer\)).[^cite_note-49]

## Skyline tour

A walkway,[^cite_note-50] known under a sponsorship deal as [Kellogg's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kellogg's) Skyline Tour Croke Park, opened on 1 June 2012.[^cite_note-51] From 44 metres above the ground, it offers views of Dublin city and the surrounding area.[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53] The [Olympic Torch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Torch) was brought to the stadium and along the walkway on 6 June 2012.

## GAA Hall of Fame



Statue of [Michael Cusack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Cusack_\(Gaelic_Athletic_Association\)) outside the Croke Park GAA Museum

On 11 February 2013, the GAA opened the Hall of Fame section in the Croke Park Museum. The foundation of the award scheme is the Teams of the Millennium [the football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GAA_Football_Team_of_the_Millennium) which was announced in 1999 and [the hurling team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GAA_Hurling_Team_of_the_Millennium) in 2000 and all 30 players were inducted into the hall of fame along with [Limerick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limerick_GAA) hurler [Éamonn Cregan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89amonn_Cregan) and [Offaly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offaly_GAA) footballer [Tony McTague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_McTague) who were chosen by a GAA sub-committee from the years 1970–74.[^cite_note-54] New inductees will be chosen on an annual basis from the succeeding five-year intervals as well as from the years preceding 1970.[^cite_note-55] In April 2014, Kerry legend Mick O'Dwyer, Sligo footballer Micheál Kerins, along with hurlers Noel Skehan of Kilkenny and Pat McGrath of Waterford became the second group of former players to receive Hall of Fame awards.[^cite_note-56]

## National Handball Centre

Main article: [National Handball Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Handball_Centre)

The new National Handball Centre, located at the southeast corner of the stadium on Sackville Avenue,[^cite_note-irishtimes.com-57] is close to completion,[^cite_note-58] with the final minor stages of building delayed slightly due to the COVID-19 coronavirus pandemic. The new centre contains three 4-Wall [handball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_handball) courts – including a three-sided glass wall show court with amphitheatre style seating for a capacity of 500 spectators, a Softball show court with seating capacity for 200 spectators and three [1-Wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_fronton) courts[^cite_note-59] as well as offices for GAA Handball staff, a bar and cafe as well as a community centre.[^cite_note-irishtimes.com-57] The centre was used by Ireland's national health service, the [Health Service Executive](4017657.md) for [COVID-19 testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_testing_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland) during the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland).[^cite_note-60]

While the centre's official opening was delayed due to both the COVID-19 pandemic as well as the final completion of remaining building works, the centre had a 'soft' opening in December 2021, allowing registered players to book the courts through Croke Park.[^cite_note-61] The first competitive [One-wall handball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-wall_handball) match took place in the centre in December 2022 during the [European 1-Wall Tour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_1-Wall_Tour) "EliteStop" held in the Centre on Saturday and Sunday, 10–11 December 2022.[^cite_note-62] The first competitive Four-wall handball matches to be played in the new Centre took place on Sunday, 2 April 2023 from 10 am with the [GAA Handball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GAA_Handball) [O'Neills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O'Neills)-sponsored All-Ireland 4-Wall Senior Doubles semi-finals taking place in both Men's and Ladies codes.[^cite_note-63]
