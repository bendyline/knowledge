The **Charlotte Area Transit System** (**CATS**) is the agency responsible for [public transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_transportation) in the [Charlotte metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_metropolitan_area). Established in 1999, CATS operates bus and rail transit services in [Mecklenburg County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecklenburg_County,_North_Carolina) and surrounding areas. CATS is governed by the Metropolitan Transit Commission and is operated as a department of the City of Charlotte.[^cite_note-4] In 2025, the system had a ridership of 15,683,300, or about 50,700 per weekday as of the second quarter of 2026.

## History

### Origins

Prior to 1976, public transportation in [Charlotte](57447.md) was entirely privatized. Trolleys operated in the city from 1891 until 1938.[^cite_note-August16-5] Privately operated bus routes also ran in [Charlotte](57447.md) until 1976.[^cite_note-6]

In 1976, the City of Charlotte began operating bus routes under the **Charlotte Transit** brand, which operated from 1976 until CATS was founded in 2000.[^cite_note-7] (Charlotte Transit and the Charlotte Area Transit System are not to be confused despite the similarity in name.) Charlotte Transit operated almost entirely local bus routes, with the exception of two express routes. As the [Charlotte metro area's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_metropolitan_area) population grew rapidly, the bus service operated by Charlotte Transit proved to be inadequate. In 1998, a [Mecklenburg County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecklenburg_County,_North_Carolina) referendum was approved by citizens that enacted a 0.5% sales tax increase to improve public transportation in the area. The Metropolitan Transit Commission (MTC) was created as a result of the vote, leading to the consolidation of Charlotte Transit and the MTC in 2000 as the Charlotte Area Transit System, creating CATS as it is today.[^cite_note-8]

### 2000–2006: early years

After the founding of CATS, more express routes were added to the edges of Mecklenburg County and local bus service was expanded, especially to the fast-growing southern areas of Charlotte. Some commuter/express routes were also initiated outside [Mecklenburg County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecklenburg_County,_North_Carolina). CATS also expanded bus infrastructure throughout the area during this period.[^cite_note-9] In 2005, CATS built three community transit bus centers to supplement the central Charlotte Transportation Center in [Uptown Charlotte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uptown_Charlotte). The centers were built in SouthPark (inside the SouthPark Mall parking facility), Eastland (at the old Eastland mall), and at Rosa Parks Place in the Wilson Heights neighborhood north of [Uptown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uptown_Charlotte).[^cite_note-10] As a result of CATS' expansion during its early years, transit ridership in the [Charlotte metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_metropolitan_area) increased 55 percent,[^cite_note-11] largely due to the expansion of express bus services.[^cite_note-12]

### 2006–2015: the advent of rail

CATS chief Ron Tober began the planning process for what would become the [Lynx Blue Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynx_Blue_Line) in the early 2000s.[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14] Under Tober's leadership, the MTC adopted the 2030 Transit Corridor System Plan in 2006.[^cite_note-auto-15] The initial plan called for the "Red Line" commuter rail with service to Northern [Mecklenburg County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecklenburg_County,_North_Carolina), the Blue Line, the [CityLynx Gold Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CityLynx_Gold_Line) streetcar, and a busway along the route of what is now planned to be the [Lynx Silver Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynx_Silver_Line). In 2007, the initial portion of the Blue Line opened, connecting the [Uptown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uptown_Charlotte), [South End](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_End_\(Charlotte_neighborhood\)), Scaleybark, Tyvola, and Arrowood neighborhoods.[^cite_note-auto-15] The initial phase of the Blue Line spurred an explosion of development along the rail corridor, particularly in [South End](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_End_\(Charlotte_neighborhood\)).[^cite_note-16] Shortly after the Blue Line opened, CEO Ron Tober, who had led CATS since its inception, retired.[^cite_note-17]

After Tober's departure, CATS hired Carolyn Flowers, who had previously headed the bus system in [Los Angeles County](79734.md).[^cite_note-18] During Flowers' tenure, the transit system pushed ahead with the Gold Line and Blue Line extension projects in the wake of the [Great Recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession). In 2014, Flowers resigned to take a [Federal Transit Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Transit_Administration) position.[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20] John M. Lewis Jr. was then hired as the new head of the transit system.[^cite_note-21]

After the success of the Blue Line, CATS reversed course on its initial plans to expand bus service to Matthews and the airport, instead planning for a second light rail line, the Lynx Silver Line, that would travel southeast to Matthews and west to [Charlotte Douglas International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_Douglas_International_Airport) and across the [Catawba River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catawba_River) to [Belmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belmont,_North_Carolina).[^cite_note-22]

### 2015–present: further expansion

In 2015, the first phase of the [CityLynx Gold Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CityLynx_Gold_Line) [streetcar](30733.md) opened.[^cite_note-23] The initial line connected the [Charlotte Transportation Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_Transportation_Center) in Uptown to Hawthorne Lane and 5th Street, through the [First Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Ward,_Charlotte) and Cherry neighborhoods. The first phase of the Gold Line utilized retro trolley streetcars, while future phases will incorporate [Siemens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens) S70 streetcar vehicles.[^cite_note-CityLYNX_Gold_Line_Phase_2-24] In 2018, the Blue Line extension opened, connecting the existing Blue Line to the [University of North Carolina at Charlotte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_North_Carolina_at_Charlotte), connecting the [University City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_City_\(Charlotte_neighborhood\)), [NoDa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NoDa), Optimist Park, [Plaza Midwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_Midwood), and Belmont neighborhoods.[^cite_note-25] on June 30 2022, the gold line was completed, this has extend the existing line to Sunnyside Avenue in [Plaza Midwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_Midwood) and French Street by [Johnson C. Smith University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnson_C._Smith_University). In September 2024, the city of Charlotte voted in approval of the purchase of the rail corridor for the Red Line from Norfolk Southern and a sales tax increase to support operations.[^cite_note-26] The Red Line project, an integral piece of the 2030 System Plan, has run into significant headwinds due to disagreements with [Norfolk Southern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norfolk_Southern), which owns the tracks the Red Line was planned to operate on, and funding concerns.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28]

CATS is currently in the planning and design stages for the [Lynx Silver Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynx_Silver_Line), a multibillion-dollar east–west [light rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_rail) line that will run from [Matthews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthews,_North_Carolina) through [Uptown Charlotte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uptown_Charlotte) and west to [Charlotte Douglas International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_Douglas_International_Airport) and across the [Catawba River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catawba_River) to [Belmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belmont,_North_Carolina).[^cite_note-29] The line, projected to open in 2030, will connect to the Blue Line at 11th Street and the future intermodal [Charlotte Gateway Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_Gateway_Station).[^cite_note-30]

## Bus

Main article: [List of Charlotte Area Transit System bus routes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Charlotte_Area_Transit_System_bus_routes)

CATS bus service primarily serves [Mecklenburg County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecklenburg_County,_North_Carolina), with service in [Charlotte](57447.md), [Davidson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davidson,_North_Carolina), [Huntersville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huntersville,_North_Carolina), [Cornelius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelius,_North_Carolina), [Matthews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthews,_North_Carolina), [Pineville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pineville,_North_Carolina), and [Mint Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mint_Hill,_North_Carolina). Limited local and express service operated by CATS also extends to [Iredell County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iredell_County), [Gaston County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaston_County), and [Union County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_County,_North_Carolina).[^cite_note-auto1-31]

CATS operates local routes within the [City of Charlotte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Charlotte) and [Mecklenburg County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecklenburg_County,_North_Carolina), with the majority of those multiple-stop routes serving the [Charlotte Transportation Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_Transportation_Center_/_Arena_\(LYNX_station\)) in [Uptown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uptown_Charlotte). Other routes that do not serve Uptown mainly connect directly between Lynx rail stations and outlying neighborhoods.[^cite_note-auto1-31] Three community transit centers in different parts of the city were built in the mid-2000s: the Eastland Community Transit Center in East Charlotte located near the now-closed [Eastland Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastland_Mall_\(Charlotte,_North_Carolina\)), the SouthPark Community Transit Center in South Charlotte located under [SouthPark Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SouthPark_Mall_\(Charlotte,_North_Carolina\)), and the [Rosa Parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosa_Parks) Place Community Transit Center in North Charlotte located near [Johnson C. Smith University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnson_C._Smith_University).[^cite_note-32]

Express buses in the CATS system serve [Union County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_County,_North_Carolina), far northern [Mecklenburg County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecklenburg_County,_North_Carolina), the [Lake Norman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Norman) area, and [Gastonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastonia,_North_Carolina).[^cite_note-auto1-31]

CATS operates one [bus rapid transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus_rapid_transit) line, the Sprinter service from the CTC to [Charlotte Douglas International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_Douglas_International_Airport). This center city to airport link will be replaced by the Lynx Silver Line upon its completion.

CATS also operates the Special Transportation Service (STS), a [paratransit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paratransit) service which provides transportation to people with disabilities certified as eligible based on the Americans with Disabilities Act guidelines. STS provides service during the same times and in the same locations as the fixed route bus service.[^cite_note-33]

During a typical week, CATS buses carry about 190,000 riders.[^cite_note-34]

## Rail

CATS operates two rail lines under the "Lynx" (stylized as "LYNX") system umbrella.[^cite_note-37] Announced on February 22, 2006, the name fits in with the city's cat theme (the [NFL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NFL) team is the [Carolina Panthers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolina_Panthers) and the [NBA](22093.md) team was known as the [Charlotte Bobcats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_Hornets) when the name was chosen); also, "Lynx" is a [homophone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homophone) of "links", and was mainly chosen because the light rail is about "connectivity." The color scheme of the rail cars is silver, with black and blue accents and gold around the "Lynx" logo to tie in the history of the Charlotte region being home to the first major U.S. [Gold Rush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_Rush).

CATS rail service first began on June 28, 2004 with the [Charlotte Trolley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_Trolley), operating three Birney-style replica streetcars between [Atherton Mill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atherton_Mill_station) and [9th Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/9th_Street_station_\(Charlotte\)). In 2006, the trolley service was suspended to allow the line's conversion to light-rail. Resumed on a limited schedule in 2008, the [heritage streetcar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heritage_streetcar) line was discontinued two years later on June 28, 2010.

### Lynx Blue Line

Main article: [Lynx Blue Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynx_Blue_Line)

The [Lynx Blue Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynx_Blue_Line) is a 19.3-mile (31.1 km) [light rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_rail) in [Charlotte](57447.md), [North Carolina](21650.md). Opened on November 24, 2007 and hailed as the first major rapid rail service of any kind in North Carolina, the line has 15 stations and ran 9.6 miles (15.4 km) between [I-485/South Boulevard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I-485/South_Boulevard_station), near [Pineville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pineville,_North_Carolina), and [7th Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7th_Street_station_\(Charlotte\)), in [Uptown Charlotte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uptown_Charlotte); the line was partly shared with the Charlotte Trolley from 2008–2010.[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40] On March 16, 2018, a 9.7-mile (15.6 km) extension was opened that added 11 stations north from 7th Street to [UNC Charlotte–Main](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNC_Charlotte%E2%80%93Main_station), at the [University of North Carolina at Charlotte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_North_Carolina_at_Charlotte)[^cite_note-41] The line uses the [Siemens S70](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens_S700_and_S70) as rolling stock, which connects its 26 stations.[^cite_note-42]

### CityLynx Gold Line

Main article: [CityLynx Gold Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CityLynx_Gold_Line)

The [CityLynx Gold Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CityLynx_Gold_Line) is a 4-mile modern [streetcar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streetcar) route that runs from [ French Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Street_station) to [Sunnyside Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunnyside_Avenue_station) through central Charlotte. When the final phase is completed the line will run from Rosa Parks Place Community Transit Center through [Uptown Charlotte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uptown_Charlotte) and down Central Avenue, terminating at Eastland Community Transit Center. A Federal Urban Circulator Grant was awarded in July 2010, allowing construction of phase 1.[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44]

The initial 1.5-mile (2.4 km) segment of the line commenced service on July 14, 2015.[^cite_note-July142015-45] This section runs from the [Charlotte Transportation Center / Arena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_Transportation_Center_/_Arena_\(LYNX_station\)) station to Hawthorne Lane & 5th Street. The initial phase utilized replica [Birney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birney) trolley cars. After several delays, the second 2.5-mile (4.0 km) segment opened on August 30, 2021,[^cite_note-Aug262021-46] extending service to French Street in Biddleville and Sunnyside Avenue. The trolley cars were then replaced with [Siemens S700](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens_S700) streetcar vehicles.[^cite_note-CityLYNX_Gold_Line_Phase_2-24] Phase 3, which would extend service north along Beatties Ford Road to Rosa Parks Place Community Transit Center and east along Central Avenue to Eastland Community Transit Center, is still in the planning stages.[^cite_note-47]

#### Siemens S70

The vehicles are 93.6 ft (28.5 m) long and 8.7 ft (2.65 m) wide, with each having an empty weight of 99,500 lb (45,100 kilograms).[^cite_note-Charlotte_S70-51] Each vehicle contains 68 seats and has a maximum capacity of 230 passengers complete with four wheelchair spaces and four bike racks. Maximum operational speed is 66 miles per hour (106 km/h), with a top speed of 71.5 miles per hour (115.1 km/h);[^cite_note-Charlotte_S70-51] power comes from a 750-volt [overhead wire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhead_wire).[^cite_note-Light_Rail_Vehicles-52][^cite_note-53] When not in use, the vehicles are stored at the South Boulevard Light Rail Facility, located along South Boulevard, between the [New Bern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Bern_\(LYNX_station\)) and [Scaleybark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaleybark_\(LYNX_station\)) stations in the [Sedgefield neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedgefield_\(neighborhood\)). The facility is approximately 92,000 square feet (8,547.1 m<sup>2</sup>), and houses the Lynx rail maintenance staff, operations staff and the Rail Operations Control Center. Officially dedicated on June 23, 2007, the facility contains 2.5 miles (4.0 km) of track and 5,200 ties.[^cite_note-LightRailFacility-54]

In January 2004, CATS began the process of accepting bids for construction of the system's vehicles. Original estimates for the vehicles was \$3.5 million per car with the firms [Bombardier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombardier_Transportation), [Siemens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens_Mobility) and [Kinki Sharyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinki_Sharyo) bidding for the final contract. The \$52 million contract was awarded to Siemens on February 25, 2004.[^cite_note-February_26,_2004-55] The original order of 16 [S70 Avanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens_S70) vehicles, similar to those in operation on the [METRORail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/METRORail) system in [Houston](13774.md), [Texas](29810.md),[^cite_note-Light_Rail_Vehicles-52] was delivered between 2006 and 2007; these cars are numbered 101–116. Car 101 arrived via flatbed truck on Friday, June 23, 2006, from the Siemens facility in [Florin, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florin,_California).[^cite_note-June24-56] Testing of the vehicles began in August 2006 along a 1.3-mile (2.1 km) stretch of track between Tremont Avenue and the light rail maintenance facility off South Boulevard. During the testing phase, each car logged 1,000 miles (1,600 km) to test the acceleration, braking and overall performance for each vehicle.[^cite_note-June24-56]

With an option in place to purchase up to an additional 25 vehicles, and better than expected ridership, in May 2008 CATS announced the purchase of four additional S70 vehicles to expand the fleet's capacity beyond the existing 16 vehicles.[^cite_note-January_20,_2010-57] The vehicles cost \$3.8 million each and were delivered by Siemens between January and March 2010.[^cite_note-January_20,_2010-57] In 2012, after only four years of operation, the original 16 vehicles underwent significant maintenance at the Siemens facility in California for an estimated cost of \$400,000 each, having run by then for nearly 300,000 miles (480,000 km).[^cite_note-repairs-58]

In January 2014, CATS announced it would buy 22 more Siemens S70 light-rail vehicles for the Blue Line Extension at a cost of \$96.2 million. This purchase allowed CATS to have more three-car trains on the existing Blue Line.[^cite_note-January_13,_2014-59] The first car was delivered in late October 2014,[^cite_note-taut-2015jan-60] and the last in spring 2017. After delivery of the 22 additional S70 cars, the fleet had a total of 42 light rail vehicles. The first series is numbered 101–116, the second series 201–204 (originally 117–120; renumbered 2018), and the third series 301–322 (originally 121–142).[^cite_note-taut-2020nov-48] From late 2021 to at least 2025, the original (Series 1) vehicles will undergo extensive mid-life overhauls in Sacramento at a total cost of \$30–50 million. Vehicles will be sent to California two at a time; overhauling each one will take approximately six to nine months.[^cite_note-61]

#### Siemens S700

Six [Siemens S700](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens_S700)[^cite_note-retroactive-rebrand-49] low-floor streetcars service the Gold line.[^cite_note-nov_2016_ra-62] They were delivered between August 2019 and April 2020 and are numbered 401–406.[^cite_note-taut-2020nov-48] The vehicles have 56 passenger seats, with a total capacity of 255 passengers, and have four wheelchair spaces and two bicycle racks.[^cite_note-Phase2_FAQ-63][^cite_note-Charlotte_S700-64] They are 85.25 ft (26 m) long by 8.67 ft (2.64 m) wide, with each weighing 101,081 lb (45,850 kilograms).[^cite_note-Phase2_FAQ-63][^cite_note-Charlotte_S700-64] Maximum speed is 35 miles per hour (56 km/h), with an operational speed of 25 miles per hour (40 km/h).[^cite_note-65][^cite_note-Charlotte_S700-64] Their design and color schemes are similar to the vehicles used on the [Blue Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynx_Blue_Line#Rolling_stock).[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-Phase2_FAQ-63] The streetcars are equipped with a hybrid wireless onboard energy storage system (OESS); they draw power from 750V DC [overhead wires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhead_line) along most of the line except between [Mint Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mint_Street_station) and the Charlotte Transportation Center, where they run on battery power.[^cite_note-Phase2_FAQ-63][^cite_note-hybrid-67] The batteries are recharged when the vehicles resume running on-wire.[^cite_note-hybrid-67]

Birney-style replica streetcar

Delivered in the fall of 2004, the three [heritage streetcars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heritage_streetcar), from [Gomaco Trolley Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gomaco_Trolley_Company), were replacements of three non-owned historic streetcars that initially started the Charlotte Trolley in 1988. The streetcars operated along the, [grade-separated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grade_separation), Charlotte Trolley from 2004 to 2006 and then on a limited schedule from 2008 to 2010, when the Charlotte Trolley ceased operations. The streetcars were then put back into service during the first phase of the CityLynx Gold Line; from 2015 to 2019, they operated along 1.5-mile (2.4 km) line along Trade Street and Hawthorne Lane.[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69] In 2020, the replica trolleys were retired and sold to the [Memphis Area Transit Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memphis_Area_Transit_Authority) (MATA) for \$550,000.[^cite_note-70][^cite_note-expansion-71][^cite_note-Phase2_FAQ-63]

Built in [Ida Grove, Iowa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ida_Grove,_Iowa), they were 48.9 ft (14.9 m) long, 10 ft (3.0 m) wide and 12.9 ft (3.9 m) tall, with a weight of 48,000 lb (22,000 kg), and a [crush load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crush_load) of 105 people (48 sitting and 57 standing). The exterior is painted green and yellow, while the interior was wood, including oak, cherry, birch, and plywood. Each streetcar had an [accessible-compliant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americans_with_Disabilities_Act_of_1990) integrated [wheelchair lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheelchair_lift) and [air conditioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_conditioning). Running equipment was a 30HP General Electric traction motor, utilizing 650 [V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volt) [DC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_current) via [overhead lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhead_line). The streetcars had a top speed of 30 mph (48 km/h).[^cite_note-72]

## Future service

A blue and gray train stopped at a covered, side platformed station with several passengers entering.

Boarding a southbound train at [Brooklyn Village station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn_Village_station)

Future expansion includes plans for [light rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_rail), [streetcars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streetcars) and [bus rapid transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus_rapid_transit) along the corridors in the 2030 Transit Corridor System Plan adopted in 2006 by Metropolitan Transit Commission (MTC).[^cite_note-2030Transit-76] On May 6, 2013, a 30-member transit funding task force released a draft report in which they estimated it would cost \$3.3 billion to build the remaining transit corridors, and \$1.7 billion to operate and maintain the lines through 2024. To fund the build-out by sales taxes alone would require a 0.78 cent increase in the sales tax, which would need to be approved by the state General Assembly. The committee recommended any sales tax increase be limited to 0.5 cent and other methods used to raise funds; some suggested methods included:

- Using the federal Transportation Infrastructure Finance and Innovation Act (TFIA) to quickly begin construction. TFIA loans could pay for 33% of the streetcar project and 30% of the rapid transit line along Independence Boulevard. It could allow CATS to begin collecting new property taxes from projects built along the rail line, which could be used to pay off the loans.

- Expanding advertising on train cars and buses, possibly selling naming rights and sponsorships.

- Entering into partnership with a private company to help finance part of the project.

  [^cite_note-May62013-77]

Although build-out of the entire system has been estimated for completion by 2030,[^cite_note-Report-78] by July 2015, the Charlotte Area Transit System reported it lacked the funds to support any future transit projects apart from the already budgeted 2.5-mile long Phase 2 segment of the CityLYNX Gold Line.[^cite_note-July142015-2-79]

CATS has entered into a partnership with [Duke Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_Energy) to pilot the use of [electric buses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battery_electric_bus). 18 BEBs from three manufacturers will be used over 18 months.[^cite_note-80]

### Proposed

#### Silver Line

Main article: [Lynx Silver Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynx_Silver_Line)

The Lynx Silver Line is a proposed 29-mile (47 km) east-west [light rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_rail) line that would connect the outlying cities and towns of [Belmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belmont,_North_Carolina), [Matthews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthews,_North_Carolina), [Stallings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stallings,_North_Carolina) and [Indian Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Trail,_North_Carolina) to [Uptown Charlotte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uptown_Charlotte) and the [Charlotte Douglas International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_Douglas_International_Airport) using its 30 stations.[^cite_note-81][^cite_note-82] Originally setup as two separate projects known as the Southeast Corridor and West Corridor, they were merged in 2019 by the Metropolitan Transit Commission.[^cite_note-83] Tentative opening date in 2037.[^cite_note-84]

#### Red Line

Main article: [Lynx Red Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynx_Red_Line)

The Lynx Red Line is a proposed 25-mile (40 km) [commuter rail](1395672.md) service routed along the existing [Norfolk Southern O-Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norfolk_Southern_O-Line) right-of-way, connecting the towns in northern [Mecklenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecklenburg_County,_North_Carolina) and southern [Iredell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iredell_County,_North_Carolina) counties to [Uptown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uptown_Charlotte) [Charlotte](57447.md), [North Carolina](21650.md). However, several issues had arisen that included ineligibility for federal funding and Norfolk Southern refusal to allow access to its right-of-way. In 2019, after a reevaluation of the entire corridor, the CATS decided to move forward with a [bus rapid transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus_rapid_transit) (BRT) service instead, the I-77 Bus Rapid Transit, and shelve the commuter rail service, which was met with frustration by various city leaders and residents impacted by it.[^cite_note-85] However, in June 2024, it was announced that the City of Charlotte plans to purchase a section of the O-Line between Charlotte and Mooresville, NC for commuter rail service, thus placing the commuter rail option for the Red Line back into consideration. But a few weeks after the letter of understanding was made, local lawmakers passed a law stating that the city of Charlotte cannot buy land outside Mecklenburg County without the approval of each county and municipality with the railway that is being acquired, which could possibly limit potential expansion outside of Davidson.[^cite_note-86]

#### Microtransit

CATS is currently working to launch microtransit in 2024, covering the University and North End zones, as well as a zone including Charlotte, Cornelius, Davidson, and Huntersville.[^cite_note-87][^cite_note-\:0-88] This is proposed to replace the [North Meck Village Rider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Meck_Village_Rider?action=edit\&redlink=1) service covering the latter three cities.[^cite_note-\:0-88] A pilot program was deployed in 2022.[^cite_note-\:0-88]
