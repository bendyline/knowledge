# Lunar craters

"Lunar crater" redirects here. For the volcanic crater in Nevada, see [Lunar Crater volcanic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Crater_volcanic_field).



The crater [Webb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Webb_\(crater\)), as seen from [Lunar Orbiter 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Orbiter_1). Several smaller craters can be seen in and around Webb.



Side view of the crater [Moltke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moltke_\(crater\)) taken from [Apollo 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_10).

**Lunar craters** are [impact craters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_crater) on [Earth](9228.md)'s [Moon](19331.md). The Moon's surface has many craters, all of which were formed by impacts. The [International Astronomical Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Astronomical_Union) currently recognizes 9,137 craters, of which 1,675 have been dated.[^cite_note-yang-1]

## History

The word *crater* was adopted from the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) word for "vessel" (Κρατήρ, a Greek vessel used to mix wine and water). [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo) built his [first telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refracting_telescope) in late 1609, and turned it to the Moon for the first time on November 30, 1609. He discovered that, contrary to general opinion at that time, the Moon was not a perfect sphere, but had both mountains and cup-like depressions. These were named craters by [Johann Hieronymus Schröter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Hieronymus_Schr%C3%B6ter) (1791), extending its previous use with [volcanoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcano).

[Robert Hooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hooke) in *[Micrographia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micrographia)* (1665) proposed two hypotheses for lunar crater formation: one, that the craters were caused by projectile bombardment from space, the other, that they were the products of subterranean lunar [volcanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanism).[^cite_note-2]

[Scientific opinion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_opinion) as to the origin of craters swung back and forth over the ensuing centuries. The competing theories were:

1. [volcanic eruptions](8921015.md) blasting holes in the Moon

2. [meteoric impact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteoric_impact)

3. a theory known as the [Welteislehre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welteislehre) developed in Germany between the two [world wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wars) which suggested [glacial motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glacial_motion) creating the craters.

[Grove Karl Gilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grove_Karl_Gilbert) suggested in 1893 that the Moon's craters were formed by large asteroid impacts. [Ralph Baldwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Baldwin) in 1949 wrote that the Moon's craters were mostly of impact origin. Around 1960, [Gene Shoemaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_Shoemaker) revived the idea. According to [David H. Levy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_H._Levy), Shoemaker "saw the craters on the Moon as logical impact sites that were formed not gradually, in [eons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologic_time_scale), but explosively, in seconds."[^cite_note-Levy-3]

Lunar craters as captured through the backyard telescope of an amateur astronomer, partially illuminated by the sun on a waning crescent moon.

Lunar craters as captured through the backyard telescope of an amateur astronomer, partially illuminated by the sun on a waning crescent moon.

Evidence collected during the [Apollo Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_Project) and from uncrewed spacecraft of the same period proved conclusively that meteoric impact, or impact by [asteroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroids) for larger craters, was the origin of almost all lunar craters, and by implication, most craters on other bodies as well.

The formation of new craters is studied in the lunar impact monitoring program at [NASA](18426568.md).[^cite_note-4] The biggest recorded crater was caused by an impact recorded on March 17, 2013.[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6] Visible to the [naked eye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naked_eye), the impact is believed to be from an approximately 40 kg (88 lb) meteoroid striking the surface at a speed of 90,000 km/h (56,000 mph; 16 mi/s).

In March 2018, the discovery of around 7,000 formerly unidentified lunar craters via [convolutional neural network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convolutional_neural_network) developed at the [University of Toronto Scarborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto_Scarborough), Canada was announced.[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8] A similar study in December 2020 identified around 109,000 new craters using a [deep neural network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_neural_network).[^cite_note-yang-1]

## Characteristics

Because of the Moon's lack of [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_water), [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_the_Moon), and [tectonic plates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tectonic_plates), there is little erosion, and craters are found that exceed two billion years in age. The age of large craters is determined by the number of smaller craters contained within it, older craters generally accumulating more small, contained craters.



The lunar crater [Eratosthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eratosthenes_\(crater\)) (center left) as imaged from Earth by amateur astronomer Joel Frohlich using an 8-inch [Schmidt–Cassegrain telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schmidt%E2%80%93Cassegrain_telescope).

The smallest craters found have been microscopic in size, found in rocks returned to Earth from the Moon. The largest crater called such is about 290 km (180 mi) across in diameter, located near the [lunar south pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_south_pole). However, it is believed that many of the [lunar maria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_mare) were formed by giant impacts, with the resulting depression filled by upwelling [lava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava).

Craters typically will have some or all of the following features:

- a surrounding area with materials splashed out of the ground when the crater was formed; this is typically lighter in shade than older materials due to exposure to [solar radiation](27979.md) for a lesser time

- raised rim, consisting of materials ejected but landing very close by

- crater wall, the downward-sloping portion of the crater

- crater floor, a more or less smooth, flat area, which as it ages accumulates small craters of its own

- central peak, found only in some craters with a diameter exceeding 26 km (16 mi); this is generally a splash effect caused by the kinetic energy of the impacting object being turned to heat and melting some lunar material.

## Statistics

There are at least 1.3 million craters larger than 1 km (0.62 mi) in diameter; of these, 83,000 are greater than 5 km (3 mi) in diameter, and 6,972 are greater than 20 km (12 mi) in diameter.[^cite_note-9] Smaller craters than this are being regularly formed, with a recent [NELIOTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NELIOTA#Results) survey covering 283.5 hours of observation time discovering that at least 192 new craters of a size of 1.5 to 3 meters (4.9 to 9.8 ft) were created during the observation period.[^cite_note-10]

## Lunar crater categorization

In 1978, [Chuck Wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Wood_\(astronomer\)) and [Leif Andersson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leif_Erland_Andersson) of the Lunar & Planetary Lab devised a system of categorization of lunar impact craters.[^cite_note-Wood_1978_fresh-11] They sampled craters that were relatively unmodified by subsequent impacts, then grouped the results into five broad categories. These successfully accounted for about 99% of all lunar impact craters.

The LPC Crater Types were as follows:

- *ALC* — small, cup-shaped craters with a diameter of about 10 km (6 mi) or less, and no central floor. The [archetype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archetype) for this category is [Albategnius C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albategnius_\(crater\)).

- *BIO* — similar to an ALC, but with small, flat floors. Typical diameter is about 15 km (9 mi). The lunar crater archetype is [Biot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biot_\(crater\)).

- *SOS* — the interior floor is wide and flat, with no central peak. The inner walls are not [terraced](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/terrace). The diameter is normally in the range of 15–25 km (9.3–16 mi). The archetype is [Sosigenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sosigenes_\(crater\)).

- *TRI* — these complex craters are large enough so that their inner walls have slumped to the floor. They can range in size from 15–50 km (9.3–31 mi) in diameter. The archetype crater is [Triesnecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triesnecker_\(crater\)).

- *TYC* — these are larger than 50 km, with terraced inner walls and relatively flat floors. They frequently have large central peak formations. [Tycho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tycho_\(crater\)) is the archetype for this class.

Beyond a couple of hundred kilometers in diameter, the central peak of the TYC class disappear and they are classed as [basins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_crater). Large craters, similar in size to maria, but without (or with a small amount of) dark lava filling, are sometimes called thalassoids.[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-glossary_2005-14][^cite_note-Mosher_1970-15]

Beginning in 2009 [Nadine G. Barlow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nadine_G._Barlow) of [Northern Arizona University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Arizona_University), the U.S. began to convert the Wood and Andersson lunar impact-crater database into digital format.[^cite_note-16] Barlow is also creating a new lunar impact crater database similar to Wood and Andersson's, except hers will include all impact craters greater than or equal to five kilometers in diameter and is based on the *[Clementine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clementine_\(spacecraft\))* spacecraft's images of the lunar surface.

The Moon Zoo project within the [Zooniverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zooniverse_\(citizen_science_project\)) program aimed to use [citizen scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizen_scientist) to map the size and shape of as many craters as possible using data from the NASA [Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter](15970718.md). However, it has since been retired.[^cite_note-17]

## Names

Craters constitute 95% of all [named](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_nomenclature) lunar features.[^cite_note-gazetteer-18] Usually they are named after deceased scientists and other explorers.[^cite_note-gazetteer_categories-19] This tradition comes from [Giovanni Battista Riccioli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Battista_Riccioli), who started it in 1651.[^cite_note-Greeley_1990-20][^cite_note-21] Since 1919, assignment of these names is regulated by the [International Astronomical Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Astronomical_Union).[^cite_note-Greeley_1990-20]

Small craters of special interest (for example, visited by lunar missions) receive human first names (Robert, José, Louise etc.). One of the biggest lunar craters, [Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_\(crater\)), is named after [Apollo missions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_program). Many smaller craters inside and near it bear the names of deceased American astronauts, and many craters inside and near [Mare Moscoviense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mare_Moscoviense) bear the names of deceased Soviet cosmonauts.[^cite_note-gazetteer-18][^cite_note-gazetteer_categories-19] Besides this, in 1970 twelve craters were named after twelve living astronauts (6 Soviet and 6 American).[^cite_note-gazetteer-18]

The majority of named lunar craters are *satellite craters*: their names consist of the name of a nearby named crater and a capital letter (for example, [Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicus_\(lunar_crater\)) A, Copernicus B, Copernicus C and so on).[^cite_note-gazetteer-18]

Lunar [crater chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crater_chain) are usually named after a nearby crater. Their Latin names contain the word *Catena* ("chain"). For example, *[Catena Davy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catena_Davy)* is situated near the crater [Davy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davy_\(crater\)).[^cite_note-gazetteer-18][^cite_note-gazetteer_terms-22]
