The **Children's Online Privacy Protection Act of 1998** (**COPPA**) is a [United States federal law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_federal_law). The act, effective April 21, 2000, applies to the online collection of personal information by persons or entities under [U.S. jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_jurisdiction_\(United_States\)) about [children](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children) under 13 years of age, including children outside the U.S. if the website or service is U.S.-based.[^cite_note-FTC_COPPA_FAQ_USbased-1] It details what a [website](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Website) operator must include in a [privacy policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privacy_policy), when and how to seek verifiable consent from a [parent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parent) or [guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_guardian), and what responsibilities an operator has to protect children's privacy and safety online, including restrictions on the marketing of those under 13.[^cite_note-FTC_COPPA_FAQ-2]

Although children under 13 can legally give out personal information with their parents' permission, many websites, particularly [social media](5897742.md) sites, but also other sites that collect most personal info, disallow children under 13 from using their services altogether due to the cost and work involved in complying with the law.[^cite_note-CSMWhatAge-3][^cite_note-BiltonLetting15-4][^cite_note-RochmanShould11-5]

## Background

In the 1990s, [electronic commerce](9611.md) was on its rise of popularity, but various concerns were expressed about the [data collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_collection) practices and the impact of Internet commerce on user privacy—especially for children under 13, because very few websites had their own privacy policies.[^cite_note-MagidNewLaw00-6] The Center for Media Education petitioned the [Federal Trade Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Trade_Commission) (FTC) to investigate the data collection and use practices of the [KidsCom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KidsCom) website, and take legal action since the data practices violated Section 5 of FTC Act concerning "unfair/deceptive practices." With the passing of the Drivers Privacy Protection Act in 1997, new precedents had been set in regard to the ability of congress to regulate information held by state agencies.[^cite_note-7] After the FTC completed its investigation, it issued the "KidsCom Letter". The report stated that the data collection and use practices were indeed subject to legal action.[^cite_note-WarmundCOPPA01-8][^cite_note-FTCStaff97-9] This resulted in the need to inform parents about the risks of children's online privacy, as well as to parental consent necessity. This ultimately resulted in the drafting of COPPA.

COPPA was passed in 1998 and took effect reportedly in April 2000. The rule was issued by the Federal Trade Commission, and it is updated quite frequently to stay up to date with new technological advancements. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) is an agency that works to protect people from illegal practices, scammers, create protection rules, and ensure they are helping people protect their data and information. COPPA was necessary because it has been reported that 89% of children's websites were taking personal information, and many of those websites were not giving privacy notices. Parents were not very involved with their children's website uses or made aware of the dangers of their children's private information being taken without knowledge.[^cite_note-FTC19982-10][^cite_note-FTCKidsPrivacy-11][^cite_note-EPICPrivacy-12][^cite_note-FTCWhatItDoes-13]

The new millennium ushered in an era of regulation that many were simply unaware of. The early years of the transition were fraught with confusion and a lot of animosity. One of the main concerns of the time was the eventual accessibility of child-based websites at the fear many were unwilling to change their business practices.[^cite_note-14] Many were left with a series of loose guidelines that determined what was correct.[^cite_note-15] The simplification of COPPA provided by the FTC was met with a follow-up of demands to law enforcement that the: "... Commission should continue law enforcement efforts by targeting significant violations and seeking increasingly larger civil penalties, when appropriate, to deter unlawful conduct".[^cite_note-16] A mandatory review of the COPPA regulations were conducted in 2005 (resulting with no changes to the original guidelines), found that there were no adverse effects to the online landscape.

The [Federal Trade Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Trade_Commission) (FTC) has the authority to issue regulations and enforce COPPA. Also, under the terms of COPPA, the FTC-designated "safe harbor" provisioning is designed to encourage increased industrial self-regulation. Under this provision, industry groups and others may request Commission approval of self-regulatory guidelines to govern participants' compliance, such that website operators in Commission-approved programs would first be subject to the disciplinary procedures of the safe harbor program in lieu of FTC enforcement. As of June 2016, the FTC has approved seven safe harbor programs operated by [TrustArc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TRUSTe), [ESRB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entertainment_Software_Rating_Board), [CARU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CARU), [PRIVO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PRIVO?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Aristotle, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle,_Inc.), Samet Privacy (kidSAFE), and the Internet Keep Safe Coalition (iKeepSafe).[^cite_note-ThomasFTCAug14-17][^cite_note-ThomasFTCFeb14-18] In August 2021, [Aristotle, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle,_Inc.) withdrew from the safe harbor program after FTC staff expressed serious concerns about its enforcement of its safe harbor provisions and communicated their intent to recommend the revocation of Aristotle's approval to run a safe harbor program. The FTC also announced its intention to more closely scrutinize the practices of the other six present safe harbors.[^cite_note-FTCAug4-19]

In September 2011, the FTC announced proposed revisions to the COPPA rules, the first significant changes to the act since the issuance of the rules in 2000. The proposed rule changes expanded the definition of what it meant to "collect" data from children. The proposed rules presented a [data retention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_retention) and deletion requirement, which mandated that data obtained from children be retained only for the amount of time necessary to achieve the purpose that it was collected for. It also added the requirement that operators ensure that any third parties to whom a child's information is disclosed have reasonable procedures in place to protect the information.[^cite_note-KardellFTC11-20]

In 2013, the COPPA rules were updated due to the increase in children's use of the internet and mobile devices. The 2013 revision expanded the types of online services and categories of personal information covered by the rules, and reinforced that verifiable parental consent was required for collecting personal information on child-directed platforms. The FTC reviews rules and regulations every ten years, but due to the constant evolution of technology, it opened for review again in 2019.[^cite_note-21]

The act applies to websites and online services operated for commercial purposes that are either directed toward children under 13 or have actual knowledge that children under 13 are providing information online. Most recognized non-profit organizations are exempt from most of the requirements of COPPA.[^cite_note-FTC_COPPA_FAQ-2] However, the [Supreme Court](31737.md) ruled that non-profits operated for the benefit of their members' commercial activities are subject to FTC regulation and consequently COPPA as well.[^cite_note-22] The type of "verifiable parental consent" that is required before collecting and using information provided by children under 13 is based upon a "sliding scale" set forth in a [Federal Trade Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Trade_Commission) regulation[^cite_note-FTC16CFR312-23] that takes into account the manner in which the information is being collected and the uses to which the information will be put.

With technological advancements, children have become more accustomed to using online services in their everyday lives. Though COPPA tries to keep children's personal information and data safe, enforcement remains inconsistent. There are many apps and websites that are accessible by children that do not comply with COPPA rules. Furthermore, even if those apps do have terms and conditions that correlate with COPPA rules, many times the terms are vast and not legible by the children. Many privacy policies do not disclose the extent of information they are sharing to a third party. Many times developers are unaware of the target audience their app or website will reach, making it more difficult to incorporate COPPA rules to protect children. This can expose children to privacy risk and lead to leaking sensitive information. Studies have shown that data taken from children has been provided to third-party advertising, which leads to promoting content that leads to impulse spending or promoting unhealthy products.[^cite_note-24]

COPPA 2.0 was introduced to expand the age range covered by COPPA to minors under 17. It was introduced in the Senate alongside the [Kids Online Safety Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kids_Online_Safety_Act) (KOSA). Both KOSA and COPPA 2.0 passed the Senate on a 91–3 vote on July 30, 2024.[^cite_note-COPPA2-25] COPPA 2.0 would have required youth aged 13, 14, 15 or 16 to consent to the processing of their own data, but would not have required the parents of 13-16 year olds to consent to the data processing.[^cite_note-COPPA2-25] COPPA 2.0, as well as KOSA, had not passed the House when the 118th US Congress expired on January 3, 2025.[^cite_note-26] It was reintroduced in the Senate and passed unanimously in March 2026.[^cite_note-27]

## Violations

According to the FTC, courts may fine violators of COPPA up to \$53,088 in civil penalties for each violation.[^cite_note-28] The FTC has brought a number of actions against website operators for failing to comply with COPPA requirements, including actions against [Google](1092923.md), [TikTok](56822861.md), [miHoYo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MiHoYo), *[Girls' Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girls'_Life_\(magazine\))*,[^cite_note-FTCAnn01-29] [American Pop Corn Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Pop_Corn_Company),[^cite_note-FTCPopcorn02-30] [Lisa Frank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisa_Frank), Inc.,[^cite_note-FTCWebSite01-31] [Mrs. Fields Cookies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mrs._Fields), and [The Hershey Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hershey_Company).[^cite_note-FTCReceives03-32]

In February 2004, UMG Recordings, Inc. was fined US\$400,000 for COPPA violations in connection with a website that promoted the then 13-year-old rapper [Lil' Romeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romeo_Miller) and hosted child-oriented games and activities, and Bonzi Software, which offered downloads of an animated figure "[BonziBuddy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BonziBuddy)" that provided shopping advice, jokes, and trivia was fined \$75,000 for COPPA violations.[^cite_note-FTC_UMG04-33] Similarly, the owners of the [Xanga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xanga) website were fined US\$1,000,000 in 2006 for COPPA violations of repeatedly allowing children under 13 to sign up for the service without getting their parent's consent.[^cite_note-SullivanFTC06-34]

In 2016, the mobile advertising network [inMobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/InMobi) was fined US\$950,000 for tracking the [geo-location](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geolocation) of all users (including those under 12) without their knowledge. The advertising software continuously tracked user location despite privacy preferences on the mobile device.[^cite_note-35] Other websites that were directed towards children and fined due to COPPA include Imbee (2008),[^cite_note-FTCImbee08-36] Kidswirl (2011)[^cite_note-EngleKids11-37] and Skid-e-Kids (2011).[^cite_note-FTCOper11-38]

In February 2019, the FTC issued a fine of \$5.7 million to [ByteDance](54450737.md) for failing to comply with COPPA with their [TikTok](56822861.md) app (then called Musical.ly). ByteDance agreed to pay the largest COPPA fine since the bill's enactment and to add a kids-only mode to the TikTok app.[^cite_note-39]

Three dating apps by Wildec were pulled by Apple and Google from their respective app stores, after the FTC determined that the dating apps allowed users under 13 to register, that Wildec knew there were significant numbers of minor users, and that this allowed inappropriate contact with minors.[^cite_note-40]

See also: [YouTube and privacy § COPPA settlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YouTube_and_privacy#COPPA_settlement)

On September 4, 2019, the FTC issued a fine of \$170 million to [YouTube](3524766.md) for COPPA violations, including tracking the viewing history of minors in order to facilitate [targeted advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Targeted_advertising).[^cite_note-41] Many notable social media platforms were subjected to scrutiny from the FTC, especially groups like Facebook where the platform had users ignoring COPPA guidelines since inception.[^cite_note-42] As a result, YouTube announced that as part of the settlement, in 2020 it would require channel operators to mark videos that are "child-oriented" as such, and would use [machine learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_learning) to automatically mark those as clearly "child-oriented" if not marked already. In the settlement terms, channel operators that failed to mark videos as "child-oriented" could be fined by the FTC for up to \$42,530 per video,[^cite_note-43] which has raised criticism towards the settlement terms.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45] The decision came in terms that, despite good faith, created many issues among the content creators on the site. Users such as [Ryan's World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryan's_World), [Philip DeFranco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_DeFranco) and [TheOdd1sOut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TheOdd1sOut) with vastly different content found themselves in conflict for their appealing content for children.[^cite_note-46] The following guidelines were implemented on the basis set by the following rules:

> The Rule sets out additional factors the FTC will consider in determining whether your content is child-directed:
>
> - the subject matter,
>
> - visual content,
>
> - the use of animated characters or child-oriented activities and incentives,
>
> - the kind of music or other audio content,
>
> - the age of models,
>
> - the presence of child celebrities or celebrities who appeal to children,
>
> - language or other characteristics of the site,
>
> - whether advertising that promotes or appears on the site is directed to children, and
>
> - competent and reliable empirical evidence about the age of the audience.
>
>   [^cite_note-47]

In 2022, [Epic Games](573199.md) settled a Federal Trade Commission complaint in part by agreeing to pay a \$275 million penalty for COPPA violations. The FTC complaint alleged that Epic illegally collected personal information from children under the age of 13 and made it difficult for parents to get such information deleted. The full agreement included an additional \$245 million to refund users who were manipulated into making unintended purchases.[^cite_note-48]

The DOJ and FTC jointly filed a lawsuit against [TikTok](56822861.md) and its owner [ByteDance](54450737.md) for violations of COPPA in August 2024, asserting that the app collecting private information from minor users as well as to allow them to interact with adults and adult content.[^cite_note-49] In August 2026, TikTok and ByteDance agreed to pay \$400 million to settle the lawsuit.[^cite_note-50]

In August 2026, [Meta](62420226.md) reached agreements with 29 states to settle COPPA claims as part of broader settlements over alleged harms to children from its social media platforms.[^cite_note-51]

## Compliance

In December 2012, the [Federal Trade Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Trade_Commission) issued revisions effective July 1, 2013, which created additional parental notice and consent requirements, amended definitions, and added other obligations for organizations that (1) operate a website or online service that is "directed to children" under 12 and that collects "personal information" from users or (2) knowingly collects personal information from people under 13 through a website or online service.[^cite_note-PercivalNew13-52] After July 1, 2013, operators must:[^cite_note-LaroseGuide13-53]

- Post a clear and comprehensive online privacy policy describing their information practices for personal information collected online from persons under age 13;

- Make reasonable efforts (taking into account available technology) to provide direct notice to parents of the operator's practices with regard to the collection, use, or disclosure of personal information from persons under 13, including notice of any material change to such practices to which the parents have previously consented;

- Obtain verifiable parental consent, with limited exceptions, prior to any collection, use, and/or disclosure of personal information from persons under age 13;

- Provide a reasonable means for a parent to review the personal information collected from their child and to refuse to permit its further use or maintenance;

- Establish and maintain reasonable procedures to protect the confidentiality, security, and integrity of the personal information collected from children under age 13, including by taking reasonable steps to disclose/release such personal information only to parties capable of maintaining its confidentiality and security;

- Retain personal information collected online from a child for only as long as is necessary to fulfill the purpose for which it was collected and delete the information using reasonable measures to protect against its unauthorized access or use; and

- Operators are prohibited from conditioning a child's participation in an online activity on the child providing more information than is reasonably necessary to participate in that activity.

  [^cite_note-LaroseAmended13-54]

According to a notice issued by the [Federal Trade Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Trade_Commission), an operator has actual knowledge of a user's age if the site or service asks for—and receives—information from the user that allows it to determine the person's age.[^cite_note-FTCChild13-55] An example, cited by the FTC, includes an operator who asks for a date of birth on a site's registration page has actual knowledge as defined by COPPA if a user responds with a year that suggests they are under 13. Another example cited by the FTC is that an operator may have actual knowledge based on answers to "age identifying" questions like "What grade are you in?" or "What type of school do you go to? (a) elementary; (b) middle; (c) high school; (d) college."

A small fee was charged by [Microsoft](19001.md) under COPPA as a way to verify parental consent. The fee was donated to the [National Center for Missing and Exploited Children](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Center_for_Missing_and_Exploited_Children).[^cite_note-56] Google, however, charges a small fee as a way to verify one's date of birth.

In the changes effective July 1, 2013, the definition of an operator was updated to make clear that COPPA covers a child-directed site or service that integrates outside services, such as plug-ins or advertising networks, that collect personal information from its visitors.[^cite_note-FTCStrength12-57] The definition of a website or online service directed to children is expanded to include plug-ins or ad networks that have actual knowledge that they are collecting personal information through a child-directed website or online service. Websites and services that target children as a secondary audience may differentiate among users, and are required to provide notice and obtain parental consent only for those users who identify themselves as being younger than 13.[^cite_note-PercivalNew13-52] The definition of personal information requiring parental notice and consent before collection now includes "persistent identifiers" that can be used to recognize users over time and across different websites or online services. However, no parental notice and consent are required when an operator collects a persistent identifier for the sole purpose of supporting the website or online service's internal operations.[^cite_note-FTCStrength12-57] The definition of personal information after July 1, 2013, also includes geolocation information, as well as photos, videos, and audio files that contain a child's image or voice.[^cite_note-LaroseGuide13-53]

On November 19, 2015, the FTC announced it had approved an additional method for obtaining verifiable parental consent: "face match to verified photo identification" (FMVPI). The two-step process allows a parent to submit a government-sanctioned ID for authentication, then submit an impromptu photo via mobile device or web camera, which is then compared to the photo on the ID.[^cite_note-FTCGrants15-58]

## International scope

The FTC has asserted that COPPA applies to any online service that is directed to U.S. users or knowingly collects information from children in the U.S., regardless of its country of origin. Referring to their official website, the following embodies such views:

The FTC's Office of International Affairs directs the agency's international activities for competition and consumer protection, which include:

- strengthening relationships with foreign competition and consumer protection agencies

- developing formal and informal arrangements and agreements with competition and consumer protection agencies around the world

- engaging in cooperative dialogues and submitting reports at international forums for competition and consumer protection

- helping agencies around the world develop and enhance their own competition and consumer protection programs

- sharing information with foreign law enforcement authorities through the U.S. Safe Web Act

- maintaining a robust International Fellows Program

  [^cite_note-59]

However, the FTC rarely performs enforcement actions against foreign companies, and faces a number of practical challenges in doing so.[^cite_note-60] The general assumption is that, despite the interconnected world of internet services, jurisdiction only applies to domestic operation. Nevertheless, it has successfully enforced COPPA against at least one foreign company with a significant US userbase, securing a \$5.7 million settlement against the Chinese company [ByteDance](54450737.md) over their [TikTok](56822861.md) app.[^cite_note-61]

## Criticisms

See also: [YouTube and privacy § COPPA settlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YouTube_and_privacy#COPPA_settlement)

COPPA is controversial and has been criticized as ineffective and potentially unconstitutional by legal experts[^cite_note-MateckiUpdate10-62] and mass media[^cite_note-MagidUnin12-63] since it was drafted.[^cite_note-64] Complaints leveled against the legislation include website owners banning users 12 and under—which only "encourages age fraud and allows websites to bypass the burden of obtaining parental consent"[^cite_note-MateckiUpdate10-62]—and the active suppression of children's rights to freedom of speech, self-expression, and other [First Amendment](31653.md) rights[^cite_note-MagidProtect14-65][^cite_note-MorrisAsk10-66] due to necessity of registering accounts to do so.

Delays in obtaining parental consent often result in children moving on to other activities that are less appropriate for their age or pose bigger privacy risks.[^cite_note-PuckettIns13-67]

In addition, age restrictions and the "parental consent" process are easy for children to circumvent, and parents generally help them to lie about their age.[^cite_note-BoydWhy11-68][^cite_note-GriggsParents11-69]

An Internet Safety Technical Task Force composed of experts from academia and commercial companies found in 2012 that mandatory age verification is not only a poor solution for privacy but also constitutes a violation of privacy.[^cite_note-PerlrothVeri12-70] The law has also many safety flaws. For example, it does not protect kids from predatory advertising,[^cite_note-KluverParent13-71] it does not prevent kids from accessing [pornography](5710507.md) or lying about their age,[^cite_note-FTC_COPPA_FAQ-2] and it does not ensure a totally safe environment online. Tech journalist [Larry Magid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larry_Magid), a long-time vocal opponent of the law,[^cite_note-MagidUnin12-63][^cite_note-MagidProtect14-65][^cite_note-MagidNewLaw00-6] also notes that parents, not the government, hold the bulk of responsibility of protecting children online.[^cite_note-MagidNewLaw00-6] COPPA has also been criticized for its potential [chilling effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilling_effect) on children's apps, content, websites and online services. For example, [Snapchat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snapchat) released a Snapkidz version of its app in June 2013, but unlike Snapchat, Snapkidz did not allow photo sharing at all due to COPPA regulations.[^cite_note-ChaeySnap13-72] Similarly, it has been pointed out that the COPPA Rule was not necessarily about privacy protection but more about "enforcing the laws."[^cite_note-PuckettIns13-67]

COPPA's penalties (\$40,000 per violation) can be potentially catastrophic for small businesses, undermining their business model.[^cite_note-KamenetzHow13-73][^cite_note-DavisIAB12-74] By contrast, the FTC has been criticized, including by COPPA author [Ed Markey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Markey), and FTC commissioner [Rohit Chopra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rohit_Chopra), for not fining major and [big tech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_tech) companies harshly enough for their COPPA violations, especially in comparison to their revenue. In contrast, violators of the [European Union](9317.md)'s [General Data Protection Regulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Data_Protection_Regulation) (GDPR) may be fined up to 4% of their annual global revenue.[^cite_note-75][^cite_note-76][^cite_note-77]

With the rise of virtual education, COPPA may inadequately represent the role of administrators, teachers, and the school in protecting student privacy under the assumption of *[loco parentis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_loco_parentis)*.[^cite_note-78]

[Mark Zuckerberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Zuckerberg), co-founder and CEO of [Facebook](7529378.md), expressed opposition to COPPA in 2011 and stated "That will be a fight we take on at some point. My philosophy is that for education you need to start at a really, really young age."[^cite_note-Lev-RamZuck11-79] The next year, [Jim Steyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Steyer), the CEO of [Common Sense Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Sense_Media), called for updates to COPPA, calling the time of the act's creation "the stone age of digital media" and pointing out the lack of platforms such as Google, YouTube, Facebook and Twitter at the time.[^cite_note-80]

In 2019, the [Government of the State of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_New_York_\(state\)) sued [YouTube](3524766.md) for violating COPPA by illegally retaining information related to children under 13 years of age. YouTube responded by dividing its content strictly into "for kids" and "not for kids". This has met with extremely harsh criticism from the YouTube community, especially from gamers, with many alleging that the [FTC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Trade_Commission) of the United States intends to fine [content creators](57313961.md) \$42,530 for "each mislabeled video", possibly putting all users at risk.[^cite_note-81][^cite_note-82][^cite_note-83] However, some have expressed skepticism over this, feeling that the fines may actually be in reference to civil penalties, possibly intended for the site's operators and/or warranted by more serious of COPPA violations or specific cases of "mislabeling videos".[^cite_note-84][^cite_note-85][^cite_note-86] As of December 2022, no individual YouTuber has been fined, however in 2025, Disney was fined 10 million dollars for not properly labeling their videos as "made for kids".[^cite_note-87][^cite_note-88]

Several bills have been proposed to amend COPPA. Markey and [Josh Hawley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josh_Hawley) introduced multiple bills (in the House in 2018 as the "Do Not Track Kids Act", and in 2019 as a Senate measure) proposing that COPPA ban the use of [targeted advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Targeted_advertising) to users under 13, require personal consent before the collection of personal information from users ages 13–15, require connected devices and toys directed towards children to meet security standards and include a privacy policy disclosure on their packaging, and require services to offer an "eraser button" to "permit users to eliminate publicly available personal information content submitted by the child, when technologically feasible". In January 2020, [Bobby Rush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Rush) and [Tim Walberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Walberg) introduced a similar house bill known as the Preventing Real Online Threats Endangering Children Today (PROTECT Kids) Act, which would extend all existing COPPA consent requirements to users under the age of 16, and explicitly add [mobile apps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_app), "precise geolocation", and biometric data to its remit.[^cite_note-89][^cite_note-90][^cite_note-91]

## Effects in the mobile app and ad-tech ecosystem

While the internet continues to advance, children are now moving their attention of internet use from webpages to apps where applying COPPA rules is more complex to address. These advancements make it more difficult to keep up with the new kinds of risks that the original notice and rules do not comply with. Scholars have argued that the COPPA consent framework struggles to stay relevant with new features such as the third-party advertising, persistent identifiers, and real-time behavioral tracking that operate in ways that children along with parents are unaware of. Sandoval (2024) states that even if companies were able to follow direct COPPA rules, there is still a risk of being tracked due to lack of laws to fully protect individuals. Mobile apps such as app stores and networks are constantly collecting background data making individuals still vulnerable.[^cite_note-92]

SDKs, also known as software development kits, are tools that developers use to build applications. Many child-directed mobile apps use SDKs along with other third-party trackers that create data collection pathways that users are unaware of. Researchers have states that these tracking tools transmit persistent identifiers, which are digital markers that track a device or user through time. Furthermore, they also track interaction patterns and device information, creating a behavioral profile regardless of COPPA regulations. Research has found that there are blind sports in child-directed apps that track their information that is not disclosed of.[^cite_note-93] Other researchers have explained that because many child-directed apps profit off advertisement, they are more inclined and focused on collecting data for profit than children's privacy.[^cite_note-94]

Age verification is important in protecting children online but it remains a challenge in the mobile environment. Apps tailored for children are simple to ensure that they have simple and fast access to play or stream as they please. The minimal standard for setup is simply entering an age, but this system is easy to bypass because children are able to enter an age much older than their age recorded at birth. Since children tend to use technology that is shared within the household, it makes it more difficult for apps to determine the age of the user. Policies that also allow for age verification tend to be an inconvenience for users because they require addition steps such as email confirmation or credit card validation.[^cite_note-95] With COPPA's regulations it has been argued that the cutoff age of 13 does not regulate children's interaction with digital platforms.[^cite_note-96]
