A **nightclub** or **dance club** is a venue that is open at [night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Night), usually for [drinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drinking), [dancing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dancing) and other [entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entertainment). Nightclubs often have a [bar](272207.md) and **discotheque** (usually simply known as **[disco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disco)**) with a dance floor, [laser lighting displays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_lighting_display), and a stage for [live music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_music) or a [disc jockey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disc_jockey) (DJ) who mixes recorded music. Nightclubs tend to be smaller than live music venues like [theatres](20913771.md) and [stadiums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stadium), with few or no seats for [customers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customer).

Nightclubs generally restrict access to people in terms of age, [attire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attire), and behaviors. Nightclubs typically have [dress codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dress_code) to prohibit people wearing informal, indecent, offensive, gym, or gang-related attire from entering. Unlike other entertainment venues, nightclubs are more likely to use [bouncers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouncer) to screen prospective patrons for entry.[^cite_note-Witherspoon2002-1]

The busiest nights for a nightclub are Friday and Saturday nights. Most nightclubs cater to a particular music genre or sound for branding effects. Some nightclubs may offer food and beverages (including alcoholic beverages).[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-McGoey2023-3]

## Terminology

In some countries, nightclubs are also referred to as "discos" or "discothèques" ([German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language): *Disko* or *Diskothek*, outdated; nowadays: *Club*); [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language): *discothèque*; [Italian](14708.md), [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language), and [Spanish](26825.md): *discoteca,* *antro* (common in Mexico), and *boliche* (common in Argentina, Uruguay, and Paraguay), *discos* is commonly used in all others in Latin America). In [Japanese](15606.md) ディスコ, *disuko* refers to an older, smaller, less fashionable venue; while クラブ, *kurabu* refers to a more recent, larger, more popular venue. The term *night* is used to refer to an evening focusing on a specific genre, such as "[retro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retro) music night" or a "singles night". In Hong Kong and China, nightclub is used as a [euphemism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphemism) for a [hostess club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_and_hostess_clubs), and the association of the term with the sex trade has driven out the regular usage of the term.

## History

### Early history

In the [United States](3434750.md), [New York](645042.md) increasingly became the national capital for tourism and entertainment. Grand hotels were built for upscale visitors.[^cite_note-4] [New York's theater district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theater_District,_Manhattan) gradually moved northward during this half century, from The [Bowery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowery) up [Broadway](4626.md) through [Union Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Square,_Manhattan) and [Madison Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madison_Square), settling around [Times Square](31389.md) at the end of the 19th century. Stars such as [Edwin Booth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Booth) and [Lillian Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lillian_Russell) were among the early [Broadway](725252.md) performers.[^cite_note-5] [Prostitutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prostitution) served a wide variety of clientele, from sailors on leave to [playboys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playboy_Lifestyle?action=edit\&redlink=1).[^cite_note-6]

The first nightclubs appeared in New York City in the 1840s and 1850s, including McGlory's, and the Haymarket. They enjoyed a national reputation for [vaudeville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaudeville), live music, and dance. They tolerated unlicensed liquor, commercial sex, and [gambling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gambling) cards, chiefly [Faro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faro_\(card_game\)). Practically all gambling was illegal in the city (except upscale [horseracing](74711.md) tracks), and regular payoffs to political and police leadership was necessary. Prices were high and they were patronized by an upscale audience. [Timothy Gilfoyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_Gilfoyle) called them "the first nightclubs".[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8] By contrast, [Owney Geoghegan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owney_Geoghegan) ran the toughest nightclub in New York from 1880 to 1883. It catered to a downscale clientele and besides the usual illegal liquor, gambling, and prostitution, it featured nightly fistfights and occasional shootings, stabbings, and police raids.[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10] [Webster Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Webster_Hall) is credited as the first modern nightclub,[^cite_note-11] being built in 1886 and starting off as a "social hall", originally functioning as a home for dance and political activism events. [Reisenweber's Cafe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reisenweber's_Cafe) is credited for introducing [jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz) and [cabaret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabaret) to New Yorkers.[^cite_note-12]

### Jukebox and prohibition

The [jukebox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jukebox) (a coin-operated record-player) was invented by the Pacific Phonograph Company in 1889 by its managers Louis Glass and his partner William S. Arnold.[^cite_note-13] The first was installed at the Palais Royale Saloon, [San Francisco](49728.md) on November 23, 1889, becoming an overnight sensation.[^cite_note-14]

The advent of the jukebox fueled the [Prohibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prohibition_in_the_United_States)-era boom in underground illegal [speakeasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speakeasy) bars, which needed music but could not afford a live band and needed precious space for paying customers.[^cite_note-15] Webster Hall stayed open, with rumors circulating of [Al Capone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Capone)'s involvement and police bribery.

From about 1900 to 1920, [working class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_class) Americans would gather at [honky tonks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honky_tonk) or [juke joints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juke_joint) to dance to music played on a piano or a jukebox. With the [repeal of Prohibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repeal_of_Prohibition_in_the_United_States) in February 1933, nightclubs were revived, such as New York's [21 Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/21_Club), [Copacabana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copacabana_\(nightclub\)), [El Morocco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Morocco), and the [Stork Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stork_Club). These nightclubs featured [big bands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_band).

During America's Prohibition, new speakeasies and nightclubs appeared on a weekly basis. [Texas Guinan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Guinan) opened and ran many, and had many padlocked by the police. [Harlem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlem) had its own clubs including the [Cotton Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_Club). Midtown New York had a string of nightclubs, many named after bandleaders such as [Paul Whiteman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Whiteman), [Vincent Lopez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_Lopez), and [Roger Wolfe Kahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Wolfe_Kahn) who opened Le Perroquet de Paris at a cost of \$250,000. It was billed as America's most beautiful and sophisticated nightclub and featured the young Kahn and his band most evenings.[^cite_note-16]

### Pre-WWII

#### Europe



The "Kakadu" (1919–1937), a Pre-World War II nightclub in Berlin,

[^cite_note-morat_2016-17]

&#x20;offered a bar, a dance floor, live music played by [jazz band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz_band), and [cabaret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabaret).

Pre-World War II [Soho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soho) in [London](17867.md) offered [café society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caf%C3%A9_society), [cabaret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabaret), [burlesque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burlesque) jazz, and bohemian clubs similar to those in New York, [Paris](22989.md), and [Berlin](3354.md).[^cite_note-18] Nightclubs in London were tied much to the idea of "[high society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_society_\(social_class\))", via organisations such as the [Kit Kat Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kit_Kat_Club_\(nightclub\)?action=edit\&redlink=1)[^cite_note-19] (which took its name from the political [Kit-Cat Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kit-Cat_Club) in [Pall Mall, London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pall_Mall,_London)) and the [Café de Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caf%C3%A9_de_Paris_\(London\)). The [43 Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/43_Club) on [Gerrard Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerrard_Street,_London) was run by [Kate Meyrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kate_Meyrick) the 'Night Club Queen'. Meyrick ran several London nightclubs in the 1920s and early 1930s, during which time she served prison sentences for breaching licensing laws and bribing a police officer. In this era, nightclubbing was generally the preserve of those with money.

In Paris, [Josephine Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephine_Baker) ran several nightclubs during the 1920s including Chez Josephine, as did her friend [Bricktop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bricktop) who ran Bricktops. Jazz singer and Broadway star [Adelaide Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adelaide_Hall) and her husband Bert Hicks opened the nightclub *La Grosse Pomme* on Rue Pigalle in [Montmartre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montmartre) on December 9, 1937.[^cite_note-20] Hall and Hicks also owned the chic Florida Club in London's Mayfair.[^cite_note-21]

In Germany during the [Golden Twenties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Twenties), there was a need to dance away the memories of the [First World War](4764461.md). In Berlin, where a "[tango](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tango) fever" had already swept dancing establishments in the early 1910s, 899 venues with a dancing licence were registered by 1930, including the [Moka Efti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moka_Efti?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Casanova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casanova_\(nightclub\)?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Scala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scala_\(nightclub\)?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Delphi-Palast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphi-Palast?action=edit\&redlink=1) (destroyed in WW2, replaced by the [Delphi Filmpalast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphi_Filmpalast)[^cite_note-delphi-22]), [Kakadu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kakadu_\(nightclub\)?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Femina-Palast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Femina-Palast?action=edit\&redlink=1), *Palais am Zoo*, *Gourmenia-Palast*, *Uhlandeck*, and the [Haus Vaterland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haus_Vaterland).[^cite_note-theguardian_mokaefti-23][^cite_note-morat_2016-17][^cite_note-spiegel_haus_vaterland-24] In the 1920s, the [nightlife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nightlife) of the city was dominated by [party drugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Club_drug) such as [cocaine](7701.md).[^cite_note-spiegel_berlin_crime-25][^cite_note-spiegel_golden_twenties-26] Hundreds of venues in the city, which at the time had a sinful reputation, offered in addition to bars, stages, and dance floors an erotic nightlife, such as [small booths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_room_\(sexuality\)) where lovers could withdraw to for intimate moments. These venues were aimed at rich and poor people, [gays](5171128.md), [lesbians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesbian), [nudists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nudist), and gangsters alike.[^cite_note-spiegel_golden_twenties-26]

#### Asia

In 1930s [Shanghai](27643.md), the big clubs were [The Paramount Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Paramount_Club?action=edit\&redlink=1) (opened in 1933) and [Ciro's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciro's_\(Shanghai\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) (opened in 1936). Other clubs of the era were the Metropole and the [Canidrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canidrome_\(Shanghai\)). [Jazz bands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz_band), big bands, and singers performed for a bowtied clientele. The Paramount and Ciro's in particular were fiercely rivalrous and attracted many customers from the underworld. Shanghai's clubs fell into decline after the [Japanese invasion of 1937](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Sino-Japanese_War#1937:_Full-scale_invasion_of_China) and eventually closed. The Paramount reopened after the [communist victory in 1949](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Revolution) as [The Red Capitol Cinema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Red_Capitol_Cinema?action=edit\&redlink=1), dedicated to [Maoist](46636.md) propaganda films, before fading into obscurity. It reopened as The Paramount in 2008.[^cite_note-27]

### World War II years

In [occupied France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupied_France), [jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz) and [bebop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop) music, and the [jitterbug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jitterbug) dance were banned by the [Nazis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazis) as "decadent American influences", so as an act of resistance, people met at hidden basements called *discothèques*[^cite_note-28] where they danced to jazz and [swing music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_music), played on a single turntable when a jukebox was not available. These discothèques were also patronized by anti-[Vichy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vichy_France) youth called [zazous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zazou). In [Nazi Germany](21212.md), there were underground discothèques patronized by [anti-Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-nazism) youth called the "[Swing Kids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_Kids)".

### Post-WWII: Emergence of the disc jockey and discothèque

The end of World War II saw the beginning of a transformation in the nightclub: no longer the preserve of a moneyed elite, over several decades, the nightclub steadily became a mass phenomenon.

In Germany, the first discothèque on record that involved a [disc jockey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disc_jockey) was [Scotch-Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotch-Club), which opened in 1959.[^cite_note-spiegel_scotchclub-29] Its, and therefore the world's first DJ was 19-year-old local cub reporter Klaus Quirini who had been sent to write a story about the strange new phenomenon of public record-playing; fueled by [whisky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whisky), he jumped on stage and started announcing records as he played them and took the stage-name DJ Heinrich.[^cite_note-30]

In the US, [Connie's Inn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connie's_Inn) and the [Cotton Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_Club) in [Harlem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlem), NY were popular venues for white audiences. Before 1953 and some years thereafter, most bars and nightclubs used a jukebox or mostly live bands.

In Paris, at a club named [Le Whisky à Gogo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Whisky_%C3%A0_Gogo?action=edit\&redlink=1), founded in 1947 on the [rue de Seine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rue_de_Seine) by Paul Pacine,[^cite_note-31][^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33] [Régine Zylberberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%A9gine_Zylberberg) in 1953 laid down a dance floor, suspended coloured lights, and replaced the jukebox with two turntables that she operated herself so there would be no breaks between the music. This was the world's first-ever "discothèque".[^cite_note-34] The Whisky à Gogo set into place the standard elements of the modern post-World War II *discothèque*-style nightclub.

In London, by the end of the 1950s, several of the [coffee bars](370815.md) in London's Soho introduced afternoon dancing. These prototype discothèques were nothing like modern-day nightclubs, as they were unlicensed, daytime venues where coffee was the drink of choice and that catered to a very young public – mostly made up of French and Italians working illegally, mostly in catering, to learn English, as well as [au pair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Au_pair) girls from most of western Europe.

A well known venue was [Les Enfants Terribles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Enfants_Terribles_\(club\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) at 93 Dean St., in [Soho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soho), London. Initially opening as a coffee-bar, it was run by Betty Passes who claimed to be the inventor of [disco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disco) after she pioneered the idea of dancing to records at her premises' basement in 1957. It stayed popular into the 1960s. It later became a 1940s-themed club called the Black Gardenia but has since closed.[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36]

[The Flamingo Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Flamingo_Club) on [Wardour Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wardour_Street) in London ran between 1952 and 1967 and was known for its role in the growth of [rhythm and blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhythm_and_blues) and jazz in the UK. It earned a controversial reputation with gangsters and prostitutes said to have been frequent visitors in the 1960s, along with musicians such as [the Beatles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beatles).

### 1960s

Discothèques began to appear in New York City in 1964: the [Village Vanguard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Village_Vanguard) offered dancing between jazz sets; Shepheard's, located in the basement of the [Drake Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drake_Hotel_\(New_York_City\)), was small but popular; L'Interdit and Il Mio (at [Delmonico's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delmonico's)) were private; the [El Morocco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Morocco) had an on-premises disco called Garrison; and the [Stork Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stork_Club) had one in its Shermaine suite. Larger discos opened in 1966: Cheetah, with room for 2000 dancers, the [Electric Circus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_Circus_\(nightclub\)), and Dom.[^cite_note-37]

While the discothèque swept Europe throughout the 1960s, it did not become widely popular in the United States until the 1970s,[^cite_note-spiegel_scotchclub-29] where the first [rock and roll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_and_roll) generation preferred rough and tumble bars and taverns to nightclubs until the disco era. In the early 1960s, [Mark Birley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Birley) opened a members-only discothèque nightclub, [Annabel's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annabel's), in [Berkeley Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkeley_Square), London. In 1962, the [Peppermint Lounge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peppermint_Lounge) in New York City became popular and is the place where [go-go dancing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go-go_dancing) originated. [Sybil Burton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sybil_Christopher) opened the [Arthur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_\(nightclub\)) discothèque in 1965 on East [54th Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/54th_Street_\(Manhattan\)) in [Manhattan](45470.md) on the site of the old El Morocco nightclub and it became the first, foremost, and hottest disco in New York City through 1969.[^cite_note-38]

In Germany in the 1960s, when Berlin was divided by the [Wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Wall), [Munich](19058.md) became Germany's epicenter of nightlife for the next two decades with numerous nightclubs and discothèques such as [Big Apple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Apple_\(club\)), [PN hit-house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PN_hit-house?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Tiffany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiffany_\(nightclub\)?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Domicile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domicile_\(nightclub\)?action=edit\&redlink=1), *Hot Club*, *Piper Club*, *Why Not*, *Crash*, *Sugar Shack*, the underwater discothèque [Yellow Submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_Submarine_\(club\)), and [Mrs. Henderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mrs._Henderson_\(nightclub\)?action=edit\&redlink=1), where stars such as [Mick Jagger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mick_Jagger), [Keith Richards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Richards), [Freddie Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freddie_Mercury), and [David Bowie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bowie) went in and out and which led to artists such as [Giorgio Moroder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giorgio_Moroder), [Donna Summer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donna_Summer), and Mercury settling in the city.[^cite_note-mjunikdisco-39][^cite_note-tz_70ies_Munich-40][^cite_note-mitvergnuegen-41] In 1967, Germany's first large-scale discothèque opened in Munich as the club [Blow Up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blow_Up_\(club\)), which because of its extravagance and excesses quickly gained international reputation.[^cite_note-mjunikdisco-39][^cite_note-tz_70ies_Munich-40]

In parallel, the [hippie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippie) movement spawned Britain's first club for [psychedelic music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychedelic_music), the [UFO Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UFO_Club)[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46][^cite_note-47] (at the Blarney Club, 31 [Tottenham Court Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tottenham_Court_Road), London from 23 Dec 1966 to Oct 1967) which then became the [Middle Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Earth_\(club\)) club[^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49] (at 43 King Street) and eventually the [Roundhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundhouse_\(venue\)) in 1968. Both the UFO Club and Middle Earth were short-lived but saw performances by artists such as house-band [Pink Floyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pink_Floyd), [Soft Machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_Machine), [Procol Harum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procol_Harum), [Fairport Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairport_Convention), [Arthur Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Brown_\(musician\)), and [Jimi Hendrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimi_Hendrix); DJ [John Peel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Peel) was a regular. These clubs germinated what would later become the underground gig scene of the 1970s and 1980s, at venues such as the [100 Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/100_Club) and [The Clarendon in Hammersmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Clarendon_Hotel,_Hammersmith). During the 1960s, the Clarendon was a [country & western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country_music) club, having earlier been an upmarket jazz, [dining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dining_club), and dancing club in the pre-War era.

In the north of England, the distinct [northern soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_soul) movement spanned [Manchester](20206.md)'s [Twisted Wheel Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twisted_Wheel_Club),[^cite_note-50] the [Blackpool Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackpool_Mecca),[^cite_note-51] [Cleethorpes Pier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleethorpes_Pier),[^cite_note-52] and the [Wigan Casino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wigan_Casino), known for the [acrobatic dancing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acro_dance) of its clubgoers; each of these clubs was known for all-nighters.

### 1970s: Disco

Disco has its roots in the underground club scene. During the early 1970s in New York City, disco clubs were places where oppressed or [marginalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_exclusion) groups such as gay people, [African Americans](2154.md), [Latinos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic_and_Latino_Americans), [Italian Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Americans), and [Jews](25955086.md) could party without following male to female dance protocol or exclusive club policies. Discothèques had a law where for every three men, there was one woman.[^cite_note-53] The women often sought these experiences to seek safety in a venue that embraced the [independent woman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_woman) – with an eye to one or more of the same or opposite sex or none. Although the culture that surrounded disco was progressive in dance couples, [cross-genre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-genre) music, and a push to put the physical over the rational, the role of women looked to be placed in the role of safety net.[^cite_note-54] It brought together people from different backgrounds.[^cite_note-55] These clubs acted as safe havens for [homosexual](5488304.md) partygoers to dance in peace and away from public scrutiny.[^cite_note-56]

By the late 1970s, many major U.S. cities had thriving disco club scenes centered on discothèques, nightclubs, and private loft parties where DJs would play disco hits through powerful [PA systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PA_system) for the dancers. The DJs played "a smooth mix of long single records to keep people 'dancing all night long'".[^cite_note-57] Some of the most prestigious clubs had elaborate lighting systems that throbbed to the beat of the music.

The genre of disco has changed through the years. It is classified both as a musical genre and as a nightclub; and in the late seventies, disco began to act as a safe haven for social outcasts. This club culture that originated in downtown New York, was attended by a variety of different ethnicities and economic backgrounds. It was an inexpensive activity to indulge in, and discos united a multitude of different minorities in a way never seen before; including those in the gay and [psychedelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychedelia) communities. The music ultimately was what brought people together.[^cite_note-58]

Some cities had disco dance instructors or [dance schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance_studio) that taught people how to do popular disco dances such as "touch dancing", the "[hustle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hustle_\(dance\))", and the "[cha-cha-cha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cha-cha-cha_\(dance\))". There were also disco fashions that discotheque-goers wore for nights out at their local disco, such as sheer, flowing [Halston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halston) dresses for women and shiny polyester [Qiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qiana) shirts for men. Disco clubs and "hedonistic loft parties" had a club culture with many Italian American, African American, gay, and Hispanic people.[^cite_note-59]

In addition to the dance and fashion aspects of the disco club scene, there was also a thriving [drug subculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_culture), particularly for [recreational drugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recreational_drug_use) that would enhance the experience of dancing to the loud music and the flashing lights, such as [cocaine](7701.md)[^cite_note-60] (nicknamed "blow"), [amyl nitrite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amyl_nitrite) "[poppers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poppers)",[^cite_note-61] and the "other quintessential 1970s club drug [Quaalude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methaqualone), which suspended motor coordination and turned one's arms and legs to [Jell-O](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jell-O)".[^cite_note-Braunstein-62] The "massive quantities of drugs ingested in discotheques by newly liberated gay men produced the next cultural phenomenon of the disco era: rampant [promiscuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promiscuity) and [public sex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_sex). While the dance floor was the central arena of seduction, actual sex usually took place in the nether regions of the disco: bathroom stalls, exit stairwells, and so on. In other cases, the disco became a kind of "main course" in a [hedonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedonism)'s menu for a night out."[^cite_note-Braunstein-62]

Well known 1970s discothèques included celebrity hangouts such as Manhattan's [Studio 54](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Studio_54), which was operated by [Steve Rubell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Rubell) and [Ian Schrager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Schrager).[^cite_note-63] Studio 54 was notorious for the hedonism that went on within; the balconies were known for sexual encounters, and drug use was rampant. Its dance floor was decorated with an image of the "[Man in the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man_in_the_Moon)" that included an animated [cocaine spoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocaine_spoon). Other 1970s discothèques in New York City were Manhattan's Starship Discovery One at 350 West [42nd Street](61068.md), [Roseland Ballroom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roseland_Ballroom), [Xenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenon_\(nightclub\)), [The Loft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Loft_\(New_York_City\)), the [Paradise Garage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Garage), a recently renovated [Copacabana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copacabana_\(nightclub\)), and Aux Puces, one of the first gay disco bars. The album cover of Saturday Night Band's *Come On and Dance, Dance* featured two dancers in the Starship Discovery One. In San Francisco, there was the [Trocadero Transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trocadero_Transfer), the [I-Beam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I-Beam_\(nightclub\)), and the [End Up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_Up).

In Spain during the 1970s, the first clubs and discos opened in [Ibiza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibiza), an island which had been a popular destination for hippie travelers since the 1960s and now was experiencing a tourist boom.[^cite_note-64] The first ever "[Superclub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superclub)" in Ibiza was the now-abandoned "Festival Club" at [Sant Josep de sa Talaia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sant_Josep_de_sa_Talaia), which was built between 1969 and 1972 and serviced tourists who were bused in until it closed in 1974.[^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66] Responding to this influx of visitors, locals opened the first large clubs [Pacha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacha_Group), [Amnesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amnesia_\(nightclub\)), and the [Ku-club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privilege_Ibiza) (renamed Privilege in 1995).[^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70]

By the early 1980s, the term "disco" had largely fallen out of favour in the [United States](3434750.md).

### 1970s: Disco, hip hop, punk rock, and glam foundations

Nightclub culture was often pioneered by [African American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_American) and [Latino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latino_\(demonym\)) communities in the United States. In the 1970s and 80s, Black DJs created the blueprint for the modern dance floor. [Chicago house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_house), born in venues like [The Warehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warehouse_\(nightclub\)) with [Frankie Knuckles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankie_Knuckles), and [Detroit techno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit_techno), pioneered by the "Belleville Three" ([Juan Atkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Atkins), [Kevin Saunderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Saunderson), and [Derrick May](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derrick_May_\(musician\))), turned the DJ into a cultural icon and established the "non-stop beat" format used globally today.

In parallel to the disco scene and quite separate from it, the [glam rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glam_rock) ([T. Rex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._Rex_\(band\)), David Bowie, [Roxy Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roxy_Music)) and [punk rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punk_rock) cultures in London produced their own set of nightclubs, starting with [Billy's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billy's?action=edit\&redlink=1) at 69 [Dean Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_Street) in Soho (known for its David Bowie nights),[^cite_note-71]

Louise's on [Poland Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland_Street) (the first true punk club and hangout of the [Sex Pistols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sex_Pistols), [Siouxsie Sioux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siouxsie_Sioux) plus the [Bromley Contingent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bromley_Contingent),[^cite_note-72] and then Blitz (the home of the [Blitz Kids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blitz_Kids_\(New_Romantics\))). [Crackers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crackers_\(nightclub\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) was a key part of the jazz-[funk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funk) scene and also the early punk scene via its Vortex nights.[^cite_note-73]

The underground warehouse party scene was kicked off by [Toyah Willcox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyah_Willcox) with her [Mayhem Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayhem_Studios?action=edit\&redlink=1) at [Patcham Terrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patcham_Terrace?action=edit\&redlink=1) in [Battersea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battersea).[^cite_note-74][^cite_note-75][^cite_note-76] The emergence of this highly experimental artistic scene in London can be credited almost entirely to [Rusty Egan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rusty_Egan), [Steve Strange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Strange), the Bromley Contingent's [Philip Sallon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Sallon), and Chris Sullivan.[^cite_note-77]

The [new wave music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_wave_music) scene grew out of Blitz and the [Cha Cha Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cha_Cha_Club?action=edit\&redlink=1) in [Charing Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charing_Cross). Whilst overall, the club scene was fairly small and hidden away in basements, cellars, and warehouses, London's complicated mix of punk, [New Romantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Romantic), New Wave, and gay clubs in the late 1970s and early 1980s paved the way for [acid house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_house) to flourish in the late 1980s, initially with [Shoom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoom) and two acid house nights at [Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaven_\(nightclub\)): Spectrum and Rage.

On 3 July 1980, at Gossip's[^cite_note-timeout/35-gazs-78] on Dean Street, Soho, Gaz Mayall, son of [John Mayall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Mayall), began *Gaz's Rockin' Blues*,[^cite_note-bassline/Gaz's-79] later at Billy's Club, now the longest running *club night* (one-nighter), an independent promotion event series, in London,[^cite_note-shapersofthe80s/gaz-still-80][^cite_note-nhcarnival/gazs-81][^cite_note-hnB/gazs-41-82][^cite_note-londonletter/Returns-83] still going as of 2021.[^cite_note-84][^cite_note-85][^cite_note-86]

In the north of England, what later became the "alternative" scene was centered around the Roxy/Bowie room at Pips in Manchester,[^cite_note-Cooper-87][^cite_note-88] which opened in 1972; as small as this scene was, many notable figures attended the club, and Joy Division played their first gig there, billed as "Warsaw" before changing their name that night.[^cite_note-Cooper-87]

### 1980s: New wave, post-punk, goth, rave, and acid house



A disc jockey (DJ) mixing vinyl records on [turntables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turntable) ([Inland Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inland_Empire), 2009)

During the 1980s, during the New Romantic movement, London had a vibrant nightclub scene, which included clubs like The Blitz, [the Batcave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batcave_\(club\)), the [Camden Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camden_Palace), and [Club for Heroes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Club_for_Heroes?action=edit\&redlink=1). These clubs grew out of the earlier [Mandrake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gargoyle_Club#The_Mandrake) and Billy's (later Gossip's)[^cite_note-89][^cite_note-90] at 69 Dean Street, in the basement below the ground floor [Gargoyle Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gargoyle_Club). Both music and fashion embraced the aesthetics of the movement. Bands included [Depeche Mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depeche_Mode), [Yazoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yazoo_\(band\)), [The Human League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Human_League), [Duran Duran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duran_Duran), [Eurythmics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurythmics), and [Ultravox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultravox). [Reggae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reggae)-influenced bands included [Boy George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boy_George) and [Culture Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_Club), and [electronic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_music) vibe bands included [Visage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visage_\(band\)). At London nightclubs, young men would often wear make-up and young women would wear men's suits. [Leigh Bowery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leigh_Bowery)'s [Taboo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taboo_\(nightclub\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) (which opened in 1985)[^cite_note-91] bridged the New Romantic and acid house scenes.

With the birth of [house music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_music) in the mid-1980s and then acid house, kickstarted by Chris Sullivan's [The Wag Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wag_Club)[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94] (on the site of the earlier [The Flamingo Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Flamingo_Club)), a cultural revolution swept around the world; first in [Chicago](6886.md) at the [Warehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warehouse_\(nightclub\)) and then London and New York City. London clubs such as [Clink Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clink_Street), Revolution in Progress (RiP), Philip Sallon's [The Mudd Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mudd_Club?action=edit\&redlink=1),[^cite_note-95] [Danny Rampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danny_Rampling)'s [Shoom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoom) (starting in December 1987 in the basement of Southwark's Fitness Centre), [Paul Oakenfold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Oakenfold)'s [Spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_\(nightclub\)?action=edit\&redlink=1), and [Nicky Holloway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicky_Holloway)'s [The Trip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trip_\(nightclub\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) fused the eclecticism and ethos of \[Ibiza with the new electronic music from the US.

The largest [UK](31717.md) cities like [Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham), [Leeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeds) (The Orbit), [Liverpool](18081.md) ([Quadrant Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrant_Park) and 051), Manchester ([The Haçienda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ha%C3%A7ienda)), [Newcastle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcastle_upon_Tyne), and [Swansea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swansea), and several key European places like Paris ([Les Bains-Douches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Bains-Douches)), Ibiza ([Pacha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacha_Group)), and [Rimini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rimini), also played a significant role in the evolution of [clubbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clubbing_\(subculture\)), DJ culture, and nightlife.

Significant New York nightclubs of the period were [Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area_\(nightclub\)), [Danceteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danceteria), and [The Limelight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Limelight).[^cite_note-96]

However, the seismic shift in nightlife was the emergence of [rave culture](43488.md) in the UK. A mixture of free and commercial outdoor parties were held in fields, warehouses, and abandoned buildings, by various groups such as Biology, Sunrise, Confusion, Hedonism, Rage & Energy, and many others. This laid the ground for what was unfold in the 1990s, initially in the United Kingdom, Germany, and the United States and then worldwide from the 2000s onwards.

### 1990s, 2000s, and 2010s



Club DJ using digital [CDJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CDJ) players for mixing music ([Munich](19058.md), 2010s)

In Europe and North America, nightclubs play disco-influenced dance music such as [house music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_music), [techno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techno), [Eurodance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurodance) and other dance music styles such as [electronica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronica), [breakbeat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakbeat), and [trance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trance_music). Most nightclubs in major cities in the U.S. that have an [early adulthood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_adulthood) clientele, play [hip hop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hip_hop), [dance-pop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance-pop), house, and/or trance music. These clubs are generally the largest and most frequented of all of the different types of clubs.

Techno clubs are popular around the world since the early 1990s. Well known examples of the 1990s include [Tresor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tresor_\(club\)), [E-Werk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-Werk_\(Berlin\)), and [Bunker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunker_\(Berlin\)) in [Berlin](3354.md); Omen and [Dorian Gray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorian_Gray_\(club\)) in [Frankfurt](10992.md); [Ultraschall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraschall), [KW – Das Heizkraftwerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KW_%E2%80%93_Das_Heizkraftwerk), and [Natraj Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natraj_Temple) in Munich; and Stammheim in [Kassel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kassel).[^cite_note-ronaldhitzler-97]

In 1992, the [Castlemorton Common Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castlemorton_Common_Festival) triggered the UK government's [Criminal Justice Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_Justice_Act), which largely ended the rave movement by criminalizing any gathering of 20 or more people where music ("sounds wholly or predominantly characterized by the emission of a succession of repetitive beats") was played. Commercial clubs immediately capitalized on the situation causing a boom in "Superclubs" in the UK, such as [Ministry of Sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Sound) (London), [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_\(club\)), and [Cream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cream_\(nightclub\)) ([Liverpool](18081.md)). These developed the club-as-spectacle theme pioneered in the 1970s and 1980s by Pacha (Ibiza) and [Juliana's Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juliana's) (Japan), creating a global phenomenon; however, many clubs such as [The Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cross_\(nightclub\)) in London, preserved the more underground feel of the former era.

By 1995, [Fatboy Slim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatboy_Slim) co-founded a popular *club night* at the Concorde, a 300-capacity bingo hall,[^cite_note-Shoup-2024-98] in [Brighton](49796.md), the *Big Beat Boutique*, where he played music from genres including [northern soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_soul), [acid house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_house), [hip-hop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hip-hop) and reggae, combined with [breakbeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakbeat).[^cite_note-Shoup-2024-98][^cite_note-Cook-2025-99]

Since the late 2000s, venues that received high media attention include [Berghain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berghain) in Berlin and [Fabric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabric_\(club\)) in London.

[Video art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_art) has been used in nightclubs since the 1960s, but especially with the rise of [electronic dance music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_dance_music) since the late 1980s. [VJing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VJing) gained more and more importance. VJs ("video jockeys") mix video content in a similar manner that DJs mix audio content, creating a visual experience that is intended to complement the music.

In the mid-to-late 2010s, the musical landscape of North American nightclubs underwent a significant shift. While [EDM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_dance_music) and [Dance-pop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance-pop) dominated the early part of the decade, by approximately 2016, [hip hop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hip_hop_music) and [trap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trap_music) became the primary genres in mainstream American nightlife. This era saw the rise of "club rap," where high-energy production from artists like [Drake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drake_\(musician\)), [Future](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_\(rapper\)), [Rae Sremmurd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rae_Sremmurd), [Kendrick Lamar](29909823.md), and [Migos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migos) replaced traditional house and trance in many top-tier venues. By 2018 and 2019, the "superclub" format in cities like [Las Vegas](47737.md), [Miami](53846.md), and [Atlanta](3138.md) frequently billed hip hop artists alongside or in place of resident DJs to cater to the evolving tastes of the [millennial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennials) and [Gen Z](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gen_Z) demographic.

### 2020s

In early 2020s, the global [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md), triggered nightclubs to close worldwide – the first ever synchronized, global shutdown of nightlife. In response, online "virtual nightclubs" developed, hosted on [video-conferencing](2304367.md) platforms such as [Zoom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoom_\(software\)).[^cite_note-100] As countries relaxed lockdown rules following drops in case numbers, some nightclubs reopened in repurposed form as sat-down pubs.[^cite_note-101][^cite_note-102] As vaccine rollouts reached advanced stages, nightclubs were able to reopen with nil or looser restrictions, such as producing [certification of full vaccination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaccine_passports_during_the_COVID-19_pandemic) upon entry.[^cite_note-103]

## Entry criteria



A bouncer checks patrons' IDs at the entrance to a nightclub

Many nightclubs use [bouncers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouncer_\(doorman\)) to choose who can enter the club, or specific lounges or VIP areas. Some nightclubs have one group of bouncers to screen [clients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Client_\(business\)) for entry at the main door, and then other bouncers to screen for entry to other dance floors, lounges, or VIP areas. For legal reasons, in most jurisdictions, the bouncers have to check ID to ensure that prospective patrons are of legal drinking age and that they are not intoxicated already. In this respect, a nightclub's use of bouncers is no different from the use of bouncers by [pubs](24578.md) and [sports bars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_bar). However, in some nightclubs, bouncers may screen patrons using criteria other than just age and intoxication status, such as [dress code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dress_code), [guest list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guest_list?action=edit\&redlink=1) inclusion, and physical appearance.

This type of screening is used by clubs to make their club "exclusive", by denying entry to people who are not dressed in a stylish enough manner. While some clubs have written dress codes, such as no ripped jeans, no jeans, no gang clothing, and so on, other clubs may not post their policies. As such, the club's bouncers may deny entry to anybody at their discretion. The guest list is typically used for private parties and events held by [celebrities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celebrity). At private parties, the hosts may only want their friends to attend. At celebrity events, the hosts may wish the club to only be attended by [A-list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A-list) individuals.

### Cover charge



Advance ticket sales for diverse nightclubs on [Ibiza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibiza) island

In most cases, entering a nightclub requires a flat fee, called a [cover charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cover_charge). Some clubs waive or reduce the cover charge for early arrivers, special guests, or women (in the [United Kingdom](31717.md) this latter option is illegal under the [Equality Act 2010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equality_Act_2010),[^cite_note-104] but the law is rarely enforced, and open violations are frequent). Friends of the [doorman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouncer_\(doorman\)) or the club owner may gain free entrance. Sometimes, especially at larger clubs in Continental European countries, one gets only a pay card at the entrance, on which all money spent in the discothèque (often including the entrance fee) is marked. Sometimes, entrance fee and cloakroom costs are paid by cash, and only the drinks in the club are paid using a pay card.

Some clubs offer patrons the chance to sign up on their guest list. A club's guest list is a special promotion the venue offers separate from general admission. Each club has different benefits when you are signed up on their guest list. Some of the benefits of being on a club's guestlist include: free entry, discounted cover charge, the ability to skip the line, and free drinks. Many clubs hire a promotions team to find and sign up guests to the club's guest list.

### Dress code



Dancers in [fetish fashion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fetish_fashion) at a nightclub

Many nightclubs enforce a dress code in order to ensure a certain type of clientele is in attendance at the venue. Some upscale nightclubs ban attendees from wearing trainers (sneakers) or jeans while other nightclubs will advertise a vague "dress to impress" dress code that allows the bouncers to discriminate at will against those vying for entry to the club.

Many exceptions are made to nightclub dress codes, with denied entry usually reserved for the most glaring rule breakers or those thought to be unsuitable for the party.

[Rave parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rave_parties) typically both allow and encourage the wearing of clubwear, deliberately skimpy and outrageous clothing designed for dancing and exhibitionism.

Certain nightclubs like [fetish nightclubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fetish_club) may apply a dress code ([BDSM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fetish_fashion)) to a leather-only, rubber-only, or fantasy dress code.

Dress code criteria can be an excuse for discriminatory practices, such as in the case of *Carpenter v. Limelight Entertainment Ltd.*[^cite_note-105][^cite_note-106]

### Exclusive nightclubs



[Bottle service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bottle_service) at a nightclub

Large cosmopolitan cities that are home to large affluent populations (such as [Atlanta](3138.md), [Chicago](6886.md), [Sydney](27862.md), [Los Angeles](18110.md), [Melbourne](17306237.md), [Miami](53846.md), [New York City](645042.md), and [London](17867.md)) often have what are known as exclusive boutique nightclubs. This type of club typically has a capacity of less than 200 occupants and a very strict entrance policy, which usually requires an entrant to be on the club's guest list. While not explicitly members only clubs, such as [Soho House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soho_House_\(club\)), exclusive nightclubs operate with a similar level of exclusivity. As they are off limits to most of the public and ensure the privacy of guests, many celebrities favor these types of clubs to other, less exclusive, clubs that do not cater as well to their needs.

Another differentiating feature of exclusive nightclubs is that they are known for having a certain type of crowd, for instance, a fashion-forward, [affluent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affluent) crowd or a crowd with a high concentration of [fashion models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fashion_models). Many exclusive boutique clubs market themselves as being a place to socialize with models and celebrities. Affluent patrons who find that marketing message appealing are often willing to purchase [bottle service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bottle_service) at a markup of several times the retail cost of the liquor.[^cite_note-107]

## Substance abuse

A distinctive feature of a nightclub is also the fact that it can serve as a hub for substances like [alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_\(drug\)), which could affect third parties, creating a negative externality of consumption. The culture of nightclubs create a sense of consuming alcohol in larger quantities than usual. A study in São Paulo looking to identify causes of binge drinking found that environmental variables such as more number of dancefloors, higher level of noise, and 'all you can drink' services to be significantly linked to binge drinking.[^cite_note-108] Furthermore, the culture created around nightclubs to indulge in 'pre-drinking' accentuates the amount of alcohol consumed, which leads to more problems in residential areas off nightclub premises (for example, a higher chance of participating in a fight).[^cite_note-\:0-109]

Moreover, young consumers of nightclubs who tend to binge drink are often found to be less safe during sexual encounters as a result of the alcohol,[^cite_note-110] which could lead to the spread of [STDs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexually_transmitted_infection).

A big issue that stems from [alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_abuse) and [drug abuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substance_abuse) in nightclubs is transportation. Private cars are the most prominent mode of transportation to and from nightclubs, and the use of drugs and alcohol in nightclubs are reported to increase the number of risky behaviors, such as [driving under the influence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Driving_under_the_influence) or taking a lift from someone under the influence.[^cite_note-111] A portion of driving customers, despite drinking less than non-driving customers, are still observed to have alcohol levels above the legal threshold after a night out at a nightclub.[^cite_note-112]

## Photography

In some nightclubs professional [photographers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photography) will take publicity photos of patrons, to use in advertising for the nightclub. [Digital SLR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_single-lens_reflex_camera) cameras and speedlight flash units are typically used.[^cite_note-114] [Concert photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concert_photography) and [event photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event_photography) are used to provide clubgoers with a memorable keepsake in addition to promo material used by clubs. Some nightclubs (and in particular techno clubs) pursue a strict no photo policy in order to protect the clubbing experience, and smartphone camera lenses of visitors are occasionally taped up with stickers when one enters the venue.[^cite_note-115][^cite_note-116]

## Bouncer

Most nightclubs employ teams of [bouncers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouncer_\(doorman\)), who have the power to restrict entry to the club and remove people. Upon entry, some bouncers use handheld [metal detectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal_detector) to prevent weapons being brought into clubs or conduct a pat down for weapons.[^cite_note-Hobbs2003-117] Bouncers often eject patrons for reasons such as possession of [party drugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recreational_drug_use) in the venue, physical altercations with other patrons, and behavior deemed to be inappropriate or troublesome.[^cite_note-118][^cite_note-119] Bouncers only allow a certain number of people into a club at a time by counting heads in order to prevent [stampedes](19377820.md), and [fire code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_code), or [liquor licensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquor_licensing) violations. They also enforce a club's [dress code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dress_code) upon entry. Many clubs have balcony areas specifically for the security team to watch over the clubbers.

## Floor show

Some nightclubs present a 'floor show', a series of acts by comedians, dancers, models, singers, and other entertainers, which can be similar to [cabaret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabaret).[^cite_note-120][^cite_note-cambridge-dictionary-floor-show-121]
