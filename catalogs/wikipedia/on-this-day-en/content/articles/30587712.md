**Benjamin Roberts-Smith** (born 1 November 1978) is an Australian former soldier in the [Special Air Service Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Air_Service_Regiment) (SASR). He is one of Australia's most highly decorated soldiers, having received the [Medal for Gallantry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medal_for_Gallantry) (2006), the [Victoria Cross for Australia](12059116.md) (2011)—the highest award for gallantry in battle that can be awarded to a member of the [Australian Defence Force](67538.md) (ADF)—and a [Commendation for Distinguished Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commendation_for_Distinguished_Service) (2012). He was widely considered a national hero, but his reputation deteriorated following a 2023 Federal Court ruling in which a judge found that there was enough evidence, according to the [standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burden_of_proof_\(law\)#Civil_standard) required in Australian [defamation](28661.md) law, to establish that he had murdered four unarmed Afghans and broken the [rules of military engagement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rules_of_engagement).

After his discharge in 2013, Roberts-Smith was named [Australian Father of the Year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Father_of_the_Year), was awarded a scholarship to study business at the [University of Queensland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Queensland) and received many other honours. He was appointed by media mogul [Kerry Stokes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerry_Stokes) to top executive positions at [Seven Queensland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Queensland) and ultimately [Seven Brisbane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Brisbane), from which he stepped down in 2021 to focus on his defamation action against [Nine Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nine_Entertainment).

In October 2017, Roberts-Smith's conduct in Afghanistan came under scrutiny after reports emerged that he had killed an unarmed teenager and bullied other soldiers. He commenced defamation proceedings in 2018; however, in June 2023, Justice [Anthony Besanko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Besanko) dismissed the case, writing that he had "disgraced his country". In 2025, appeals to the [Federal Court of Australia](4688076.md) and the [High Court of Australia](314201.md) were dismissed and refused in full.

In April 2026, Roberts-Smith was arrested and charged with five counts of the [war crime](48626.md) of murder. He was released on bail later that month.

## Early life, family and education

Roberts-Smith was born on 1 November 1978 in Perth, Western Australia.[^cite_note-1] He is the elder son of Sue (nee Holloway)[^cite_note-2] and [Len Roberts-Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Len_Roberts-Smith). His father is a former justice of the [Supreme Court of Western Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Western_Australia), [Army Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Army_Reserve) major general and [Judge Advocate General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judge_Advocate_General_\(Australia\)) of the [Australian Defence Force](67538.md).[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-McKenzie-2023-4]<sup>: 22 </sup> His brother, [Sam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Roberts-Smith), is an opera singer.[^cite_note-5]

In his biography of Roberts-Smith, *Crossing the Line*, journalist [Nick McKenzie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_McKenzie) alleges that Roberts-Smith developed a reputation for intimidating smaller students at school. He struggled academically, achieving average grades at Perth's [Hale School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hale_School).[^cite_note-McKenzie-2023-4]<sup>: 23 </sup> During his time at Hale , he played both rugby and basketball for the school. In 1995, his final year, the school's first XV rugby team recognised him as its best and fairest player.[^cite_note-Cowan-2011-6][^cite_note-7]

## Military career and honours

### Early career

Roberts-Smith [enlisted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enlisted_rank) in the Australian Army in 1996 at age eighteen. After completing basic training at the [Army Recruit Training Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_Recruit_Training_Centre) at Blamey Barracks in Kapooka, he underwent initial employment training at the [School of Infantry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_of_Infantry) at [Lone Pine Barracks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lone_Pine_Barracks) in Singleton; and from there, Roberts-Smith was posted to the [3rd Battalion, Royal Australian Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3rd_Battalion,_Royal_Australian_Regiment) (3 RAR) in Holsworthy, all in New South Wales. Initially part of a [rifle company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rifle_company), he subsequently became a [section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_\(military_unit\)) commander in the Direct Fire Support Weapons Platoon.[^cite_note-DoD-8] With 3 RAR and as a part of [Rifle Company Butterworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rifle_Company_Butterworth), Roberts-Smith was deployed twice in Malaysia[^cite_note-Dixon-2011-9] and participated in two operations in East Timor in 1999, including as part of the [International Force East Timor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Force_East_Timor).[^cite_note-DoD-8][^cite_note-Dixon-2011-9] By 2001, following his service in Malaysia and East Timor, he held the rank of [corporal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporal#Australia).[^cite_note-Masters-2023-10]<sup>: 21 </sup>

After completing the [Special Air Service Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Air_Service_Regiment) (SASR) selection course in 2003 and the SASR reinforcement cycle, Roberts-Smith was posted to 3 Squadron, SASR at [Campbell Barracks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campbell_Barracks_\(Western_Australia\)) in Perth.[^cite_note-McKenzie-2023-4]<sup>: 24–25 </sup> Joining SASR meant surrendering his rank of corporal and starting at the base rank of [trooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trooper_\(rank\)).[^cite_note-Masters-2023-10]<sup>: 22 </sup> While in 3 Squadron, he was a member of training and assistance teams throughout Southeast Asia.[^cite_note-DoD-8] He also took part in preparations to evacuate Australian citizens from Fiji in 2004 and was part of [personnel security detachments in Iraq](5043324.md) throughout 2005 and 2006.[^cite_note-DoD-8]

### Afghanistan

Ben Roberts-Smith's medals on display at the Australian War Memorial in December 2011

Roberts-Smith's medals on display at the [Australian War Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_War_Memorial)

Roberts-Smith was deployed to Afghanistan on six occasions, in 2006, 2007, 2009, 2010 and 2012, as part of [Australia's contribution](407018.md) to the [War in Afghanistan (2001–2021)](19666611.md). His deployments were with the SASR, which conducted long-range covert intelligence, reconnaissance and strike operations, often behind enemy lines. The regiment's operations have been characterised as secretive.[^cite_note-DoD-8][^cite_note-AWM2-11][^cite_note-12]

#### Medal for Gallantry

On 31 May 2006, Roberts-Smith was a scout and sniper in a patrol[^cite_note-15] deployed during the [2006 Dutch/Australian Offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Dutch/Australian_Offensive).[^cite_note-Masters-2023-10]<sup>: 5 </sup>[^cite_note-AWM2-11] The patrol was commanded by Sergeant [Matthew Locke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Locke_\(soldier\)).[^cite_note-Dalton-2017-16] The patrol's objective was to establish an observation post on the mountain Koran Ghar, overlooking the Chora Pass in [Uruzgan Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruzgan_Province).[^cite_note-Masters-2023-10]<sup>: 5 </sup>[^cite_note-AWM2-11] Besides Roberts-Smith and Locke, members of the patrol comprised two junior soldiers, a British sergeant from the [Special Boat Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Boat_Service) and an American special forces radio operator. The patrol's aim was to monitor and report on [Anti-Coalition Militia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Coalition_Militia) movements in the pass below and to maintain concealment.[^cite_note-Masters-2023-10]<sup>: 5 </sup>

On 2 June, a lone Afghan teenager approached the patrol observation post and left the area shortly thereafter. The American had seen the teenager and communicated what he saw to the rest of the team. After hearing of the teen's exit, Roberts-Smith and Locke arrived at the observation post and enquired why the teen had not been engaged. The other soldiers replied that their orders were to observe and not attract attention.[^cite_note-Masters-2023-10]<sup>: 6 </sup> Although the other soldiers had decided it was not necessary to engage the Afghan, Roberts-Smith and Locke "decided to hunt down and shoot dead the two 'enemy' after concluding they had spotted the patrol".[^cite_note-McKenzie-2017-17] The patrol report had identified only a single Afghan unarmed "spotter", but Roberts-Smith said in an oral account provided to the [Australian War Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_War_Memorial) that two armed insurgents had approached the position. Roberts-Smith later wrote to the War Memorial writing "Without the benefit of the patrol report to guide me it would appear I have confused my many engagements and identified two insurgents initially locating our observation post as opposed to one".[^cite_note-McKenzie-2017-17]

Following the shooting of the teen, the post became the focus of the Anti-Coalition Militia's attempts to identify and surround it. In one instance, the militia attempted to outflank the position, and Roberts-Smith was one of two members of the patrol who moved out of their secure position to kill the enemy combatants. Two members of the militia attempted to attack the post from a different position, and Roberts-Smith killed them. Seeing that the post was vulnerable, Roberts-Smith made the decision to divide the team and take a position advantageous for his sniper rifle. While separated, he identified 16 Taliban advancing towards the post. While under fire, Roberts-Smith used his rifle to stop their advance. After being joined by another member of the patrol, he was able to hold off the militia until air support arrived.[^cite_note-AWM2-11][^cite_note-Masters-2023-10]<sup>: 6–11 </sup>

During the battle, a junior soldier's gun had jammed and he was unable to assist for a period of time.[^cite_note-Masters-2023-10]<sup>: 6 </sup> It was later alleged that, following the patrol's return to base, Roberts-Smith had threatened to shoot the junior soldier in the back of the head.[^cite_note-Masters-2023-10]<sup>: 8 </sup> In 2006, Roberts-Smith and Locke were awarded the [Medal for Gallantry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medal_for_Gallantry) in recognition of their actions on 2 June.[^cite_note-AWM2-11][^cite_note-Masters-2023-10]<sup>: 9 </sup> His Medal for Gallantry citation states that Roberts-Smith's actions while under "fire and in a precarious position, threatened by a numerically superior force, are testament to his courage, tenacity and sense of duty to his patrol".[^cite_note-AWM2-11][^cite_note-18]

#### Victoria Cross

After completing junior leadership training in 2009, Roberts-Smith was posted to 2 Squadron, SASR as a patrol [second-in-command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second-in-command) (2IC). On 11 June 2010, at Tizak in the Kandahar Province, he and other SASR soldiers were on a mission searching for a Taliban commander during the [Shah Wali Kot Offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shah_Wali_Kot_Offensive).[^cite_note-DoD-8][^cite_note-McKenzie-2023-4]<sup>: 20 </sup>[^cite_note-Cowan-2011-6] Immediately upon helicopter insertion, the soldiers became pinned down by machine gun and rocket-propelled grenade (RPG) fire from multiple positions.[^cite_note-DoD-8][^cite_note-McKenzie-2023-4]<sup>: 20 </sup> With air support, Roberts-Smith and his patrol manoeuvred to within 70 metres of their opponents' positions to launch an assault. When the assault began, the patrol drew sustained fire and was unable to advance closer than 40 metres from their objective. Roberts-Smith then identified a structure from which he would be able to get cover from fire. He approached the position and killed an enemy soldier armed with an RPG. The rest of his patrol was still pinned down by machine gun fire from three positions. He exposed his location to the enemy to draw fire away from the rest of his patrol. This was successful, and Roberts-Smith's commander was able to throw a grenade, which neutralised one of the positions. Disregarding his own safety, Roberts-Smith then stormed the remaining two positions.[^cite_note-DoD-8][^cite_note-McKenzie-2023-4]<sup>: 20 </sup>

For his actions at Tizak he was presented with the Victoria Cross for Australia[^cite_note-20] (VC) by the [governor-general of Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor-general_of_Australia), [Quentin Bryce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quentin_Bryce), at a ceremony held at Campbell Barracks on 23 January 2011.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22] After being awarded the VC, he became "Australia's most highly decorated combat soldier from the conflict".[^cite_note-Chrysanthos-2025-23][^cite_note-Yan_Zhuang-2021-24] During defamation proceedings in 2022, Person 41, a serving SASR member, testified that fellow soldiers Person 7 and Person 42 believed the events leading to Roberts-Smith's VC may have been falsified and that he was undeserving of the award.[^cite_note-McKinnell-2022-25][^cite_note-The_Guardian-2022-26][^cite_note-Whitbourn-2022-27] Person 7 and Person 42, who were given pseudonyms for national security reasons, were senior soldiers who appeared in court as witnesses against Roberts-Smith in defamation proceedings.[^cite_note-Doherty-2022a-28][^cite_note-Doherty-2022b-29][^cite_note-McKenzie-2026-30]

#### Patrol commander

Roberts-Smith's final deployment to Afghanistan was in 2012, by which time he had been promoted to corporal and served as a patrol commander.[^cite_note-McKenzie-2023-4]<sup>: 30 </sup>[^cite_note-Masters-2023-10]<sup>: 21 </sup> He left the full-time army in 2013 at age thirty-five, then joining the Army Reserve.[^cite_note-ABC-2013-31] Roberts-Smith was awarded the [Commendation for Distinguished Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commendation_for_Distinguished_Service) as part of the [2014 Australia Day Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Australia_Day_Honours) on 26 January 2014.[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33] The award arose from the 2012 tour of Afghanistan, in which he "distinguished himself as an outstanding junior leader on more than 50 high risk" operations.[^cite_note-34] Roberts-Smith left the Army Reserve in 2015.[^cite_note-35]

## Post service career and war crime allegations

Further information: [War crimes in Afghanistan by the Australian Defence Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_crimes_in_Afghanistan_by_the_Australian_Defence_Force)

The University of Queensland (UQ) offered Roberts-Smith a scholarship to study a [Master of Business Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Business_Administration) (MBA) in October 2013, after he announced that he was leaving the full-time army. At this time the university was looking to establish a program to support soldiers to enter corporate roles.[^cite_note-ABC-2013-31][^cite_note-Koopman-2016-36]

[Samantha Crompvoets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samantha_Crompvoets), an expert on organisational culture, was hired by the Department of Defence to investigate cultural problems in the Australian Special Forces in 2015.[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38] Crompvoets reported serious allegations concerning conduct in Afghanistan between 2009 and 2013. It was alleged to her that during this period, the SASR unlawfully killed thirty-nine Afghan nationals, in incidents characterised as "competition killing" and driven by blood lust.[^cite_note-Crompvoets-2021-14]<sup>: vii </sup>[^cite_note-39]

In April 2015, Roberts-Smith was appointed by Seven West Media's owner [Kerry Stokes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerry_Stokes) to be deputy general manager of regional television for [Seven Queensland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Queensland), based in Maroochydore. Two months later, he was promoted to general manager.[^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41] When he was first promoted to general manager, some staff joked "that it would have been difficult to find a less qualified person than Roberts-Smith".[^cite_note-McKenzie-2023-4]<sup>: 94 </sup> He also admitted that he was "shocked" at being offered the position.[^cite_note-McKenzie-2023-4]<sup>: 94 </sup>

In her 2016 report, *[Special Operations Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Operations_Command_\(Australia\)) (SOCOMD) Culture and Interactions: Insights and Reflections*, Crompvoets recounted that a source described 2012 as the worst year, with recurring but uninvestigated reports from an Afghan interpreter that special forces soldiers were executing farmers. Crompvoets had soldiers confide to her that there was a culture of strong pressure to conform, and that some committed atrocities to fit in. Patrol commanders were identified as wielding excessive power, reportedly acting with impunity and beyond the control of their officers.[^cite_note-Masters-2023-10]<sup>: 16 </sup> The [Brereton inquiry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brereton_inquiry), conducted by the [Inspector-General of the Australian Defence Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inspector-General_of_the_Australian_Defence_Force), was established to examine the claims of war crimes raised in Crompvoets's report.[^cite_note-McKenzie-2023-4]<sup>: 111 </sup>

In April 2016, Roberts-Smith was also made general manager of Seven Brisbane following the resignation of [Max Walters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell_Walters).[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43] He graduated with his MBA from UQ in December 2016 at age 38 and said, "I joined the army at 18 so I hadn't gone to university for a Bachelor degree and I didn't have the base level of business knowledge because there were many things I just hadn't been exposed to."[^cite_note-Koopman-2016-36][^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45]

In October 2017, journalist [Chris Masters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Masters_\(writer\)) published *No Front Line*, a history of Australian special forces in Afghanistan. The book drew attention to discrepancies between Roberts-Smith's own accounts of a 2006 incident in which he and Matthew Locke killed an Afghan man they suspected of being a Taliban spotter. [ABC News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABC_News_\(Australia\)) journalist Dan Oakes reported on the book, linking it to the ongoing Brereton inquiry. Responding to the publication of Masters's book, Roberts-Smith described it as inaccurate and called the scrutiny of his record "[un-Australian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Un-Australian)". He also said the book was damaging to the legacy of Locke, who had been killed in action.[^cite_note-46][^cite_note-Dalton-2017-16]

In June 2018, a joint ABC and [Fairfax Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairfax_Media) investigation detailed an assault on the village of Darwan in September 2012 during which a handcuffed man was kicked off a cliff by an Australian special forces soldier nicknamed "Leonidas" after the famed [Spartan king](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonidas_I).[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49] Roberts-Smith was identified in later reporting as the soldier previously referred to as "Leonidas".[^cite_note-Parkes-Hupton-2023-50] On 6 July 2018, Fairfax Media reported that he was "one of a small number of soldiers subject to investigation by an inquiry looking into the actions of Australian special forces soldiers in Afghanistan".[^cite_note-51] The next month, Fairfax Media reported that Roberts-Smith bullied several of his fellow soldiers and alleged that he had assaulted a woman he was having an affair with. Roberts-Smith denied the bullying and assault allegations.[^cite_note-52]

In response to these 2018 allegations, Roberts-Smith commenced [defamation proceedings in the Federal Court against Fairfax Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roberts-Smith_v_Fairfax_Media_Publications_Pty_Limited) (a subsidiary of Nine Entertainment) and two Fairfax journalists, Nick McKenzie and Chris Masters, as well as a former Fairfax journalist, David Wroe.[^cite_note-53] Fairfax mounted a truth defence, based on its reporting being "[substantially true](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substantially_true)". As part of this defence, Fairfax detailed a series of six unlawful killings alleged to have been carried out by Roberts-Smith in Afghanistan, including those in Darwan.[^cite_note-54] The defamation trial was labelled by lawyers and media as "the trial of the century".[^cite_note-McKenzie-2023-4]<sup>: 220 </sup>[^cite_note-55]

In November 2018, the [Australian Federal Police](341221.md) (AFP) announced that they "received a referral to investigate allegations of war crimes committed by Australian soldiers during the Afghanistan conflict".[^cite_note-56] That same month, former AFP commissioner [Mick Keelty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mick_Keelty) informed Roberts-Smith that the AFP had received referrals regarding alleged war crimes.[^cite_note-Middleton-2024-57] Keelty's disclosure came days after the AFP began a covert operation targeting Roberts-Smith. After learning from Keelty that he was under investigation, Roberts-Smith allegedly began using burner phones to obstruct it.[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-McKenzie-2023-4]<sup>: 191–201 </sup>

### Civil trial and judgment (2020–2023)

In August 2020, it was reported that legal experts had raised concerns about a personal relationship between Roberts-Smith and his defamation lawyer, saying it could constitute unprofessional conduct by her. News Corp Australia published a photo of Roberts-Smith holding hands with the lawyer, who they reported was visiting him at his new apartment in Brisbane.[^cite_note-59] The lawyer conceded that it was "unwise to spend time with him socially".[^cite_note-60]

On 1 September 2020, Fairfax/Nine Entertainment lawyer [Sandy Dawson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandy_Dawson) told the Federal Court that the Australian Federal Police had information, including an eyewitness, that allegedly implicated Roberts-Smith in Afghanistan war crimes.[^cite_note-61] The Federal Court of Australia noted later that month that no charges against Roberts-Smith had been laid.[^cite_note-62]

In November 2020, the Federal Court heard that Roberts-Smith had asked his wife to falsely claim they were separated during his affair with a woman referred to in the trial as Person 17, contradicting the couple's earlier public statements.[^cite_note-63] That same month, Kerry Stokes' private investment company, Australian Capital Equity (ACE), extended Roberts-Smith a line of credit to fund his legal action, against which he drew [A\$](81215.md)1.9 million.[^cite_note-64] Stokes and another director of ACE were also on the board of the Australian War Memorial (AWM). Calls were made at the time for Stokes, as then AWM chairman, to stand down over his public and private support for soldiers accused of war crimes in Afghanistan.[^cite_note-65]

The findings of the Brereton inquiry, the Brereton Report, were published on 19 November 2020. The report recommended that dozens of incidents involving current and former special forces personnel be referred for criminal investigation.[^cite_note-66]

In April 2021, the AFP confirmed it was conducting a probe into allegations that Roberts-Smith had destroyed or buried evidence directly related to the ongoing investigation.[^cite_note-67] An article published that month alleged that Roberts-Smith had attempted to cover up the alleged crimes by hiding incriminating images on a USB drive buried in his backyard, which had since been obtained by the Australian Federal Police.[^cite_note-68] Also in April 2021, Roberts-Smith temporarily stepped down from Seven Queensland to focus on his defamation action against Nine Entertainment.[^cite_note-69]

In June 2021, *ABC News* reported that 21 current and former SAS soldiers would make an "unprecedented" appearance at court to testify against Roberts-Smith in the defamation trial.[^cite_note-70] The defamation trial, expected to last for ten weeks, commenced that month in Sydney.[^cite_note-71]

An SAS soldier described an incident to the court where he witnessed Roberts-Smith kick a handcuffed farmer (Ali Jan) "in the chest" and made the Afghan fall backwards over a cliff edge with his face hitting a large rock, knocking out a number of his teeth, with Roberts-Smith later ordering his execution with another SAS operator under his command shooting the farmer dead. Other SAS veterans testified that Roberts-Smith machine gunned an already detained and unarmed Afghan with a prosthetic leg in 2009. Evidence from the eyewitnesses corroborated that Roberts-Smith also pressured junior soldiers to execute unarmed prisoners.[^cite_note-McKenzie-2026-30] A colleague of Roberts-Smith, referred to as Person 16, told the court that Roberts-Smith had shot dead an unarmed Afghan teenage prisoner in 2012 and bragged about it.[^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73]

It was alleged during defamation proceedings in February 2022 that Roberts-Smith had employed a private investigator, John McLeod, to pose as a barman during a Seven Queensland work event to listen to staff at the event and discern their opinions of him.[^cite_note-74]

Several serving members of the SASR provided testimony during the trial regarding bullying and threats made by Roberts-Smith during his service both within Australia and Afghanistan. Person 1, the SASR member whose weapon had jammed during the 2006 battle in the Chora Valley, said that Roberts-Smith had stated he would "put a bullet in the back of his head" if he did not improve his performance. After this, Person 1 was advised by other members to report Roberts-Smith's threat which he did, leading to Roberts-Smith threatening him again, stating: "If you're going to make accusations, cunt, you better have some fucking proof." Reports of Roberts-Smith's bullying were also reiterated by Person 43 and Person 10, also serving members of the SASR.[^cite_note-75][^cite_note-76] During defamation proceedings that year, Person 41, a serving SASR member, testified that fellow soldiers Person 7 and Person 42 believed the events leading to Roberts-Smith's Victoria Cross may have been falsified and that he was undeserving of the award.[^cite_note-McKinnell-2022-25][^cite_note-The_Guardian-2022-26][^cite_note-Whitbourn-2022-27] Person 7 and Person 42, who were given pseudonyms for national security reasons, were senior soldiers who appeared in court as witnesses against Roberts-Smith in defamation proceedings.[^cite_note-Doherty-2022a-28][^cite_note-Doherty-2022b-29][^cite_note-McKenzie-2026-30]

Fairfax Media's defence against Roberts-Smith's suit concluded in early April 2022 after calling witnesses for eleven weeks.[^cite_note-77] Evidence submissions ended in July 2022 after 110 days of information gathering during which 41 witnesses were called.[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-McKenzie-2026-30] Among the witnesses were 32 Australian soldiers, of whom some had served alongside Roberts-Smith, as well as several civilian eyewitnesses from Afghanistan.[^cite_note-79] According to the *Sydney Morning Herald*, the soldiers "testified in public and on oath" but, due to national security orders, their identities were suppressed and they were referred to in court using pseudonyms such as Person 1, Person 14 and Person 18.[^cite_note-McKenzie-2026-30]

Roberts-Smith's uniform on display in the Australian War Memorial in 2026, with a label noting the findings of the defamation case and appeals

On 1 June 2023, Justice Anthony Besanko dismissed the defamation case brought by Roberts-Smith. Besanko found that the newspapers on trial—*The Sydney Morning Herald*, *The Age* and *The Canberra Times*—had established substantial or contextual truth of many of their allegations, including that Roberts-Smith "broke the moral and legal rules of military engagement and is therefore a criminal".[^cite_note-80][^cite_note-Doherty-2023-81][^cite_note-Visontay-2023-82] As a defamation suit is a civil proceeding, Besanko was required by the Evidence Act 1995 (Cth) to assess the evidence using the civil standard of proof, the [balance of probabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burden_of_proof_\(law\)#Civil_standard), instead of the criminal standard of proof, [beyond reasonable doubt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burden_of_proof_\(law\)#Criminal_standard).[^cite_note-Doherty-2023-81][^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84] Due to the gravity of the allegations, Besanko followed the [Briginshaw principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Briginshaw_principle), which required stronger evidence than would be necessary for a less serious matter.[^cite_note-85][^cite_note-86]

Besanko found that four murder allegations against Roberts-Smith had been proven.[^cite_note-87][^cite_note-88] The judgment concluded that it was substantially true that:

- during the Whiskey 108 mission in 2009, Roberts-Smith committed murder "by machine gunning a man with a prosthetic leg". Roberts-Smith later asked other soldiers to drink from the prosthetic leg;

  [^cite_note-Whitbourn-2023-89]

  [^cite_note-McKinnell-2023-90]

  [^cite_note-The_Guardian-2023-91]

- during the same Whiskey 108 mission Roberts-Smith committed murder "by pressuring a newly deployed and inexperienced SASR soldier to execute an elderly, unarmed Afghan in order to 'blood the rookie'";

  [^cite_note-Whitbourn-2023-89]

  [^cite_note-McKinnell-2023-90]

  [^cite_note-The_Guardian-2023-91]

- during the Darwan mission in September 2012, Roberts-Smith "murdered an unarmed and defenceless Afghan civilian, by kicking him off a cliff and procuring the soldiers under his command to shoot him";

  [^cite_note-Whitbourn-2023-89]

  [^cite_note-McKinnell-2023-90]

  [^cite_note-The_Guardian-2023-91]

- during the Chinartu mission in October 2012, Roberts-Smith ordered another soldier "to shoot an Afghan male who was under detention"; with instructions being given "to an [NDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Directorate_of_Security)-Wakunish soldier who then shot the Afghan male in circumstances amounting to murder", rendering Roberts-Smith "complicit in and responsible for murder".

  [^cite_note-92]

  [^cite_note-93]

It was ruled that two allegations of murder at Syahchow and Fasil in 2012 were not proven.[^cite_note-Whitbourn-2023-89][^cite_note-Visontay-2023-82]

Besanko separately found that it was proven that:

- in 2010, Roberts-Smith physically attacked an unarmed Afghan man until two patrol commanders ordered him to stop;

  [^cite_note-Wootton-2023-94]

- in 2012, Roberts-Smith assaulted a second unarmed Afghan man and authorised the assault of a third unarmed Afghan man who was being held in custody and did not pose a threat;

  [^cite_note-Wootton-2023-94]

  &#x20;and

- Roberts-Smith engaged in a "campaign of bullying" and threatened violence against an Australian soldier.

  [^cite_note-Whitbourn-2023-89]

  [^cite_note-Wootton-2023-94]

Allegations that Roberts-Smith committed domestic violence and threatened to report another soldier to the [International Criminal Court](14880.md) were found to have not been proven. The judgement concluded that this did not further harm Roberts-Smith's reputation given the other substantially true allegations, thus establishing contextual truth.[^cite_note-Visontay-2023-82] Judge Besanko also stated that Roberts-Smith was not a reliable witness due to having an obvious motive to lie. Besanko also stated that he believed that Roberts-Smith had threatened a soldier who gave testimony against him.[^cite_note-95]

Roberts-Smith resigned from Seven on 2 June 2023, following the case's unsuccessful outcome.[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-97] Later in June 2023, the ABC reported allegations that he directed another SASR soldier to kill an elderly [imam](15240.md) during an August 2012 operation in Afghanistan. It was alleged that this led to the man being dragged from a mosque and killed despite him being unarmed and a prisoner of the Australians. The incident was among those which the Brereton Report recommended be considered by war crimes investigators.[^cite_note-98]

On 15 June 2023, Roberts-Smith stated that he was proud of his actions in Afghanistan and would not be apologising.[^cite_note-99] Later in June, he accepted his liability to pay the legal costs of his failed defamation suit from 17 March 2020 against the three newspapers.[^cite_note-McKinnell-2023a-100][^cite_note-McKinnell-2023b-101] Chris Masters, one of the respondents in the case, stated that Nine Entertainment spent approximately \$30 million defending it.[^cite_note-McKinnell-2023a-100]

In 2023, [Kim Beazley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Beazley), Chair of the Australian War Memorial Council, acknowledged "the gravity of the decision in the Ben Roberts-Smith VC MG defamation case and its broader impact on all involved in the Australian community".[^cite_note-AWM-102] Beazley added that careful consideration was being given to the additional content and context to be included in collection items on display.[^cite_note-AWM-102][^cite_note-Chrysanthos-2025-23]

In November 2023, it was ruled that Roberts-Smith should pay approximately ninety-five per cent of the costs incurred by Nine Entertainment from when he began proceedings against them in 2018.[^cite_note-103] In December 2023, Kerry Stokes's private investment company, Australian Capital Equity, was ordered to pay costs.[^cite_note-104][^cite_note-105]

### Appeals (2023–2025)

On 11 July 2023, Roberts-Smith filed an appeal against Justice Besanko's judgment to the Full Court of the Federal Court, after the court granted an extension to the standard deadline for lodging it.[^cite_note-McKinnell-2023b-101][^cite_note-106] Nine Entertainment said it would oppose the appeal.[^cite_note-McKinnell-2023b-101] In October, the Federal Court ordered Roberts-Smith to pay \$910,000 security for costs, ahead of the appeal trial.[^cite_note-107] Commencing on 5 February 2024, the Full Court heard Roberts-Smith's appeal over ten days.[^cite_note-108][^cite_note-Mason-2025-109][^cite_note-110]

In June 2024, Roberts-Smith attended [Government House, Perth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_House,_Perth), to receive the [King Charles III Coronation Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Charles_III_Coronation_Medal) from the [governor of Western Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor_of_Western_Australia), [Chris Dawson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Dawson_\(governor\)), bestowed by King [Charles III](125248.md) on all living Australian recipients of the Victoria Cross.[^cite_note-111] Australian Prime Minister [Anthony Albanese](556745.md) commented that the decision to include Roberts-Smith had been made by Buckingham Palace and not the Australian government, although they had previously indicated to the palace that they had "no objections" to the making of the awards.[^cite_note-112][^cite_note-113]

In August 2024, former AFP commissioner Mick Keelty gave up his Order of Australia honour, six years after his 2018 disclosure to Roberts-Smith.[^cite_note-Middleton-2024-57]

*The Sydney Morning Herald* reported in September 2024 that Roberts-Smith had attended a recent Australian Defence Force gala dinner to celebrate the sixtieth anniversary of the SASR and that some who had served in the special forces found this inappropriate. The same article said that the Office of the Special Investigator could soon bring criminal charges against Roberts-Smith, including for war crimes and other criminal offences.[^cite_note-114]

Lawyers for Roberts-Smith filed an interlocutory application with the Federal Court on 27 March 2025, seeking to amend his appeal after a secret recording emerged of Nick McKenzie appearing to say he had been briefed on Roberts-Smith's legal strategy by his ex-partner and her friend, which his lawyers argued may have affected the outcome of the trial.[^cite_note-115][^cite_note-Meade-2025-116] Roberts-Smith's lawyers sought "wide-ranging subpoenas" as part of the application to re-open the appeal, which Nine Entertainment's lawyers opposed, calling them a "fishing expedition".[^cite_note-Duffin-2025-117] Justice [Nye Perram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nye_Perram) denied the majority of Roberts-Smith's subpoenas, allowing only subpoenas for communications between Nick McKenzie's and Nine Entertainment's lawyers, and between Roberts-Smith and a friend of his ex-partner.[^cite_note-Duffin-2025-117]

In May 2025, the secret recording between McKenzie and a witness known as Person 17, was admitted into evidence.[^cite_note-Mason-2025-109] McKenzie was recorded telling Person 17 that Roberts-Smith's ex-partner and her friend had been "actively briefing us on his legal strategy in respect of you ... we anticipated most of it. One or two things now we know", Roberts-Smith's lawyers told the court.[^cite_note-118] In court, Roberts-Smith argued that his ex-partner had accessed his email account and passed on [privileged communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privileged_communication) to McKenzie.[^cite_note-119]

Roberts-Smith lost his appeal against Besanko's ruling on 16 May 2025.[^cite_note-Lyons-2025-120][^cite_note-121] The Full Court of the Federal Court unanimously found that he was not defamed when Nick McKenzie and Chris Masters published reports alleging he had committed war crimes in Afghanistan.[^cite_note-Lyons-2025-120][^cite_note-Whitbourn-2025-122][^cite_note-123] "We are unanimously of the opinion that the evidence was sufficiently cogent to support the findings that \[Roberts-Smith] ... murdered four Afghan men" the court stated, ordering Roberts-Smith to pay the respondents' legal costs.[^cite_note-Whitbourn-2025-122] The interlocutory application to amend the appeal was also dismissed, with judges expressing doubts about the integrity of the secret recording, stating they could not be confident "that the contents of the recording have not been doctored by removing sections or splicing together different parts of a recorded conversation".[^cite_note-Meade-2025-116][^cite_note-124] The trial cost the parties a combined \$30 million in legal costs, with the appeal costing a further \$4 million.[^cite_note-Whitbourn-2025a-125]

On 17 June 2025, Roberts-Smith sought special leave in the High Court of Australia to [appeal](314201.md#Appellate_jurisdiction) against the judgment of the Full Federal Court.[^cite_note-Whitbourn-2025a-125] Roberts-Smith argued that Besanko's judgment had branded him a war criminal, despite coming as a consequence of civil proceedings; and that the [rules of evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rules_of_evidence) should have been applied more strictly, given that Roberts-Smith had not been charged with any criminal offence and was entitled to a presumption of innocence.[^cite_note-126]

On 4 September 2025, the High Court refused his application for leave to appeal and ordered that he pay costs—which will include the further cost of the application to the High Court.[^cite_note-127] Following the High Court's rejection of Roberts-Smith's final appeal that month, the Australian War Memorial updated the wording of his display to state that he had exhausted all legal avenues to challenge the defamation judgment.[^cite_note-128]

#### Funding

Throughout his legal actions, Roberts-Smith has been supported by several prominent Australians.[^cite_note-Masters-2025-129] Stokes funded his original civil proceedings, as noted above.[^cite_note-Masters-2025-129][^cite_note-Mason-2025a-130] [John Singleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Singleton_\(Australian_entrepreneur\)) paid for newspaper advertising which called attacks on Roberts-Smith "disgraceful".[^cite_note-Masters-2025-129] In May 2025, [Gina Rinehart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gina_Rinehart), Australia's richest person, said: "Many patriotic Australians query, is it fair that this brave and patriotic man who risked his life on overseas missions which he was sent on by our government, is under such attack."[^cite_note-Mason-2025a-130] When questioned, Rinehart refused to rule out funding Roberts-Smith's defamation action.[^cite_note-Mason-2025a-130] The media respondents obtained and served a Federal Court subpoena for Rinehart to produce documents showing that she or any of her companies were involved in funding Roberts-Smith's legal proceedings.[^cite_note-131] However, they later accepted Rinehart's lawyers' assurance that she possessed no such documents.[^cite_note-132]

On 9 September 2025, the Federal Court ordered Roberts-Smith [together with](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_and_several_liability) Kerry Stokes's private company Australian Capital Equity to pay costs fixed at \$13,270,950 to the media respondents, plus a costs assessment fee of \$224,872 for the application for costs.[^cite_note-133][^cite_note-134]

### Arrest and charges (2026)

| video icon [Vision of Ben Roberts-Smith under arrest at Sydney Airport (7 April 2026)](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-04-07/vision-of-ben-roberts-smith-under-arrest-at-sydney-airport/106539106)[^cite_note-136] |
| video icon [AFP & OSI press conference announcing arrest and charges (7 April 2026)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Iistx5Ulr84)[^cite_note-137] |

The abandonment of the earlier AFP probe led to the establishment of a new joint task force with personnel from the [Office of the Special Investigator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_the_Special_Investigator) and a new team of AFP investigators, known as Operation Emerald-Argon, to investigate the allegations.[^cite_note-138][^cite_note-139]

On 7 April 2026, Roberts-Smith was arrested by the Australian Federal Police at [Sydney Airport](266313.md).[^cite_note-140][^cite_note-141][^cite_note-Bucci-2026-142] He was subsequently charged under section 268.70(1) of the [Criminal Code Act 1995](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_Code_Act_1995) with five counts of the [war crime](48626.md) of murder[^cite_note-warcrime-144] (three charges of aid, abet, counsel, or procure an offence, one charge of actual offence and one charge of joint commission of an offence) with a potential maximum sentence of [life imprisonment](148681.md). The charges relate to the alleged killing of an Afghan man in Kakarak in April 2009, and the deaths of civilians in Darwan and Syahchow in September and October 2012.[^cite_note-145][^cite_note-146][^cite_note-147] He was remanded to the [Silverwater Correctional Complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silverwater_Correctional_Complex) to appear before the online bail court on 8 April 2026.[^cite_note-Bucci-2026-142] His legal counsel declined to apply for bail at that hearing.[^cite_note-148] Subject to strict conditions and a \$250,000 surety, bail was granted on 17 April.[^cite_note-149][^cite_note-150][^cite_note-Pengilley-2026-151]

Roberts-Smith has sought funding for his defence from the Afghanistan Inquiry Legal Assistance Scheme, a [Commonwealth Attorney-General's Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attorney-General_of_Australia) program that provides assistance for legal representation and related costs for current and former ADF members who have been "charged with a crime in relation to matters that are the subject of the AFP or OSI Afghanistan investigation".[^cite_note-152] Although no formal court plea has been entered, Roberts-Smith said in a public statement on 19 April 2026 that he denies the charges.[^cite_note-Pengilley-2026-151] On 1 September 2026, Judge Susan Horan made 206 orders under the National Security Information Act to allow national security information to be provided to Roberts-Smith in a brief of evidence.[^cite_note-153][^cite_note-\:1-154] At a hearing on 8 September 2026, prosecutors advised a local court that they had provided his defence with a "substantial proportion" of the evidence against him, including about 4,000 documents and 234 witness statements. Of the persons named in the evidence provided to Roberts-Smith, 723 had pseudonyms in line with the orders made a week earlier. His lawyer, Karen Espiner, argued to Judge Horan that the use of so many pseudonyms would make it harder to prepare a defence against the charges of the war crime of murder. The matter is due to return to court on 13 October 2026.[^cite_note-155][^cite_note-\:1-154]

## Public perceptions

Ben Roberts-Smith placing a wreath at a memorial

Ben Roberts-Smith placing a wreath at the [Tomb of the Unknown Soldier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_the_Unknown_Soldier), [Arlington National Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arlington_National_Cemetery), USA, in 2012

Prior to the publication of war crimes and domestic violence allegations, Roberts-Smith was widely regarded as one of Australia's most admired and highly decorated soldiers.[^cite_note-156][^cite_note-McKinnell-2021-157] He was considered a national hero.[^cite_note-Parkes-Hupton-2023-50] Roberts-Smith was the [Fremantle Football Club](312661.md)'s [number-one ticket holder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number-one_ticket_holder) for the 2012 to 2015 [Australian Football League](4732785.md) seasons.[^cite_note-158][^cite_note-159] In 2013, he was named [Australian Father of the Year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Father_of_the_Year) by The Shepherd Centre, a not-for-profit charitable organisation.[^cite_note-Parkes-Hupton-2023-50][^cite_note-160] In 2014, Roberts-Smith was made deputy chair for [Tony Abbott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Abbott)'s mental health advisory committee.[^cite_note-161]

The combat uniform Roberts-Smith wore during the battle in Afghanistan for which he was awarded the Victoria Cross is displayed at the Australian War Memorial.[^cite_note-162][^cite_note-Yan_Zhuang-2021-24][^cite_note-163] The Memorial also commissioned a 2014 painting of him, [Michael Zavros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Zavros)'s [*Pistol Grip*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pistol_Grip_\(Ben_Roberts-Smith_VC\)).[^cite_note-164] From 2014 to 2017, Roberts-Smith served as Chair of the [National Australia Day Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Australia_Day_Council), a [social enterprise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_enterprise) owned by the Australian Government.[^cite_note-165] His voice, alongside others, was featured on Australian country singer [Lee Kernaghan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Kernaghan)'s 2015 track "Lest We Forget" from the album [*Spirit of the Anzacs*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirit_of_the_Anzacs_\(album\)).[^cite_note-166] The [National Portrait Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Portrait_Gallery_\(Australia\)) commissioned a photographic portrait of him by Julian Kingma in 2018.[^cite_note-167]

Roberts-Smith said that after the allegations against him were published he lost approximately \$475,000 worth of speaking engagements.[^cite_note-McKinnell-2021-157][^cite_note-Mason-2021-168] He had been offered a partnership at consulting firm [PwC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PwC); however, after he had informed them of the allegations, the job had not proceeded.[^cite_note-Mason-2021-168] When his civil action was dismissed in June 2023, Justice Besanko found that Roberts-Smith's "reputation has been lowered so far ... that two unproven allegations of battlefield murder weren't enough to defame him", and that he had "disgraced his country Australia and the Australian army by his conduct as a member of the SASR in Afghanistan".[^cite_note-Parkes-Hupton-2023-50]

Roberts-Smith's arrest and prosecution has generally divided opinion along existing political and cultural lines. Former prime ministers [John Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Howard) and [Tony Abbott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Abbott) have both defended him publicly. Howard described him as the "modern personification of the [Anzac tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anzac_spirit)". Abbott said the rules of engagement in Afghanistan had been overly restrictive and that combat actions should not be judged by civilian standards. One Nation senator Pauline Hanson has voiced continuing support. Support for Roberts-Smith has extended to figures on the far right. Not all conservative figures have backed him unconditionally. [Liberal Party](18453.md) politician [Andrew Hastie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Hastie), who had testified against Roberts-Smith during the defamation trial, said that no one was above the law. [Columnist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columnist) [Andrew Bolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Bolt) challenged Roberts-Smith's supporters directly. He asked them to consider the specific nature of the allegations, including the killing of unarmed and injured prisoners.[^cite_note-169]

## Personal life

Roberts-Smith met his former wife, Emma Groom, in 1998 at an army ball in Sydney when he was 19.[^cite_note-McKenzie-2023-4]<sup>: 22 </sup> She came from a military family. On 6 December 2003, they married at the [University of Western Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Western_Australia).[^cite_note-170][^cite_note-McKenzie-2023-4]<sup>: 48 </sup> Their twin daughters were born in 2010. On retirement from the army in 2015, Roberts-Smith moved to Queensland with his wife and daughters.[^cite_note-171]

In April 2018, Person 17 arrived at Groom's house and showed her hundreds of [text messages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS) sent by Roberts-Smith, revealing that Roberts-Smith had been having an affair.[^cite_note-McKenzie-2023-4]<sup>: xiii, 145–147 </sup> Roberts-Smith's and Groom's divorce was finalised in December 2020.[^cite_note-172][^cite_note-173][^cite_note-174] In January 2022, Roberts-Smith was ordered to pay his former wife's legal costs after unsuccessfully trying to sue her in the Federal Court over allegations that she had accessed confidential emails.[^cite_note-175]
