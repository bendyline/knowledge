During [his second term](78298108.md) as [president of the United States](24113.md), [Donald Trump](4848272.md) enacted a series of steep [tariffs](55551.md) affecting [nearly all goods imported into the country](52437025.md).[^cite_note-1] From January to April 2025, the overall average effective US tariff rate rose from 2.5% to an estimated 27%—the highest level in over a century.[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-Reddy-3] After changes, negotiations, and the invalidation of certain tariffs by the [Supreme Court of the United States](31737.md), the overall average effective tariff rate was 12.1% as of July 21, 2026.[^cite_note-yale-4] The items most affected by the tariffs are metals, items mostly made of metal, and vehicles.

Although the [Constitution of the United States](31644.md) grants [Congress](31756.md) the sole authority to levy taxes—including tariffs—since 1930, Congress has passed laws allowing the president to impose tariffs unilaterally for national security reasons.[^cite_note-Looney-5]

Under [Section 232](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_232) of the 1962 [Trade Expansion Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_Expansion_Act), Trump raised tariffs on [cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cars), [steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel), [aluminum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminum), [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper), and related derivative products as high as 50%. New tariffs on large-scale batteries, cast iron and iron fittings, plastic piping, industrial chemicals and power grid and telecom equipment are under consideration.[^cite_note-6]

In April 2025, Trump also claimed unprecedented authority under the [International Emergency Economic Powers Act](2434972.md) (IEEPA) to impose [Liberation Day tariffs](79617151.md) of at least 10% on goods from nearly all countries. Trump also used the law to impose punitive tariffs against several countries and to order the early closure of the [*de minimis* exemption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_minimis_exemption). In February 2026, the Supreme Court ruled the IEEPA tariffs illegal in the case *[Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump](80234268.md)*.[^cite_note-7] The government collected an estimated \$166 billion in IEEPA tariffs from more than 330,000 businesses that was required to be refunded.[^cite_note-refund-8]

After the decision of the Supreme Court, Trump announced a global tariff of 10% under [Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_122_of_the_Trade_Act_of_1974), to remain in effect for 150 days, until July 24, 2026.[^cite_note-Tobin-9][^cite_note-global-10] After a lawsuit, the [United States Court of International Trade](1220886.md) ruled that these tariffs are also illegal and the case is being appealed.[^cite_note-replacement-11]

Trump invoked [Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974](5916549.md), which allows the USTR to investigate and penalize foreign trade practices deemed unfair, to impose or threaten tariffs on goods produced using [forced labor](541048.md) in 60 countries. He also used Section 338 of the [Tariff Act of 1930](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoot%E2%80%93Hawley_Tariff_Act) (Smoot–Hawley) to impose tariffs of 50% on motor vehicles, dairy, and alcohol imports from Canada, not presently effective.

The Trump administration argues that its tariffs will promote domestic manufacturing, protect national security, and substitute for federal income taxes. The administration views [trade deficits](4810.md) as inherently harmful, a stance economists criticized as a flawed understanding of trade.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-Schneid-13][^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15] Studies have shown that the tariffs have increased expenses and reduced earnings for companies,[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-Popat-17][^cite_note-J.P._Morgan_Chase-18][^cite_note-Al_Jazeera-19] and have increased costs for households.[^cite_note-Popat-17] The promised growth in manufacturing jobs has not been realized.[^cite_note-Egan-20] Corporate bankruptcies increased to the highest level since 2010.[^cite_note-Bankrupt-21][^cite_note-Bankrupt-aol-22][^cite_note-Wallace-23] However, although many economists predicted slower growth and even a possible [recession](25382.md) due to the tariffs, US [GDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GDP) has continued to grow. This was partially attributed to Trump's backtracking on the initial high tariff rates.[^cite_note-Miller-24][^cite_note-Sargen-25] There has also been no definitive indication of a significant aggregate effect on the labor market so far; however, industries most exposed to tariffs show some signs of weakness relative to the pre-2025 trend.[^cite_note-tracking-26]

## Background

See also: [History of tariffs in the United States § 2000 to present: Deindustrialization and wage deflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_tariffs_in_the_United_States#2000_to_present:_Deindustrialization_and_wage_deflation), and [Protectionism in the United States § 1980s to present](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protectionism_in_the_United_States#1980s_to_present)

During the 1990s and 2000s, [globalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globalization) and [international trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_trade) expanded as improvements in communications and shipping technology made [offshoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offshoring) more feasible and trade deals reduced tariffs and other barriers to commerce.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28] Although the aggregate gains to the United States were substantial, the benefits were unevenly distributed; high-skilled workers generally prospered, while Americans with fewer skills and less geographic mobility struggled to adapt to rapid economic change.[^cite_note-29][^cite_note-Rosalsky-30]

Although U.S. manufacturing employment had been declining for decades, job losses accelerated in the 2000s after the United States normalized trade relations with China.[^cite_note-Casselman-31] The surge in [outsourcing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outsourcing), alongside advancements in [automation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automation), led to localized depressions in many manufacturing-dependent communities.[^cite_note-Rosalsky-30][^cite_note-Casselman-31] Laid off manufacturing workers typically did not relocate or transition into new industries, even after their communities rebounded, and instead remained unemployed long term.[^cite_note-Rosalsky-30] Research indicates that these regions became more politically polarized and that their legislators increasingly supported protectionist policies.[^cite_note-Frieden-32]

During the 2000s, public opinion surveys and political rhetoric began to reflect growing skepticism toward international economic integration, a trend that intensified following the [2008 financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_financial_crisis).[^cite_note-Frieden-32] In 2008, [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) blamed [NAFTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAFTA) for the loss of a million jobs and stated, "we can't keep passing unfair trade deals like NAFTA that put special interests over workers' interests."[^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34]

According to [Jeffry Frieden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffry_Frieden), 2016 "was the first time since the 1930s that both major parties had candidates for the presidential nomination who were openly hostile to economic integration, and that any major party had an actual nominee with similar views. The victory of Donald Trump in the [2016 presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_States_presidential_election) marked the victory of an [economic nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_nationalism) that had long been far from the mainstream of American politics."[^cite_note-Frieden-32] Trump's embrace of restrictive trade policies and broad tariffs represented a sharp departure from the [Republican Party's](32070.md) general support for free trade over the preceding 70 years, and a partial return to the party's [protectionist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protectionist) orientation in the early 20th century.[^cite_note-Frieden-32]

### First Trump administration

Main article: [Tariffs in the first Trump administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tariffs_in_the_first_Trump_administration)

In 2018, Trump imposed [tariffs on steel and aluminum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tariffs_in_the_first_Trump_administration#Steel_and_aluminum) imports.[^cite_note-35] He also initiated the [China–United States trade war](57055272.md), which subjected 60% of US-China trade to 20% tariffs.[^cite_note-36] Most sources say that the trade war did not achieve its goals: instead, the U.S. trade deficit widened, growth slowed, manufacturing jobs did not return, and prices rose.[^cite_note-37]

In May 2019, Trump used tariff threats of up to 25% on [Mexico](3966054.md) to negotiate an expansion of his "[Remain in Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remain_in_Mexico)" policy and the deployment of Mexican soldiers to help control [illegal immigration](5044573.md).[^cite_note-38] The Mexican government, led by [Andrés Manuel López Obrador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9s_Manuel_L%C3%B3pez_Obrador), deployed nearly 15,000 troops to the [Mexico–US border](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border) and 6,500 troops to the [Guatemala–Mexico border](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guatemala%E2%80%93Mexico_border).[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40]

In 2020, the US, Mexico and Canada renegotiated the [North American Free Trade Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Free_Trade_Agreement) (NAFTA) as the [United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement](58620732.md) (USMCA) and recommitted to 0% tariffs on most products traded between the three countries. Five weeks after the USMCA went into effect, Trump used an exemption for national security concerns to implement a 10% tariff on Canadian aluminum after claiming it was flooding the US market.[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42] He withdrew the tariff a month later, three hours before the [29th Canadian Ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/29th_Canadian_Ministry) planned to retaliate.[^cite_note-43]

### 2024 presidential campaign

During his [2024 presidential campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump_2024_presidential_campaign), Trump [pledged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agenda_47#Economy) to impose even larger tariffs than in his first term, including 60% on China, 100% on Mexico, and 20% on all other countries. He also proposed tariffs to penalize US companies that outsourced manufacturing, such as a 200% tariff on [John Deere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Deere).[^cite_note-44] He proposed using tariffs to achieve a wide range of goals, including preventing war, reducing [trade deficits](4810.md), improving [border security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_security_in_the_United_States), and subsidizing childcare.[^cite_note-45] Citing President [William McKinley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_McKinley) and his legislation on tariffs on multiple occasions, Trump would use the example to emphasize his perceived benefits from tariffs.[^cite_note-46]

In April 2025, Trump suggested tariff revenues could eventually replace income taxes, at least for those making less than \$200,000 per year. Economists disagreed; the [Tax Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_Foundation) deemed the idea "mathematically impossible".[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49][^cite_note-Minton-50]

Shortly after the [2024 United States presidential election](59568546.md), Trump acknowledged that tariffs might cause "some pain" for Americans but said, "it will all be worth the price that must be paid".[^cite_note-51]

In December 2024, Trump appointed tariff hard liner [Peter Navarro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Navarro) as his [Senior Counselor for Trade and Manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counselor_to_the_President).[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53] [Stephen Miran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Miran), another tariff hard liner, was appointed chair of the [Council of Economic Advisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Economic_Advisers).[^cite_note-54]

## Section 232 tariffs



Trump administration reciprocal tariffs (April 2025)

Section 232 of the [Trade Expansion Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_Expansion_Act) (TEA) allows the President to modify imports if the [Secretary of Commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_Commerce) conducts an investigation, holds public hearings, and determines that the imports threaten national security.[^cite_note-Looney-5][^cite_note-Christopher-55][^cite_note-Spangler-56]

### Metal and metal products

Trump ordered a probe into copper imports in February 2025.[^cite_note-57]

In March 2025, the US imposed 25% tariffs on all steel and aluminum imports, including and derivative products, aiming to strengthen domestic production; in 2023, the US imported 44% of its aluminum and 26% of its steel, mostly from Canada.[^cite_note-cfr-58][^cite_note-Sherman-59][^cite_note-Lawder-2025a-60] The measures expanded Trump's first-term steel and aluminum tariffs by eliminating all exemptions and raising the aluminum tariff from 10% to 25%.[^cite_note-Christopher-55] Trump also mandated that steel be "melted and poured" and aluminum "smelted and cast" in the US to qualify for duty-free status to prevent tariff circumvention.[^cite_note-61]

In April 2025, aluminum tariffs were expanded to include empty aluminum [drink cans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drink_can) and canned beer.[^cite_note-Lucas-62]

Tariffs on steel and aluminum imports were increased to 50% in June 2025, with the exception of tariffs on imports from the UK, which remained at 25%.[^cite_note-signs2-63][^cite_note-bbc-trump-64] On June 12, 2025, the White House expanded steel tariffs to include many major household appliances, effective on June 23.[^cite_note-Reuters-65]

In July 2025, Trump announced a 50% tariff on copper imports, effective August 1, 2025.[^cite_note-Loh-66] Later that month, Trump clarified that the tariffs would not apply to [cathode copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathode_copper); most of the copper imported to the US is cathode [copper from Chile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_mining_in_Chile).[^cite_note-Loh-66][^cite_note-bbc-trump-64][^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69]

The steel and aluminum tariffs were expanded to include 407 additional products in August 2025.[^cite_note-Doherty-70]

In April 2026, Trump clarified the tariffs on metals as follows: Articles made entirely or almost entirely of aluminum, steel, or copper will pay a flat 50% on their full value — for example, steel coils and aluminum sheet. Derivative articles substantially made of steel, aluminum, or copper will pay a flat 25% on their full value. Certain metal-intensive industrial equipment and electrical grid equipment will pay 15% through 2027. Products made abroad but entirely with American steel, aluminum, and copper will be subject to lower tariffs of 10%. Products made of 15% or less steel, aluminum, or copper will no longer be subject to Section 232 metals tariffs.[^cite_note-71]

### Automobiles and auto parts

In January 2025, Trump announced broad tariffs on Canada and Mexico, threatening the highly integrated North American auto supply chain.[^cite_note-Domonoske-2025-72] Due to decades of free trade agreements including [USMCA](58620732.md), factories in the US, Canada, and Mexico were accustomed to shipping auto parts back and forth multiple times during the manufacturing process.[^cite_note-Domonoske-2025-72] The [Big Three automobile manufacturers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Three_\(automobile_manufacturers\)) lobbied for exemptions, warning the tariffs would hurt American companies more than foreign competitors.[^cite_note-Colias-73][^cite_note-Domonoske-2025-72] Trump agreed to delay tariffs on USMCA-compliant vehicles.[^cite_note-Green-74] Non-USMCA compliant brands manufacturing in Canada or Mexico, such as [BMW](3772.md), were affected starting March 4, 2025.[^cite_note-Colias-73][^cite_note-75]

In April 2025, Trump imposed a new 25% tariff on all imported cars, including those from Mexico and Canada; car prices were expected to increase.[^cite_note-bbc-trump-64][^cite_note-76]

While the 25% tariff extended to auto parts in May 2025, Trump exempted parts made in Mexico or Canada that were compliant with the USMCA.[^cite_note-77][^cite_note-Confirms-78] In April 2025, he exempted carmakers that pay a 25% on imported cars from paying other tariffs, such as those on steel and aluminum, and provided a rebate on a proportion of tariffs paid for the next two years.[^cite_note-79]

### Solar panels from Southeast Asian countries

In April 2025, in support of American manufacturers complaining of [dumping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumping_\(pricing_policy\)) and subsidies, the Commerce Department furthered plans first issued by the [Biden Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biden_Administration) to tariff Chinese [solar panel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_panel) makers with factories in Malaysia, Cambodia, Vietnam, and Thailand. Proposed tariff rates ranged from 41% to 3,521% in the case of Cambodia because its producers did not cooperate with the US investigation.[^cite_note-80]

In February 2026, the Commerce Department announced tariffs on solar cells and panels imported by companies in India, Indonesia and Laos to counteract subsidies offered by those countries.[^cite_note-81]

### Lumber and furniture

In September 2025, Trump announced 10% tariffs on imports of softwood timber and lumber. He also announced 25% tariffs on kitchen cabinets, bathroom vanities, and upholstered furniture effective October 14.[^cite_note-82]

Also in September 2025, Trump announced tariffs on large trucks, kitchen cabinets, bathroom vanities, and upholstered furniture, to begin on October 1.[^cite_note-83]

### Pharmaceuticals

In April 2025, the [United States Department of Commerce](70243.md) initiated a Section 232 investigation into [pharmaceuticals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmaceuticals), including "both finished generic and non-generic drug products, medical countermeasures, critical inputs such as active pharmaceutical ingredients and key starting materials, and derivative products of those items."[^cite_note-84]

In September 2025, Trump announced, effective on October 1, 2025, a 100% tariff on "any branded or patented" pharmaceutical product, unless the companies were "breaking ground" or had "under construction" pharmaceutical manufacturing plants in the US.[^cite_note-Jie-85] The tariff was expected to have limited impact because most large drug manufacturers already had or were building American facilities.[^cite_note-Luhby-86] Trump also agreed to honor previously negotiated trade deals that capped pharmaceutical tariffs at 15%. Following the announcement, several large branded pharmaceutical companies announced or accelerated U.S. manufacturing investments to qualify for tariff exemptions tied to domestic production.[^cite_note-87]

In December 2025, the UK secured a 0% tariff on its pharmaceutical and medical technology exports in exchange for a commitment to invest more money into the US. The country also pledged to spend around 25% more in new and effective treatments, the first major increase in over two decades.[^cite_note-AP_News-88]

### Semiconductors

In April 2025, the Department of Commerce initiated a Section 232 investigation into ""imports of [semiconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductors) and semiconductor manufacturing equipment."[^cite_note-Muller-89]

In January 2026, Trump issued 25% tariffs on a narrow range of semiconductor imports, primarily advanced computing chips, including the [Nvidia H200](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nvidia_H200) and the [AMD](2400.md) MI325X, with the exemption of chips imported to support the buildout of the U.S. technology supply chain or to bolster domestic manufacturing capacity for derivatives of semiconductors. Semiconductors used for [data centers](579730.md), research and development, and non-data center consumer applications are exempted.[^cite_note-90]

## Country-specific tariffs implemented under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974

Trump imposed tariffs under [Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974](5916549.md), which allows the President to modify imports to penalise foreign trade practices deemed unfair if the [Secretary of Commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_Commerce) conducts an investigation and holds public hearings.[^cite_note-91]

Under the law, the U.S. imposed phased-in tariffs on imports from [Nicaragua](21362.md) that do not qualify under the [Dominican Republic–Central America Free Trade Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominican_Republic%E2%80%93Central_America_Free_Trade_Agreement) (CAFTA-DR). The tariff rate is 10% in 2027 and 15% in 2028. The tariffs "address Nicaragua's acts, policies, and practices related to abuses of labor rights, abuses of human rights and fundamental freedoms, and dismantling of the rule of law".[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93]

In June 2026, the USTR announced, for goods manufactured with [forced labour](541048.md), 10% tariffs on imports from Canada, the EU, Britain, Indonesia, Mexico, Pakistan, Argentina, Bangladesh, Cambodia, El Salvador, Guatemala, Indonesia, Malaysia and Taiwan and 12.5% tariffs on such goods from 45 other countries.[^cite_note-94]

In July 2026, the USTR used Section 301 to implement 25% tariffs on imports of furniture, ethanol, machinery, footwear, sugar and other goods from Brazil.[^cite_note-95]

## Country-specific tariffs implemented under Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930

Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 empowers the President to, among other things, impose tariffs on imports of a foreign country to offset the burden or disadvantage from a foreign country's unequal imposition on or discrimination against the commerce of the United States. In July 2026, Trump used the Act to impose 50% tariffs on imports from Canada of motor vehicles, dairy, and alcohol.[^cite_note-96]

## Country-specific tariffs implemented under IEEPA

Main article: [Liberation Day tariffs](79617151.md)

See also: [2025 United States proposed tariffs against the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_United_States_proposed_tariffs_against_the_European_Union), [2025–2026 United States trade war with Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025%E2%80%932026_United_States_trade_war_with_Canada), [2025–2026 United States trade war with Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025%E2%80%932026_United_States_trade_war_with_Mexico), [China–United States trade war](57055272.md), [2025 United States–India diplomatic and trade crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_United_States%E2%80%93India_diplomatic_and_trade_crisis), [Pakistan–United States trade deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan%E2%80%93United_States_trade_deal), [Russian Customs Tariff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Customs_Tariff), and [2025 Brazil–United States diplomatic dispute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Brazil%E2%80%93United_States_diplomatic_dispute)

Trump invoked unprecedented powers under the [National Emergencies Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Emergencies_Act) (NEA) and the [International Emergency Economic Powers Act](2434972.md) (IEEPA) by declaring [multiple "national emergencies"](59622928.md) related to border security, energy, and trade deficits.[^cite_note-Domonoske-2025a2-97][^cite_note-Palmer-98] The administration claimed that declaring these emergencies allowed Trump to enact tariffs quickly without following the complex procedures required by TEA or other trade statutes and did not require congressional approval.[^cite_note-99] While the IEEPA had been used to enact [economic sanctions](411315.md), it had never before been used to enact tariffs. As he signed the orders, Trump stated that declaring an emergency "means you can do whatever you have to do to get out of that problem."[^cite_note-Domonoske-2025a2-97]

On February 13, 2025, Trump directed his staff to research both monetary and non-monetary [trade barriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_barrier) imposed by foreign countries against US exports and to develop custom "reciprocal tariffs" to counter and penalize each one.[^cite_note-100] He instructed them to consider factors such as existing tariffs, exchange rates, and trade balances in their analysis.[^cite_note-101][^cite_note-102] Trump also threatened tariffs on countries that [purchase Russian oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Russian_oil_products_sanctions_and_price_cap)[^cite_note-103][^cite_note-104] and countries that trade with [Iran](14653.md).[^cite_note-105]

In March 2025, following actions such as the [March 2025 Venezuelan deportations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_2025_Venezuelan_deportations), in what he called "secondary tariffs", Trump signed an [executive order](210541.md) imposing a 25% tariff on nations that purchase oil from [Venezuela](32374.md) at the Secretary of State's discretion, under IEEPA.[^cite_note-106][^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108][^cite_note-Boak-2-109]

On April 2, 2025, a day he called "Liberation Day", Trump declared a national emergency to address what he described as a "large and persistent US trade deficit" and invoked the [IEEPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEPA) to impose a 10% minimum "reciprocal tariff" on nearly all other countries, effective April 5, 2025.[^cite_note-Palmer-98] He also announced higher "reciprocal tariffs" for 57 countries and territories that would begin April 9.[^cite_note-Pichee-110] Tariffs on certain goods were excluded.

The "reciprocal" tariff announcement led to the [2025 stock market crash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_stock_market_crash). Bond prices dropped and interest rates soared in a scenario called [bond vigilantism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_vigilante).[^cite_note-111]

After prices of U.S. assets including equities, the [US dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_dollar), and bonds declined significantly on the morning of April 9, Trump announced on *[Truth Social](69077351.md)* that "reciprocal" tariffs above 10%, which had gone into effect that morning, would be paused for 90 days for all countries except China.[^cite_note-112] China's minimum tariff rate was increased to 145%,[^cite_note-Swanson-113] while imports from all other countries were sustained at the 10% rate.[^cite_note-114] Other global tariffs imposed under section 232 also remained in effect.[^cite_note-Lalljee-115]

Stocks surged within minutes of the pause announcement, with the S\&P 500 rising 9.52% for its largest one-day gain since 2008.[^cite_note-116][^cite_note-117]

To terminate a national emergency under the NEA, a member of Congress may file a privileged resolution requiring their [chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_chamber) to vote on the topic within 15 days. In May 2025, [Democratic](5043544.md) representatives introduced resolutions to end several of Trump's national emergencies justifying tariffs, but these efforts were blocked by the [Republican](32070.md) congressional majority, with [JD Vance](53396477.md) casting a tie-breaking vote in the Senate.[^cite_note-Li-118]

In May 2025, several countries initiated disputes regarding Trump's tariffs with the [World Trade Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Trade_Organization) (WTO), a regulator of [international trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_trade).[^cite_note-119][^cite_note-120] However, the United States [has de facto paralyzed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispute_settlement_in_the_World_Trade_Organization#Paralyzation) the WTO since 2019 by blocking new appointments to its [Appellate Body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellate_Body) and therefore, no final rulings can be made.[^cite_note-121] In March 2025, the US notified the WTO that it would suspend contributions to its budget indefinitely. The US was set to provide about 11% of the WTO's \$232 million 2024 budget, a fee based on the country's share of global trade.[^cite_note-122]

Markets continued to rise in after further policy rollbacks and the S\&P 500 set a new all-time high on June 27, 2025.[^cite_note-123] Analysts suggested Trump's threats had lost credibility.

The administration announced only three trade agreements by July. While negotiations took place, the tariffs were delayed again to August 7, when they finally took effect after modifications as a result of various trade agreements.[^cite_note-Hale-124][^cite_note-125]

### Legal challenges to IEEPA tariffs

At least seven cases were filed in American federal courts challenging Trump's authority to impose tariffs under IEEPA.[^cite_note-126][^cite_note-127] Central to each case is the argument that the imposition of tariffs, in the absence of clear congressional authorization, may constitute an overextension of executive power into areas that are traditionally reserved for the legislative branch.[^cite_note-Mangan-128][^cite_note-Gottlieb-129][^cite_note-PC2-130] Several complaints invoke the [nondelegation doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondelegation_doctrine), asserting that the IEEPA, as applied, grants the executive branch an excessively broad scope of discretion. In addition to constitutional claims, the cases highlight significant economic consequences for states, businesses, and individuals, emphasizing the potential for such executive action to set a precedent for expansive policymaking beyond the intended purpose of emergency economic legislation.[^cite_note-Mangan-128][^cite_note-Gottlieb-129][^cite_note-PC2-130]

In May 2025, the [United States Court of International Trade](1220886.md) (CIT) issued a summary judgement for *V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States* and *Oregon v. Department of Homeland Security* and ruled Trump had exceeded his authority under the IEEPA.[^cite_note-131][^cite_note-132] The CIT found the IEEPA tariffs "illegal because the triggering emergency (fentanyl trafficking and trade deficits) bore no rational connection to the trade measures imposed."[^cite_note-Engelland-133] In *[Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump](80234268.md)*, a Washington D.C. district court went further by holding that the IEEPA does not authorize tariffs at all.[^cite_note-Engelland-133] The [United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Federal_Circuit) upheld the CIT's decision in August 2025.[^cite_note-134] The rulings were [stayed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stay_of_proceedings) pending appeal, allowing the tariffs to temporarily remain in effect until the Supreme Court issued a final decision.[^cite_note-135]

The Supreme Court consolidated *V.O.S. Selections* and *Learning Resources* into a single case and heard oral arguments in November 2025.[^cite_note-136]

In February 2026, the Supreme Court, in a 6-3 ruling, affirmed the decision of the appeals court that Trump's use of emergency powers to enact the tariffs under IEEPA was not legal.[^cite_note-137] The government collected an estimated \$166 billion in IEEPA tariffs, later declared unconstitutional by the Supreme Court, from more than 330,000 businesses that was required to be refunded.[^cite_note-refund-8] In April 2026, [US Customs and Border Protection](757040.md) (CBP) launched the Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries, or CAPE, portal to allow for companies to submit requests for tariff refund claims.[^cite_note-138]

The [*de minimis* exemption waived tariffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duty-free_de_minimis_rule_for_United_States_imports) on packages valued under \$800 to reduce administrative burdens.

On Liberation Day, President Donald Trump also signed Executive Order 14256, which eliminated the *de minimis* exemption for imports from China and [Hong Kong](13404.md),[^cite_note-Jun-139][^cite_note-140] and Executive Order 14257, which directed the United States to end the *de minimis* exemption globally once customs infrastructure was capable of doing so.[^cite_note-Garland-141]

On July 4, 2025, Trump signed the [One Big Beautiful Bill Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Big_Beautiful_Bill_Act) which included a provision to eliminate the *de minimis* exemption for all countries beginning on July 1, 2027.[^cite_note-142] A few weeks later, Trump imposed an earlier deadline by signing Executive Order 14324. This order, which cited the IEEPA, ended the *de minimis* exemption globally on August 29, 2025.[^cite_note-143]

Although the Supreme Court invalidated Trump's authority to impose other tariffs under the IEEPA on February 20, 2026, Trump issued an executive order arguing the ruling did not apply to the *de minimis* exemption and stating it would remain closed.[^cite_note-Tobin-9]

## Timeline of key events and negotiations

See also: [Timeline of the 2025–2026 United States trade war with Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_the_2025%E2%80%932026_United_States_trade_war_with_Canada) and [China–United States trade war § 2025](57055272.md#2025)

### January–March 2025

Trump was inaugurated for a second term on January 20, 2025. In [his inaugural address](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_inauguration_of_Donald_Trump), he pledged to "immediately begin the overhaul of our trade system to protect American workers and families."[^cite_note-144]

On February 1, 2025, he declared several "[national emergencies](59622928.md)" regarding [fentanyl](141915.md) trafficking and invoked the [IEEPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEPA) to impose 25% tariffs on most goods from Mexico and Canada and 10% on goods from China.[^cite_note-145] Tariffs on [USMCA](58620732.md)-compliant Mexican and Canadian goods were quickly suspended,[^cite_note-Green-74] but the "fentanyl tariff" on Chinese goods was raised to 20% on March 4.[^cite_note-146]

On February 13, 2025, Trump announced plans to impose "reciprocal tariffs" on all countries with trade barriers against the US in April, prompting a wave of diplomatic outreach.[^cite_note-Sherman-2-147][^cite_note-148] Analysts and foreign governments expressed confusion over the administration's tariff strategies and openness to negotiation.[^cite_note-149][^cite_note-150] Canada alleged that tariffs on its exports were intended to damage its economy and pressure [annexation into the US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movements_for_the_annexation_of_Canada_to_the_United_States).[^cite_note-151] Trump and Navarro refused to negotiate with any country.[^cite_note-152][^cite_note-153]

On March 12, 2025, the administration imposed 25% tariffs on imported steel and aluminum products.[^cite_note-Lawder-2025a-60] First-quarter US GDP fell -.05%, but investors attributed the decline to a rush to import goods before tariffs took effect rather than to underlying weakness.[^cite_note-154]

### April–June 2025



A timeline of the US average effective tariff rate from January 2025 through June 2025, based on calculations by employees of the [Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Bank_of_Richmond)

On April 2, 2025, a day he called "Liberation Day", Trump declared a national emergency regarding the national trade deficit and announced "reciprocal tariffs" on all countries not subject to other sanctions. A minimum 10% tariff on almost all US imports took effect on April 5.[^cite_note-Pichee-110]

Panic induced by the announcement led to the [2025 stock market crash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_stock_market_crash) and new country-specific tariffs, planned for April 9, were paused for 90 days.[^cite_note-155][^cite_note-156] However, after a retaliatory spiral, US tariffs on Chinese goods were increased to 145% while Chinese tariffs on US goods were increased to 125%.[^cite_note-157] Steel and aluminum tariffs, a 25% tariff on automobiles imposed April 3, and other sector-specific duties also remained.[^cite_note-Lalljee-115] *[Politico](9172097.md)* estimated that despite the 90 day pause, the remaining tariffs brought the average applied US tariff rate to 27%–the highest level in over a century.[^cite_note-Reddy-3]

CEOs of major US retailers warned Trump that the trade war with China would lead to visible price increases and product shortages by mid-May.[^cite_note-158] The first cargo ships carrying Chinese goods tariffed at 145% arrived on May 6 with shipments cut in half.[^cite_note-159] Trump reportedly pivoted his focus toward negotiation, sidelining Navarro and promoting [Scott Bessent](38532044.md) as his lead economic advisor.[^cite_note-160][^cite_note-161]

By the end of June 2025, the US had only signed a deal with the UK and a 90-day temporary deal with China.[^cite_note-162] Under terms expiring November 9, as extended, China lowered tariffs on US goods to 10% and resumed exports of [rare-earth elements](145440.md), while the US lowered its tariff on Chinese goods to 30% (10% baseline + 20% fentanyl) and cut *de minimis* tariffs to 54%.[^cite_note-163]

Trump reduced some of his tariffs that preceded the [2025 stock market crash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_stock_market_crash), inspiring the phrase "[Trump Always Chickens Out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trump_Always_Chickens_Out)." The stock market subsequently reached new highs.[^cite_note-164][^cite_note-165]

On June 4, 2025, Trump doubled the tariffs on steel and aluminum imports from 25% to 50%,[^cite_note-signs2-63] and on June 12, he expanded them to include household appliances like refrigerators and dishwashers.[^cite_note-Reuters-65]

US GDP rebounded to 3.8% in the second quarter of 2025 as imports normalized.[^cite_note-166]

### July–September 2025

On August 1, 2025, the [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Labor_Statistics) released revised data showing a sharp slowdown in hiring, which some attributed to [policy uncertainty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policy_uncertainty) caused by tariffs.[^cite_note-167] Trump dismissed the data as "rigged" and fired the BLS Commissioner.[^cite_note-Murray-168] Trump also called for the removal of [Federal Reserve](10819.md) Chairman [Jerome Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome_Powell), who had warned that tariffs could fuel inflation and resisted Trump's calls to cut the [federal funds rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_funds_rate).[^cite_note-169]

Country-specific [Liberation Day tariffs](79617151.md) were postponed and changed several times before finally taking effect on August 7.[^cite_note-170][^cite_note-Hale-124]

Trump notified foreign governments of adjustments to their [Liberation Day tariffs](79617151.md) rates in a series of letters.[^cite_note-171] In one letter to Brazil, he threatened a 50% tariff while denouncing charges against [Jair Bolsonaro](46603496.md), an ally facing trial for the [2022 Brazilian coup plot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Brazilian_coup_plot).[^cite_note-172] On July 30, Trump declared Brazil's actions a US "national emergency" and imposed an additional 40% tariff on top of a 10% "reciprocal" tariff.[^cite_note-173]

In August 2025, Trump ordered an additional 25% tariff on imports from [India](14533.md), intended to penalize [India for purchasing Russian oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India_and_the_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine).[^cite_note-Doherty-2-174][^cite_note-Open-175] Beginning August 27, 2025, Indian exports faced an extra 25% tariff and a total baseline tariff of 50%.[^cite_note-Doherty-2-174][^cite_note-Inamdar-176] Trump withdrew the tariff in February 2026 after several Indian oil companies agreed to stop buying Russian oil unless absolutely necessary or unless related to the fulfillment of previously agreed to contracts.[^cite_note-Inamdar-176][^cite_note-o619-177][^cite_note-178]

### October–December 2025

On October 1, 2025, new tariffs on large trucks, cabinets, and furniture took effect. A 100% tariff on "any branded or patented" pharmaceutical product also began, unless the companies were building manufacturing plants in the US.[^cite_note-Jie-85]

On October 10, 2025, Trump made new threats against China, including an additional 100% tariff starting November 1, in retaliation of China's broadening of export controls on [rare earths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rare_earth_industry_in_China).[^cite_note-179] China announced retaliatory measures.[^cite_note-Jun-139] However, after meeting with [CCP General Secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Secretary_of_the_Chinese_Communist_Party) [Xi Jinping](2017814.md) in South Korea on October 30, Trump announced the US would cut the "fentanyl tariff" on Chinese goods entering the US from 20% to 10% in return for soybean and other farm product purchases and easier access to rare earths. The US also was expected to suspend an expansion of export controls.[^cite_note-180] The trade deal lowered tariffs on Chinese goods to levels similar to or below those on other countries.[^cite_note-181]

On November 14, 2025, Trump exempted a variety of agricultural products from "reciprocal" tariffs or Brazilian tariffs after acknowledging domestic demand and lack of production capacity. Newly exempted products included coffee, tea, tropical fruits, cocoa, spices, bananas, oranges, tomatoes, and beef.[^cite_note-182]

On November 27, 2025, journalist [Annie Lowrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annie_Lowrey) published a report on [The Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Atlantic) highlighting how the Trump administration's economic policies, particularly its tariffs on imported goods, make holiday shopping and everyday shopping significantly more expensive for American families and small business owners. The article concluded that the average American family is expected to spend \$132 more on [Christmas and holiday season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christmas_and_holiday_season) shopping due to Trump's tariffs.[^cite_note-183]

By December 2025, half of all US imports had been exempted from Trump's IEEPA tariffs, although some of the remaining were targeted by section 232 tariffs.[^cite_note-184]

### January–March 2026

On January 17, 2026, Trump threatened tariffs of up to 25% on goods from 8 European countries as part of the [Greenland Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland_Crisis).[^cite_note-185] On January 21, 2026, he retracted the threat after saying he had reached "framework of a future deal" with NATO Secretary-General [Mark Rutte](4321056.md).[^cite_note-186] Diplomatic talks began soon after.[^cite_note-187]

On February 2, 2026, Trump cut tariffs on Indian exports from 50% to 18% after several Indian oil companies agreed to stop buying Russian oil unless absolutely necessary or unless related to the fulfillment of previously agreed to contracts.[^cite_note-Inamdar-176][^cite_note-o619-177]

On February 10, 2026, House Republicans attempted to pass a resolution to extend the prohibition of the chamber from canceling Trump's tariffs, but failed after three House Republicans voted against the resolution, with Republican Representative [Don Bacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Bacon) who voted against the prohibition, stating that the Constitution places authority over taxes and tariffs with Congress for a reason.[^cite_note-Kyle-188] With the prohibition expired, on February 11, six House Republicans crossed party lines to vote to end Trump's tariffs on Canada; Trump said that he would veto the measure.[^cite_note-Kyle-188]

On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court of the United States ruled in *[Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump](80234268.md)* that the IEEPA does not grant the President authority to impose tariffs.[^cite_note-Fritze-189] The government collected an estimated \$166 billion in IEEPA tariffs, later declared unconstitutional by the Supreme Court, from more than 330,000 businesses that was required to be refunded.[^cite_note-refund-8]

After the decision of the Supreme Court, Trump announced a universal 10% tariff, to remain in effect for 150 days, until July 24, 2026, under [Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_122_of_the_Trade_Act_of_1974) to replace the invalidated tariffs.[^cite_note-190] He also threatened to increase the tariff to 15%.[^cite_note-\:1-191][^cite_note-global-10] After a lawsuit, the [United States Court of International Trade](1220886.md) ruled that these tariffs are also illegal and the case is being appealed.[^cite_note-replacement-11]

Existing exemptions were maintained.[^cite_note-\:1-191] Trump also issued an executive order to maintain the closure of the *de minimis* exemption under the IEEPA.[^cite_note-Tobin-9]

### July 2026

After Trump's failure to keep the old tariff model up, Trump imposed new tariffs on July 24, 2026. The duties are imposed under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974.[^cite_note-192] There are 2 forms of tariffs imposed, a 10% levy and a 12.5% one.[^cite_note-193] The EU reaction consisted mostly of delight because the tariff rate subsided (towards 10% with a tariff exempt for example for cork and diamonds).[^cite_note-194]

The tariffs affected 60 trading partners, including the [United Kingdom](31717.md), [Canada](5042916.md), [China](5405.md), [Mexico](3966054.md), and the [European Union](9317.md). The U.S. Trade Representative said the measures were introduced following an investigation into restrictions on imports of goods produced with forced labour in these economies. However, critics questioned the justification for applying similar tariff rates to countries with differing records on combating forced labour. The [USTR](211349.md) also announced exemptions for certain products and circumstances.[^cite_note-\:0-195][^cite_note-196][^cite_note-197]

The measures drew criticism from several affected countries. [Brazil](3383.md) described the tariffs as "arbitrary and unjustified", while Australia's trade minister said the tariffs were inconsistent with the [Australia–United States Free Trade Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia%E2%80%93United_States_Free_Trade_Agreement) and should be removed. Mexico said it would not impose retaliatory tariffs, with President [Claudia Sheinbaum](57813311.md) stating that most Mexican exports remained protected under the [United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement](58620732.md) (USMCA).[^cite_note-\:0-195][^cite_note-198]

The new tariffs also prompted a legal challenge in the United States. Two small American businesses, Burlap and Barrel, a [New York](645042.md) spice retailer, and Collective Horology, a [California](5407.md) watch seller, sued the Trump administration over the duties affecting more than 80 countries. The lawsuit, filed by the [Liberty Justice Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberty_Justice_Center) in a federal trade court, argued that the administration had exceeded its legal authority in imposing the measures. The businesses argued that the use of Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 did not allow for such broad tariffs and that the government needed more specific findings to justify them. The Liberty Justice Center had previously challenged Trump’s tariff policies in court and won cases related to earlier tariff actions.[^cite_note-199][^cite_note-200]

## Impacts



Upon imposing the highest U.S. tariffs since the Great Depression (called "Liberation Day" in April 2025), Trump claimed that "jobs and factories will come roaring back". However, manufacturing employment declined every month for the rest of the year.

[^cite_note-201]



Though Trump claimed in December 2025 that "[inflation](38286.md) has stopped", the [consumer price index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_price_index_in_the_United_States) (CPI) began increasing in the months following his April 2025 announcement of tariffs.

[^cite_note-202]



Consumer sentiment declined during the early part of Trump's second term.

[^cite_note-UMich_ConsumerSentiment-203]

&#x20;The University of Michigan Index of Consumer Sentiment reached a record low in April 2026.

[^cite_note-Axios_20260411-204]



Trump asserted tariffs on Chinese goods in February and April 2025, igniting a trade war that injected uncertainty as China turned to other sources.

[^cite_note-205]



In January 2026, the US dollar reached its lowest point in four years.

[^cite_note-206]

&#x20;A lower dollar makes US goods less expensive abroad, but it also makes foreign products more expensive in the US and thus tends to increase inflation.

[^cite_note-207]

Studies have shown that the tariffs have increased expenses and reduced earnings for companies[^cite_note-208][^cite_note-Popat-17][^cite_note-J.P._Morgan_Chase-18][^cite_note-Al_Jazeera-19] and have increased costs for households.[^cite_note-Popat-17]

[Goldman Sachs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldman_Sachs) estimated that as of August 2025, tariff [incidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_incidence) was paid 37% by US consumers, 51% by US businesses, and 9% by foreign exporters. About 3% was attributed to potential tariff evasion.[^cite_note-209] A study by the [Federal Reserve Bank of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Bank_of_New_York) estimated that in the first 11 months of 2025, between 6% and 14% of tariff costs were paid by foreign exporters.[^cite_note-Financial_Times-210] Studies by the [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Economic_Research) and the [Kiel Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiel_Institute) estimated that US consumers and businesses pay a total of 94% and 96% of the tariffs, respectively.[^cite_note-Financial_Times-210]

The promised growth in manufacturing jobs has not been realized.[^cite_note-Egan-20]

The tariffs led to [policy uncertainty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policy_uncertainty) and prompted a drop in consumer confidence.[^cite_note-211] More than half of Americans blamed the Trump administration responsible for the rising cost of living[^cite_note-212] and disapproved of the Trump administration increasing tariffs.[^cite_note-213] Corporate bankruptcies in the United States increased to the highest level since 2010.[^cite_note-Bankrupt-21][^cite_note-Bankrupt-aol-22][^cite_note-Wallace-23]

The tariffs have led to a sharp reduction in the exports of U.S. crops due to retaliatory tariffs by other countries, particularly China, leading to many bankruptcies of U.S. farms; the impact has been partially mitigated by the [Trump administration farmer bailouts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trump_administration_farmer_bailouts).[^cite_note-214]

However, although many economists and economic research bodies predicted slower growth and even a possible [recession](25382.md) due to the tariffs, U.S. [GDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GDP) has continued to grow, which partially has been attributed to backtracking by Trump off of the initial high tariff rates.[^cite_note-Miller-24][^cite_note-Sargen-25] There has also been no definitive indication of a significant aggregate effect on the labor market so far; however, industries most exposed to tariffs show some signs of weakness relative to the pre-2025 trend.[^cite_note-tracking-26]
