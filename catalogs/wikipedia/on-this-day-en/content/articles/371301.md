**Electronic voting** is [voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting) that uses [electronic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics) means to either aid or handle casting and counting [ballots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballot) including voting time.

Depending on the particular implementation, e-voting may use standalone *[electronic voting machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_machine)* (also called EVM) or computers connected to the Internet (**online voting**). It may encompass a range of Internet services, from basic transmission of tabulated results to full-function online voting through common connectable household devices. The degree of [automation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automation) may be limited to marking a paper ballot, or may be a comprehensive system of vote input, vote recording, data encryption and transmission to servers, and consolidation and tabulation of election results.

A e-voting system must perform most of these tasks while complying with a set of standards established by regulatory bodies, and must be aligned with requirements associated with [security](7398.md), [accuracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accuracy), [speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed), [privacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privacy), [auditability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_audit), [accessibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accessibility), [data integrity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_integrity), [cost-effectiveness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost-effectiveness_analysis), [scalability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalability), [anonymity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anonymity), [trustworthiness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trustworthiness), and [sustainability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainability).[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2]

Electronic voting technology can include [punched cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punched_card), [optical scan voting systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_scan_voting_system) and specialized voting kiosks (including self-contained [direct-recording electronic voting systems](#Direct-recording_electronic_\(DRE\)_voting_system), or DRE). It can also involve transmission of [ballots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballot) and votes via telephones, private [computer networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_network), or the Internet. The functions of electronic voting depend primarily on what the organizers intend to achieve.

In general, two main types of e-voting can be identified:[^cite_note-3]

- e-voting which is physically supervised by representatives of governmental or independent electoral authorities (e.g. electronic voting machines located at polling stations);

- remote e-voting via the Internet (also called i-voting) where the voter submits their vote electronically to the election authorities, from any location.

Many countries have used electronic voting for at least some elections, including [Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_Argentina), [Australia](4689264.md), [Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_Bangladesh), [Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_Belgium), [Brazil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_Brazil), [Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_Canada), [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_France), [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_Germany), [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_India), [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_Italy), [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_Japan), [Kazakhstan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_Kazakhstan), [South Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_South_Korea), [Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_Malaysia), the [Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_the_Netherlands), [Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_Norway), the [Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_Philippines), [Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_Spain), [Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_Switzerland), [Thailand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_Thailand), the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_the_United_Kingdom), and the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_the_United_States). As of 2023, Brazil is the only country in which all elections are conducted through electronic voting.[^cite_note-9]

## Benefits

Electronic voting technology intends to speed the counting of ballots, reduce the cost of paying staff to count votes manually and can provide improved accessibility for disabled voters. Also in the long term, expenses are expected to decrease.[^cite_note-how-10] Results can be reported and published faster.[^cite_note-how-10] Voters save time and cost by being able to vote independently from their location. This may increase overall voter turnout. The citizen groups benefiting most from electronic elections are the ones living abroad, citizens living in rural areas far away from polling stations and the disabled with mobility impairments.[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-how-10]

## Concerns and improvements

Further information: [Vote counting § Errors in optical scans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_counting#Errors_in_optical_scans), and [Vote counting § Errors in direct-recording electronic voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_counting#Errors_in_direct-recording_electronic_voting)

In a 2004 article for [OpenDemocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenDemocracy), security analyst [Bruce Schneier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Schneier) claimed that computer security experts at the time were "unanimous on what to do" about concerns regarding electronic voting. "DRE machines must have [voter-verifiable paper audit trails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter-verified_paper_audit_trail)," he said, and "software used on DRE machines must be open to public scrutiny"[^cite_note-12] to ensure the accuracy of the voting system. Verifiable ballots are necessary because computers can and do malfunction and because voting machines can be compromised.

Concerns regarding security lapses in aging voting machines came to a head shortly before and during the [2016 United States presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_States_presidential_election).[^cite_note-An_Incredibly_Insecure_Voting_Machine-13][^cite_note-Feldman,_Halterman_&_Felten-14][^cite_note-15] Cases were reported at the time of machines making unpredictable, inconsistent errors. The expert consensus centered on three primary solutions: the openness of a system to public examination from outside experts, the creation of an authenticable [paper record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter-verified_paper_audit_trail) of votes cast, and a chain of custody for records.[^cite_note-What's_Wrong_With_Electronic_Voting_Machines?-16][^cite_note-Wichita_State_mathematician_says_Kansas_voting_machines_need_to_be_audited_to_check_accuracy-17]

Several major reforms took place after the 2016 U.S. election, including the widespread adoption of voting machines that produce [voter-verified paper audit trails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter-verified_paper_audit_trail) (VVPATs). These paper records allow election officials to conduct audits and recounts, significantly enhancing transparency and security. Congress provided \$380 million in funding through the [Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consolidated_Appropriations_Act,_2018) under the framework of the [Help America Vote Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help_America_Vote_Act) to replace old machines with more secure models with modern cybersecurity protections. By 2020, 93% of U.S. votes had a paper record, and only 0.5 percent of jurisdictions reported using electronic voting machines without paper audit trails.[^cite_note-18] This reduced the risk of undetected cyber interference or machine malfunction by enabling verification through physical ballots.

In collaboration with the [U.S. Department of Homeland Security](58236.md) and other organizations, election officials also took steps to harden voting systems against potential cyberattacks. This included training election officials, sharing threat intelligence, and establishing secure systems for vote transmission and counting.[^cite_note-19]

In addition to concerns about [electoral fraud](382165.md) and auditability, electronic voting has been criticized as unnecessary and expensive to introduce. While countries like India continue to use electronic voting, several countries have cancelled e-voting systems or decided against a large-scale rollout, notably the [Netherlands](21148.md), Ireland, Germany and the United Kingdom due to issues in reliability or transparency of EVMs.[^cite_note-Why_electronic_voting_isn't_secure-20][^cite_note-Should_Britain_introduce_electronic_voting?-21]

Moreover, people without internet or the skills to use it are excluded from the service. The so-called digital divide describes the gap between those who have access to the internet and those who do not. Depending on the country or even regions in a country the gap differs. This concern is expected to become less important in future since the number of internet users tends to increase.[^cite_note-22]

Expenses for the installation of an electronic voting system are high. For some governments they may be too high so that they do not invest. This aspect is even more important if it is not sure whether electronic voting is a long-term solution.[^cite_note-how-10]

### New South Wales 2021 iVote failures

During the 2021 NSW Local Government Elections the online voting system "iVote" had technical issues that caused some access problems for some voters. Analysis done of these failures indicated a significant chance of the outages having impacted on the electoral results for the final positions. In the Kempsey ward, where the margin between the last elected and first non-elected candidates was only 69 votes, the electoral commission determined that the outage caused a 60% chance that the wrong final candidate was elected. Singleton had a 40% chance of having elected the wrong councillor, Shellharbour was a 7% chance and two other races were impacted by a sub-1% chance of having elected the wrong candidate. The NSW Supreme Court ordered the elections in Kempsey, Singleton and Shellharbour Ward A to be re-run. In the 2022 Kempsey re-vote the highest placed non-elected candidate from 2021, Dean Saul, was instead one of the first councillors elected.[^cite_note-23] This failure caused the NSW Government to suspend the iVote system from use in the [2023 New South Wales state election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_New_South_Wales_state_election).

## Types of system

### Electronic voting machines

Main article: [Electronic voting machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_machine)

Counting ballots by an optical scanner, San Jose, California, 2018

Electronic voting systems for electorates have been in use since the 1960s when [punched card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_machine#Punched_card_voting) systems debuted. Their first widespread use was in the US where 7 counties switched to this method for the 1964 presidential election.[^cite_note-24] The newer [optical scan voting systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_scan_voting_system) allow a computer to count a voter's mark on a ballot. [DRE voting machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DRE_voting_machine) which collect and tabulate votes in a single machine, are used by all voters in all elections in Brazil and [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_voting_machines), and also on a large scale in [Venezuela](32374.md) and the United States. They have been used on a large scale in the [Netherlands](21148.md) but have been decommissioned after public concerns.[^cite_note-25] In Brazil, the use of DRE voting machines has been associated with a decrease in error-ridden and uncounted votes, promoting a larger enfranchisement of mainly less educated people in the electoral process, shifting government spending toward public healthcare, particularly beneficial to the poor.[^cite_note-26]

#### Paper-based electronic voting system

Further information: [Vote counting § Optical scan counting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_counting#Optical_scan_counting), [Voting machine § Optical scan (marksense)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_machine#Optical_scan_\(marksense\)), and [Electronic voting in the United States § Optical scan counting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_the_United_States#Optical_scan_counting)



A cart holding an [ES\&S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ES\&S) M100 [ballot scanner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_scan_voting_system) and an AutoMARK [assistive device](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assistive_device), as used in [Johnson County, Iowa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnson_County,_Iowa), United States in 2010

[Paper-based voting systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting#Paper-based_methods) originated as a system where votes are cast and [counted by hand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_counting_system#Manual_counting), using paper ballots. With the advent of [electronic tabulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_counting_system#Optical_scan_counting) came systems where paper cards or sheets could be marked by hand, but counted electronically. These systems included [punched card voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_machine#Punched_card_voting), [marksense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_scan_voting_system#Marksense_systems) and later [digital pen voting systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_scan_voting_system#Digital_pen_voting_systems).[^cite_note-27]

These systems can include a [ballot marking device](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballot_marking_device) or electronic ballot marker that allows voters to make their selections using an [electronic input device](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_machine#Direct-recording_electronic_\(DRE\)), usually a [touch screen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Touch_screen) system similar to a DRE. Systems including a ballot marking device can incorporate different forms of [assistive technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assistive_technology). In 2004, Open Voting Consortium demonstrated the 'Dechert Design', a [General Public License](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Public_License) [open source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-source_license) paper ballot printing system with open source [bar codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar_codes) on each ballot.[^cite_note-28]

#### Direct-recording electronic (DRE) voting system

Main article: [DRE voting machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DRE_voting_machine)

Further information: [Voting machine § Direct-recording electronic (DRE)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_machine#Direct-recording_electronic_\(DRE\)), [Vote counting § Direct-recording electronic counting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_counting#Direct-recording_electronic_counting), [Electronic voting in the United States § Direct-recording electronic counting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_the_United_States#Direct-recording_electronic_counting), and [Electronic voting in India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_India)

A direct-recording electronic (DRE) [voting machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_machine) records votes by means of a [ballot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballot) display provided with mechanical or electro-optical components that can be activated by the voter (typically buttons or a [touchscreen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Touchscreen)); that processes data with computer software; and that records voting data and ballot images in [memory components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_memory). After the election it produces a tabulation of the voting data stored in a removable memory component and as a printed copy. The system may also provide a means for transmitting individual ballots or vote totals to a central location for consolidating and reporting results from precincts at the central location. These systems use a precinct count method that tabulates ballots at the polling place. They typically tabulate ballots as they are cast and print the results after the close of polling.[^cite_note-29]

In 2002, in the United States, the [Help America Vote Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help_America_Vote_Act) mandated that one handicapped accessible voting system be provided per polling place, which most jurisdictions have chosen to satisfy with the use of DRE voting machines, some switching entirely over to DRE. In 2004, 28.9% of the registered voters in the United States used some type of direct recording electronic voting system,[^cite_note-30] up from 7.7% in 1996.[^cite_note-31]



VVPAT used with Indian [electronic voting machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_voting_machines) in Indian Elections

In 2004, India adopted [Electronic Voting Machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_voting_machines) (EVM) for its elections to its parliament with 380 million voters casting their ballots using more than one million voting machines.[^cite_note-pib.nic.in-32] The Indian EVMs are designed and developed by two government-owned defence equipment manufacturing units, [Bharat Electronics Limited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bharat_Electronics_Limited) (BEL) and [Electronics Corporation of India Limited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics_Corporation_of_India) (ECIL). Both systems are identical, and are developed to the specifications of [Election Commission of India](47863083.md). The system is a set of two devices running on 7.5 volt batteries. One device, the voting Unit is used by the voter, and another device called the control unit is operated by the electoral officer. Both units are connected by a five-metre cable. The voting unit has a blue button for each candidate. The unit can hold 16 candidates, but up to four units can be chained, to accommodate 64 candidates. The control unit has three buttons on the surface – one button to release a single vote, one button to see the total number of votes cast till now, and one button to close the election process. The result button is hidden and sealed. It cannot be pressed unless the close button has already been pressed. A controversy was raised when the voting machine malfunctioned which was shown in Delhi assembly.[^cite_note-33] On 9 April 2019, the Supreme Court ordered the ECI to increase [voter-verified paper audit trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter-verified_paper_audit_trail) (VVPAT) slips vote count to five randomly selected EVMs per assembly constituency, which means ECI has to count VVPAT slips of 20,625 EVMs before it certifies the final election results.[^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36]

##### Public network DRE voting system

A public network DRE voting system is an election system that uses electronic ballots and transmits vote data from the polling place to another location over a public network.[^cite_note-Voting_Systems_Standards_2002_p._12-37] Vote data may be transmitted as individual ballots as they are cast, periodically as batches of ballots throughout the election day, or as one batch at the close of voting. Public network DRE voting system can utilize either precinct count or central count method. The central count method tabulates ballots from multiple precincts at a central location.

Not to be confused with [open-access poll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-access_poll).



Smartphones are the mainstream for online voting used by the Japanese private sector, but e-voting is not possible due to the law in public office elections in Japan.

#### Online government elections

Internet voting systems have gained popularity and have been used for government and membership organization elections and referendums in [Estonia](28222445.md), and Switzerland[^cite_note-Serdült-38] as well as municipal elections in Canada and party primary elections in the United States and France.[^cite_note-39] Internet voting has also been widely used in sub-national [participatory budgeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Participatory_budgeting) processes, including in Brazil, France, United States, Portugal and Spain.[^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45]

Security experts have found security problems in every attempt at governmental online voting,[^cite_note-appel-dem-46][^cite_note-vv-int-47][^cite_note-nas-sec-48][^cite_note-eac-nist-49] including systems in Australia,[^cite_note-aust-50][^cite_note-teague-51] Estonia,[^cite_note-estan-52][^cite_note-osce-est-53] Switzerland,[^cite_note-appel-switz-54][^cite_note-swiss-sctyl-55] Russia,[^cite_note-56][^cite_note-moscow-57][^cite_note-risks-moscow-58] and the United States.[^cite_note-vice-mit-59][^cite_note-appel-dem-46] In 2026, computer scientists working in election security summarized "that internet voting is insecure and that there is no known or foreseeable technology that can make it secure."[^cite_note-60]

#### Effects on turnout

It has been argued political parties that have more support from less-wealthy voters—who tend to have less access to and familiarity with the Internet—may suffer in the elections due to e-voting, which tends to increase participation among wealthier voters. It is unsure as to whether narrowing the [digital divide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_divide) would promote equal voting opportunities for people across various social, economic, and ethnic backgrounds.[^cite_note-Bochsler_2010-61]

The effects of internet voting on overall voter turnout are unclear. A 2017 study of online voting in two Swiss cantons found that it had no effect on turnout,[^cite_note-62] and a 2009 study of Estonia's national election found similar results.[^cite_note-63] To the contrary, however, the introduction of online voting in municipal elections in the Canadian province of [Ontario](22218.md) resulted in an average increase in turnout of around 3.5 percentage points.[^cite_note-64] Similarly, a further study of the Swiss case found that while online voting did not increase overall turnout, it did induce some occasional voters to participate who would have abstained were online voting not an option.[^cite_note-65] In Switzerland, where it is already an established part of local referendums, voters get their passwords to access the ballot through the postal service.

A paper on "remote electronic voting and turnout in the Estonian 2007 parliamentary elections" showed that rather than eliminating inequalities, e-voting might have enhanced the [digital divide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_divide) between higher and lower socioeconomic classes. People who lived greater distances from polling areas voted at higher levels with this service now available. The 2007 Estonian elections yielded a higher voter turnout from those who lived in higher income regions and who received formal education.[^cite_note-Bochsler_2010-61] Still regarding the Estonian Internet voting system, it was proved to be more cost-efficient than the rest of the voting systems offered in 2017 local elections.[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-Kramer_2020-67] Most voters in Estonia can cast their vote in local and parliamentary elections, if they want to, via the Internet, as most of those on the electoral roll have access to an e-voting system, the largest run by any [European Union](9317.md) country. It has been made possible because most Estonians carry a national identity card equipped with a computer-readable microchip and it is these cards which they use to get access to the online ballot. All a voter needs is a computer, an electronic card reader, their ID card and its PIN, and they can vote from anywhere in the world. [Estonian e-votes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_Estonia) can only be cast during the days of [advance voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advance_poll). On election day itself people have to go to polling stations and fill in a paper ballot.

Electronic voting is perceived to be favored moreover by a certain demographic, namely the younger generation such as Generation X and Y voters. However, in recent elections about a quarter of e-votes were cast by the older demographic, such as individuals over the age of 55. Including this, about 20% of e-votes came from voters between the ages of 45 and 54. This goes to show that e-voting is not supported exclusively by the younger generations, but finding some popularity amongst Gen X and Baby Boomers as well.[^cite_note-68] In terms of electoral results as well, the expectation that online voting would favor younger candidates has not been borne out in the data, with mayors in Ontario, Canada who were elected in online elections actually being slightly older on average than those elected by pencil and paper.[^cite_note-69]

#### Online private elections

Online voting is widely used privately for shareholder votes for board members and shareholder resolutions,[^cite_note-fisch-70] and other private organizations.[^cite_note-71][^cite_note-esc-sectors-72][^cite_note-73] The election management companies do not promise accuracy[^cite_note-ebuddy-74] or privacy.[^cite_note-esc-75][^cite_note-broad-terms-76][^cite_note-cshare-77] In fact one company uses an individual's past votes for research,[^cite_note-brav-78] and to target ads.[^cite_note-broad-target-79] One provider offers "a wide range of options ensuring the proper balance between security, complexity and cost of each election," while saying that it both "guarantees security"[^cite_note-esc-rei-80][^cite_note-esc-cfai-81][^cite_note-esc-pcc-82] and has "reasonable security safeguards."[^cite_note-esc-priv-83]

#### Blockchain voting

Blockchain technology has been proposed as an underlying infrastructure for electronic voting to address persistent challenges regarding election security, privacy, and transparency. In a blockchain-based voting system, votes are recorded as transactions on a distributed, append-only ledger, which relies on cryptographic hashing to prevent the tampering or deletion of past entries. To manage the election process, these systems frequently utilize smart contracts—programmable protocols that automatically execute without a middleman once predefined conditions are met. Smart contracts can be configured to manage the lifecycle of an election by handling voter registration, automatically tallying results in real-time, and issuing a unique transaction ID to each voter. This allows individuals to independently verify that their ballot was correctly counted on the blockchain without revealing their personal identity or how they voted.[^cite_note-Hjálmarsson_2018-84]

Implementations of blockchain voting can be built on either public or private networks. While public blockchains are accessible to anyone, researchers note they can be inefficient and risky for official elections due to volatile transaction fees (gas costs), potential network congestion, and vulnerabilities to 51% attacks. Consequently, proposed e-voting frameworks frequently recommend private, or "permissioned," blockchains. In a permissioned system, access is restricted, and only authorized nodes—such as those hosted by trusted institutions or specific voting districts—can validate transactions and interact with the ledger. This controlled environment helps administrators implement secure authentication measures, such as integrating government identity verification services, to ensure only eligible individuals can cast a ballot.[^cite_note-Hjálmarsson_2018-84]

#### Sybil attacks

Main article: [Sybil attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sybil_attack)

One of the biggest weaknesses of online voting is the difficulty of dealing with fake identities, especially when voting is implemented using software without the cooperation of some kind of government agency.[^cite_note-85] These attacks use sybils—fake or duplicate identities—to influence community decisions. Since a single vote has the potential to tilt a group decision, prevention of sybil attacks is an important priority in ensuring the security of voting.[^cite_note-86] Sybil attacks are a common issue with implementations on open, peer-to-peer networks, as the system must have a way to prevent fake identities to prevent gaming of the vote.[^cite_note-Lalley2017-1-87]

Some future possible avenues of inquiries include to investigate more intersectional [proof of personhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_of_personhood) systems that aren't directly blockchain-based.[^cite_note-Siddarth_2020-88] For example, extending the [web of trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_of_trust) by having a protocol that verifies proof of identities using social interactions would allow a community of users to assign corresponding levels of trusts to different candidates in relation with others. However, this would require a fully decentralized system.[^cite_note-Siddarth_2020-88] This web-of-trust protocol could even expand to allowing candidates to provide proof of personhood by physical attendance, which could lead to trusted clusters that grow into communities.[^cite_note-89]

### Hybrid systems

There are also hybrid systems that include an electronic ballot marking device (usually a touch screen system similar to a DRE) or other [assistive technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assistive_technology) to print a [voter verified paper audit trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter_verified_paper_audit_trail), then use a separate machine for electronic tabulation. Hybrid voting often includes both e-voting and mail-in paper ballots.[^cite_note-90]

Internet voting can use remote locations (voting from any Internet capable computer) or can use traditional polling locations with voting booths consisting of Internet connected voting systems.

### Spreadsheets

Data from different tally sheets or machines are often summed in spreadsheets. This step can introduce errors. Austria,[^cite_note-aus-91] Scotland,[^cite_note-org-scot-92] and Anne Arundel County, MD,[^cite_note-cbs-aa-93] have made mistakes in summing tallies in spreadsheets. Arapahoe County, CO, made mistakes in releasing its spreadsheet for public use.[^cite_note-arap-94]

## Analysis



ISG TopVoter, a machine designed specifically to be used by voters with disabilities

Electronic voting systems may offer advantages compared to other voting techniques. An electronic voting system can be involved in any one of a number of steps in the setup, distributing, voting, collecting, and counting of ballots, and thus may or may not introduce advantages into any of these steps. Potential disadvantages exist as well including the potential for flaws or weakness in any electronic component.

Charles Stewart of the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](18879.md) estimates that 1 million more ballots were counted in the 2004 US presidential election than in 2000 because electronic voting machines detected votes that paper-based machines would have missed.[^cite_note-95]

In May 2004 the U.S. [Government Accountability Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Accountability_Office) released a report titled "Electronic Voting Offers Opportunities and Presents Challenges",[^cite_note-96] analyzing both the benefits and concerns created by electronic voting. A second report was released in September 2005 detailing some of the concerns with electronic voting, and ongoing improvements, titled "Federal Efforts to Improve Security and Reliability of Electronic Voting Systems Are Under Way, but Key Activities Need to Be Completed".[^cite_note-97]

### Electronic ballots

Electronic voting systems may use *electronic ballot* to store votes in [computer memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_memory). Systems which use them exclusively are called DRE voting systems. When electronic ballots are used there is no risk of exhausting the supply of ballots. Additionally, these electronic ballots remove the need for printing of paper ballots, a significant cost.[^cite_note-98] When administering elections in which ballots are offered in multiple languages (in some areas of the United States, public elections are required by the [National Voting Rights Act of 1965](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act)), electronic ballots can be programmed to provide ballots in multiple languages for a single machine. The advantage with respect to ballots in different languages appears to be unique to electronic voting. For example, [King County, Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_County,_Washington)'s demographics require them under U.S. federal election law to provide ballot access in Chinese (Mandarin?). With any type of paper ballot, the county has to decide how many Chinese-language ballots to print, how many to make available at each polling place, etc. Any strategy that can assure that Chinese-language ballots will be available at all polling places is certain, at the very least, to result in a significant number of wasted ballots. (The situation with lever machines would be even worse than with paper: the only apparent way to reliably meet the need would be to set up a Chinese-language lever machine at each polling place, few of which would be used at all.)

Critics argue the need for extra ballots in any language can be mitigated by providing a process to print ballots at voting locations. They argue further, the cost of software validation, compiler trust validation, installation validation, delivery validation and validation of other steps related to electronic voting is complex and expensive, thus electronic ballots are not guaranteed to be less costly than printed ballots.

### Accessibility



A Hart eSlate DRE voting machine with jelly buttons for people with manual dexterity disabilities

Electronic voting machines can be made fully accessible for persons with disabilities. Punched card and optical scan machines are not fully accessible for the blind or visually impaired, and lever machines can be difficult for voters with limited mobility and strength.[^cite_note-99] Electronic machines can use headphones, [sip and puff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sip-and-puff), foot pedals, joy sticks and other [adaptive technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptive_technology) to provide the necessary [accessibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accessibility).

Organizations such as the [Verified Voting Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verified_Voting_Foundation) have criticized the accessibility of electronic voting machines[^cite_note-100] and advocate alternatives. Some disabled voters (including the visually impaired) could use a [tactile ballot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tactile_ballot?action=edit\&redlink=1), a ballot system using physical markers to indicate where a mark should be made, to vote a secret paper ballot. These ballots can be designed identically to those used by other voters.[^cite_note-101] However, other disabled voters (including voters with dexterity disabilities) could be unable to use these ballots.

### Cryptographic verification

The concept of election verifiability through cryptographic solutions has emerged in the academic literature to introduce transparency and trust in electronic voting systems.[^cite_note-102][^cite_note-103] It allows voters and election observers to verify that votes have been recorded, tallied and declared correctly, in a manner independent from the hardware and software running the election. Three aspects of verifiability are considered:[^cite_note-104] individual, universal, and eligibility. Individual verifiability allows a voter to check that her own vote is included in the election outcome, universal verifiability allows voters or election observers to check that the election outcome corresponds to the votes cast, and eligibility verifiability allows voters and observers to check that each vote in the election outcome was cast by a uniquely registered voter.

### Voter intent

Electronic voting machines are able to provide immediate feedback to the voter detecting such possible problems as [undervoting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undervote) and [overvoting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overvote) which may result in a [spoiled ballot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoiled_ballot). This immediate feedback can be helpful in successfully determining [voter intent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter_intent?action=edit\&redlink=1).

### Transparency

It has been alleged by groups such as the UK-based [Open Rights Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Rights_Group)[^cite_note-105][^cite_note-106] that a lack of testing, inadequate audit procedures, and insufficient attention given to system or process design with electronic voting leaves "elections open to error and [fraud](382165.md)".

In 2009, the [Federal Constitutional Court of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Constitutional_Court_of_Germany) found that when using voting machines the "verification of the result must be possible by the citizen reliably and without any specialist knowledge of the subject." The [DRE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DRE_voting_machine) Nedap-computers used till then did not fulfill that requirement. The decision did not ban electronic voting as such, but requires all essential steps in elections to be subject to public examinability.[^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108]

In 2013, The [California Association of Voting Officials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Association_of_Voting_Officials) was formed to maintain efforts toward publicly owned General Public License open source voting systems

### Coercion evidence

In 2013, researchers from Europe proposed that the electronic voting systems should be coercion evident.[^cite_note-Grewal_2013-109] There should be a public evidence of the amount of coercion that took place in a particular elections. An internet voting system called "[Caveat Coercitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caveat_Coercitor?action=edit\&redlink=1)"[^cite_note-110] shows how coercion evidence in voting systems can be achieved.[^cite_note-Grewal_2013-109]

### Audit trails

Further information: [Voter Verified Paper Audit Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter_Verified_Paper_Audit_Trail) and [End-to-end auditable voting systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End-to-end_auditable_voting_systems)

A fundamental challenge with any [voting machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_machine) is to produce evidence that the votes were recorded as cast and tabulated as recorded. Election results produced by voting systems that rely on voter-marked paper ballots can be verified with manual hand counts (either valid sampling or full recounts). [Paperless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_machine#Direct-recording_electronic_\(DRE\)) ballot voting systems must support auditability in different ways. An independently auditable system, sometimes called an Independent Verification, can be used in recounts or audits. These systems can include the ability for voters to verify how their votes were cast or enable officials to verify that votes were tabulated correctly.

A discussion draft argued by researchers at the [National Institute of Standards and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology) (NIST) states, "Simply put, the DRE architecture's inability to provide for independent audits of its electronic records makes it a poor choice for an environment in which detecting errors and fraud is important."[^cite_note-111] The report does not represent the official position of NIST, and misinterpretations of the report has led NIST to explain that "Some statements in the report have been misinterpreted. The draft report includes statements from election officials, voting system vendors, computer scientists and other experts in the field about what is potentially possible in terms of attacks on DREs. However, these statements are not report conclusions."[^cite_note-112]



A Diebold Election Systems, Inc. model AccuVote-TSx DRE voting machine with VVPAT attachment

Various technologies can be used to assure DRE voters that their votes were cast correctly, and allow officials to detect possible fraud or malfunction, and to provide a means to audit the tabulated results. Some systems include technologies such as cryptography (visual or mathematical), paper (kept by the voter or verified and left with election officials), audio verification, and dual recording or witness systems (other than with paper).

Dr. [Rebecca Mercuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebecca_Mercuri), the creator of the [Voter Verified Paper Audit Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter_Verified_Paper_Audit_Trail) (VVPAT) concept (as described in her Ph.D. dissertation in October 2000 on the basic voter verifiable ballot system), proposes to answer the auditability question by having the voting machine print a paper ballot or other paper facsimile that can be visually verified by the voter before being entered into a secure location. Subsequently, this is sometimes referred to as the "[Mercuri method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercuri_method)." To be truly *voter*-verified, the record itself must be verified by the voter and able to be done without assistance, such as visually or audibly. If the voter must use a bar-code scanner or other electronic device to verify, then the record is not truly voter-verifiable, since it is actually the electronic device that is verifying the record for the voter. VVPAT is the form of Independent Verification most commonly found in [elections in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_the_United_States) and other countries such as Venezuela.[^cite_note-113]

[End-to-end auditable voting systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End-to-end_auditable_voting_systems) can provide the voter with a receipt that can be taken home. This receipt does not allow voters to prove to others how they voted, but it does allow them to verify that the system detected their vote correctly. End-to-end (E2E) systems include [Punchscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punchscan), [ThreeBallot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ThreeBallot) and [Prêt à Voter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pr%C3%AAt_%C3%A0_Voter). [Scantegrity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scantegrity) is an add-on that extends current optical scan voting systems with an E2E layer. The city of [Takoma Park, Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takoma_Park,_Maryland) used [Scantegrity II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scantegrity_II) for its November 2009 election.[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115]

Systems that allow the voter to prove how they voted are never used in U.S. public elections, and are outlawed by most state constitutions. The primary concerns with this solution are [voter intimidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter_intimidation) and [vote selling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_selling).

An audit system can be used in measured random recounts to detect possible malfunction or fraud. With the VVPAT method, the paper ballot is often treated as the official ballot of record. In this scenario, the ballot is primary and the electronic records are used only for an initial count. In any subsequent recounts or challenges, the paper, not the electronic ballot, would be used for tabulation. Whenever a paper record serves as the legal ballot, that system will be subject to the same benefits and concerns as any paper ballot system.

To successfully audit any voting machine, a strict [chain of custody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_of_custody) is required.

The solution was first demonstrated (New York City, March 2001) and used (Sacramento, California 2002) by AVANTE International Technology, Inc.. In 2004 Nevada was the first state to successfully implement a DRE voting system that printed an electronic record. The \$9.3 million voting system provided by [Sequoia Voting Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequoia_Voting_Systems) included more than 2,600 [AVC EDGE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AVC_EDGE?action=edit\&redlink=1) touchscreen DREs equipped with the [VeriVote VVPAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VeriVote_VVPAT?action=edit\&redlink=1) component. [^cite_note-116] The new systems, implemented under the direction of then Secretary of State [Dean Heller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_Heller) replaced largely punched card voting systems and were chosen after feedback was solicited from the community through town hall meetings and input solicited from the [Nevada Gaming Control Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevada_Gaming_Control_Board).[^cite_note-117]

### Hardware



Brazilian [DRE voting machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DRE_voting_machine)

Inadequately secured hardware can be subject to [physical tampering](382165.md#Tampering_with_electronic_voting_systems). Some critics, such as the group "[Wij vertrouwen stemcomputers niet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wij_vertrouwen_stemcomputers_niet?action=edit\&redlink=1)" ("We do not trust voting machines"), charge that, for instance, foreign hardware could be inserted into the machine, or between the user and the central mechanism of the machine itself, using a [man in the middle attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man_in_the_middle_attack) technique, and thus even sealing DRE machines may not be sufficient protection.[^cite_note-118] This claim is countered by the position that review and testing procedures can detect fraudulent code or hardware, if such things are present, and that a thorough, verifiable [chain of custody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_of_custody) would prevent the insertion of such hardware or software. [Security seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_seal) are commonly employed in an attempt to detect tampering, but testing by [Argonne National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argonne_National_Laboratory) and others demonstrates that existing seals can usually be quickly defeated by a trained person using low-tech methods.[^cite_note-119]

### Software

Security experts, such as [Bruce Schneier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Schneier), have demanded that voting machine [source code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source_code) should be publicly available for inspection.[^cite_note-120] Others have also suggested publishing voting machine software under a [free software license](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_software_license) as is done in Australia.[^cite_note-121]

### Testing and certification

See also: [Certification of voting machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Certification_of_voting_machines)

One method to detect errors with voting machines is [parallel testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_testing?action=edit\&redlink=1), which are conducted on the Election Day with randomly picked machines. The [ACM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_for_Computing_Machinery) published a study showing that, to change the outcome of the 2000 U.S. presidential election, only 2 votes in each precinct would have needed to be changed.[^cite_note-122]

### Cost

Cost of having electronic machines receive the voter's choices, print a ballot and scan the ballots to tally results is higher than the cost of printing blank ballots, having voters mark them directly (with machine-marking only when voters want it) and scanning ballots to tally results, according to studies in Georgia,[^cite_note-oset-reality-123][^cite_note-bmd-ga-124] New York[^cite_note-bmdny-125] and Pennsylvania.[^cite_note-pitt_cost-126]

## Adoption worldwide

This section is an excerpt from [Electronic voting by country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_by_country).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Electronic_voting_by_country\&action=edit)]

[Electronic voting by country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_by_country) varies and may include voting machines in polling places, centralized tallying of paper ballots, and internet voting. Many countries use centralized tallying. Some also use electronic voting machines in polling places. Very few use internet voting. Several countries have tried electronic approaches and stopped because of difficulties or concerns about security and reliability.

Electronic voting requires capital spending every few years to update equipment, as well as annual spending for maintenance, security, and supplies. If it works well, its speed can be an advantage where many contests are on each ballot. Hand-counting is more feasible in parliamentary systems where each level of government is elected at different times, and only one contest is on each ballot, for the national or regional member of parliament, or for a local council member.

Polling place electronic voting or Internet voting examples have taken place in Australia,[^cite_note-127] Belgium,[^cite_note-128][^cite_note-129] Brazil,[^cite_note-130] Estonia,[^cite_note-131][^cite_note-132] France, Germany, India,[^cite_note-Electronic_voting_by_country_pib.nic.in-133] Italy, Namibia, the Netherlands ([Rijnland Internet Election System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rijnland_Internet_Election_System)), Norway, Peru, Switzerland, the UK,[^cite_note-134] Venezuela,[^cite_note-135] Pakistan and the Philippines.[^cite_note-136]

To this date no Free or Open Source electronic voting systems have been used in elections.[^cite_note-137]

Filmed over three years, it documents American citizens investigating anomalies and irregularities with electronic voting systems that occurred during America's 2000 and 2004 elections, especially in [Volusia County, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volusia_County,_Florida). The film investigates the flawed integrity of electronic voting machines, particularly those made by [Diebold Election Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diebold_Election_Systems) and culminates in the hacking of a [Diebold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diebold) election system in [Leon County, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_County,_Florida).

The central conflict in the [MMO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massively_multiplayer_online_game) video game [Infantry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infantry_\(video_game\)) resulted from the global institution of [direct democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_democracy) through the use of personal voting devices sometime in the 22nd century AD. The practice gave rise to a 'voting class' of citizens composed mostly of homemakers and retirees who tended to be at home all day. Because they had the most free time to participate in voting, their opinions ultimately came to dominate politics.[^cite_note-139]
