**Italian** (*italiano*, pronounced [\[itaˈljaːno\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Italian) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/0/0e/It-italiano.ogg/It-italiano.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/0/0e/It-italiano.ogg/It-italiano.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>, or *lingua italiana*, pronounced [\[ˈliŋɡwa itaˈljaːna\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Italian)) is a [Romance language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_language) of the [Indo-European language family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_language_family). It is a standardised form of literary [Florentine Tuscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florentine_dialect)[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11] and, together with [Sardinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardinian_language), is the least differentiated language from Latin.[^cite_note-12] Current estimates indicate that between 68 and 85 million people speak Italian, including approximately 64 million native speakers as of 2024.

Italian is an [official language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_language) in [Italy](14532.md), [San Marino](27248.md), [Switzerland](26748.md) ([Ticino](84147.md) and part of the [Grisons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Grisons)), and [Vatican City](32408.md), and it has official [minority status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minority_language) in [Croatia](5573.md), [Slovenia](27338.md) ([Istria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovene_Istria)), [Romania](25445.md),[^cite_note-coe-6][^cite_note-13] [Bosnia and Herzegovina](3463.md),[^cite_note-coe-6] and in [6 municipalities of Brazil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Brazil#Language_co-officialization).[^cite_note-encantado-14][^cite_note-15] It is also spoken in other European and non-EU countries, most notably in [Malta](19137.md) (by 66% of the population),[^cite_note-16] [Albania](738.md) (upwards of 70%),[^cite_note-Zonova,_Tatiana_2013-17] and [Monaco](19261.md),[^cite_note-Italian_language-2] as well as by large [immigrant and expatriate communities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_diaspora) in the [Americas](29833.md), [Australia](4689264.md), and on other continents.[^cite_note-e25-1]

Italian is a major [language in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_language), being one of the official languages of the [Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe](22796.md) and one of the working languages of the [Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Europe). It is the third-most-widely spoken native [language in the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_the_European_Union) (13% of the EU population) and it is spoken as a second language by 13 million EU citizens (3%).[^cite_note-europa2024-18][^cite_note-forbes-19][^cite_note-europa2012-20] Italian is the main working language of the [Holy See](13393.md), serving as the [lingua franca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_franca) in the [Roman Catholic hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_hierarchy) and the official language of the [Sovereign Military Order of Malta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign_Military_Order_of_Malta). Italian influence led to the development of [derivated languages and dialects](#Influence_and_derived_languages) worldwide. It is also widespread in various sectors and markets, with [its loanwords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_English_words_of_Italian_origin) used in arts, luxury goods, fashion, sports and cuisine; it has a significant use in [musical terminology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_terminology) and [opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opera), with numerous Italian words referring to music that have become international terms taken into various languages worldwide, including [in English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Italian_musical_terms_used_in_English).[^cite_note-21]

Italian is considered a conservative Romance language in phonology, lexicon, and morphology. Almost all native Italian words end with [vowels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vowel), and the language has a 7-vowel [sound system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_system_\(linguistics\)) ("e" and "o" have mid-low and mid-high sounds).[^cite_note-22] Italian has contrast between short and [long consonants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consonant_length) and [gemination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemination) (doubling) of consonants.

## History

"History of Italian" redirects here. For the history of the Italian people, see [Italians](385155.md). For the history of the Italian culture, see [culture of Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Italy).

### Origins



The [Veronese Riddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veronese_Riddle) (c. 8th or early 9th century), a riddle reflecting either a form of Medieval Latin or the earliest extant example of Romance vernacular in Italy

The Italian language has developed through a long and slow process, which began after the [Western Roman Empire's fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire) and the onset of the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) in the 5th century.[^cite_note-23]

Latin, the predominant language of the western Roman Empire, remained the established written language in Europe during the Middle Ages, although most people were illiterate. Over centuries, the [Vulgar Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulgar_Latin) popularly spoken in various areas of Europe—including the [Italian peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_peninsula)—evolved into local varieties, or dialects, unaffected by formal standards and teachings. These varieties are not in any sense "dialects" of standard Italian, which itself started off as one of these local tongues, but [sister languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sister_language) of Italian.[^cite_note-\:0-24][^cite_note-25] The Latin-speaking class referred to the collective Romance vernaculars of Europe as *Romanz*, *Romance*, or, in Italy, *Romanzo* or *Volgare*.[^cite_note-26]

The linguistic and historical demarcations between late Vulgar Latin and early Romance varieties in Italy are imprecise. The earliest surviving texts that can definitely be called vernacular (as distinct from its predecessor Vulgar Latin) are legal formulae known as the [Placiti Cassinesi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placiti_Cassinesi) from the [province of Benevento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Benevento) that date from 960 to 963, although the [Veronese Riddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veronese_Riddle), probably from the 8th or early 9th century, contains a late form of Vulgar Latin that can be seen as a very early sample of a vernacular dialect of Italy.[^cite_note-27] The [Commodilla catacomb inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodilla_catacomb_inscription) likewise probably dates to the early 9th century and appears to reflect a language somewhere between late Vulgar Latin and early vernacular.



[Dante Alighieri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Alighieri), whose works helped establish modern Italian language, is considered one of the greatest poets of the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages). His epic poem *[Divine Comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy)* ranks among the finest works of [world literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_literature).

[^cite_note-28]

The language that came to be thought of as Italian developed in central Tuscany and was first formalized in the early 14th century through the works of Tuscan writer [Dante Alighieri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Alighieri), written in his native [Florentine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florentine_dialect). Dante's [epic poems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_poems), known collectively as the *[Commedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy)*, to which another Tuscan poet [Giovanni Boccaccio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Boccaccio) later affixed the title *Divina*, were read throughout the Italian peninsula. His written vernacular became the touchstone for elaborating a "canonical standard" that all educated Italians could understand. The poetry of [Petrarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrarch) was also widely admired and influential in the development of the literary language, and would be identified as a model for vernacular writing by [Pietro Bembo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietro_Bembo) in the 16th century.

In addition to the widespread exposure gained through literature, Florentine also gained prestige due to the political and cultural significance of Florence at the time and the fact that it was linguistically a middle way between the northern and the southern Italian dialects.

Italian was progressively made an official language of most of the Italian states predating unification, slowly replacing Latin, even when ruled by foreign powers (such as Spain in the [Kingdom of Naples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Naples), or Austria in the [Kingdom of Lombardy–Venetia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Lombardy%E2%80%93Venetia)), although the masses kept speaking primarily their local vernaculars. Italian was also one of the many recognised languages in the [Austro-Hungarian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_Empire).

Italy has always had a distinctive vernacular for each city because the cities, until recently, were thought of as [city-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City-state#Italy). Those local languages have considerable [variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variety_\(linguistics\)), and as Tuscan-derived Italian came to be used throughout Italy, features of local speech were naturally adopted, producing various versions of [Regional Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_Italian). The most characteristic differences, for instance, between [Roman](25458.md) Italian and [Milanese](36511.md) Italian are [syntactic gemination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntactic_gemination) of initial [consonants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consonant) in some contexts and the pronunciation of stressed "e", and of "s" between vowels in many words: e.g. *va bene* 'all right' is pronounced \[vabˈbɛːne] by a Roman (and by any standard Italian speaker), \[vaˈbeːne] by a Milanese (and by any speaker whose native dialect lies to the north of the [La Spezia–Rimini Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Spezia%E2%80%93Rimini_Line)); *a casa* 'at home' is \[akˈkaːsa] for Roman, \[akˈkaːsa] or \[akˈkaːza] for standard, \[aˈkaːza] for Milanese and generally northern.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBerloco2018-29]

In contrast to the [Gallo-Italic linguistic panorama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallo-Italic_languages) of northern Italy, the [Italo-Dalmatian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italo-Dalmatian), [Neapolitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neapolitan_language) and its related dialects were largely unaffected by the Franco-[Occitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occitan_language) influences introduced to Italy mainly by [bards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bard) from France during the Middle Ages, but after the [Norman conquest of southern Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_conquest_of_southern_Italy), Sicily became the first Italian land to adopt Occitan lyric moods (and words) in poetry. Even in the case of northern Italian languages, however, scholars are careful not to overstate the effects of outsiders on the natural indigenous developments of the languages.

The economic might and relatively advanced development of Tuscany at the time ([Late Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Middle_Ages)) gave its language weight, although [Venetian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_language) remained widespread in medieval Italian commercial life, and [Ligurian (or Genoese)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligurian_language_\(Romance\)) remained in use in maritime trade alongside the Mediterranean. The increasing political and cultural relevance of Florence during the periods of the rise of the [Medici Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medici_Bank), [humanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_humanism), and the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance) made its dialect, or rather a refined version of it, a standard in the arts.

### Renaissance

The [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance) era, known as *il Rinascimento* in Italian, was seen as a time of rebirth, which is the literal meaning of both *renaissance* (from French) and *rinascimento* (Italian). Among its many manifestations, the Renaissance saw a reinvigorated interest in both classical antiquity and vernacular literature.[^cite_note-30]

Advancements in technology played a crucial role in the diffusion of the Italian language. The [printing press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_press) was invented in the 15th century, and spread rapidly. By the year 1500, there were 56 printing presses in Italy, more than anywhere else in Europe. The printing press enabled the production of literature and documents in higher volumes and at lower cost, further accelerating the spread of Italian.[^cite_note-31]

Italian became the language used in the courts of every state in the [Italian peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_peninsula), and the [prestige variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prestige_variety) used on the island of [Corsica](5714828.md)[^cite_note-32] (but not in the neighbouring [Sardinia](29376.md), which on the contrary underwent [Italianization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italianization) well into the late 18th century, under [Savoyard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Savoy) sway: the island's linguistic composition, roofed by the prestige of Spanish among the [Sardinians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardinians), would therein make for a rather slow process of [assimilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_assimilation) to the Italian cultural sphere[^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34]). The rediscovery of Dante's *[De vulgari eloquentia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_vulgari_eloquentia)*, and a renewed interest in linguistics in the 16th century, sparked a debate that raged throughout Italy concerning the criteria that should govern the establishment of a modern Italian literary and spoken language. This discussion, known as [questione della lingua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Questione_della_lingua) (i.e., the *problem of the language*), ran through the Italian culture until the end of the 19th century, often linked to the political debate on achieving a united Italian state. Renaissance scholars divided into three main factions:

- The [purists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purism_\(language\)), headed by Venetian [Pietro Bembo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietro_Bembo) (who, in his *[Gli Asolani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gli_Asolani)*, claimed the language might be based only on the great literary classics, such as [Petrarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrarch) and some part of Boccaccio). The purists thought the *[Divine Comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy)* was not dignified enough because it used elements from non-lyric registers of the language.

- [Niccolò Machiavelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niccol%C3%B2_Machiavelli) and other [Florentines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence) preferred the version spoken by ordinary people in their own times.

- The [courtiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courtier), such as [Baldassare Castiglione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldassare_Castiglione) and [Gian Giorgio Trissino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gian_Giorgio_Trissino), insisted that each local vernacular contribute to the new standard.

A fourth faction claimed that the best Italian was the one that the papal court adopted, which was a mixture of the [Tuscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuscan_dialect) and [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_dialect) dialects.[^cite_note-35] Eventually, Bembo's ideas prevailed, and the foundation of the [Accademia della Crusca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accademia_della_Crusca) in Florence (1582–1583), the official legislative body of the Italian language, led to the publication of [Agnolo Monosini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnolo_Monosini)'s Latin tome *[Floris italicae linguae libri novem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floris_Italicae_lingue_libri_novem)* in 1604 followed by the first Italian dictionary in 1612.

An important event that helped the diffusion of Italian was the conquest and occupation of Italy by [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon) (himself of Italian-Corsican descent) in the early 19th century. This conquest propelled the [unification of Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unification_of_Italy) some decades after and pushed the Italian language into the status of a [lingua franca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_franca), used not only among clerks, nobility, and functionaries in the Italian courts, but also by the [bourgeoisie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourgeoisie).

Today Italy has reached linguistic unity and an overwhelming majority of its 56 million citizens speak Italian. Many dialects are still alive, especially by the older generations. Today, Italian is one of the most studied foreign languages in the world.[^cite_note-36]

### Contemporary times



[Alessandro Manzoni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alessandro_Manzoni) is famous for the novel *[The Betrothed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Betrothed)* (1827), ranked among the masterpieces of world literature.

[^cite_note-britannica-37]

&#x20;He contributed to the nationwide use of the Italian language.

[^cite_note-38]

The publication of Italian literature's first modern novel, *I promessi sposi* (*[The Betrothed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Betrothed)*) by [Alessandro Manzoni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alessandro_Manzoni), both reflected and furthered the growing trend towards Italian as a national standard language. Manzoni, a Milanesian, chose to write the book in the Florentine dialect, describing this choice, in the preface to his 1840 edition, as "rinsing" his Milanese "in the waters of the [Arno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arno)" ([Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence)'s river). The novel is commonly described as "the most widely read work in the Italian language".[^cite_note-Betrothed-39] It became a model for subsequent Italian literary fiction,[^cite_note-Betrothed-39] helping to galvanize national linguistic unity around the Florentine dialect.

This growth was initially relative; linguistic diversity continued during the unification of Italy (1848–1871). The Italian linguist [Tullio De Mauro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tullio_De_Mauro) estimated that only 2.5% of Italy's population could speak the Italian standardized language properly in 1861,[^cite_note-40] while Arrigo Castellani estimated the same value as 10%.[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42]

## Classification

Italian is a [Romance language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_language), a descendant of [Vulgar Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulgar_Latin) (colloquial spoken Latin). Standard Italian is based on [Tuscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuscan_language), especially its [Florentine dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florentine_dialect), and is, therefore, an [Italo-Dalmatian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italo-Dalmatian_language), a classification that includes most other central and southern Italian languages and the extinct [Dalmatian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalmatian_language). As in most Romance languages, [stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_\(linguistics\)) is distinctive in Italian.[^cite_note-43]

According to *[Ethnologue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnologue)*, [lexical similarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexical_similarity) is 89% with French, 87% with [Catalan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan_language), 85% with [Sardinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardinian_language), 82% with Spanish, 82% with [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language), 78% with [Ladin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ladin_language), 77% with [Romanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_language).[^cite_note-e25-1] Estimates may differ according to sources.[^cite_note-ezglot.com-44]

A 1949 study by the linguist [Mario Pei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Pei) concluded that out of seven Romance languages, Italian's stressed vowel phonology was the second-closest to that of Classical Latin (after [Logudorese Sardinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logudorese_Sardinian)).[^cite_note-45] The study emphasized, however, that it represented only "a very elementary, incomplete and tentative demonstration" of how statistical methods could measure linguistic change, assigned "frankly arbitrary" point values to various types of change, and did not compare languages in the sample with respect to any characteristics or forms of divergence other than stressed vowels, among other caveats.[^cite_note-46][^cite_note-47]

Italian is the official language of Italy and [San Marino](27248.md) and is spoken fluently by the majority of the countries' populations. Italian is the third most spoken language in [Switzerland](26748.md) (after German and French; see [Swiss Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_Italian)). It is official both on the national level and on regional level in two [cantons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canton_of_Switzerland): [Ticino](84147.md) and Grisons. Ticino, which includes [Lugano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lugano), the largest Italian-speaking city outside Italy, is the only canton where Italian is predominant.[^cite_note-48] Italian is also used in administration and official documents in [Vatican City](32408.md).[^cite_note-49]

Italian is also spoken by a minority in [Monaco](19261.md) and France, especially in the southeastern part of the country.[^cite_note-2008census-50][^cite_note-e25-1] Italian was the official language in [Savoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savoy) and in [Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nice) until 1860, when they were both annexed by France under the [Treaty of Turin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Turin_\(1860\)), a development that triggered the "[Niçard exodus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ni%C3%A7ard_exodus)", or the emigration of a quarter of the [Niçard Italians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ni%C3%A7ard_Italians) to Italy,[^cite_note-51] and the [Niçard Vespers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ni%C3%A7ard_Vespers). [Giuseppe Garibaldi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Garibaldi) complained about the referendum that allowed France to annex Savoy and Nice, and a group of his followers (among the [Italian Savoyards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Savoyards)) took refuge in Italy in the following years. [Corsica](5714828.md) passed from the [Republic of Genoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Genoa) to France in 1769 after the [Treaty of Versailles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Versailles_\(1768\)). Italian was the official language of [Corsica](5714828.md) until 1859.[^cite_note-52] Giuseppe Garibaldi called for the inclusion of the "[Corsican Italians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corsican_Italians)" within Italy when [Rome was annexed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capture_of_Rome) to the [Kingdom of Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Italy), but King [Victor Emmanuel II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Emmanuel_II) did not agree to it. Italian is generally understood in Corsica by the population resident therein who speak [Corsican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corsican_language), which is an Italo-Romance language similar to Tuscan.[^cite_note-Corsican-53] [Francization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francization) occurred in Nice case, and caused a near-disappearance of the Italian language as many of the Italian speakers in these areas migrated to Italy.[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-rivistaetnie-55] In Corsica, on the other hand, almost everyone still speaks the [Corsican language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corsican_language), which, due to its linguistic proximity to the Italian standard language, appears both linguistically as an Italian dialect and therefore as a carrier of Italian culture, despite the French government's decades-long efforts to cut Corsica off from the Italian motherland. Italian was the official language in [Monaco](19261.md) until 1860, when it was replaced by the French.[^cite_note-miglioverde-56] This was due to the annexation of the surrounding [County of Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_Nice) to France following the [Treaty of Turin (1860)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Turin_\(1860\)).[^cite_note-miglioverde-56]



Percent of inhabitants with Italian native tongue in [Croatia](5573.md)'s and [Slovenia](27338.md)'s [Istria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istria)

It formerly had official status in [Montenegro](20760.md) (because of the [Venetian Albania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_Albania)), parts of [Slovenia](27338.md) and [Croatia](5573.md) (because of the [Venetian Istria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_Istria) and [Venetian Dalmatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_Dalmatia)), parts of [Greece](12108.md) (because of the [Venetian rule in the Ionian Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_rule_in_the_Ionian_Islands) and by the [Kingdom of Italy in the Dodecanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Islands_of_the_Aegean)). Italian is widely spoken in [Malta](19137.md), where nearly two-thirds of the population can speak it fluently (see [Maltese Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltese_Italian)).[^cite_note-Europoll-57] Italian served as Malta's official language until 1934, when it was abolished by the British colonial administration amid strong local opposition.[^cite_note-58] [Italian language in Slovenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_language_in_Slovenia) is an officially recognised [minority language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minority_language) in the country.[^cite_note-ita-slo-59] The official census, carried out in 2002, reported 2,258 ethnic Italians ([Istrian Italians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istrian_Italians)) in [Slovenia](27338.md) (0.11% of the total population).[^cite_note-stat-60] [Italian language in Croatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_language_in_Croatia) is an official minority language in the country, with many schools and public announcements published in both languages.[^cite_note-ita-slo-59] The 2001 census in [Croatia](5573.md) reported 19,636 ethnic Italians (Istrian Italians and [Dalmatian Italians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalmatian_Italians)) in the country (some 0.42% of the total population).[^cite_note-dzs-61] Their numbers dropped dramatically after [World War II](32927.md) following the [Istrian–Dalmatian exodus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istrian%E2%80%93Dalmatian_exodus), which caused the emigration of between 230,000 and 350,000 Istrian Italians and Dalmatian Italians.[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-query.nytimes.com-63] Italian was the official language of the [Republic of Ragusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Ragusa) from 1492 to 1807.[^cite_note-64]



Italy and [its colonial possessions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Empire) during [WW2](32927.md)

It formerly had official status in [Albania](738.md) due to the [annexation of the country to the Kingdom of Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_invasion_of_Albania) (1939–1943). Albania has a large population of non-native speakers, with over half of the population having some knowledge of the Italian language.[^cite_note-Zonova,_Tatiana_2013-17] The Albanian government has pushed to make Italian a compulsory second language in schools.[^cite_note-65] The Italian language is well-known and studied in Albania,[^cite_note-66] due to its historical ties and geographical proximity to Italy and to the diffusion of Italian television in the country.[^cite_note-67]

Due to heavy Italian influence during the [Italian colonial period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Empire), Italian is still understood by some in former colonies such as Libya.[^cite_note-e25-1] Although it was the primary language in [Libya](17633.md) since [colonial rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Libya), Italian greatly declined under the [rule of Muammar Gaddafi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Libya_under_Muammar_Gaddafi), who expelled the [Italian Libyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Libyan) population and made [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Standard_Arabic) the sole official language of the country.[^cite_note-68] A few hundred Italian settlers returned to Libya in the 2000s.

Italian was the official language of [Eritrea](17238590.md) during [Italian colonisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Eritrea). Italian is today used in commerce, and it is still spoken especially among elders; besides that, Italian words are incorporated as loan words in the main language spoken in the country (Tigrinya). The capital city of Eritrea, [Asmara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asmara), still has several Italian schools, established during the colonial period. In the early 19th century, Eritrea was the country with the highest number of Italians abroad, and the [Italian Eritreans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Eritreans) grew from 4,000 during World War I to nearly 100,000 at the beginning of World War II.[^cite_note-69] In Asmara there are two Italian schools, the [Istituto Italiano Statale Omnicomprensivo di Asmara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istituto_Italiano_Statale_Omnicomprensivo_di_Asmara) (Italian primary school with a [Montessori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montessori) department) and the [Liceo Sperimentale "G. Marconi"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istituto_Italiano_Statale_Omnicomprensivo_di_Asmara#Divisions) (Italian international senior high school).

Italian was also introduced to [Somalia](27358.md) through colonialism and was the sole official language of administration and education during the [colonial period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Somaliland) but fell out of use after government, educational and economic infrastructure were destroyed in the [Somali Civil War](3070332.md).



[Italian language in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_language_in_the_United_States)

Italian is also spoken by large [immigrant and expatriate communities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_diaspora) in the Americas and Australia.[^cite_note-e25-1] Although over 17 million [Americans are of Italian descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_American), only a little over one million people in the United States speak Italian at home.[^cite_note-70] Nevertheless, an Italian language media market does exist in the country.[^cite_note-71] In Canada, Italian is the second most spoken non-official language when [varieties of Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varieties_of_Chinese) are not grouped together, with 375,645 claiming Italian as their [mother tongue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demolinguistic_descriptors_used_in_Canada#Mother_tongue) in 2016.[^cite_note-72]

Italian immigrants to South America have also brought a presence of the language to that continent. According to some sources, Italian is the second most spoken language in [Argentina](18951905.md)[^cite_note-73] after the official language of Spanish, although its number of speakers, mainly of the older generation, is decreasing. Italian bilingual speakers can be found scattered across the southeast of Brazil and in the south.[^cite_note-e25-1] In [Venezuela](32374.md), Italian is the most spoken language after Spanish and Portuguese, with around 200,000 speakers.[^cite_note-L'ITALIANO_IN_VENEZUELA-74] In [Uruguay](31843.md), people who speak Italian as their home language are 1.1% of the total population of the country.[^cite_note-75] In Australia, Italian is the second most spoken foreign language after Chinese, with 1.4% of the population speaking it as their home language.[^cite_note-76]

### Education



[Italian Secondary School in Rijeka/Fiume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Secondary_School,_Rijeka), [Croatia](5573.md)

Italian is widely taught in many schools around the world. In the 21st century, technology also allows for the continual spread of the Italian language, as people have new ways to learn how to speak, read, and write languages at their own pace and at any given time. For example, the free website and application [Duolingo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duolingo) has 4.94 million English speakers learning the Italian language.[^cite_note-80]

According to the [Italian Ministry of Foreign Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Ministry_of_Foreign_Affairs), every year there are more than 200,000 foreign students who study the Italian language; they are distributed among the 90 [Institutes of Italian Culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Cultural_Institute) that are located around the world, in the 179 Italian schools located abroad, or in the 111 Italian lecturer sections belonging to foreign schools where Italian is taught as a language of culture.[^cite_note-esteri.it-81]

As of 2022, Australia had the highest number of students learning Italian in the world. This occurred because of support by the Italian community in Australia and the Italian Government and also because of successful educational reform efforts led by local governments in Australia.[^cite_note-82]

### Lingua franca

See also: [Mediterranean Lingua Franca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Lingua_Franca)

Starting in late [medieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval) times in much of Europe and the Mediterranean, Latin was replaced as the primary commercial language by languages of Italy, especially Tuscan and Venetian. These varieties were consolidated during the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance) with the strength of Italy and the rise of [humanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_humanism) and [the arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_arts).

Italy came to enjoy increasing artistic prestige within Europe. A mark of the educated gentlemen was to make the [Grand Tour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Tour), visiting Italy to see its great historical monuments and works of art. It was expected that the visitor would learn at least some Italian, understood as language based on Florentine. In England, while the classical languages [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) and [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) were the first to be learned, Italian became the second most common modern language after French, a position it held until the late 18th century when it tended to be replaced by German. [John Milton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Milton), for instance, wrote some of his early poetry in Italian.

Within the [Catholic Church](606848.md), Italian is known by a large part of the ecclesiastical hierarchy and is used in substitution for Latin in some official documents.

Italian [loanwords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loanword) continue to be used in most languages in matters of art and music (especially classical music including opera), in the design and fashion industries, in some sports such as [football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_\(association\))[^cite_note-83] and especially in culinary terms.

## Languages and dialects

See also: [Languages of Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Italy) and [Regional Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_Italian)



Linguistic map of Italy according to Clemente Merlo and Carlo Tagliavini (1937)



Italy's ethno-linguistic minorities

[^cite_note-84]

In Italy, almost all the [other languages spoken as the vernacular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Italy)—other than standard Italian and some languages spoken among immigrant communities—are often called "[Italian dialects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Italy)", a label that can be very misleading if it is understood to mean "dialects of Italian". The Romance dialects of Italy are local evolutions of spoken Latin that pre-date the establishment of Italian, and as such are [sister languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sister_language) to the Tuscan that was the historical source of Italian. They can be quite different from Italian and from each other, with some belonging to different linguistic branches of Romance. The only exceptions to this are twelve groups considered "[historical language minorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minority_languages_of_Italy)", which are officially recognised as distinct [minority languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minority_language) by the law. On the other hand, [Corsican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corsican_language) (a language spoken on the French island of [Corsica](5714828.md)) is closely related to medieval [Tuscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuscan_dialect), from which standard Italian derives and evolved.

The differences in the evolution of Latin in the different regions of Italy can be attributed to the natural [changes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_change#Causes) that all languages in regular use are subject to, and to some extent to the presence of three other types of languages: [substrata, superstrata, and adstrata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratum_\(linguistics\)). The most prevalent were substrata (the language of the original inhabitants), as the Italian dialects were most probably simply Latin as spoken by native cultural groups. Superstrata and adstrata were both less important. Foreign conquerors of Italy that dominated different regions at different times left behind little to no influence on the dialects. Foreign cultures with which Italy engaged in peaceful relations with, such as trade, had no significant influence either.[^cite_note-\:0-24]<sup>: 19–20 </sup>

Throughout Italy, regional varieties of standard Italian, called [Regional Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_Italian), are spoken. Regional differences can be recognised by various factors: the openness of vowels, the length of the consonants, and influence of the local language (for example, in informal situations *_[andà](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/and%C3%A0#Italian)_*, *_[annà](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/ann%C3%A0#Italian)_* and *_[nare](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/nare#Italian)_* replace the standard Italian *_[andare](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/andare#Italian)_* in the area of Tuscany, Rome and Venice respectively for the infinitive 'to go').

There is no definitive date when the various Italian variants of Latin—including varieties that contributed to modern standard Italian—began to be distinct enough from Latin to be considered separate languages. One criterion for determining that two language variants are to be considered separate languages rather than variants of a single language is that they have evolved so that they are no longer [mutually intelligible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutually_intelligible); this diagnostic is effective if mutual intelligibility is minimal or absent (e.g. in Romance, Romanian and Portuguese), but it fails in cases such as Spanish-Portuguese or Spanish-Italian, as educated native speakers of either pairing (particularly Spanish-Portuguese) can understand each other well if they choose to do so; however, the level of intelligibility is markedly lower between Italian-Spanish, and considerably higher between the Iberian sister languages of Portuguese-Spanish. Speakers of this latter pair can communicate with one another with remarkable ease, each speaking to the other in his own native language, without slang/jargon.

Nevertheless, on the basis of accumulated differences in morphology, syntax, phonology, and to some extent lexicon, it is not difficult to identify that for the Romance varieties of Italy, the first extant written evidence of languages that can no longer be considered Latin comes from the 9th and 10th centuries CE. These written sources demonstrate certain vernacular characteristics and sometimes explicitly mention the use of the vernacular in Italy.

Full literary manifestations of the vernacular began to surface around the 13th century in the form of various religious texts and poetry.[^cite_note-\:0-24]<sup>: 21 </sup>Although these are the first written records of Italian varieties separate from Latin, the spoken language had probably diverged long before the first written records appeared since those who were literate generally wrote in Latin even if they spoke other Romance varieties in person.

Throughout the 19th and 20th centuries, the use of standard Italian became increasingly widespread and was mirrored by a decline in the use of the dialects. An increase in literacy was one of the main driving factors (one can assume that only literates were capable of learning standard Italian, whereas those who were illiterate had access only to their native dialect). The percentage of literates rose from 25% in 1861 to 60% in 1911, and then on to 78.1% in 1951. [Tullio De Mauro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tullio_De_Mauro), an Italian linguist, has asserted that in 1861, only 2.5% of the population of Italy could speak standard Italian. He reports that in 1951, that percentage had risen to 87%. The ability to speak Italian did not necessarily mean that it was in everyday use, and most people (63.5%) still usually spoke their native dialects. In addition, other factors such as mass emigration, industrialization, and urbanization, and internal migrations after [World War II](32927.md), contributed to the proliferation of standard Italian. The Italians who emigrated during the [Italian diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_diaspora) beginning in 1861 were often of the uneducated lower class, and thus the emigration had the effect of increasing the percentage of literates, who often knew and understood the importance of standard Italian back home in Italy. A large percentage of those who had emigrated also eventually returned to Italy, often more educated than when they had left.[^cite_note-\:0-24]<sup>: 35 </sup>

Although use of the Italian dialects has declined in the [modern era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_era), as Italy unified under standard Italian and continues to do so aided by mass media from newspapers to radio to television, [diglossia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diglossia) is still frequently encountered in Italy and [triglossia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triglossia) is not uncommon in emigrant communities among older speakers. Both situations normally involve some degree of [code-switching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code-switching) and [code-mixing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code-mixing).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPrifti2014-85]

- Italian *settimana* 'week' < Latin septimāna (cf. Romanian *săptămână*, Spanish and Portuguese *semana*, French *semaine* /səmɛn/, [Catalan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan_language) *setmana*)

### Evolution from Latin

The conservative nature of Italian phonology is partly explained by its origin. Italian stems from a literary language that is derived from the 13th-century speech of the city of [Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence) in the region of [Tuscany](21967242.md), and has changed little in the last 700 years or so. Furthermore, the Tuscan dialect is the most conservative of all [Italian dialects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_Italian), radically different from the [Gallo-Italian languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallo-Italian_languages) less than 160 kilometres (100 mi) to the north (across the [La Spezia–Rimini Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Spezia%E2%80%93Rimini_Line)).

The following are some of the conservative phonological features of Italian, as compared with the common [Western Romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Romance) languages (French, Spanish, [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language), [Galician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galician_language), [Catalan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan_language)). Some of these features are also present in [Romanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_language).

- Little or no [phonemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonemic) [lenition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenition) of consonants between vowels, e.g. vīta > *vita* 'life' (cf. Romanian *viață*, Spanish *vida* \[ˈbiða], French *vie*), pedem > *piede* 'foot' (cf. Spanish *pie*, French *pied* /pje/).&#x20;

  - Words that are an exception to this rule exist, such as: scvtella > *scodella* 'bowl', recipere > *ricevere* 'receive', lacvs > *lago* 'lake', acvs > *ago* 'needle', (only in the [Tuscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuscan_dialect) accent and historical standard Italian) vīsus > *viso* /vizo/ 'face'.

    [^cite_note-109]

- Preservation of geminate consonants, e.g. annum > /ˈanːo/ *anno* 'year' (cf. Spanish *año* /ˈaɲo/, French *an* /ɑ̃/, Romanian *an*, Portuguese *ano* /ˈɐnu/).

- Preservation of all [Proto-Romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Romance) final vowels, e.g. pacem > *pace* 'peace' (cf. Romanian *pace*, Spanish *paz*, French *paix* /pɛ/), octō > *otto* 'eight' (cf. Romanian *opt*, Spanish *ocho*, French *huit* /ɥi(t)/), fēcī > *feci* 'I did' (cf. Romanian dialectal *feci*, Spanish *hice*, French *fis* /fi/).

- Preservation of most intertonic vowels (those between the stressed syllable and either the beginning or ending syllable). This accounts for some of the most noticeable differences, as in the forms *quattordici* and *settimana* given above.

- Slower consonant development, e.g. folia > Italo-Western /fɔʎʎa/ > *foglia* /ˈfɔʎʎa/ 'leaf' (cf. Romanian *foaie* /ˈfo̯aje/, Spanish *hoja* /ˈoxa/, French *feuille* /fœj/; but note Portuguese *folha* /ˈfoʎɐ/).

Compared with most other Romance languages, Italian has many inconsistent outcomes, where the same underlying sound produces different results in different words, e.g. laxāre > *lasciare* and *lassare*, captiāre > *cacciare* and *cazzare*, (ex)dēroteolāre > *sdrucciolare*, *druzzolare* and *ruzzolare*, rēgīna > *regina* and *reina*. Although in all these examples the second form has fallen out of usage, the dimorphism is thought to reflect the several-hundred-year period during which Italian developed as a literary language divorced from any native-speaking population, with an origin in 12th/13th-century Tuscan but with many words borrowed from [languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Italy) farther to the north, with different sound outcomes. (The [La Spezia–Rimini Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Spezia%E2%80%93Rimini_Line), the most important [isogloss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isogloss) in the entire Romance-language area, passes only about 30 kilometres or 20 miles north of Florence). Dual outcomes of Latin /p t k/ between vowels, such as lŏcvm > *luogo* but fŏcvm > *fuoco*, was once thought to be due to borrowing of northern voiced forms, but is now generally viewed as the result of early phonetic variation within Tuscany.

Some other features that distinguish Italian from the Western Romance languages:

- Latin ce-,ci- becomes /tʃe, tʃi/ rather than /(t)se, (t)si/.

- Latin -ct- becomes /tt/ rather than /jt/ or /tʃ/: octō > *otto* 'eight' (cf. Spanish *ocho*, French *huit,* Portuguese *oito*).

- Vulgar Latin -cl- becomes *cchi* /kkj/ rather than /ʎ/: oclum > *occhio* 'eye' (cf. Portuguese *olho* /ˈoʎu/, French *œil* /œj/ < /œʎ/); but Romanian *ochi* /okʲ/.

- Final /s/ is not preserved, and vowel changes rather than /s/ are used to mark the plural: *amico*, *amici* 'male friend(s)', *amica*, *amiche* 'female friend(s)' (cf. Romanian *amic*, *amici* and *amică*, *amice*; Spanish *amigo(s)* 'male friend(s)', *amiga(s)* 'female friend(s)'); trēs, sex → *tre, sei* 'three, six' (cf. Romanian *trei*, *șase*; Spanish *tres*, *seis*).

Standard Italian also differs in some respects from most nearby Italian languages:

- Perhaps most noticeable is the total lack of [metaphony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphony_\(Romance_languages\)), although metaphony is a feature characterizing nearly every other [Italian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Italy).

- No simplification of original /nd/, /mb/ (which often became /nn/, /mm/ elsewhere).

## Writing system

Main article: [Italian orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_orthography)



An Italian handwriting script, taught in primary school

Italian has a [shallow orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shallow_orthography), meaning very regular spelling with an almost one-to-one correspondence between letters and sounds. In linguistic terms, the writing system is close to being a [phonemic orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonemic_orthography).[^cite_note-110] The most important of the few exceptions are the following (see below for more details):

- The letter c represents the sound /k/ at the end of words and before the letters a, o, and u but represents the sound /[tʃ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_postalveolar_affricate)/ (as the first sound in the English word *chair*) before the letters e and i.

- The letter g represents the sound /ɡ/ at the end of words and before the letters a, o, and u but represents the sound /[dʒ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiced_postalveolar_affricate)/ (as the first sound in the English word *gem*) before the letters e and i.

- The letter n represents the phoneme /n/, which is pronounced \[ŋ] (as in the English word *sing*) before the letters c and g when these represent velar plosives /k/ or /ɡ/, as in *banco* \[ˈbaŋko], *fungo* \[ˈfuŋɡo]. The letter q represents /k/ pronounced \[k], thus n also represents \[ŋ] in the position preceding it: *cinque* \[ˈt͡ʃiŋkwe]. Elsewhere the letter n represents /n/ pronounced \[n], including before the [affricates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affricate) /tʃ/ or /dʒ/ spelt with c or g before the letters i and e : *mancia* \[ˈmant͡ʃa], *mangia* \[ˈmand͡ʒa].

- The letter *h* is always silent: *hotel* /oˈtɛl/; *hanno* 'they have' and *anno* 'year' both represent /ˈanno/. It is used to form a [digraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digraph_\(orthography\)) with *c* or *g* to represent /k/ or /ɡ/ before *i* or *e*: *chi* /ki/ 'who', *che* /ke/ 'what'; *aghi* /ˈaɡi/ 'needles', *ghetto* /ˈɡetto/.

- The spellings *ci* and *gi* before another vowel represent only /tʃ/ or /dʒ/ with no /i/ sound (*ciuccio* /ˈtʃuttʃo/ 'pacifier', *Giorgio* /ˈdʒordʒo/) unless *c* or *g* precede stressed /i/ (*farmacia* /farmaˈtʃi.a/ 'pharmacy', *biologia* /bioloˈdʒi.a/ 'biology'). Elsewhere *ci* and *gi* represent /tʃ/ and /dʒ/ followed by /i/: *cibo* /ˈtʃibo/ 'food', *baci* /ˈbatʃi/ 'kisses'; *gita* /ˈdʒita/ 'trip', *Tamigi* /taˈmidʒi/ 'Thames'.\*

The Italian alphabet is typically considered to consist of 21 letters. The letters j, k, w, x, y are traditionally excluded, although they appear in loanwords such as *jeans*, *whisky*, *taxi*, and *xilofono*. The letter ⟨x⟩ has become common in standard Italian with the prefix *extra-*, although *(e)stra-* is traditionally used; it is also common to use the Latin particle *ex(-)* to mean 'former(ly)' as in *la mia ex* ('my ex-girlfriend'), "Ex-Jugoslavia" ('Former Yugoslavia'). The letter ⟨j⟩ appears in the first name *Jacopo* and in some Italian place-names, such as [Bajardo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bajardo), [Bojano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bojano), [Joppolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joppolo), [Jerzu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerzu), [Jesolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesolo), [Jesi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesi), [Ajaccio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajaccio), among others, and in *Mar Jonio*, an alternative spelling of *Mar Ionio* (the [Ionian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionian_Sea)). The letter ⟨j⟩ may appear in dialectal words, but its use is discouraged in contemporary standard Italian.[^cite_note-Clivio-111] Letters used in foreign words can be replaced with [phonetically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonetically) equivalent native Italian letters and [digraphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digraph_\(orthography\)): ⟨gi⟩, ⟨ge⟩, or ⟨i⟩ for ⟨j⟩; ⟨c⟩ or ⟨ch⟩ for ⟨k⟩ (including in the standard prefix *kilo-*); ⟨o⟩, ⟨u⟩ or ⟨v⟩ for ⟨w⟩; ⟨s⟩, ⟨ss⟩, ⟨z⟩, ⟨zz⟩ or ⟨cs⟩ for ⟨x⟩; and ⟨e⟩ or ⟨i⟩ for ⟨y⟩.

- The [acute accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_accent) is used over word-final ⟨e⟩ to indicate a stressed [front close-mid vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close-mid_front_unrounded_vowel), as in *perché* 'why, because'. In dictionaries, it is also used over ⟨o⟩ to indicate a stressed [back close-mid vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close-mid_back_rounded_vowel) (*azióne*). The [grave accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grave_accent) is used over word-final ⟨e⟩ and ⟨o⟩ to indicate a [front open-mid vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-mid_front_unrounded_vowel) and a [back open-mid vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-mid_back_rounded_vowel) respectively, as in *tè* 'tea', and *può* '(he) can'. The grave accent is used over any vowel to indicate word-final stress, as in *gioventù* 'youth'. Unlike ⟨é⟩, which is a *close*-mid vowel, a stressed final ⟨o⟩ is almost always a [back open-mid vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-mid_back_rounded_vowel) (*andrò*), with a few exceptions, such as *metró*, with a stressed final [back close-mid vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close-mid_back_rounded_vowel), making ⟨ó⟩ for the most part unnecessary outside of dictionaries. Most of the time, the penultimate syllable is stressed. But if the stressed vowel is the final letter of the word, the accent is mandatory, otherwise, it is virtually always omitted. Exceptions are typically either in dictionaries, where all or most stressed vowels are commonly marked. Accents can optionally be used to disambiguate words that differ only by stress, as for *prìncipi* 'princes' and *princìpi* 'principles', or *àncora* 'anchor' and *ancóra* 'stil&#x6C;*/*&#x79;et'. For monosyllabic words, the rule is different: when two orthographically identical monosyllabic words with different meanings exist, one is accented and the other is not (example: *è* 'is', *e* 'and').

- The letter ⟨h⟩ distinguishes *ho*, *hai*, *ha*, *hanno* (present indicative of *avere* 'to have') from *o* ('or'), *ai* ('to the'), *a* ('to'), *anno* ('year'). In the spoken language, the letter is always silent. The ⟨h⟩ in *ho* additionally marks the contrasting open pronunciation of the ⟨o⟩. The letter ⟨h⟩ is also used in combinations with other letters. No [phoneme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoneme) /h/ exists in Italian. In nativized foreign words, the ⟨h⟩ is silent. For example, *hotel* and *hovercraft* are pronounced /oˈtɛl/ and /ˈɔverkraft/ respectively. (Where ⟨h⟩ existed in Latin, it either disappeared or, in a few cases before a back vowel, changed to \[ɡ]: *traggo* 'I pull' ← Lat. trahō.)

- The letters ⟨s⟩ and ⟨z⟩ can symbolize [voiced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiced) or [voiceless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless) consonants. ⟨z⟩ symbolizes /dz/ or /ts/ depending on context, with few minimal pairs. For example: *zanzara* /dzanˈdzara/ 'mosquito' and *nazione* /natˈtsjone/ 'nation'. ⟨s⟩ symbolizes /s/ word-initially before a vowel, when clustered with a voiceless consonant (⟨p, f, c, ch⟩), and when doubled; it symbolizes /z/ when between vowels and when clustered with voiced consonants. Intervocalic ⟨s⟩ varies regionally between /s/ and /z/, with /z/ being more dominant in northern Italy and /s/ in the south.

- The letters ⟨c⟩ and ⟨g⟩ vary in pronunciation between [plosives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plosives) and [affricates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affricates) depending on following vowels. The letter ⟨c⟩ symbolizes /k/ when word-final and before the back vowels ⟨a, o, u⟩. It symbolizes /[tʃ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_postalveolar_affricate)/ as in *chair* before the front vowels ⟨e, i⟩. The letter ⟨g⟩ symbolizes /ɡ/ when word-final and before the back vowels ⟨a, o, u⟩. It symbolizes /[dʒ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiced_postalveolar_affricate)/ as in *gem* before the front vowels ⟨e, i⟩. Other Romance languages and, to an extent, English have similar variations for ⟨c, g⟩. Compare [hard and soft C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_and_soft_C), [hard and soft G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_and_soft_G). (See also [palatalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palatalization_\(sound_change\)).)

- The [digraphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digraph_\(orthography\)) ⟨ch⟩ and ⟨gh⟩ indicate (/k/ and /ɡ/) before ⟨i, e⟩. The digraphs ⟨ci⟩ and ⟨gi⟩ indicate 'softness' (/tʃ/ and /dʒ/, the [affricate consonants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affricate_consonant) of English *church* and *judge*) before ⟨a, o, u⟩. For example:

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBerloco2018-29]

Italian has geminate, or double, consonants, which are distinguished by [length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consonant_length) and intensity. Length is distinctive for all consonants except for /ʃ/, /dz/, /ts/, /ʎ/, /ɲ/, which are always geminate when between vowels, and /z/, which is always single. Geminate plosives and affricates are realized as lengthened closures. Geminate fricatives, nasals, and /l/ are realized as lengthened [continuants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuant). There is only one vibrant phoneme /r/ but the actual pronunciation depends on the context and regional accent. Generally one can find a flap consonant \[ɾ] in an unstressed position whereas \[r] is more common in stressed syllables, but there may be exceptions. Especially people from the northern part of Italy ([Parma](24231.md), Aosta Valley, [South Tyrol](169611.md)) may pronounce /r/ as \[ʀ], \[ʁ], or \[ʋ].[^cite_note-112]

Of special interest to the linguistic study of [Regional Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_Italian) is the *[gorgia toscana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gorgia_toscana)*, or "Tuscan throat", the weakening or [lenition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenition) of [intervocalic](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/intervocalic) /p/, /t/, and /k/ in the [Tuscan language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuscan_language).

The [voiced postalveolar fricative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiced_postalveolar_fricative) /ʒ/ is present as a phoneme only in loanwords: for example, *garage* \[ɡaˈraːʒ]. Phonetic \[ʒ] is common in central and southern Italy as an intervocalic allophone of /dʒ/: *gente* \[ˈdʒɛnte] 'people' but *la gente* \[laˈʒɛnte] 'the people', *ragione* \[raˈʒoːne] 'reason'.

## Grammar

Main article: [Italian grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_grammar)

See also: [Italian verbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_verbs)

Italian [grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammar) is typical of the grammar of [Romance languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_languages) in general. [Cases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_case) exist for personal pronouns ([nominative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nominative), [oblique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblique_case), [accusative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accusative), [dative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dative)), but not for nouns.

There are two basic classes of nouns in Italian, referred to as [genders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_gender), masculine and feminine. Gender may be [natural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gender) (*ragazzo* 'boy', *ragazza* 'girl') or simply grammatical with no possible reference to biological gender (masculine *costo* 'cost', feminine *costa* 'coast'). Masculine nouns typically end in *-o* (*ragazzo* 'boy'), with plural marked by *-i* (*ragazzi* 'boys'), and feminine nouns typically end in *-a*, with plural marked by *-e* (*ragazza* 'girl', *ragazze* 'girls'). For a group composed of boys and girls, *ragazzi* is the plural, suggesting that *-i* is a general neutral plural. A third category of nouns is [unmarked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marker_\(linguistics\)) for gender, ending in *-e* in the singular and *-i* in the plural: *legge* 'law, f. sg.', *leggi* 'laws, f. pl.'; *fiume* 'river, m. sg.', *fiumi* 'rivers, m. pl.', thus assignment of gender is arbitrary in terms of form, enough so that terms may be identical but of distinct genders: *fine* meaning 'aim', 'purpose' is masculine, while *fine* meaning 'end, ending' (e.g. of a movie) is feminine, and both are *fini* in the plural, a clear instance of *-i* as a non-gendered default plural marker. These nouns often, but not always, denote [inanimates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animacy). There are a number of nouns that have a masculine singular and a feminine plural, most commonly of the pattern m. sg. *-o*, f. pl. *-a* (*miglio* 'mile, m. sg.', *miglia* 'miles, f. pl.'; *paio* 'pair, m. sg.', *paia* 'pairs, f. pl.'), and thus are sometimes considered neuter (these are usually derived from [neuter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_gender) Latin nouns). An instance of neuter gender also exists in pronouns of the third person singular.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESimone2010-113]

Examples:[^cite_note-\:6-114]

| Definition | Gender | Singular Form | Plural Form |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Son | Masculine | Figlio | Figli |
| House | Feminine | Casa | Case |
| Love | Masculine | Amore | Amori |
| Art | Feminine | Arte | Arti |

Nouns, adjectives, and articles [inflect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflect) for gender and number (singular and plural).

Like in English, common nouns are capitalized when occurring at the beginning of a sentence. Unlike English, nouns referring to languages (e.g. Italian) and adjectives pertaining to ethnicity are never capitalized, while speakers of languages, or inhabitants of an area (e.g. Italians) used to always be capitalized, but, starting from the 19th century, this convention has been subject to various changes.[^cite_note-115][^cite_note-\:5-116]

There are three types of [adjectives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjective): descriptive, invariable and form-changing. Descriptive adjectives are the most common, and their endings change to match the number and gender of the noun they modify. Invariable adjectives are adjectives whose endings do not change. The form-changing adjectives *buono* 'good', *bello* 'beautiful', *grande* 'big', and *santo* 'saint/holy' change in form when placed before different types of nouns. Italian has three degrees for comparison of adjectives: positive, comparative, and superlative.[^cite_note-\:5-116]

The order of words in the phrase is relatively free compared to most European languages.[^cite_note-Clivio-111] The position of the verb in the phrase is highly mobile. Word order often has a lesser grammatical function in Italian than in English. Adjectives are sometimes placed before their noun and sometimes after. Subject nouns generally come before the verb. Italian is a [null-subject language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null-subject_language), so nominative pronouns are usually absent, with subject indicated by verbal [inflections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflection) (e.g. *amo* 'I love', *ama* '(s)he loves', *amano* 'they love'). Noun objects normally come after the verb, as do pronoun objects after imperative verbs, infinitives and gerunds, but otherwise, pronoun objects come before the verb.

There are both indefinite and definite [articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_\(grammar\)) in Italian. There are four indefinite articles, selected by the gender of the noun they modify and by the phonological structure of the word that immediately follows the article. *Uno* is masculine singular, used before *z* (/ts/ or /dz/), *s+consonant*, *gn* (/ɲ/), *pn* or *ps*, while masculine singular *un* is used before a word beginning with any other sound. The noun *zio* 'uncle' selects masculine singular, thus *uno zio* 'an uncle' or *uno zio anziano* 'an old uncle,' but *un mio zio* 'an uncle of mine'. The feminine singular indefinite articles are *una*, used before any consonant sound, and its abbreviated form, written *un',* used before vowels: *una camicia* 'a shirt', *una camicia bianca* 'a white shirt', *un'altra camicia* 'a different shirt'. There are seven forms for definite articles, both singular and plural. In the singular: *lo*, which corresponds to the uses of *uno*; *il*, which corresponds to the uses with the consonant of *un*; *la,* which corresponds to the uses of *una*; *l',* used for both masculine and feminine singular before vowels. In the plural: *gli* is the masculine plural of *lo and l'*; *i* is the plural of *il*; and *le* is the plural of feminine *la* and *l*'.[^cite_note-\:5-116]

There are numerous [contractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contraction_\(grammar\)) of [prepositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preposition) with subsequent [articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_\(grammar\)). There are numerous productive [suffixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffix) for [diminutive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_diminutive), [augmentative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augmentative#Italian), pejorative, attenuating, etc., which are also used to create [neologisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neologism).

There are 27 pronouns, grouped in [clitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clitic) and tonic pronouns. Personal pronouns are separated into three groups: subject, object (which takes the place of both direct and indirect objects), and reflexive. Second-person subject pronouns have both a polite and a familiar form. These two different types of addresses are very important in Italian social distinctions. All object pronouns have two forms: stressed and unstressed (clitics). Unstressed object pronouns are much more frequently used, and come before a verb conjugated for subject-verb (*la vedi*: 'you see her'), after (in writing, attached to) non-conjugated verbs (*vedendola*: 'seeing her'). Stressed object pronouns come after the verb, and are used when the emphasis is required, for contrast, or to avoid ambiguity (*vedo lui, ma non lei*: 'I see him, but not her'). Aside from personal pronouns, Italian also has demonstrative, interrogative, possessive, and relative pronouns. There are two types of demonstrative pronouns: relatively near (this) and relatively far (that); there exists a third type of demonstrative denoting vicinity only to the listener, but it has fallen out of use. Demonstratives in Italian are repeated before each noun, unlike in English.[^cite_note-\:5-116]

There are three regular sets of verbal [conjugations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_conjugation), and various verbs are irregularly conjugated. Within each of these sets of conjugations, there are four simple (one-word) verbal conjugations by person/number in the [indicative mood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indicative_mood) ([present tense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Present_tense); [past tense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Past_tense) with [imperfective aspect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperfective_aspect), past tense with [perfective aspect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfective_aspect), and [future tense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_tense)), two simple conjugations in the [subjunctive mood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subjunctive_mood) (present tense and past tense), one simple conjugation in the [conditional mood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_mood), and one simple conjugation in the [imperative mood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperative_mood). Corresponding to each of the simple conjugations, there is a compound conjugation involving a simple conjugation of "to be" or "to have" followed by a [past participle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Past_participle). "To have" is used to form compound conjugation when the verb is transitive (*ha detto, ha fatto*: 'he/she has said, he/she has made/done'), while "to be" is used in the case of verbs of motion and some other intransitive verbs (*è andato, è stato*: 'he has gone, he has been'). "To be" may be used with transitive verbs, but in such a case it makes the verb passive (*è detto, è fatto*: 'it is said, it is made/done'). This rule is not absolute, and some exceptions do exist.
