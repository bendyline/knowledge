# Tourism in the United States



[Walt Disney World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Disney_World)'s [Magic Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_Kingdom) in [Bay Lake, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_Lake,_Florida) is the most visited theme park in the world. In 2016, [Orlando, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlando,_Florida) was the most visited destination in the United States, and continues to be one of the most visited destinations in the world.



The [Grand Canyon](46989.md) of [Arizona](21883824.md) attracts approximately 4.41 million visitors annually.

In the United States, [tourism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism) is a large industry that serves millions of international and domestic tourists yearly. Foreigners visit the U.S. to see natural wonders, cities, historic landmarks, and entertainment venues. Americans seek similar attractions, as well as recreation and vacation areas.

Tourism in the United States grew rapidly in the form of [urban tourism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_tourism) during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. By the 1850s, tourism in the United States was well established both as a cultural activity and as an industry. [New York City](645042.md), [Los Angeles](18110.md), [Chicago](6886.md), [Boston](24437894.md), [Philadelphia](50585.md), [Washington, D.C.](108956.md), and [San Francisco](49728.md), all major U.S. cities, have attracted numerous tourists since the 1890s. By 1915, city touring had marked significant shifts in the way Americans perceived, organized, and moved.

During the early 20th century, many more people started to travel, partly because of the spread of the automobile. Similarly [air travel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_travel) revolutionized travel during 1945–1969, contributing greatly to tourism in the United States. Purchases of travel and tourism-related goods and services by international visitors traveling in the United States totaled \$10.9 billion during February 2013.[^cite_note-1]

According to the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, travel and tourism generated \$840 billion in value added in 2023, equal to 3.03 percent of U.S. gross domestic product. Total tourism-related employment was 10.0 million jobs, consisting of 6.5 million direct tourism jobs and 3.6 million indirect tourism jobs.[^cite_note-2] As of 2007, there are 2,462 registered [National Historic Landmarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Historic_Landmark) (NHL) recognized by the [United States government](195149.md). As of 2023, New York City is the most visited destination in the United States, followed by [Miami](53846.md), [Los Angeles](18110.md), [Orlando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlando,_Florida), and [San Francisco](49728.md).[^cite_note-3]

Tourists spend more money in the United States than in any other country, but the United States attracts only the third-highest number of tourists, after [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_France) and [Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_Spain).[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-c18f-5] The discrepancy may be explained by longer stays in the US.[^cite_note-c18f-5]

## History

### 19th century

The rise of urban tourism in the United States during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries represented a major cultural transformation concerning urban space and leisure natural activity and as an industry. Package tours did not exist until the 1870s and 1880s, when entrepreneurs of various sorts from hotel keepers and agents for railroad lines to artists and writers recognized the profit to be gained from the prospering tourism industry.[^cite_note-1850s-6] The rise of [locomotive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locomotive) steam-powered trains during the 1800s enabled tourists to travel more easily and quickly.[^cite_note-7]

In the United States, 2,800 miles (4,500 km) of rail track had been completed by 1840, by 1860 all major eastern US cities were linked by rail, and by 1869 the first trans-American railroad link was completed.[^cite_note-8] This made it possible for those living in the East and mid-West to visit the West Coast.[^cite_note-9] [Yosemite Park](48664.md) was promoted as a tourist attraction in the late 1850s and early 1860s for an audience who wanted a national icon and place to symbolize exotic wonder of its region.[^cite_note-1850s-6] Photography played an important role for the first time in the development of tourist attractions, making it possible to distribute hundreds of images showing various places of interest.[^cite_note-1850s-6]

[New York City](645042.md), [Chicago](6886.md), [Boston](24437894.md), [Philadelphia](50585.md), [Washington, D.C.](108956.md), and [San Francisco](49728.md) were all attracting numerous tourists by the 1890s.[^cite_note-early_urban-10] New York's population grew from 300,000 in 1840 to 800,000 in 1850.[^cite_note-11] Chicago experienced a dramatic increase from 4,000 residents in 1840 to 300,000 by 1870.

There was not much urban tourism during the 19th century, perhaps because American cities lacked the [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture) and [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art) which attracted thousands to Europe. American cities tended to offend the sensitive with ugliness and [commercialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercialism) rather than inspire awe or aesthetic pleasure.[^cite_note-12] Some tourists were fascinated by the rapid growth of the new urban areas: "It is an absorbing thing to watch the process of world-making; both the formation of the natural and the conventional world," wrote English writer Harriet Martineau in 1837.[^cite_note-nineteenth_century_urban-13]



The [Perkins School for the Blind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perkins_School_for_the_Blind) in [Watertown, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watertown,_Massachusetts) was one of several institutions classed as tourist attractions in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

As American cities developed, new institutions to accommodate and care for the insane, disabled and criminal were constructed. The [Hartford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartford,_Connecticut), Connecticut [American School for the Deaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_School_for_the_Deaf) opened in 1817, [Ossining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ossining_\(village\),_New_York), [New York](8210131.md) state prison (now known as [Sing Sing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sing_Sing)) in 1825, the Connecticut State Penitentiary at [Wethersfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wethersfield,_Connecticut) in 1827, [Mount Auburn Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Auburn_Cemetery) in 1831, the [Perkins School for the Blind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perkins_School_for_the_Blind) in 1832, and the [Worcester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worcester,_Massachusetts) State Hospital in 1833.[^cite_note-14] These institutions attracted the curiosity of American and foreign visitors. The English writer and actress [Fanny Kemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fanny_Kemble), an admirer of the American [prison](19008450.md) system, was also concerned that nature was being destroyed in favor of new developments. Guidebooks published in the 1830s, 40s and 50s described new prisons, asylums and institutions for the deaf and blind, and urged tourists to visit these sights.[^cite_note-guidebooks-accounts-15]

Accounts of these visits written by [Charles Dickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens), [Harriet Martineau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harriet_Martineau), [Lydia Sigourney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lydia_Sigourney) and [Caroline Gilman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caroline_Gilman) were published in magazines and [travel books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel_literature).[^cite_note-guidebooks-accounts-15] Sigourney's *Scenes in My Native Land* (1845) included descriptions of her tour of [Niagara Falls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niagara_Falls) and other places of scenic interest with accounts of her visits to prisons and asylums.[^cite_note-guidebooks-accounts-15] Many visited these institutions because nothing like them had existed before.[^cite_note-institutions-16] The buildings which housed them were themselves monumental, often placed on hilltops as a symbol of accomplishment.[^cite_note-institutions-16]

### Early tourism



The Art Deco district of [South Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Beach) in [Miami](53846.md) was developed during the 1930s.

By 1915, city touring had marked significant shifts in the way Americans perceived, organized and moved around in urban environments.[^cite_note-early_urban-10] Urban tourism became a profitable industry in 1915 as the number of tour agencies, railroad passenger departments, guidebook publishers and travel writers grew at a fast pace.[^cite_note-early_urban-10] The expense of pleasure tours between 1850 and 1915 meant that only a minority of Americans could experience the luxury of tourism.[^cite_note-early_urban-10] Many Americans traveled to find work, but few found time for enjoyment of the urban environment. As transportation networks improved, the length of commuting decreased, and income rose.[^cite_note-early_urban-10] A growing number of Americans were able to afford short vacations by 1915. Still, mass tourism was not possible until after [World War II](32927.md).[^cite_note-early_urban-10]

During the 19th century, tourism of any form had been available only to the upper and middle classes. This changed during the early 20th century. In 1895, popular publications printed articles showing the car was cheaper to operate than the horse.[^cite_note-17] The development of automobiles in the early 1900s included the introduction of the [Ford Model T](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Model_T) in 1908. In 1900, 8,000 cars were registered in the US, but this had increased to 619,000 by 1911.[^cite_note-automobilestats-18] By the time of the Model T's introduction in 1908, there were 44 US households per car.[^cite_note-automobilestats-18] Early cars were a luxury for the wealthy, but after Ford began to dramatically drop prices after 1913, more were able to afford one.[^cite_note-19]

The development of hotels with leisure complexes had become popular in the U.S. during the 1930s.[^cite_note-hotels-20] The range of club type holidays available appealed to a broad segment of the holiday market.[^cite_note-hotels-20] As more families traveled independently by car, hotels failed to cater to their needs.[^cite_note-hotels-20] [Kemmons Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kemmons_Wilson) opened the first [motel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motel) as a new form of accommodation in [Memphis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memphis,_Tennessee), Tennessee in 1952.[^cite_note-hotels-20]

Although thousands of tourists visited [Florida](18933066.md) during the early 1900s, it was not until after [World War II](32927.md) that the tourist industry quickly became Florida's largest source of income.[^cite_note-21] Florida's white sandy beaches, warm winter temperatures and wide range of activities such as swimming, fishing, boating and hiking all attracted tourists to the state. During the 1930s, architects designed [Art Deco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Deco) style buildings in [Miami Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Beach).[^cite_note-art_deco-22] Visitors are still attracted to the Art Deco district of Miami.[^cite_note-art_deco-22] Theme parks were soon built across Florida. One of the largest resorts in the world, [Walt Disney World Resort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Disney_World_Resort), was opened near Orlando, Florida in 1971.[^cite_note-florida-23] In its first year, the 28,000-acre (110 km<sup>2</sup>) park added \$14 billion to Orlando's economy.

### Late 20th century



The [Douglas DC-4](518813.md) was one of the first airliners used for U.S. commercial flights.

The revolution of [air travel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_travel) between 1945 and 1969 contributed greatly to tourism in the United States. In that quarter century, [commercial aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_aviation) evolved from 28-passenger airliners flying at less than 200 mph (320 km/h) to 150-passenger jetliners cruising continents at 600 mph (970 km/h).[^cite_note-airliner-24] During this time, air travel in the U.S. evolved from a novelty into a routine for business travelers and vacationers alike. Rapid developments in aviation technology, economic prosperity in the United States and the demand for air travel all contributed to the early beginnings of commercial aviation in the US.[^cite_note-airliner-24]

During the first four decades of the 20th century, long-haul journeys between large American cities were accomplished using trains. By the 1950s, air travel was part of everyday life for many Americans.[^cite_note-airliner-24] This was also helped by the establishment of the [Interstate Highway System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_Highway_System) as well as the [reliance of automobiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1950s_American_automobile_culture) of which Americans saw cars as their new personal found freedom and enjoyment. The tourism industry in the U.S. experienced [exponential growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_growth) as tourists could travel almost anywhere with a fast, reliable and routine system.[^cite_note-airliner-24] For some, a vacation in Hawaii was now a more frequent pleasure. Air travel changed everything from family vacations to [Major League Baseball](38776.md), as had steam-powered trains in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.[^cite_note-airliner-24]

By the end of the twentieth century, tourism had significantly grown throughout the world. The World Tourism Organisation (WTO, 1998) recorded that, in 1950, arrivals of tourists from abroad, excluding same-day visits, numbered about 25.2 million.[^cite_note-wtostats-25] By 1997, the figure was 612.8 million. In 1950 receipts from international movements were [US\$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Dollar) 2.1 billion, in 1997 they were \$443.7 billion.[^cite_note-wtostats-25]

It was also during the late 20th century that individual states began to adopt [their own tourism slogans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._state_tourism_slogans), with the aim to increase both domestic and international visitors. Some of these slogans have become iconic to the state itself, such as Michigan's "[Pure Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_Michigan)" campaign, New York's "[I Love New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Love_New_York)" merchandise, and Virginia's "[Virginia is for Lovers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_is_for_Lovers)" slogan. Other states adopted slogans based on civilian submissions, such as Pennsylvania holding the "Penn a Phrase" contest, in which the slogan "[Pursue Your Happiness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life,_Liberty_and_the_pursuit_of_Happiness)" was ultimately selected. Some slogans became the targets of campaigns to rename them launched by state residents, such as a failed 2013 campaign to change Kentucky's slogan from "Unbridled Spirit" to "Kentucky Kicks Ass".[^cite_note-26][^cite_note-27]

### 21st century

The travel and tourism industry in the U.S. was among the first commercial casualties of the [September 11 attacks](5058690.md), a series of terrorist attacks on the U.S. Terrorists used four commercial airliners as weapons of destruction, all of which were destroyed in the attacks on New York City, Washington, D.C., and in Pennsylvania with nearly 3,000 deaths.[^cite_note-Sept11-28] In the first full week after flights resumed, passenger numbers fell by nearly 45 percent, from 9 million in the week before September 11 to 5 million.[^cite_note-Sept11-28] Hotels and travel agencies received cancellations across the world. The hotel industry suffered an estimated \$700 million loss in revenue during the four days following the attacks.[^cite_note-Sept11-28] The situation recovered over the following months as the [Federal Reserve](10819.md) kept the financial system afloat. The [U.S. Congress](31756.md) issued a \$5 billion grant to the nation's airlines and \$10 billion in loan guarantees to keep them flying.[^cite_note-Sept11-28]

In the U.S., tourism is either the first, second, or third largest employer in 29 states, employing 7.3 million in 2004, to take care of 1.19 billion trips tourists took in the U.S. in 2005.[^cite_note-29] The U.S. outbound holiday market is sensitive in the short term, but possibly one of the most surprising results from the September 11, 2001 attacks was that by February 2002 it had bounced back. This quick revival was generally quicker than many commentators had predicted only five months earlier.[^cite_note-30]

The [United States economy](32022.md) began to slow significantly in 2007, mostly because of a [real-estate slump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subprime_mortgage_crisis), [gas prices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_of_petroleum) and related financial problems.[^cite_note-US_Economy-31] Many economists believe that the economy entered a recession at the end of 2007 or early in 2008.[^cite_note-US_Economy-31] Some state budgets for tourism marketing have decreased, such as [Connecticut](6466.md) which is facing soaring gas prices.[^cite_note-32] 100 million tourists visited Florida in 2015, a record for the nation.[^cite_note-33]

In August 2019, after various mass shootings in the U.S., [Amnesty International](18947898.md) and some countries issued a travel warning.[^cite_note-Travel_warning-34][^cite_note-Growing_list_of_countries_with_travel_warnings-35]

Various actions stemming from the [Trump administration](78298108.md), particularly [tariffs and immigration policies and practices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_policy_of_the_second_Trump_administration), affected U.S. tourism, projected, in August 2025, to decline by \$12.5 billion during 2025, a 22.5 percent decrease, according to the [World Travel and Tourism Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Travel_and_Tourism_Council)[^cite_note-36] and Oxford Economics,[^cite_note-\:0-37] overriding earlier projections, in April, of a 9.4 percent decline year-over-year in the number of travelers arriving in the U.S. from abroad.[^cite_note-\:1-38] The U.S. Travel Association cautioned, in February, that Florida, California, Nevada, New York and Texas would face the steepest declines, particularly due to the sharp decrease in Canadian visitors.[^cite_note-\:0-37] An 8.8 percent growth rate had been predicted in December, with the largest decrease coming from Canadian travelers at a projected 20.2 percent.[^cite_note-\:1-38] Data from the U.S. government recorded in March 2025 showed sharp drops in visitors from the following markets compared to the same time last year: Germany (down 28%), Spain (25%), the United Kingdom (18%), Canada (17%), South Korea (15%), and Australia (7%), reflecting a total drop of inbound tourism by 11.6%.[^cite_note-39] In May, the U.S. was the singular country expecting a decline in its international visitor spending in 2025.[^cite_note-40]

2026 did see a small boost in tourism as a result of the United States co-hosted the [2026 FIFA World Cup](36581929.md) alongside [Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_Canada) and [Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_Mexico), with 11 of the 16 host cities located in the U.S. (including [New York City](645042.md), [Los Angeles](18110.md), [Dallas](53838.md), and [Miami](53846.md)). The tournament generated significant surges in international travel, hotel occupancy, and sports tourism spending across host metropolitan areas. In the New York and New Jersey region alone where eight matches were held, the event generated \$3.5 billion in total economic impact driven by \$1.9 billion in direct spending from over 1.2 million stadium and fan-event visitors.[^cite_note-41] Unfortunately, the small boost of the World Cup did not increase tourism to its late 2010s level, and tourism in the United States continued to decline.[^cite_note-42]
