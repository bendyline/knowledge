**Quetta**[^cite_note-4] is the capital and largest city of the [Pakistani province](446017.md) of [Balochistan](4687.md). It is the ninth [largest city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_in_Pakistan_by_population) in Pakistan with an estimated population of over 1.7 million in 2024.[^cite_note-5] It is situated in the south-west of the country, lying in the [Quetta Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quetta_Valley) region surrounded by mountains on all sides. Quetta is at an average elevation of 1,680 metres (5,510 feet) above sea level,[^cite_note-6] making it Pakistan's highest-altitude major city.&#x20;

The city is sometimes referred to as the "Fruit Garden of Pakistan" because of its numerous fruit orchards and the variety of fresh and dried fruits produced in the region.[^cite_note-Quetta_fruit_garden_of_Pakistan-7] Like [Karachi](17123.md), Quetta is considered a "[melting pot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melting_pot)" as it is one of the most ethnically diverse cities in Pakistan.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9][^cite_note-iw-10]

Located in northern [Balochistan](8431743.md) near the [Pakistan-Afghanistan border](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durand_line) and the road across to [Kandahar](17260.md), Quetta is a trade and communication centre between the two countries.[^cite_note-11] The city is near the [Bolan Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolan_Pass), which was on a major gateway from [Central Asia](6742.md) to [South Asia](21566765.md).

## Etymology

The name *Quetta* is a variation of the [Pashto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashto_language) word *Kwatkōṭ*, or *kōta* meaning "fortress".[^cite_note-12] Quetta was formerly known as Shalkot ([Pashto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashto_language): ښالکوټ, [Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu_language): شالکوٹ).[^cite_note-13]

## History

### Early history and etymology

The name Quetta is derived from the Pashto word کوټه (*kwatta*), meaning "fort", a reference to the naturally defensible character of the valley and to the historic mud-walled citadel known as the Miri Fort, which dominated the early settlement.[^cite_note-Gazetteer1907p32-14]

The earliest recorded historical reference to the region dates to the 11th century, when the Quetta valley was captured by [Mahmud of Ghazni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahmud_of_Ghazni) during his campaigns in eastern Afghanistan and the north-western Indian subcontinent.[^cite_note-Burki470-15]

In 1543, the Mughal emperor [Humayun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humayun) passed through the Quetta region while fleeing to [Safavid Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Iran) following his defeat by [Sher Shah Suri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sher_Shah_Suri).[^cite_note-Burki470-15]

### Pashtun tribal settlement and landholding

Historically, the Quetta valley traditionally known as Shal formed part of the ancestral territory of Pashtun tribes, most prominently the [Kasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kasi) (or Kansi), a Sarbani Pashtun group established as the principal cultivators and proprietary landholders of the valley.[^cite_note-Ahmad-16] It formed part of the broader [Pashtun cultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashtun_culture) and tribal sphere linking southern Afghanistan with northern Balochistan prior to its expansion under British colonial rule.[^cite_note-17]

According to the *Gazetteer of the Quetta–Pishin District*, the Kasi were recognised as the original owners of the Shal valley, holding agricultural land, grazing grounds, and irrigation systems (*karez*) under customary tribal tenure regulated by local practice and Islamic law.[^cite_note-Gazetteer1907p73-18]

A formal sanad issued by [Nasir Khan I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasir_Khan_I?action=edit\&redlink=1) of Kalat in the mid-18th century confirmed the proprietary rights of the Kasi chiefs (*arbabs*) over the Shal valley, defining its boundaries and affirming their control over land and water resources. Certain Kasi families held hereditary and revenue-free grants, which continued to be recognised under later administrations.[^cite_note-Gazetteer1907p99-19]

By the early modern period, other Pashtun tribes—including the Achakzai, Kakar and Tareen—occupied surrounding uplands and valleys, while the Shal valley itself remained primarily under Kasi ownership and cultivation.[^cite_note-Gazetteer1907p73-18]

### Afghan and Durrani period

In 1709, the Quetta region came under the authority of the Afghan [Hotak dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotak_dynasty). Following the establishment of the [Durrani Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durrani_Empire) in 1747, [Ahmad Shah Durrani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmad_Shah_Durrani) incorporated Quetta into the Afghan state. Administrative authority and revenue rights were later granted to [Nasir Khan I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasir_Khan_I?action=edit\&redlink=1) of Kalat in recognition of Baloch military assistance during Durrani campaigns; however, indigenous Pashtun landholding structures—particularly those of the Kasi—remained intact.[^cite_note-Caroe256-20]

Large tracts of land later classified under British administration as **Timur Shahi** lands were originally acquired by Afghan rulers through confiscation following disputes over revenue assessments. These lands were reassessed during colonial settlement operations.[^cite_note-Gazetteer1907p278-21]

### British era

The first recorded European visitor to Quetta was the British traveller [Charles Masson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Masson), who described the settlement in 1828 as a "mud-walled fort surrounded by three hundred mud houses."[^cite_note-Masson330-22]

During the [Second Anglo-Afghan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Anglo-Afghan_War), British forces occupied Quetta, and in 1883 formal control was established through a lease arrangement with the Khan of Kalat. The British subsequently expanded Quetta as a cantonment and civil station, acquiring land largely through purchase for military and administrative purposes.[^cite_note-Gazetteer249-23]

Colonial settlement records indicate that indigenous Pashtun tribes—particularly the Kasi and Achakzai—remained the principal proprietors of agricultural land and irrigation systems in and around Quetta. Transfers of agricultural land occurred overwhelmingly between local cultivators, while sales to non-agriculturists were legally restricted and generally confined to small urban plots.[^cite_note-Gazetteer249-23]

Detailed records further note that Achakzai and Kasi families retained hereditary and, in some cases, revenue-free shares in karez lands, with tribal leaders formally recognised as head proprietors by the colonial administration.[^cite_note-Gazetteer1907p340-24]

### Modern period

Rapid urban growth in the late 19th and early 20th centuries led to a sharp rise in land values and increased litigation over immovable property, particularly within the town and cantonment areas. Contemporary records indicate that most such disputes involved recent settlers, traders, and government employees rather than indigenous landholding tribes.[^cite_note-Gazetteer241-25]

Quetta was almost entirely destroyed by the [1935 Quetta earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1935_Quetta_earthquake), resulting in widespread devastation and loss of life. The city was subsequently rebuilt under British administration. Following the creation of Pakistan in 1947, Quetta became the provincial capital of [Balochistan](8431743.md).[^cite_note-Burki470-15]

### Pashtun tribal rule and Durrani Empire

Historically, the Quetta valley (traditionally known as Shal) was the ancestral domain of the Kasi (or Kansi) tribe, a branch of the Sarbani Pashtun confederation.[^cite_note-Ahmad-16]

In 1709, the region became part of the Afghan [Hotak dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotak_dynasty). By 1747, [Ahmad Shah Durrani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmad_Shah_Durrani) incorporated it into the [Durrani Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durrani_Empire). Durrani later granted the administration and revenue of Quetta to Nasir Khan I, the Khan of Kalat, in recognition of Baloch military assistance during the Durrani campaigns in India, though the region maintained its predominantly Pashtun tribal character.[^cite_note-Caroe-26]

### British era and modern period

The first European to visit Quetta, British explorer [Charles Masson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Masson), described it in 1828 as a "mud-walled fort surrounded by three hundred mud houses."[^cite_note-Masson-27]

During the [Second Anglo-Afghan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Anglo-Afghan_War), the British occupied the city, formalising their control via a lease agreement with the Khan of Kalat in 1883. Despite the influx of colonial administrators, British census records from 1901 confirm that the Kasi Pashtuns remained the principal indigenous landholders of the district.[^cite_note-Gazetteer-28]

### British occupation and cantonment development



Map of Quetta before the [1935 Quetta earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1935_Quetta_earthquake)

Although Quetta had long existed as an indigenous Pashtun settlement in the Shal valley, its transformation into a modern urban centre occurred during the British period. Owing to its strategic position on the north-western frontier and its proximity to routes leading towards Afghanistan, British officials identified Quetta as a key military and administrative site in the mid-19th century.[^cite_note-29]

In 1856, General [John Jacob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Jacob_\(East_India_Company_officer\)) urged the British government to occupy Quetta for frontier defence purposes. During the [Second Anglo-Afghan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Anglo-Afghan_War), British forces took military control of the area, and in 1883 a formal lease arrangement was concluded with the [Khan of Kalat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanate_of_Kalat), bringing Quetta under effective British administration.[^cite_note-Gazetteer249-23]

Under British rule, Quetta was developed as a cantonment and civil station. Land required for military, railway, and administrative purposes was largely acquired through purchase from local proprietors rather than wholesale confiscation. Colonial settlement records indicate that indigenous Pashtun tribes—particularly the Kasi and Achakzai—retained ownership of most agricultural land and irrigation systems surrounding the town, while urban expansion was concentrated within the cantonment and civil areas.[^cite_note-Gazetteer249-23]

The establishment of British administration marked the beginning of sustained urban growth, with new infrastructure, roads, and public buildings constructed to serve both military and civilian needs. Quetta subsequently emerged as the principal administrative centre of northern Balochistan during the late colonial period.[^cite_note-Gazetteer241-25]

### 20th century and modern period

Quetta was almost completely destroyed by the [1935 Quetta earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1935_Quetta_earthquake), one of the deadliest earthquakes in South Asian history, which resulted in extensive loss of life and the destruction of much of the built environment. The city was subsequently rebuilt under British supervision with revised town planning and building regulations.[^cite_note-30]

Following the creation of Pakistan in 1947, Quetta became the provincial capital of [Balochistan](8431743.md), a role it continues to serve in the contemporary period.[^cite_note-Burki470-15]

## Climate

Main article: [Climate of Quetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_Quetta)

Quetta has a [cold semi-arid climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_semi-arid_climate) ([Köppen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) *BSk*) with a significant variation between summer and winter temperatures.Summer typically begins in late May and lasts until early September, with average temperatures ranging from 24 to 26 °C (75 to 79 °F). The highest temperature recorded in Quetta is 42 °C (108 °F), which occurred on 10 July 1998. [^cite_note-pakmet.com.pk-31] Autumn starts in mid-September and continues until mid-November with average temperatures in the 12–18 °C (54–64 °F) range. Winter starts in late November and ends in late February with average temperatures near 4–5 °C (39–41 °F). The lowest temperature in Quetta is −18.3 °C (−0.9 °F) which was recorded on 8 January 1970.[^cite_note-pakmet.com.pk-31] Spring starts in early March and ends in mid-May with average temperatures close to 15 °C (59 °F). Unlike more easterly parts of Pakistan, Quetta does not have a [monsoon](57630.md) season of heavy rainfall. Highest rainfall during 24 hours in Quetta is 113 millimetres (4.4 in) which was recorded on 17 December 2000,[^cite_note-pakmet.com.pk-31] Highest monthly rainfall of 232.4 millimetres (9.15 in) was recorded in March 1982 which was also the year of the highest annual rainfall, 949.8 millimetres (37.39 in).[^cite_note-pakmet.com.pk-31] In the winter, snowfall has become quite erratic (December, January and February).

The city saw a severe drought from 1999 to 2001 during which the city did not receive snowfall and below normal rains. In 2002, the city received snow after a gap of five years. In 2004 and 2005, the city received normal rains after three years without snowfall while in 2006, 2007 and 2009 the city received no snow. In 2008, it received a snowfall of 10 centimetres (4 in) in four hours on 29 January,[^cite_note-36] followed on 2 February by 25.4 centimetres (10 in) in 10 hours[^cite_note-37] – the city's heaviest snowfall in a decade. During the winter of 2010, it received no snow and saw below normal rains due to the presence of [El-Nino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Ni%C3%B1o-Southern_Oscillation) over Pakistan.

## Administration

At the local level, the city is governed by a municipal corporation consisting of 66 ward members which elects a mayor and a deputy mayor.[^cite_note-Government-1] In addition, [Quetta Development Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quetta_Development_Authority) is responsible for provision of municipal services for the city.[^cite_note-50]

## Transportation



Passengers boarding a Chaman bound train at Baleli, Quetta



[Quetta railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quetta_railway_station) was built during the [British Raj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Raj)

Quetta is on the western side of Pakistan and is connected to the rest of the country by a network of roads, railways and its international airport close to its centre.

At an altitude of 1,605 metres (5,266 feet) above sea level, [Quetta International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quetta_International_Airport) is the second-highest airport in Pakistan.[^cite_note-51] [Pakistan International Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_International_Airlines) has regular flights to and from the other major cities of Pakistan including [Islamabad](51153.md), [Gwadar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwadar), [Karachi](17123.md), [Lahore](125315.md) and [Peshawar](66230.md).

[Quetta Railway Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quetta_Railway_Station) is one of the highest railway stations in Pakistan at 1,676 metres (5,499 feet) above sea level. The railway track was laid in the 1890s during the British era to link Quetta with rest of the country. The extensive network of [Pakistan Railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_Railways) connects Quetta to Karachi in the south, by a 863 km (536 mi) track, [Lahore](125315.md) in the northeast (1,170 km or 727 miles) and [Peshawar](66230.md) further northeast (1,587 km or 986 miles). A metalled road runs alongside the railway that connects Quetta to Karachi via the nearby town of [Sibi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sibi) to [Jacobabad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobabad) and [Rohri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rohri) in the plain of the [River Indus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Indus).[^cite_note-52]

## Sports



The [Qayyum Papa Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qayyum_Papa_Stadium) is named after [Pakistan national football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_national_football_team) captain [Qayyum Changezi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qayyum_Changezi), hailing from Quetta

[Football](10568.md) is the most popular sport among the people of Quetta.[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54] The city has produced notable footballers for the [Pakistan national football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_national_football_team) including [Abdul Wahid Durrani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdul_Wahid_Durrani), [Qayyum Changezi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qayyum_Changezi), [Ayub Dar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayub_Dar), [Mohammad Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammad_Ali_\(footballer,_born_1989\)), and [Rajab Ali Hazara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajab_Ali_\(footballer\)).[^cite_note-55] Main football clubs from Quetta include [Baloch Quetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baloch_FC_Quetta), [Hazara Club Quetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazara_FC_Quetta). [Balochistan United WFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balochistan_United_W.F.C.) won the [2014 National Women Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_National_Women_Football_Championship). The major football ground is [Ayub National Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayub_National_Stadium), a multipurpose stadium also used for athletics. Other football grounds include [Qayyum Papa Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qayyum_Papa_Stadium) and [Sadiq Shaheed Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadiq_Shaheed_Stadium).

Quetta cricket stadium

[Bugti Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bugti_Stadium), home of [Quetta Gladiators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quetta_Gladiators)

[Bugti Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bugti_Stadium) is the home of [Balochistan cricket team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balochistan_cricket_team), a [first-class cricket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-class_cricket) team which competes in domestic tournaments,[^cite_note-Cricinfo1-56] and the Quetta-based team [Quetta Gladiators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quetta_Gladiators) compete in the [Pakistan Super League](38199013.md) (PSL). They were the champion of the [PSL 2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Pakistan_Super_League).

Boxing is highly popular as well.[^cite_note-57] [Muhammad Waseem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Waseem) is a professional boxer from Quetta. In Body Building Nisar Ahmed Khilji has Mr. Balochistan and Mr. Pakistan Titles and Pakistan representation in International Body Building Contests. In hockey, Quetta has produced [Zeeshan Ashraf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeeshan_Ashraf) and [Shakeel Abbasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakeel_Abbasi), who were members of the [Pakistan's national hockey team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_men's_national_field_hockey_team).
