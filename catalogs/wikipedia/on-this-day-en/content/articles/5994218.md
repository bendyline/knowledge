The [tort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tort) of **breach of confidence** is, in [United Kingdom law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_law) and [United States law](27552742.md), a [common-law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common-law) tort that protects private information conveyed [in confidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_confidence).[^cite_note-1] A claim for breach of confidence typically requires the information to be of a confidential nature, which was communicated in confidence and was disclosed to the detriment of the claimant.

Establishing a breach of confidentiality depends on proving the existence and breach of a duty of confidentiality. Courts in the United States look at the nature of the relationship between the parties. Most commonly, breach of [confidentiality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidentiality) applies to the [patient-physician relationship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patient-physician_relationship),[^cite_note-2] but it can also apply to relationships involving banks, hospitals, insurance companies, and many others.[^cite_note-3]

There was no clear tort of breach of confidence in other common-law jurisdictions such as the [United Kingdom](31717.md) or [Australia](4689264.md); however, there is an [equitable doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equity_\(law\)) of [breach of confidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breach_of_confidence_in_English_law). In *[OBG Ltd v Allan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OBG_Ltd_v_Allan)* [\[2007\] UKHL 21](https://www.bailii.org/uk/cases/UKHL/2007/21.html) the Law Lords clarified that [Economic torts in English law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_torts_in_English_law) do exist.
