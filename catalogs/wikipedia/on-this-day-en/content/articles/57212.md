The **TGV** (French: [\[teʒeve\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/French) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/3/30/LL-Q150\_%28fra%29-Poslovitch-TGV.wav/LL-Q150\_%28fra%29-Poslovitch-TGV.wav.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/3/30/LL-Q150_%28fra%29-Poslovitch-TGV.wav/LL-Q150_%28fra%29-Poslovitch-TGV.wav.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>; ***train à grande vitesse***, [\[tʁɛ̃ a ɡʁɑ̃d vitɛs\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/French) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/c/c8/LL-Q150\_%28fra%29-WikiLucas00-train\_%C3%A0_grande_vitesse.wav/LL-Q150\_%28fra%29-WikiLucas00-train\_%C3%A0_grande_vitesse.wav.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/c/c8/LL-Q150_%28fra%29-WikiLucas00-train_%C3%A0_grande_vitesse.wav/LL-Q150_%28fra%29-WikiLucas00-train_%C3%A0_grande_vitesse.wav.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>, 'high-speed train')[^cite_note-1] is the generic name of the trainsets used for the [intercity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inter-city_rail) [high-speed rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-speed_rail) service run by France's national rail operator, [SNCF Voyageurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF_Voyageurs). It is also a quasi-[generic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generic_trademark) initialism for high-speed trains in French, even when not operated by SNCF Voyageurs.[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5] With commercial operating speeds of up to 320 km/h (200 mph) on the newer lines,[^cite_note-6] the TGV was conceived at the same period as other technological projects such as the [Ariane 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_1) rocket and [Concorde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concorde) supersonic airliner; sponsored by the [Government of France](3398091.md), those funding programmes were known as *champion national* ('[national champion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_champions)') policies. In 2025, the TGV network in France carried 168 million passengers.[^cite_note-7]

The state-owned [SNCF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF) started working on a high-speed rail network in 1966. It presented the project to President [Georges Pompidou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Pompidou) in 1974 who approved it. Originally designed as [turbotrains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbotrain) to be [powered by gas turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_turbine_train), TGV prototypes evolved into electric trains with the [1973 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis). In 1976 the SNCF ordered 87 high-speed trains from [Alstom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alstom). Following the inaugural service between [Paris](22989.md) and [Lyon](8638634.md) in 1981 on the [LGV Sud-Est](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGV_Sud-Est), the network, centred on Paris, has expanded to connect major cities across France, including [Marseille](40888948.md), [Lille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lille), [Bordeaux](4097.md), [Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strasbourg), [Rennes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rennes) and [Montpellier](73369.md), as well as in neighbouring countries on a combination of high-speed and conventional lines. The success of the first high-speed service led to a rapid development of *lignes à grande vitesse* (LGVs, 'high-speed lines') to the south ([Rhône-Alpes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGV_Rh%C3%B4ne-Alpes), [Méditerranée](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGV_M%C3%A9diterran%C3%A9e), [Nîmes–Montpellier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contournement_N%C3%AEmes_%E2%80%93_Montpellier)), west ([Atlantique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGV_Atlantique), [Bretagne-Pays de la Loire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGV_Bretagne-Pays_de_la_Loire), [Sud Europe Atlantique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGV_Sud_Europe_Atlantique)), north ([Nord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGV_Nord), [Interconnexion Est](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGV_Interconnexion_Est)) and east ([Rhin-Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGV_Rhin-Rh%C3%B4ne), [Est](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGV_Est)). Since it was launched, the TGV has not recorded a single passenger fatality in an accident on normal, high-speed service.

A specially modified TGV high-speed train known as [Project V150](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_V150), weighing only 265 tonnes, set the world record for the fastest wheeled train, reaching 574.8 km/h (357.2 mph) during a test run on 3 April 2007.[^cite_note-8] In 2007, the world's fastest scheduled rail journey was a start-to-stop average speed of 279.4 km/h (173.6 mph) between the [Gare de Champagne-Ardenne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gare_de_Champagne-Ardenne_TGV) and [Gare de Lorraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gare_de_Lorraine_TGV) on the [LGV Est](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGV_Est),[^cite_note-worldspeedsurvey2007-9][^cite_note-worldspeedsurvey2007pdf-10] not surpassed until the 2013 reported average of 283.7 km/h (176.3 mph) express service on the [Shijiazhuang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shijiazhuang) to [Zhengzhou](288264.md) segment of China's [Shijiazhuang–Wuhan high-speed railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shijiazhuang%E2%80%93Wuhan_high-speed_railway).[^cite_note-11] During the engineering phase, the [transmission voie-machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_voie-machine) (TVM) cab-signalling technology was developed, as drivers would not be able to see signals along the track-side when trains reach full speed. It allows for a train engaging in an emergency braking to request within seconds all following trains to reduce their speed; if a driver does not react within 1.5 km (0.93 mi), the system overrides the controls and reduces the train's speed automatically. The TVM safety mechanism enables TGVs using the same line to depart every three minutes.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-modular-13]

The TGV system itself extends to neighbouring countries, either directly (Italy, Spain, Belgium, Luxembourg and Germany) or through TGV-derivative networks linking France to Switzerland ([Lyria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGV_Lyria)), to Belgium, Germany and the Netherlands (former [Thalys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thalys)), as well as to the United Kingdom ([Eurostar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostar)). Several future lines are under construction or planned, including extensions within France and to surrounding countries. The [Mont d'Ambin Base Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mont_d'Ambin_Base_Tunnel), part of the [LGV Lyon–Turin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turin%E2%80%93Lyon_high-speed_railway) which is currently under construction, is set to become the longest rail tunnel in the world. Cities such as [Tours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tours) and [Le Mans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Mans) have become part of a "TGV [commuter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commuting) belt" around Paris; the TGV also serves [Charles de Gaulle Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_de_Gaulle_Airport) and [Lyon–Saint-Exupéry Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyon%E2%80%93Saint-Exup%C3%A9ry_Airport). A visitor attraction in itself, it stops at [Marne-la-Vallée–Chessy station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marne-la-Vall%C3%A9e%E2%80%93Chessy_station) serving [Disneyland Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disneyland_Paris) and in southern tourist cities such as [Avignon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avignon) and [Aix-en-Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aix-en-Provence) as well. [Brest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brest,_France), [Chambéry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamb%C3%A9ry), [Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nice), [Toulouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toulouse) and [Biarritz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biarritz) are reachable by TGVs running on a mix of LGVs and modernised lines. In 2007, the SNCF generated profits of €1.1 billion (approximately US\$1.75 billion, £875 million) driven largely by higher margins on the TGV network.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15]

## History

The idea of the TGV was first proposed in 1967, after Japan had begun construction of the [Shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinkansen) in 1959. At the time the Government of France favoured new technology, exploring the production of [hovercraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hovercraft) and the [Aérotrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%C3%A9rotrain) air-cushion vehicle. Simultaneously, the SNCF began researching high-speed trains on conventional tracks. In 1976, the administration agreed to fund the first line. By the mid-1990s, the trains were so popular that SNCF president [Louis Gallois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Gallois) declared that the TGV was "the train that saved French railways".[^cite_note-16]

### Development

Main article: [Development of the TGV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Development_of_the_TGV)



Europe's high-speed rail system, including TGV lines in France



[TGV Sud-Est](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF_TGV_Sud-Est) (left), the first equipment used on the service; and [TGV 2N2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGV_2N2) (right), the newest equipment used on the service, at [Gare de Lyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gare_de_Lyon), 2019

It was originally planned that the TGV, then standing for *_très grande vitesse_* ('very high speed') or *_turbine grande vitesse_* ('high-speed turbine'), would be propelled by [gas turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_turbine-electric_locomotive), selected for their small size, good [power-to-weight ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power-to-weight_ratio) and ability to deliver high power over an extended period. The first prototype, [TGV 001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGV_001), was the only gas-turbine TGV: following the increase in the price of [oil](23195.md) during the [1973 energy crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis), gas turbines were deemed uneconomic and the project turned to electricity from [overhead lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhead_lines), generated by [new nuclear power stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_France).

TGV 001 was not a wasted prototype:[^cite_note-17] its gas turbine was only one of its many new technologies for high-speed rail travel. It also tested high-speed brakes, needed to dissipate the large amount of [kinetic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy) of a train at high speed, high-speed aerodynamics, and signalling. It was articulated, comprising two adjacent carriages sharing a [bogie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogie), allowing free yet controlled motion with respect to one another. It reached 318 km/h (198 mph), which remains the world speed record for a non-electric train. Its interior and exterior were styled by French designer Jacques Cooper, whose work formed the basis of early TGV designs, including the distinctive nose shape of the first power cars.

Changing the TGV to electric traction required a significant design overhaul. The first electric prototype, nicknamed Zébulon, was completed in 1974, testing features such as innovative body mounting of motors, [pantographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantograph_\(rail\)), [suspension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspension_\(vehicle\)) and [braking](65423.md). Body mounting of motors allowed over 3 tonnes to be eliminated from the power cars and greatly reduced the [unsprung weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unsprung_weight). The prototype travelled almost 1,000,000 km (621,371 mi) during testing.

In 1976, the French administration funded the TGV project, and construction of the [LGV Sud-Est](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGV_Sud-Est), the first high-speed line (French: *ligne à grande vitesse*), began shortly afterwards. The line was given the designation LN1, *_Ligne Nouvelle 1_* ('New Line 1'). After two pre-production trainsets (nicknamed *Patrick* and *Sophie*) had been tested and substantially modified, the first production version was delivered on 25 April 1980.

The TGV opened to the public between [Paris](22989.md) and [Lyon](8638634.md) on 27 September 1981. Contrary to its earlier fast services, SNCF intended TGV service for all types of passengers, with the same initial ticket price as trains on the parallel conventional line. To counteract the popular misconception that the TGV would be a premium service for business travellers, SNCF started a major publicity campaign focusing on the speed, frequency, reservation policy, normal price, and broad accessibility of the service.[^cite_note-onthefasttrack1-18] This commitment to a democratised TGV service was enhanced in the [Mitterrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Mitterrand) era with the promotional slogan "Progress means nothing unless it is shared by all".[^cite_note-onthefasttrack2-19] The TGV was considerably faster (in terms of door to door travel time) than normal trains, [cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automobile), or [aeroplanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeroplanes). The trains became widely popular, the public welcoming fast and practical travel.

The [Eurostar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostar) service began operation in 1994, connecting [continental Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Europe) to [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterloo_International_railway_station) via the [Channel Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_Tunnel) and the LGV Nord-Europe with a version of the TGV designed for use in the tunnel and the United Kingdom. The first phase of the British [High Speed 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Speed_1) line was completed in 2003, the second phase in November 2007. The fastest trains take 2 hours 15 minutes London–Paris and 1 hour 51 minutes London–Brussels. The first twice-daily London-Amsterdam service ran 3 April 2018, and took 3 hours 47 minutes.[^cite_note-20]

### Milestones



Record runs of the TGV

The TGV (1981) was the world's second commercial and the fastest [standard gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_gauge) high-speed train service,[^cite_note-21] after Japan's [Shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_Series_Shinkansen), which [connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%8Dkaid%C5%8D_Shinkansen) Tokyo and [Osaka](52230.md) from 1 October 1964. It was a commercial success.

A TGV test train holds the [world speed record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_speed_record_for_railed_vehicles) for conventional trains. On 3 April 2007 a [modified TGV POS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V150_\(train\)) train reached 574.8 km/h (357.2 mph) [under test conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGV_world_speed_record) on the [LGV Est](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGV_Est) between Paris and Strasbourg. The line voltage was boosted to 31 kV, and extra ballast was tamped onto the permanent way. The train beat the 1990 [world speed record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_speed_record_for_railed_vehicles) of 515.3 km/h (320.2 mph), set by a similar TGV, along with unofficial records set during weeks preceding the official record run. The test was part of an extensive research programme by Alstom.[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23]

In 2007, the TGV was the [world's fastest conventional scheduled train](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_speed_record_for_railed_vehicles): one journey's average start-to-stop speed from Champagne-Ardenne Station to Lorraine Station is 279.3 km/h (173.5 mph).[^cite_note-worldspeedsurvey2007-9][^cite_note-worldspeedsurvey2007pdf-10] This record was surpassed on 26 December 2009 by the new [Wuhan–Guangzhou high-speed railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wuhan%E2%80%93Guangzhou_high-speed_railway)[^cite_note-24] in [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-speed_rail_in_China) where the fastest scheduled train covered 922 km (573 mi) at an average speed of 312.54 km/h (194.20 mph).[^cite_note-25]

A [Eurostar (TGV) train](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Rail_Class_373) broke the record for the longest non-stop high-speed international journey on 17 May 2006 carrying the cast and filmmakers of *[The Da Vinci Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Da_Vinci_Code_\(film\))* from London to [Cannes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannes) for the [Cannes Film Festival](101460.md). The 1,421-kilometre (883 mi) journey took 7 hours 25 minutes on an average speed of 191.6 km/h (119.1 mph).[^cite_note-26]

The fastest single long-distance run on the TGV was done by a [TGV Réseau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF_TGV_R%C3%A9seau) train from Calais-Frethun to Marseille (1,067.2 km (663.1 mi)i) in 3 hours 29 minutes at a speed of 306 km/h (190 mph) for the inauguration of the [LGV Méditerranée](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGV_M%C3%A9diterran%C3%A9e) on 26 May 2001.[^cite_note-27]

## Rolling stock



Three TGV trains at [Gare de Lyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gare_de_Lyon) station in Paris, 1985

All TGV trains are [top and tailed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top_and_tail) with two locomotive-style [power cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_car), one at each end, while traction equipment is concentrated in the end cars rather than distributed through the passenger coaches as in many [electric multiple units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_multiple_unit). Between those power cars are a set of semi-permanently coupled [articulated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Articulated_vehicle) un-powered [coaches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coach_\(rail\)). Cars are connected with [Jacobs bogies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobs_bogie), a single [bogie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogie) shared between the ends of two coaches. The only exception are the end cars, which have a standalone bogie on the side closest to the power car, which is often motorized. Power cars also have two bogies. SNCF describes the articulated arrangement, in which two carriages share a bogie, as one of the TGV prototype programme's key innovations.[^cite_note-sncf-tgv-story-32]

Trains can be lengthened by coupling two TGVs, using couplers hidden in the noses of the power cars.

The articulated design is advantageous during a derailment, as the passenger carriages are more likely to stay upright and in line with the track. Normal trains could split at [couplings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_coupling) and [jackknife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackknifing), as seen in the [Eschede train disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eschede_train_disaster). A disadvantage is that it is difficult to split sets of carriages. While power cars can be removed from trains by standard uncoupling procedures, specialized equipment is needed to split carriages, by lifting up cars off a bogie. Once uncoupled, one of the carriage ends is left without support, so a specialized frame is required.

SNCF prefers to use power cars instead of [electric multiple units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_multiple_unit) because it allows for less electrical equipment.[^cite_note-33]

The principal SNCF TGV equipment families built by [Alstom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alstom) include:

- [TGV Atlantique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF_TGV_Atlantique) (10 carriages)

- [TGV Réseau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF_TGV_R%C3%A9seau) (an upgrade of the Atlantique, 8 carriages)

- [TGV Duplex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGV_Duplex) (two floors for greater passenger capacity)

- [TGV 2N2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euroduplex) (also known as the Avelia Euroduplex, an upgrade of the TGV Duplex)

- [TGV M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avelia_Horizon) (also known as the Avelia Horizon, authorised in 2026 with first passenger service planned for September 2026)

Retired sets:

- [TGV Sud-Est](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF_TGV_Sud-Est) (retired in December 2019)&#x20;

  - [TGV La Poste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF_TGV_La_Poste) (retired in June 2015)

- [TGV POS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF_TGV_POS) (retired in 2026, power cars swapped with TGV Réseau Duplex power cars)

Across successive generations, the fleet moved from single-deck sets built mainly to establish high-speed service to rolling stock also aimed at solving capacity, interoperability and operating-cost problems. Duplex stock addressed crowding without longer platforms by adding two passenger levels, a change contemporary reporting described as 45% more seats than a single-deck trainset of the same length; TGV M extends that approach with modular double-deck coaches, up to 740 seats in a 200-metre (660 ft) trainset, and reported reductions in energy use and maintenance costs.[^cite_note-duplex-capacity-34][^cite_note-alstom-tgvm-authorisation-35]

Several TGV types have broken records, including the [V150](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V150_\(train\)) and [TGV 001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGV_001). V150 was a specially modified five-car double-deck trainset that [reached 574.8 km/h (357.2 mph)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGV_world_speed_record#Record_of_2007) under controlled conditions on a test run. It narrowly missed beating the world train speed record of 581 km/h (361 mph).[^cite_note-36] The record-breaking speed is impractical for commercial trains due to motor overcharging, empty train weight, rail and engine wear issues, elimination of all but three coaches, excessive vibration, noise and lack of [emergency stopping methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_brake_\(train\)). TGVs travel at up to 320 km/h (199 mph) in commercial use.

All TGVs are at least *bi-current*, which means that they can operate at [25 kV 50 Hz AC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/25_kV_AC_railway_electrification) (used on LGVs) and [1,500 V DC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_railway_electrification_systems#1,500_V_DC) (used on traditional lines). Trains travelling internationally must accommodate other voltages ([15 kV 16.7 Hz AC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/15_kV_AC_railway_electrification) or [3,000 V DC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_railway_electrification_systems#3_kV_DC)), requiring *tri-current* and *quad-current* TGVs.

Each TGV power car has two pantographs: one for AC use and one for DC. When passing between areas with different electric systems (identified by marker boards), trains enter a phase break zone. Just before this section, train drivers must power down the motors (allowing the train to [coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gliding_\(vehicle\))), lower the pantograph, adjust a switch to select the appropriate system, and raise the pantograph. Once the train exits the phase break zone and detects the correct electric supply, a dashboard indicator illuminates, and the driver can once again engage the motors.

### TGV Sud-Est

Main articles: [SNCF TGV Sud-Est](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF_TGV_Sud-Est) and [SNCF TGV La Poste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF_TGV_La_Poste)



A [TGV Sud-Est](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGV_Sud-Est) set in the original orange livery.

The Sud-Est fleet was built between 1978 and 1988 and operated the first TGV service, from Paris to Lyon in 1981. There were 107 passenger sets, of which nine are tri-current (including [15 kV 16.7 Hz AC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/15_kV_AC_railway_electrification) for use in Switzerland) and the rest bi-current. There were seven bi-current half-sets without seats that carried mail for [La Poste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Poste_\(France\)) between Paris, Lyon and [Provence](48503.md), in a distinctive yellow livery until they were phased out in 2015.

Each set were made up of two power cars and eight carriages (capacity 345 seats), including a powered bogie in the carriages adjacent to the power cars. They are 200 m (656 ft 2 in) long and 2.81 m (9 ft 3 in) wide. They weighed 385 tonnes (849,000 lb) with a power output of 6,450 kW under 25 kV.

The sets were originally built to run at 270 km/h (168 mph) but most were upgraded to 300 km/h (186 mph) during mid-life refurbishment in preparation for the opening of the LGV Méditerranée. The few sets that kept a maximum speed of 270 km/h (168 mph) operated on routes that include a comparatively short distance on LGV, such as to Switzerland via Dijon; SNCF did not consider it financially worthwhile to upgrade their speed for a marginal reduction in journey time.

In December 2019, the trains were phased out from service. In late 2019 and early 2020, TGV 01 (Nicknamed Patrick), the very first TGV train, did a farewell service that included all three liveries that were worn during their service.[^cite_note-37]

### TGV Atlantique

Main article: [SNCF TGV Atlantique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF_TGV_Atlantique)



TGV Atlantique at La Rochelle station

The 105 train Atlantique fleet was built between 1988 and 1992 for the opening of the [LGV Atlantique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGV_Atlantique) and entry into service began in 1989. They are all bi-current, 237.5 m (779 ft 2 in) long and 2.9 m (9 ft 6 in) wide. They weigh 444 tonnes (979,000 lb) and are made up of two power cars and ten carriages with a capacity of 485 seats. They were built with a maximum speed of 300 km/h (186 mph) and 8,800 kW of power under 25 kV. The efficiency of the Atlantique with all seats filled has been calculated at 767 [PMPG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passenger_miles_per_gallon), though with a typical occupancy of 60% it is about 460 PMPG (a Toyota Prius with three passengers is 144 PMPG).[^cite_note-38]

Modified unit 325 [set the world speed record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGV_world_speed_record) in 1990 on the LGV Atlantique before its opening. Modifications such as improved [aerodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamics), larger wheels and improved braking were made to enable speeds of over 500 km/h (311 mph). The set was reduced to two power cars and three carriages to improve the power-to-weight ratio, weighing 250 tonnes. Three carriages, including the bar carriage in the centre, is the minimum possible configuration because of the [Jacobs bogies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobs_bogie).

### TGV Réseau

Main article: [SNCF TGV Réseau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF_TGV_R%C3%A9seau)



A TGV-Reseau Lacroix with number 549 at the Luxembourg station

The first Réseau (Network) sets entered service in 1993. Fifty bi-current sets were ordered in 1990, supplemented by 40 tri-current sets in 1992/1993 (adding [3,000 V DC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_railway_electrification_systems#3_kV_DC) system used on traditional lines in Belgium). Ten tri-current sets carry the [Eurostar Red](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostar) (ex-[Thalys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thalys)) livery and are known as the PBA (Paris-Brussels-Amsterdam) sets.

They are formed of two power cars (8,800 kW under 25 kV – as TGV Atlantique) and eight carriages, giving a capacity of 377 seats. They have a top speed of 320 km/h (199 mph). They are 200 m (656 ft 2 in) long and are 2.90 m (9 ft 6 in) wide. The bi-current sets weigh 383 tonnes: owing to axle-load restrictions in Belgium the tri-current sets have a series of modifications, such as the replacement of steel with aluminum and hollow axles, to reduce the weight to under 17 t per axle.

Owing to early complaints of uncomfortable pressure changes when entering tunnels at high speed on the LGV Atlantique, the Réseau sets are now pressure-sealed. They can be coupled to a Duplex set.

### TGV Duplex

Main article: [TGV Duplex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGV_Duplex)



The TGV Duplex power cars use a more streamlined nose than previous TGVs.

The Duplex was built to increase TGV capacity without increasing train length or the number of trains. A contemporary report described the design as offering 45% more seats than a single-deck trainset of the same length.[^cite_note-duplex-capacity-34] Each carriage has two levels, with access doors at the lower level taking advantage of low French [platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_platform). A staircase gives access to the upper level, where the gangway between carriages is located. There are 512 seats per set. On busy routes such as Paris-Marseille they are operated in pairs, providing 1,024 seats in two Duplex sets or 800 in a Duplex set plus a [Réseau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF_TGV_R%C3%A9seau) set. Each set has a wheelchair accessible compartment.

After a lengthy development process starting in 1988 (during which they were known as the TGV-2N) the original batch of 30 was built between 1995 and 1998. Further deliveries started in 2000 with the Duplex fleet now totaling 160 units, making it the backbone of the SNCF TGV-fleet. They weigh 380 tonnes and are 200 m (656 ft 2 in) long, made up of two power cars and eight carriages. Extensive use of aluminum means that they weigh not much more than the TGV Réseau sets they supplement. The bi-current power cars provide 8,800 kW, and they have a slightly increased speed of 320 km/h (199 mph).

Duplex TGVs run on all of French high-speed lines.[^cite_note-39]

### TGV POS

Main article: [TGV POS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGV_POS)



TGV POS have the newer power cars unlike a TGV Réseau.

TGV POS (Paris-Ostfrankreich-Süddeutschland or Paris-Eastern France-Southern Germany) were initially used on the LGV Est to Germany. In 2012, they have been transferred to Lyria services to Switzerland. Between 2019 and their retirement, they have operated on the LGV Nord.

They consist of two Duplex power cars with eight TGV Réseau-type carriages, with a power output of 9,600 kW and a top speed of 320 km/h (199 mph). Unlike TGV-A, TGV-R and TGV-D, they have [asynchronous motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induction_motor), and isolation of an individual motor is possible in case of failure.

Since 2022, the coaches have gradually been replaced with Duplex coaches, creating a new **TGV POS-Duplex** series.[^cite_note-40]

### Avelia Euroduplex (TGV 2N2)

Main article: [Euroduplex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euroduplex)



TGV 2N2 train in Paris Gare de Lyon station

The bi-current TGV 2N2 (Avelia Euroduplex) can be regarded as the third generation of Duplex. The series was commissioned from December 2011 for links to Germany and Switzerland (tri-current trains) and to cope with the increased traffic due to the opening of the [LGV Rhin-Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGV_Rhin-Rh%C3%B4ne).

They are numbered from 800 and are limited to 320 km/h (199 mph). [ERTMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Rail_Traffic_Management_System) makes them compatible to allow access to Spain similar to [Dasye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGV_Duplex#Dasye).

### TGV M Avelia Horizon

Main article: [Avelia Horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avelia_Horizon)



TGV M Avelia Horizon train passing through [Montmélian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montm%C3%A9lian).

The design that emerged from the process was named [TGV M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGV_M), and in July 2018 SNCF Mobilités ordered 100 trainsets with deliveries beginning in 2024.[^cite_note-41] In May 2026, the [European Union Agency for Railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union_Agency_for_Railways) granted marketing authorisation, with first passenger service planned for early September 2026 after pre-commercial running.[^cite_note-alstom-tgvm-authorisation-35] The type keeps the TGV's articulated double-deck, end-power-car architecture, but uses more compact power cars and modular passenger coaches. Alstom and SNCF Voyageurs list a maximum 740-seat configuration in a 200-metre (660 ft) trainset, 20% energy savings, 30% lower maintenance costs, 20% more luggage space and 97% recyclable materials.[^cite_note-alstom-tgvm-authorisation-35][^cite_note-alstom-avelia-horizon-42]

## Lines in operation

Main article: [High-speed rail in France § Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-speed_rail_in_France#Network)

In June 2021, there were approximately 2,800 km (1,740 mi) of *_[Lignes à Grande Vitesse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-speed_rail)_* (LGV), with four additional line sections under construction. The current lines and those under construction can be grouped into four routes radiating from Paris.

## Accidents

Main article: [TGV accidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGV_accidents)

In over four decades of operation, the TGV has not recorded a single passenger fatality in an accident on normal, high-speed service. There have been several accidents, including four derailments at or above 270 km/h (168 mph), but in only one of these—a test run on a new line—did carriages overturn.

This safety record is credited in part to the stiffness that the articulated design lends to the train. There have been fatal accidents involving TGVs on *lignes classiques*, where the trains are exposed to the same dangers as normal trains, such as [level crossings](193595.md). These include one [terrorist bombing](30636.md) unrelated to the speed at which the train was traveling.

### On LGVs

- 14 December 1992: TGV 920 from Annecy to Paris, operated by set 56, derailed at 270 km/h (168 mph) at Mâcon-Loché TGV station ([Saône-et-Loire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%C3%B4ne-et-Loire)). A previous emergency stop had caused a wheel flat; the bogie concerned derailed while crossing the [points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railroad_switch) at the entrance to the station. No one on the train was injured, but 25 passengers waiting on the platform for another TGV were slightly injured by ballast that was thrown up from the trackbed.

- 21 December 1993: TGV 7150 from Valenciennes to Paris, operated by set 511, derailed at 300 km/h (186 mph) at the site of Haute Picardie TGV station, before it was built. Rain had caused a hole to open up under the track; the hole dated from the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War) but had not been detected during construction. The front power car and four carriages derailed but remained aligned with the track. Of the 200 passengers, one was slightly injured.

- 5 June 2000: Eurostar 9073 from Paris to London, operated by sets 3101/2 owned by the [National Railway Company of Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Railway_Company_of_Belgium), derailed at 250 km/h (155 mph) in the [Nord-Pas de Calais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nord-Pas_de_Calais) region near [Croisilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croisilles,_Pas-de-Calais).

  [^cite_note-48]

  &#x20;The transmission assembly on the rear bogie of the front power car failed, with parts falling onto the track. Four bogies out of 24 derailed. Out of 501 passengers, seven were bruised and others treated for shock.

  [^cite_note-49]

- 14 November 2015: TGV 2369 was involved in the [Eckwersheim derailment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eckwersheim_derailment), near Strasbourg, while being tested on the then-unopened second phase of the LGV Est. The derailment resulted in 11 deaths among those aboard, while 11 others aboard the train were seriously injured.

  [^cite_note-50]

  &#x20;Excessive speed has been cited as the cause.

  [^cite_note-dna-51]

### On classic lines

- 31 December 1983: A bomb allegedly planted by the terrorist organisation of [Carlos the Jackal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlos_the_Jackal) exploded on board a TGV from Marseille to Paris; two people were killed.

- 28 September 1988: TGV 736, operated by set 70 "Melun", collided with a lorry carrying an electric transformer weighing 100 tonnes that had become stuck on a level crossing in [Voiron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiron), Isère. The vehicle had not obtained the required crossing permit from the French *Direction départementale de l'équipement*. The weight of the lorry caused a very violent collision; the train driver and a passenger died, and 25 passengers were slightly injured.

- 4 January 1991: after a brake failure, TGV 360 ran away from Châtillon depot. The train was directed onto an unoccupied track and collided with the car loading ramp at Paris-Vaugirard station at 60 km/h (37 mph). No one was injured. The leading power car and the first two carriages were severely damaged, and were rebuilt.

- 25 September 1997: TGV 7119 from Paris to [Dunkerque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunkerque), operated by set 502, collided at 130 km/h (81 mph) with a 70 tonne asphalt paving machine on a level crossing at Bierne, near Dunkerque. The power car spun round and fell down an embankment. The front two carriages left the track and came to a stop in woods beside the track. Seven people were injured.

- 31 October 2001: TGV 8515 from Paris to Irun derailed at 130 km/h (81 mph) near [Dax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dax,_Landes) in southwest France. All ten carriages derailed and the rear power unit fell over. The cause was a broken rail.

- 30 January 2003: a TGV from Dunkerque to Paris collided at 106 km/h (66 mph) with a heavy goods vehicle stuck on the level crossing at Esquelbecq in northern France. The front power car was severely damaged, but only one bogie derailed. Only the driver was slightly injured.

- 19 December 2007: a TGV from Paris to Geneva collided at about 100 km/h (62 mph) with a truck on a level crossing near [Tossiat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tossiat) in eastern France, near the Swiss border. The driver of the truck died; on the train, one person was seriously injured and 24 were slightly injured.

  [^cite_note-52]

- 17 July 2014: a [TER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TER) train ran into the rear of a TGV [at Denguin, Pyrénées-Atlantiques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denguin_rail_crash). Forty people were injured.

Following the number of accidents at level crossings, an effort has been made to remove all level crossings on *lignes classiques* used by TGVs. The *ligne classique* from [Tours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tours) to [Bordeaux](4097.md) at the end of the LGV Atlantique has no level crossings as a result.

## Protests against the TGV

The first environmental protests against the building of an LGV occurred in May 1990 during the planning stages of the LGV Méditerranée. Protesters blocked a railway viaduct to protest against the planned route, arguing that it was unnecessary, and that trains could keep using existing lines to reach Marseille from Lyon.[^cite_note-53]

The [Turin–Lyon high-speed railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turin%E2%80%93Lyon_high-speed_railway) ([Lyon](8638634.md)-[Chambéry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamb%C3%A9ry)-[Turin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turin)), which would connect the TGV network to the Italian [TAV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treno_Alta_Velocit%C3%A0) network, has been the subject of demonstrations in Italy. While most Italian political parties agree on the construction of this line, some inhabitants of the towns where construction would take place oppose it vehemently. The concerns put forward by the protesters centre on storage of dangerous materials mined during tunnel boring, like [asbestos](21492663.md) and perhaps [uranium](31743.md), in the open air. This health danger could be avoided by using more expensive techniques for handling radioactive materials. A six-month delay in the start of construction has been decided in order to study solutions. In addition to the concerns of the residents, RFB – a ten-year-old national movement – opposes the development of Italy's [TAV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treno_Alta_Velocit%C3%A0) [high-speed rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-speed_rail) network as a whole.[^cite_note-54]

General complaints about the noise of TGVs passing near towns and villages have led the SNCF to build acoustic fencing along large sections of LGV to reduce the disturbance to residents, but protests still take place where SNCF has not addressed the issue.[^cite_note-55]

On 26 July 2024, the opening day of the 2024 Olympics, the TGV was hit by an arson attack. At least 800,000 people were affected by this. The Eurostar was specifically hit by this with 25% of trains canceled.

## Mail services

In addition to its standard services, mail delivery services were also operated by TGVs.

For many years, a service termed [SNCF TGV La Poste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF_TGV_La_Poste) transported mail for the French mail service, [La Poste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Poste_\(France\)). It used windowless but otherwise standard TGV rolling stock, painted in the yellow and blue livery of La Poste. However, the service ceased in June 2015.

## Mobile hospital service

During the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md), several TGV trains were transformed into mobile hospitals, in order to transport critically ill patients from overwhelmed hospitals in the East of France to hospitals in the West.[^cite_note-56]

Every coach allowed for up to 6 patients, allowing for the transport of several dozen patients, attended by a staff of 50 medical workers. Although the train moves at high speed, it accelerates and decelerates smoothly, allowing for medical procedures to be performed during transport.[^cite_note-57]

## Rebranding

Since July 2017, TGV services are gradually being rebranded as **TGV inOui** and **[Ouigo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouigo)** in preparation for the opening of the French HSR market to competition.[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60]

### TGV inOui



Logo used by inOui services

TGV inOui is SNCF Voyageurs' premium high-speed rail service. The name inOui was chosen because it sounds like the French word *inouï* meaning "extraordinary" (or more literally, "unheard of").[^cite_note-61] They have been operated by [SNCF Mobilités](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF_Mobilit%C3%A9s) since 27 May 2017 on [certain high speed rail services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_TGV_services).[^cite_note-62] In 2017, TGV inOui trains were tested on the [Paris](22989.md) – [Bordeaux](4097.md) – [Toulouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toulouse) line.[^cite_note-63] The brand was officially presented in September 2018. Its aim is to replace existing TGV services with "plus de confort, de services et de connectivité" ([English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language): *"more comfort, services and connectivity"*). In December 2018, trains operated between Lille, Marseille and Nice from Paris and operated on the rest of the network from 2020.[^cite_note-64]

### Ouigo

Main article: [Ouigo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouigo)

[Ouigo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouigo) is SNCF Voyageurs' low-cost rail service, using both high-speed and conventional trains. High-speed trains have a high-density one-class configuration and reduced on-board services. The literal translation of the brand name is "yes go", but the name is also a play on the English homonym, "we go".
