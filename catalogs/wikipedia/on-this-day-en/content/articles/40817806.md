***Orthoebolavirus zairense***[^cite_note-1] or **Zaire ebolavirus**, more commonly known as **Ebola virus** ([/iˈboʊlə, ɪ-/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English); **EBOV**), is one of six known species within the genus *[Orthoebolavirus](956106.md)*.[^cite_note-KuhnArch-2] Four of the six known ebolaviruses, including EBOV, cause a severe and often fatal [hemorrhagic fever](1719309.md) in humans and other [mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammals), known as [Ebola virus disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebola_virus_disease) (EVD). Ebola virus has caused the majority of human deaths from EVD, and was the cause of the [2013–2016 epidemic in western Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_African_Ebola_virus_epidemic),[^cite_note-3] which resulted in at least 28,646 suspected cases and 11,323 confirmed deaths.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5]

Ebola virus and its genus were both originally named after the [Ebola River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebola_River) and [Zaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaire) (now the [Democratic Republic of the Congo](76762.md)), the country where it was [first described](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Ebola_outbreaks),[^cite_note-KuhnArch-2] and was at first suspected to be a new "strain" of the closely related [Marburg virus](33134774.md).[^cite_note-Wordsworth-6][^cite_note-Pattyn1977-7][^cite_note-Bowen1977-8] The virus was renamed "Ebola virus" in 2010 to avoid confusion. Ebola virus is the single member of the [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species) *Zaire ebolavirus*, which is assigned to the genus *[Ebolavirus](956106.md)*, family *[Filoviridae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filoviridae)*, order *[Mononegavirales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mononegavirales)*. The members of the species are called Zaire ebolaviruses.[^cite_note-KuhnArch-2][^cite_note-9] The natural reservoir of Ebola virus is believed to be [bats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bat), particularly [fruit bats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megabat),[^cite_note-Quanmmen_2014-12-30-10] and it is primarily transmitted between humans and from animals to humans through [body fluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_fluid).[^cite_note-NYT-20141027-NA-11]

The EBOV genome is a single-stranded RNA, approximately 19,000 [nucleotides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleotide) long. It encodes seven structural proteins: [nucleoprotein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleoprotein) (NP), [polymerase cofactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofactor_\(biochemistry\)) (VP35), (VP40), GP, [transcription activator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcription_activator) (VP30), [VP24](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VP24), and [RNA-dependent RNA polymerase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA-dependent_RNA_polymerase) (L).[^cite_note-12]

Because of its high [fatality rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatality_rate) (up to 83 to 90 percent),[^cite_note-WHO2014-13][^cite_note-Elsevier/Academic_Press-14] EBOV is also listed as a [select agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Select_agent), a Risk Group 4 Pathogen (requiring [Biosafety Level 4-equivalent containment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosafety_level#Biosafety_level_4)) by the [World Health Organization](33583.md), a US Category A Priority Pathogen by the [National Institutes of Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institutes_of_Health)/[National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases](1013155.md), a Category A [Bioterrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioterrorism) Agent US [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](6811.md), and a Biological Agent for Export Control by the [Australia Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia_Group).

## Structure



A colorized [scanning electron micrograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scanning_electron_micrograph) of Ebola virus Makona variant (in red), the cause of the [Western African Ebola epidemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_African_Ebola_epidemic), on the surface of [Vero cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vero_cell)

EBOV carries a [negative-sense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative-sense) RNA genome in virions that are cylindrical/tubular, and contain [viral envelope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viral_envelope), matrix, and nucleocapsid components. The overall cylinders are generally approximately 80 [nm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanometer) in diameter, and have a virally encoded [glycoprotein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycoprotein) (GP) projecting as 7–10 nm long spikes from its [lipid bilayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipid_bilayer) surface.[^cite_note-15] The cylinders are of variable length, typically 800 nm, but sometimes up to 1000 nm long. The outer [viral envelope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viral_envelope) of the virion is derived by budding from domains of host cell membrane into which the GP spikes have been inserted during their [biosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosynthesis). Individual GP molecules appear with spacings of about 10 nm. Viral proteins [VP40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VP40) and VP24 are located between the envelope and the nucleocapsid (see following), in the *matrix space*.[^cite_note-pmid8219816-16] At the center of the virion structure is the [nucleocapsid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleocapsid), which is composed of a series of viral proteins attached to an 18–19 kb linear, negative-sense RNA without 3′-[polyadenylation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyadenylation) or 5′-capping (see following); the RNA is helically wound and complexed with the NP, VP35, VP30, and L proteins; this helix has a diameter of 80 nm.[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19]

## Genome

Each virion contains one molecule of linear, single-stranded, negative-sense RNA, 18,959 to 18,961 nucleotides in length.[^cite_note-20] The 3′ terminus is not polyadenylated and the 5′ end is not capped. This viral genome codes for seven structural proteins and one non-structural protein. The gene order is 3′ – leader – NP – VP35 – VP40 – GP/sGP – VP30 – VP24 – L – trailer – 5′; with the leader and trailer being non-transcribed regions, which carry important signals to control transcription, replication, and packaging of the viral genomes into new virions. Sections of the NP, VP35 and the L genes from filoviruses have been identified as endogenous in the genomes of several groups of small mammals.[^cite_note-Cite_pmid|_20569424-21][^cite_note-Cite_pmid|_20686665-22][^cite_note-cite_journal|doi=_10.7717/peerj.556-23]

Genomic structure of Ebola virus, mostly after GenBank KJ660346.2

It was found that 472 nucleotides from the 3' end and 731 nucleotides from the 5' end are sufficient for replication of a viral "minigenome".[^cite_note-Klenk2-24] Virus sequencing from 78 patients with confirmed Ebola virus disease, representing more than 70% of cases diagnosed in Sierra Leone from late May to mid-June 2014,[^cite_note-New_Yorker-25][^cite_note-Gire2014-26] provided evidence that the 2014 outbreak was no longer being fed by new contacts with its natural reservoir. Using [third-generation sequencing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third-generation_sequencing) technology, investigators were able to sequence samples as quickly as 48 hours.[^cite_note-27] Like other RNA viruses,[^cite_note-New_Yorker-25] Ebola virus mutates rapidly, both within a person during the progression of disease and in the reservoir among the local human population.[^cite_note-Gire2014-26] The observed mutation rate of 2.0 × 10<sup>−3</sup> substitutions per site per year is as fast as that of seasonal [influenza](19572217.md).[^cite_note-28]

## Entry



A structural render of the NPC1 protein

There are two candidates for host cell entry proteins. The first is a cholesterol transporter protein, the host-encoded Niemann–Pick C1 ([NPC1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NPC1)), which appears to be essential for entry of Ebola virions into the host cell and for its ultimate replication.[^cite_note-pmid21866103-29][^cite_note-pmid21866101-30] In one study, mice with one copy of the NPC1 gene [removed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_knockout) showed an 80 percent survival rate fifteen days after exposure to mouse-adapted Ebola virus, while only 10 percent of unmodified mice survived this long.[^cite_note-pmid21866103-29] In another study, [small molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_molecule) were shown to inhibit Ebola virus infection by preventing [viral envelope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viral_envelope) glycoprotein (GP) from binding to NPC1.[^cite_note-pmid21866101-30][^cite_note-pmid21959282-31] Hence, NPC1 was shown to be critical to entry of this [filovirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filovirus), because it mediates infection by binding directly to viral GP.[^cite_note-pmid21866101-30]

When cells from [Niemann–Pick Type C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niemann%E2%80%93Pick_disease,_type_C) individuals lacking this transporter were exposed to Ebola virus in the laboratory, the cells survived and appeared impervious to the virus, further indicating that Ebola relies on NPC1 to enter cells;[^cite_note-pmid21866103-29] mutations in the NPC1 gene in humans were conjectured as a possible mode to make some individuals resistant to this deadly viral disease. The same studies described similar results regarding NPC1's role in virus entry for [Marburg virus](33134774.md), a related [filovirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filovirus).[^cite_note-pmid21866103-29] A further study has also presented evidence that NPC1 is the critical receptor mediating Ebola infection via its direct binding to the viral GP, and that it is the second "lysosomal" domain of NPC1 that mediates this binding.[^cite_note-pmid22395071-32]

The second candidate is TIM-1 (a.k.a. [HAVCR1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HAVCR1)).[^cite_note-33] TIM-1 was shown to bind to the receptor binding domain of the EBOV glycoprotein, to increase the receptivity of [Vero cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vero_cells). Silencing its effect with siRNA prevented infection of [Vero cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vero_cells). TIM1 is expressed in tissues known to be seriously impacted by EBOV lysis (trachea, cornea, and conjunctiva). A monoclonal antibody against the IgV domain of TIM-1, ARD5, blocked EBOV binding and infection. Together, these studies suggest NPC1 and TIM-1 may be potential therapeutic targets for an Ebola anti-viral drug and as a basis for a rapid field diagnostic assay.

## Replication



Cross-sectional drawing of the Ebola virus particle, with structures of the major proteins shown and labeled at the side. Pale circles represent domains too flexible to be observed in the experimental structure.

Being acellular, viruses such as Ebola do not replicate through any type of cell division; rather, they use a combination of host- and virally encoded enzymes, alongside host cell structures, to produce multiple copies of themselves. These then self-assemble into viral [macromolecular structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macromolecular_assembly) in the host cell.[^cite_note-BiomarkerDatabase-34] The virus completes a set of steps when infecting each individual cell. The virus begins its attack by attaching to host receptors through the glycoprotein (GP) surface [peplomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peplomer) and is [endocytosed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endocytosis) into [macropinosomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macropinosome) in the host cell.[^cite_note-35] To penetrate the cell, the viral membrane fuses with [vesicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesicle_\(biology\)) membrane, and the [nucleocapsid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleocapsid) is released into the [cytoplasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cytoplasm). Encapsidated, negative-sense genomic [ssRNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/\(-\)ssRNA) is used as a template for the synthesis of polyadenylated, monocistronic mRNAs and, using the host cell's ribosomes, tRNA molecules, etc., the mRNA is translated into individual viral proteins.[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38]

These viral proteins are processed: a glycoprotein precursor (GP0) is cleaved to GP1 and GP2, which are then heavily glycosylated using cellular enzymes and substrates. These two molecules assemble, first into heterodimers, and then into trimers to give the surface peplomers. Secreted glycoprotein (sGP) precursor is cleaved to sGP and delta peptide, both of which are released from the cell. As viral protein levels rise, a switch occurs from translation to replication. Using the negative-sense genomic RNA as a template, a complementary +ssRNA is synthesized; this is then used as a template for the synthesis of new genomic (-)ssRNA, which is rapidly encapsidated. The newly formed nucleocapsids and envelope proteins associate at the host cell's plasma membrane; [budding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viral_shedding) occurs, destroying the cell.

## Ecology

Ebola virus is a [zoonotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoonosis) pathogen. Intermediary hosts have been reported to be "various species of fruit bats ... throughout central and sub-Saharan Africa". Evidence of infection in bats has been detected through molecular and serologic means. However, ebolaviruses have not been isolated in bats.[^cite_note-Quanmmen_2014-12-30-10][^cite_note-nejm52014-39] End hosts are humans and great apes, infected through bat contact or through other end hosts. Pigs in the Philippines have been reported to be infected with [Reston virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reston_virus), so other interim or amplifying hosts may exist.[^cite_note-nejm52014-39] Ebola virus outbreaks tend to occur when temperatures are lower and humidity is higher than usual for Africa.[^cite_note-40] Even after a person recovers from the acute phase of the disease, Ebola virus survives for months in certain organs such as the eyes and testes.[^cite_note-41]

## Ebola virus disease

Main article: [Ebola virus disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebola_virus_disease)

Zaire ebolavirus is one of the four ebolaviruses known to cause disease in humans. It has the highest [case-fatality rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case-fatality_rate) of these ebolaviruses, averaging 83 percent since the first outbreaks in 1976, although a fatality rate of up to 90 percent was recorded in one outbreak in the Republic of the Congo between December 2002 and April 2003. There have also been more outbreaks of Zaire ebolavirus than of any other ebolavirus. The first outbreak occurred on 26 August 1976 in [Yambuku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yambuku).[^cite_note-42] The first recorded case was Mabalo Lokela, a 44‑year-old schoolteacher. The symptoms resembled [malaria](20423.md), and subsequent patients received [quinine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine). Transmission has been attributed to reuse of unsterilized needles and close personal contact, body fluids and places where the person has touched. During the 1976 Ebola outbreak in [Zaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaire), [Ngoy Mushola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ngoy_Mushola) travelled from [Bumba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bumba,_Democratic_Republic_of_the_Congo) to [Yambuku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yambuku), where he recorded the first clinical description of the disease in his daily log:

&#x20;*The illness is characterized with a high temperature of about 39°C, [hematemesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematemesis), diarrhea with blood, retrosternal abdominal pain, prostration with "heavy" articulations, and rapid evolution death after a mean of three days.[^cite_note-43]*&#x20;

The Ebola outbreak that began in March 2014 reached epidemic proportions and killed more than 11,000 people. This outbreak was centered in West Africa, an area that had not previously been affected by the disease. The toll was particularly grave in three countries: Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone. A few cases were also reported in countries outside of West Africa, all related to international travelers who were exposed in the most affected regions and later showed symptoms of Ebola fever after reaching their destinations.[^cite_note-44]

The severity of the disease in humans varies widely, from rapid fatality to mild illness or even asymptomatic response.[^cite_note-NYT_Katze-45] Studies of outbreaks in the late twentieth century failed to find a correlation between the disease severity and the genetic nature of the virus. Hence the variability in the severity of illness was suspected to correlate with genetic differences in the victims. This has been difficult to study in animal models that respond to the virus with hemorrhagic fever in a similar manner as humans, because typical mouse models do not so respond, and the required large numbers of appropriate test subjects are not easily available. In late October 2014, a publication reported a study of the response to a mouse-adapted strain of Zaire ebolavirus presented by a genetically diverse population of mice that was bred to have a range of responses to the virus that includes fatality from hemorrhagic fever.[^cite_note-Rasmussen2014-46]

### Vaccine

Main article: [Ebola vaccine](44003094.md)

In December 2016, a study found the [VSV-EBOV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VSV-EBOV) vaccine to be 70–100% effective against the Zaire ebola virus (not the [Sudan ebolavirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudan_ebolavirus)), making it the first vaccine against the disease.[^cite_note-TL-20161222-47][^cite_note-CNN-2016122-48] VSV-EBOV was approved by the U.S. [Food and Drug Administration](11632.md) in December 2019.[^cite_note-FDA_PR-49]

## History and nomenclature



Filamentous Marburg [virions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virion) captured via negative-stained [transmission electron microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_electron_microscopy)&#x20;



[Phylogenetic tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phylogenetic_tree) comparing ebolaviruses and marburgviruses. Numbers indicate percent confidence of branches.

Ebola virus was first identified as a possible new "strain" of [Marburg virus](33134774.md) in 1976.[^cite_note-Pattyn1977-7][^cite_note-Bowen1977-8][^cite_note-50] The [International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Committee_on_Taxonomy_of_Viruses) (ICTV) identifies Ebola virus as [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species) *Zaire ebolavirus*, which is part of the genus *[Ebolavirus](956106.md)*, family *[Filoviridae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filoviridae)*, order *[Mononegavirales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mononegavirales)*. The name "Ebola virus" is derived from the [Ebola River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebola_River)—a river that was at first thought to be in close proximity to the area in [Democratic Republic of Congo](76762.md), previously called [Zaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaire), where the [1976 Zaire Ebola virus outbreak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976_Zaire_Ebola_virus_outbreak) occurred—and the taxonomic [suffix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffix) *virus*.[^cite_note-KuhnArch-2][^cite_note-Pattyn1977-7][^cite_note-Bowen1977-8][^cite_note-Johnson1977-51]

In 1998, the virus name was changed to "Zaire Ebola virus"[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53] and in 2002 to species *Zaire ebolavirus*.[^cite_note-Feldmann2005-54][^cite_note-Mayo02-55] However, most scientific articles continued to refer to "Ebola virus" or used the terms "Ebola virus" and "*Zaire ebolavirus*" in parallel. Consequently, in 2010, a group of researchers recommended that the name "Ebola virus" be adopted for a subclassification within the species *Zaire ebolavirus*, with the corresponding abbreviation EBOV.[^cite_note-KuhnArch-2] Previous abbreviations for the virus were EBOV-Z (for "Ebola virus Zaire") and ZEBOV (for "Zaire Ebola virus" or "*Zaire ebolavirus*"). In 2011, the ICTV explicitly rejected a proposal (2010.010bV) to recognize this name, as ICTV does not designate names for subtypes, variants, strains, or other subspecies level groupings.[^cite_note-56] At present, ICTV does not officially recognize "Ebola virus" as a taxonomic rank, but rather continues to use and recommend only the species designation *Zaire ebolavirus*.[^cite_note-57] The [prototype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_\(biology\)) Ebola virus, variant Mayinga (EBOV/May), was named after Mayinga N'Seka, a nurse who died during the 1976 Zaire outbreak.[^cite_note-KuhnArch-2][^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59]

According to the rules for taxon naming established by the [International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Committee_on_Taxonomy_of_Viruses) (ICTV), the name *Zaire ebolavirus* is always to be [capitalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalization), [italicized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italic_type), and to be preceded by the word "species". The names of its members (Zaire ebolaviruses) are to be capitalized, are not italicized, and used without [articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_\(grammar\)).[^cite_note-KuhnArch-2]

## Virus inclusion criteria

A virus of the genus *[Ebolavirus](956106.md)* is a member of the species *Zaire ebolavirus* if:[^cite_note-KuhnArch-2]

- it is endemic in the [Democratic Republic of the Congo](76762.md), [Gabon](12027.md), or the [Republic of the Congo](19599929.md)

- it has a genome with two or three [gene overlaps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_overlap) (*VP35*/*VP40*, *GP*/*VP30*, *VP24*/*L*)

- it has a [genomic sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genomic_sequence) that differs from the [type virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_\(biology\)) EBOV/May by less than 30%

## Evolution

*Zaire ebolavirus* diverged from its ancestors between 1960 and 1976.[^cite_note-\:0-60] The genetic diversity of *Ebolavirus* remained constant before 1900.[^cite_note-\:0-60][^cite_note-\:1-61] Then, around the 1960s, most likely due to climate change or human activities, the genetic diversity of the virus dropped rapidly and most lineages became extinct.[^cite_note-\:1-61] As the number of susceptible hosts declines, so does the effective population size and its genetic diversity. This genetic bottleneck effect has implications for the species' ability to cause [Ebola virus disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebola_virus_disease) in human hosts.

A [recombination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_recombination) event between *Zaire ebolavirus* lineages likely took place between 1996 and 2001 in wild apes giving rise to recombinant progeny viruses.[^cite_note-Wittmann2007-62] These recombinant viruses appear to have been responsible for a series of outbreaks among humans in Central Africa in 2001–2003.[^cite_note-Wittmann2007-62]

*Zaire ebolavirus* – Makona variant caused the 2014 West Africa outbreak.[^cite_note-\:2-63] The outbreak was characterized by the longest instance of human-to-human transmission of the viral species.[^cite_note-\:2-63] Pressures to adapt to the human host were seen at this time, however, no phenotypic changes in the virus (such as increased transmission, increased immune evasion by the virus) were seen.
