The **British Broadcasting Corporation** (**BBC**) is a British [public service broadcaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_service_broadcaster) headquartered at [Broadcasting House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcasting_House) in [London](17867.md), England. Originally established in 1922 as the [British Broadcasting Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Broadcasting_Company), it evolved into its current state with its current name on 1 January 1927. It is the oldest and largest local and global broadcaster by stature and by number of employees, with a total staff of 22,000.

The BBC was established under a [royal charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_charter#United_Kingdom),[^cite_note-crown-2] and operates under an agreement with the [secretary of state for culture, media and sport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Culture,_Media_and_Sport).[^cite_note-3] Its work is funded principally by an annual [television licence fee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_licensing_in_the_United_Kingdom)[^cite_note-TrustAR-4] which is charged to all British households, companies, and organisations using any type of equipment to receive or record live television broadcasts or to use the BBC's streaming service, [iPlayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPlayer).[^cite_note-TVLic-5] The fee is set by the [British government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_government), agreed by [Parliament](13964.md),[^cite_note-TVLic2-6] and is used to fund the BBC's radio, TV, and online services covering the nations and regions of the UK. Since 1 April 2014, it has also funded the [BBC World Service](37627.md) (launched in 1932 as the BBC Empire Service), which broadcasts in 43 languages as of 2026.[^cite_note-7]

Some of the BBC's revenue comes from its commercial subsidiary [BBC Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Studios) (formerly [BBC Worldwide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Worldwide)), which sells BBC programs and services internationally and also distributes the BBC's international 24-hour English-language news services [BBC News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_News_\(international_TV_channel\)), and from BBC.com, provided by BBC Global News Ltd.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9]

Since its formation on 18 October 1922, the BBC has played a prominent role in British life and culture.[^cite_note-10] It is sometimes informally referred to as **the Beeb** or **Auntie**.[^cite_note-Jackson-11][^cite_note-12] In 1923 it launched *[Radio Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Times)* (subtitled "The official organ of the BBC"), the first broadcast listings magazine; the 1988 [Christmas](6237.md) edition sold 11 million copies, the biggest-selling edition of any British magazine in history.[^cite_note-RadioTimes_Oldest-13]

The BBC has received sustained criticism over its impartiality, independence and funding. It has faced backlash over its coverage of the [Iraq War](5043324.md) and the [Israel-Palestine conflict](46216.md), and over allegations of [antisemitism](1078.md) and [colonial attitudes](7299.md). Accusations of political bias have been made from both the left and the right, and editorial controversies have led to resignations of senior executives.&#x20;

## History

For a chronological guide, see [Timeline of the BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_the_BBC).

### 1920–1922: The birth of British broadcasting

Britain's first live public broadcast was made from [the factory of Marconi Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Street_Works) in [Chelmsford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chelmsford) in June 1920. It was sponsored by the *[Daily Mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daily_Mail)*'s [Alfred Harmsworth, 1st Viscount Northcliffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Harmsworth,_1st_Viscount_Northcliffe) and featured the famous [Australian](4689264.md) soprano [Dame Nellie Melba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dame_Nellie_Melba). The Melba broadcast caught the people's imagination and marked a turning point in the British public's attitude to radio.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBriggs1985-14]<sup>: 47 </sup> However, this public enthusiasm was not shared in official circles where such broadcasts were held to interfere with important military and civil communications. By late 1920, the pressure from these quarters and uneasiness among the staff of the licensing authority, the [General Post Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Post_Office) (GPO), was sufficient to lead to a ban on further Chelmsford broadcasts.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBriggs1985-14]<sup>: 50 </sup>

But by 1922 the GPO had received nearly 100 broadcast licence requests[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECurranSeaton2018-15]<sup>: 110 </sup> and moved to rescind its ban in the wake of a petition by 63 wireless societies with over 3,000 members.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBriggs1985-14]<sup>: 50–97 </sup> Anxious to avoid the same chaotic expansion experienced in the United States, the GPO proposed that it would issue a single broadcasting licence to a company jointly owned by a consortium of leading wireless receiver manufacturers, to be known as the [British Broadcasting Company Ltd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Broadcasting_Company_Ltd), which was formed on 18 October 1922.[^cite_note-16] [John Reith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Reith,_1st_Baron_Reith), a Scottish [Calvinist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvinist), was appointed its general manager in December 1922 a few weeks after the company made its first official broadcast.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECurranSeaton2018-15]<sup>: 110 </sup> [L. Stanton Jefferies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._Stanton_Jefferies) was its first director of music.[^cite_note-17] The company was to be financed by a royalty on the sale of BBC wireless receiving sets from approved domestic manufacturers.[^cite_note-18] To this day, the BBC aims to follow the Reithian directive to "inform, educate and entertain".[^cite_note-19]

### 1923–1926: From private company to public service corporation



The first issue of the *[Radio Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Times)* (28 September 1923)

The financial arrangements soon proved inadequate. Set sales were disappointing as amateurs made their own receivers and listeners bought rival unlicensed sets.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBriggs1985-14]<sup>: 146 </sup> By mid-1923, discussions between the GPO and the BBC had become deadlocked and the Postmaster General commissioned a review of broadcasting by the Sykes Committee.[^cite_note-Company_History-20] The committee recommended a short-term reorganisation of licence fees with improved enforcement to address the BBC's immediate financial distress, and an increased share of the licence revenue split between it and the GPO. This was to be followed by a simple 10 shillings licence fee to fund broadcasts.[^cite_note-Company_History-20] The BBC's broadcasting monopoly was made explicit for the duration of its current broadcast licence, as was the prohibition on advertising. To avoid competition with newspapers, [Fleet Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleet_Street) persuaded the government to ban news bulletins before 7 pm and the BBC was required to source all news from external wire services.[^cite_note-Company_History-20] The *Radio Times*, the world's first and longest-running radio and television listings magazine, was launched by Reith in September 1923.[^cite_note-RadioTimes_Oldest-13] The first edition, subtitled "The official organ of the BBC", was priced at tuppence (two [pence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penny_\(British_pre-decimal_coin\))) on newsstands, and quickly sold out its run of a quarter of a million copies.[^cite_note-21]

Mid-1925 found the future of broadcasting under further consideration, this time by the Crawford committee. By now the BBC, under Reith's leadership, had forged a consensus favouring a continuation of the unified (monopoly) broadcasting service, but more money was still required to finance rapid expansion. Wireless manufacturers were anxious to exit the loss-making consortium, and Reith was keen that the BBC be seen as a public service rather than a commercial enterprise. The recommendations of the Crawford Committee were published in March the following year and were still under consideration by the GPO when the [1926 United Kingdom general strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1926_United_Kingdom_general_strike) broke out in May. The strike temporarily interrupted newspaper production, and with restrictions on news bulletins waived, the BBC suddenly became the primary source of news for the duration of the crisis.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECurranSeaton2018-15]<sup>: 117 </sup>

The crisis placed the BBC in a delicate position. On the one hand Reith was acutely aware that the government might exercise its right to commandeer the BBC at any time as a mouthpiece of the government if the BBC were to step out of line, but on the other he was anxious to maintain public trust by appearing to be acting independently. The [government](25318118.md) was divided on how to handle the BBC, but ended up trusting Reith, whose opposition to the strike mirrored the PM's own. Although [Winston Churchill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winston_Churchill) in particular wanted to commandeer the BBC to use it "to the best possible advantage", Reith wrote that [Stanley Baldwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Baldwin)'s government wanted to be able to say "that they did not commandeer \[the BBC], but they know that they can trust us not to be really impartial".[^cite_note-guardian180814-22] Thus the BBC was granted sufficient leeway to pursue the government's objectives largely in a manner of its own choosing. Supporters of the strike nicknamed the BBC the BFC for British Falsehood Company. Reith personally announced the end of the strike which he marked by reciting from Blake's "[Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/And_did_those_feet_in_ancient_time)" signifying that England had been saved.[^cite_note-23]

While the BBC tends to characterise its coverage of the general strike by emphasising the positive impression created by its balanced coverage of the views of government and strikers, Seaton has characterised the episode as the invention of "modern propaganda in its British form".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECurranSeaton2018-15]<sup>: 117 </sup> Reith argued that trust gained by 'authentic impartial news' could then be used. Impartial news was not necessarily an end in itself.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECurranSeaton2018-15]<sup>: 118 </sup>

The BBC did well out of the crisis, which cemented a national audience for its broadcasting, and it was followed by the Government's acceptance of the recommendation made by the Crawford Committee (1925–26) that the British Broadcasting Company be replaced by a non-commercial, Crown-chartered organisation: the British Broadcasting Corporation.[^cite_note-Company_History-20]

### 1927–1939



The *Radio Times* masthead from 25 December 1931, including the BBC motto "Nation shall speak peace unto Nation"



Television pioneer [John Logie Baird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Logie_Baird) (seen here in 1917) televised the BBC's first drama, *[The Man with the Flower in His Mouth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Man_with_the_Flower_in_His_Mouth)*, on 14 July 1930, and the first live outside broadcast, [The Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Derby), on 2 June 1931.

[^cite_note-25]

The British Broadcasting Corporation came into existence on 1 January 1927, and Reith – newly knighted – was appointed its first director general. To represent its purpose and (stated) values, the new corporation adopted the [coat of arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coat_of_arms_of_the_BBC), including the motto "Nation shall speak peace unto Nation".[^cite_note-Motto-26]

British radio audiences had little choice apart from BBC's programming approach. Reith was viewed as taking a moralistic approach as an executive, aiming to broadcast "all that is best in every department of human knowledge, endeavour and achievement", and putting the programming in moral or ethical terms, advocating "a high moral tone" as "obviously of paramount importance".[^cite_note-27] Reith succeeded in building a high wall against a more tabloid, free-for-all in radio aimed at merely attracting the largest audience (and advertising revenue). There was no paid advertising on the BBC; all the revenue came from a tax on receiving sets. Highbrow audiences, however, greatly enjoyed it.[^cite_note-28] At a time when American, Australian and Canadian stations were drawing huge audiences cheering for their local teams with the broadcast of baseball, rugby and hockey, the BBC emphasised service for a national rather than a regional audience. Boat races were well covered along with tennis and horse racing, but the BBC was reluctant to spend its severely limited air time on long football or [cricket](25675557.md) games, regardless of their popularity.[^cite_note-29]



The BBC's radio studio in Birmingham, from the *BBC Hand Book* 1928, which described it as "Europe's largest studio"

John Reith and the BBC, with support from [the Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crown), determined the universal needs of the people of Britain and broadcast content according to these perceived standards.[^cite_note-30] Reith effectively censored anything that he felt would be harmful, directly or indirectly.[^cite_note-Avery-2006-31] While recounting his time with the BBC in 1935, [Raymond Postgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Postgate) claims that BBC broadcasters were made to submit a draft of their potential broadcast for approval. It was expected that they tailored their content to accommodate the modest, church-going elderly or a member of the Clergy.[^cite_note-32] Until 1928, entertainers broadcasting on the BBC, both singers and "talkers" were expected to avoid biblical quotations, Clerical impersonations and references, references to drink or [Prohibition in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prohibition_in_the_United_States), vulgar and doubtful matter and political allusions.[^cite_note-Avery-2006-31] The BBC excluded popular foreign music and musicians from its broadcasts, while promoting British alternatives.[^cite_note-33] On 5 March 1928, Stanley Baldwin, the Prime Minister, maintained the censorship of editorial opinions on public policy, but allowed the BBC to address matters of religious, political or industrial controversy.[^cite_note-Scanell-1991-34] The resulting political "talk series", designed to inform England on political issues, were criticised by members of parliament, including Winston Churchill, [David Lloyd George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lloyd_George) and [Sir Austen Chamberlain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Austen_Chamberlain). Those who opposed these chats claimed that they silence the opinions of those in Parliament who are not nominated by Party Leaders or Party Whips, thus stifling independent, non-official views.[^cite_note-Scanell-1991-34] In October 1932, the policemen of the [Metropolitan Police Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Police_Federation) marched in protest at a proposed pay cut. Fearing dissent within the police force and public support for the movement, the BBC censored its coverage of the events, only broadcasting official statements from the government.[^cite_note-Scanell-1991-34]

Throughout the 1930s political broadcasts had been monitored by the BBC.[^cite_note-West-1987-35] In 1935, the BBC censored the broadcasts of [Oswald Mosley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Mosley) and [Harry Pollitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Pollitt).[^cite_note-Scanell-1991-34] Mosley was a leader of the [British Union of Fascists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Union_of_Fascists), and Pollitt a leader of the [Communist Party of Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_Great_Britain). They were contracted to provide a series of five broadcasts on their parties' politics. The BBC, in conjunction with [The Foreign Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Foreign_Office) of Britain, suspended this series and ultimately cancelled it without the notice of the public.[^cite_note-West-1987-35][^cite_note-Scanell-1991-34] Some politicians faced censorship. In 1938, Winston Churchill proposed a series of talks regarding British domestic and foreign politics and affairs but was censored.[^cite_note-West-1987-35] The censorship of political discourse by the BBC started the shutdown of the political debate that manifested over the BBC's wartime airwaves.[^cite_note-West-1987-35] The Foreign Office maintained that the public should not be aware of their role in the censorship.[^cite_note-Scanell-1991-34] From 1935 to 1939, the BBC attempted to unite the British Empire's radio waves, sending staff to Egypt, [Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandatory_Palestine), [Newfoundland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newfoundland), Jamaica, India, Canada and South Africa.[^cite_note-Potter-2012-36] Reith visited South Africa, lobbying for state-run radio programmes accepted by [South African Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_African_Parliament) in 1936.[^cite_note-Potter-2012-36] A similar programme was adopted in Canada. Through collaboration with these state-run broadcasting centres, Reith had an influence on the culture across the empire of Great Britain with his departure from the corporation in 1938.[^cite_note-Potter-2012-36]

Experimental television broadcasts were started in 1929 using an [electromechanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromechanical_film) 30-line system developed by [John Logie Baird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Logie_Baird).[^cite_note-37] Limited regular broadcasts using this system began in 1932, and [an expanded service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_day_of_BBC_television) (now named the [BBC Television Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Television#History_of_BBC_Television)) started from [Alexandra Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandra_Palace) in November 1936, alternating between an improved Baird mechanical 240-line system and the all-electronic [405-line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/405-line) Marconi-EMI system which had been developed by an [EMI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EMI) research team led by Sir [Isaac Shoenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Shoenberg).[^cite_note-38] The superiority of the electronic system saw the [mechanical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_system) dropped early the following year, with the Marconi-EMI system the first fully electronic television system in the world to be used in regular broadcasting.[^cite_note-39]

#### BBC versus other media



King [George V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_V) giving the 1934 [Royal Christmas message](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Christmas_message) on BBC Radio. The annual message typically reflects on the year's major events.

The success of broadcasting provoked animosities between the BBC and well-established media such as theatres, concert halls and the recording industry. By 1929, the BBC complained that the agents of many comedians refused to sign contracts for broadcasting, because they feared it harmed the artist "by making his material stale" and that it "reduces the value of the artist as a visible music-hall performer". On the other hand, the BBC was "keenly interested" in a cooperation with the recording companies who "in recent years ... have not been slow to make records of singers, orchestras, dance bands, etc. who have already proved their power to achieve popularity by wireless." Radio plays were so popular that the BBC had received 6,000 manuscripts by 1929, most of them written for stage and of little value for broadcasting: "Day in and day out, manuscripts come in, and nearly all go out again through the post, with a note saying 'We regret, etc.'"[^cite_note-40] In the 1930s music broadcasts also enjoyed great popularity, for example the friendly and wide-ranging [BBC Theatre Organ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Theatre_Organ) broadcasts at [St George's Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._George's_Hall,_London), London by [Reginald Foort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reginald_Foort), who held the official role of BBC Staff Theatre Organist from 1936 to 1938.[^cite_note-41]

### Second World War



[Statue of George Orwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue_of_George_Orwell) outside [Broadcasting House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcasting_House), headquarters of the BBC

Television broadcasting was suspended from 1 September 1939 to 7 June 1946, during [World War II](32927.md), and it was left to [BBC Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio) broadcasters such as Reginald Foort to keep the nation's spirits up. The BBC moved most of its radio operations out of London, initially to [Bristol](36741.md), and then to [Bedford](69772.md). Concerts were broadcast from the [Bedford Corn Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedford_Corn_Exchange); the Trinity Chapel in [St Paul's Church, Bedford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Paul's_Church,_Bedford) was the studio for the Daily Service (a daily 15-minute religious service first broadcast on the BBC in 1928 which continues today) from 1941 to 1945, and, in the darkest days of the war in 1941, the [Archbishops of Canterbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmo_Gordon_Lang) and [York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Temple_\(bishop\)) came to St Paul's to broadcast to the [UK](31717.md) and the world on the National Day of Prayer.[^cite_note-42] BBC employees during the war included [George Orwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Orwell) who spent two years with the broadcaster.[^cite_note-43]

During his role as prime minister during the war, Winston Churchill delivered 33 major wartime speeches by radio, all of which were carried by the BBC within the UK.[^cite_note-44] On 18 June 1940, French general [Charles de Gaulle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_de_Gaulle), in exile in London as the leader of the Free French, made a speech, broadcast by the BBC, urging the French people not to capitulate to the Nazis.[^cite_note-45] In October 1940, Princesses [Elizabeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_II) and [Margaret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princess_Margaret,_Countess_of_Snowdon) made their first radio broadcast for the BBC's *[Children's Hour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children's_Hour)*, addressing other children who had been evacuated from cities.[^cite_note-46]

In 1938, John Reith and the [Government of the United Kingdom](25318118.md), specifically the [Ministry of Information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Information_\(United_Kingdom\)) which had been set up for WWII, designed a censorship apparatus for the inevitability of war.[^cite_note-Nicholas-1996-47] Due to the BBC's advancements in [shortwave radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortwave_radio) technology, the corporation could broadcast across the world during the Second World War.[^cite_note-Cull-1995-48] Within [Europe](9239.md), the BBC European Service would gather intelligence and information regarding the current events of the war in English.[^cite_note-Nicholas-1996-47][^cite_note-Ribeiro-2011-49] Regional BBC workers, based on their regional geo-political climate, would then further censor the material their broadcasts would cover. Nothing was to be added outside the preordained news items.[^cite_note-Nicholas-1996-47][^cite_note-Ribeiro-2011-49] For example, the BBC Polish Service was heavily censored due to fears of jeopardising relations with the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union). Controversial topics, i.e. the contested Polish and Soviet border, the deportation of Polish citizens, the arrests of [Polish Home Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Home_Army) members and the [Katyn massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katyn_massacre), were not included in Polish broadcasts.[^cite_note-50] American radio broadcasts were broadcast across Europe on BBC channels. This material also passed through the BBC's censorship office, which surveilled and edited American coverage of British affairs.[^cite_note-Cull-1995-48] By 1940, across all BBC broadcasts, music by composers from enemy nations was censored. In total, 99 German, 38 Austrian and 38 Italian composers were censored. The BBC argued that like the Italian or German languages, listeners would be irritated by the inclusion of enemy composers.[^cite_note-51] Any potential broadcasters said to have pacifist, communist or [fascist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascist) ideologies were not allowed on the BBC's airwaves.[^cite_note-52] In 1937, a [MI5](100076.md) security officer was given a permanent office within the organisation. This officer would examine the files of potential political subversives and [mark the files of those deemed a security risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%22Christmas_tree%22_files) to the organisation, [blacklisting](221697.md) them. This was often done on spurious grounds; even so, the practice would continue and expand during the years of the [Cold War](325329.md).[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-Hollingsworth-54]

### Later 20th century



Produced between 1934 and 1959, the [BBC-Marconi Type A microphone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC-Marconi_Type_A_microphone) is regarded as an iconic symbol of the BBC, alongside the channel's [rotating globe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_BBC_television_idents#BBC_Television_Service/BBC_One) emblem introduced in 1963.

[^cite_note-55]

There was a widely reported urban myth that, upon resumption of the BBC television service after the war, announcer [Leslie Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leslie_Mitchell_\(broadcaster\)) started by saying, "As I was saying before we were so rudely interrupted ...". In fact, the first person to appear when transmission resumed was [Jasmine Bligh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jasmine_Bligh) and the words said were, "Good afternoon, everybody. How are you? Do you remember me, Jasmine Bligh...?"[^cite_note-56] The [European Broadcasting Union](10380.md) was formed on 12 February 1950, in [Torquay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torquay) with the BBC among the 23 founding broadcasting organisations.[^cite_note-57]

Competition to the BBC was introduced in 1955, with the commercial and independently operated television network of [Independent Television (ITV)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITV_\(TV_network\)). However, the BBC monopoly on radio services would persist until 8 October 1973 when under the control of the newly renamed [Independent Broadcasting Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Broadcasting_Authority) (IBA), the UK's first Independent local radio station, [LBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LBC) came on-air in the London area. As a result of the [Pilkington Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilkington_Committee_on_Broadcasting) report of 1962, in which the BBC was praised for the quality and range of its output, and ITV was very heavily criticised for not providing enough quality programming,[^cite_note-58] the decision was taken to award the BBC a second television channel, [BBC2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC2), in 1964, renaming the existing service [BBC1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC1). BBC2 used the higher resolution 625-line standard which had been standardised across Europe. BBC2 was broadcast in colour from 1 July 1967 and was joined by BBC1 and ITV on 15 November 1969. The 405-line [VHF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Very_high_frequency#United_Kingdom) transmissions of BBC1 (and ITV) were continued for compatibility with older television receivers until 1985.



[Television Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_Centre,_London) at [White City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_City,_London), west London (pictured in 2009), headquarters of the BBC from 1960 to 2013. Since being refurbished, the complex is now the home of [BBC Studioworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Studioworks).

Starting in 1964, a series of [pirate radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pirate_radio) stations (starting with [Radio Caroline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Caroline)) came on the air and forced the British government finally to regulate radio services to permit nationally based advertising-financed services. In response, the BBC reorganised and renamed their radio channels. On 30 September 1967, the Light Programme was split into [Radio 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_1) offering continuous "Popular" music and [Radio 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_2) more "Easy Listening".[^cite_note-59] The "Third" programme became [Radio 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_3) offering classical music and cultural programming. The Home Service became [Radio 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_4) offering news, and non-musical content such as quiz shows, readings, dramas and plays. As well as the four national channels, a series of local BBC radio stations were established in 1967, including [Radio London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_London).[^cite_note-60] In 1969, the BBC Enterprises department was formed to exploit BBC brands and programmes for commercial spin-off products. In 1979, it became a wholly owned limited company, BBC Enterprises Ltd.[^cite_note-briggs-61]

In 1974 the BBC's [teletext](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teletext) service, [Ceefax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceefax), was introduced, created initially to provide subtitling, but developed into a news and information service. In 1978, BBC staff went on strike just before the Christmas, thus blocking out the transmission of both channels and amalgamating all four radio stations into one.[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63] Since the deregulation of the UK television and radio market in the 1980s, the BBC has faced increased competition from the commercial sector (and from the advertiser-funded public service broadcaster [Channel 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_4)), especially on satellite television, cable television, and digital television services. In the late 1980s, the BBC began a process of [divestment](981983.md) by [spinning off](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_spin-off) and selling parts of its organisation. In 1988, it sold off the Hulton Press Library, a photographic archive which had been acquired from the *[Picture Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picture_Post)* magazine by the BBC in 1957. The archive was sold to Brian Deutsch and is now owned by [Getty Images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getty_Images).[^cite_note-hulton-history-64] In 1987, the BBC decided to centralise its operations by the management team with the radio and television divisions joining forces together for the first time, the activities of the news and currents departments and coordinated jointly under the new directorate.[^cite_note-65] During the 1990s, this process continued with the separation of certain operational arms of the corporation into autonomous but [wholly owned subsidiaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wholly_owned_subsidiaries), with the aim of generating additional revenue for programme-making. BBC Enterprises was reorganised and relaunched in 1995, as BBC Worldwide Ltd.[^cite_note-briggs-61] In 1998, BBC studios, outside broadcasts, post production, design, costumes and wigs were spun off into BBC Resources Ltd.[^cite_note-guardian-resources-66]

The [BBC Research & Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Research_&_Development) has played a major part in the development of broadcasting and recording techniques. The BBC was also responsible for the development of the [NICAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NICAM) stereo standard. In recent decades, a number of additional channels and radio stations have been launched: [Radio 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_5_\(former\)) was launched in 1990, as a sports and educational station, but was replaced in 1994, with [BBC Radio 5 Live](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_5_Live) to become a live radio station, following the success of the [Radio 4 service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_4_News_FM) to cover the 1991 [Gulf War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War). The new station would be a news and sport station. In 1997, [BBC News 24](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_News_24), a rolling news channel, launched on digital television services, and the following year, [BBC Choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Choice) was launched as the third general entertainment channel from the BBC. The BBC also purchased The Parliamentary Channel, which was renamed [BBC Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Parliament). In 1999, [BBC Knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Knowledge) launched as a multimedia channel, with services available on the newly launched [BBC Text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Text) digital teletext service (later rebranded as BBC Red Button), and on [BBC Online](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Online). The channel had an educational aim, which was modified later on in its life to offer documentaries.

### 2000–2011

#### Channel and branding changes



A [BBC HD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_HD) outside broadcast van in August 2005



England fans in [Manchester](20206.md) during a [2006 FIFA World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_FIFA_World_Cup) game shown on the [BBC Big Screen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Big_Screen)



[BBC Pacific Quay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Pacific_Quay) in Glasgow, which was opened in 2007

In 2002 several television and radio channels were reorganised. BBC Knowledge was replaced by [BBC Four](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Four) and became the BBC's arts and documentaries channel. [CBBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBBC), which had been a programming strand as Children's BBC since 1985, was split into CBBC and [CBeebies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBeebies), for younger children, with both new services getting a digital channel: the CBBC Channel and CBeebies Channel.[^cite_note-67] In addition to the television channels, new digital radio stations were created: [1Xtra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1Xtra), [6 Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/6_Music) and [Radio 4 Extra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_4_Extra). BBC 1Xtra was a sister station to Radio 1 and specialised in modern black music, BBC 6 Music specialised in alternative music genres and BBC7 specialised in archive, speech and children's programming.[^cite_note-68]

The following few years resulted in repositioning of some channels to conform to a larger brand: in 2003, BBC Choice was replaced by BBC Three, with programming for younger adults and shocking real-life documentaries, BBC News 24 became the [BBC News Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_News_Channel) in 2008, and BBC Radio 7 became BBC Radio 4 Extra in 2011, with new programmes to supplement those broadcast on Radio 4. In 2008, another channel was launched, [BBC Alba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Alba), a [Scottish Gaelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Gaelic) service.[^cite_note-69]

In 2006 [BBC HD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_HD) was launched as an experimental service, becoming official in December 2007. The channel broadcast HD simulcasts of programmes on [BBC One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_One), [BBC Two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Two), [BBC Three](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Three) and [BBC Four](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Four) as well as repeats of some older programmes in HD. In 2010, an HD simulcast of BBC One launched: [BBC One HD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_One_HD). The channel uses HD versions of BBC One's schedule and uses upscaled versions of programmes not currently produced in HD. The BBC HD channel closed in March 2013 and was replaced by BBC Two HD in the same month.[^cite_note-70]

The 2004 [Hutton Inquiry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutton_Inquiry) and the subsequent report raised questions about the BBC's journalistic standards and its impartiality. This led to resignations of senior management members at the time including the then Director General, [Greg Dyke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greg_Dyke). In January 2007, the BBC released minutes of the board meeting which led to Greg Dyke's resignation.[^cite_note-71]

#### Sale of divisions

During this decade the corporation began to sell off a number of its operational divisions to private owners: BBC Broadcast was spun off as a separate company in 2002,[^cite_note-broadcast-ltd-72] and in 2005, it was sold off to Australian-based Macquarie Capital Alliance Group and [Macquarie Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macquarie_Group) Limited and rebranded [Red Bee Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Bee_Media).[^cite_note-73] The BBC's [IT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IT), [telephony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephony) and [broadcast technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_technology) were brought together as BBC Technology Ltd in 2001,[^cite_note-broadcast-ltd-72] and the division was later sold to the German company [Siemens IT Solutions and Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens_IT_Solutions_and_Services) (SIS).[^cite_note-bbc-2204-74] SIS was subsequently acquired from Siemens by the French company [Atos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atos).[^cite_note-atos-ariel-75] Further divestments included [BBC Books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Books) (sold to [Random House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_House) in 2006);[^cite_note-76] BBC Outside Broadcasts Ltd (sold in 2008 to [Satellite Information Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_Information_Services));[^cite_note-OBsale-77] Costumes and Wigs (stock sold in 2008 to [Angels Costumes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angels_Costumes));[^cite_note-angels-costumes-78] and BBC Magazines (sold to [Immediate Media Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immediate_Media_Company) in 2011).[^cite_note-79] After the sales of OBs and costumes, the remainder of BBC Resources was reorganised as [BBC Studios and Post Production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Studios_and_Post_Production), which continues today as a wholly owned subsidiary of the BBC.

#### Creative Futures

On 7 March 2005 director general [Mark Thompson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Thompson_\(television_executive\)) launched the "Creative Futures" project to restructure the organisation.[^cite_note-80] A [strike in May 2005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_BBC_strike) by more than 11,000 BBC workers over a proposal to cut 4,000 jobs and to privatise parts of the BBC, disrupted much of the BBC's regular programming.[^cite_note-nyt-2005-05-24-81][^cite_note-82]

The blueprint for the future of the BBC resulting from the project was published ion 25 April 2006.[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84][^cite_note-85] On 18 October 2007, Thompson announced a six-year plan, "Delivering Creative Futures", which included merging the television current affairs department into a new "News Programmes" division.[^cite_note-reform-86][^cite_note-87] Thompson's announcement, in response to a £2 billion shortfall in funding, would, he said, deliver "a smaller but fitter BBC" in the digital age, by cutting its payroll and, in 2013, selling [Television Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Television_Centre).[^cite_note-88] The plans included a reduction in posts of 2,500; including 1,800 redundancies, consolidating news operations, reducing programming output by 10% and selling off the flagship [Television Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_Centre,_London) building in London.[^cite_note-reform-86]

#### Licence fee frozen

On 20 October 2010 the [Chancellor of the Exchequer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_of_the_Exchequer) [George Osborne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Osborne) announced that the television licence fee would be frozen at its current level until the end of the current charter in 2016. The same announcement revealed that the BBC would take on the full cost of running the [BBC World Service](37627.md) and the [BBC Monitoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Monitoring) service from the [Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office](59653.md), and partially finance the Welsh broadcaster [S4C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S4C).[^cite_note-89][^cite_note-90]

### Since 2011



The new extension to the BBC's [Broadcasting House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcasting_House), completed in 2012

Further cuts were announced on 6 October 2011, so the BBC could reach a total reduction in their budget of 20%, following the licence fee freeze in October 2010, which included cutting staff by 2,000 and sending a further 1,000 to the [MediaCityUK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MediaCityUK) development in [Salford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salford), with BBC Three moving online only in 2016, the sharing of more programmes between stations and channels, sharing of radio news bulletins, more repeats in schedules, including the whole of BBC Two daytime and for some original programming to be reduced. BBC HD was closed on 26 March 2013, and replaced with an HD simulcast of BBC Two; however, flagship programmes, other channels and full funding for CBBC and CBeebies would be retained.[^cite_note-91][^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93] Numerous BBC facilities have been sold off, including [New Broadcasting House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Broadcasting_House,_Manchester) on [Wilmslow Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilmslow_Road) in Manchester. Many major departments have been relocated to [Broadcasting House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcasting_House) in central London and MediaCityUK in Salford, particularly since the closure of BBC Television Centre in March 2013.[^cite_note-94] On 16 February 2016, the BBC Three television service was discontinued and replaced by a [digital outlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Three_\(streaming_service\)) under the same name, targeting its young adult audience with web series and other content.[^cite_note-95][^cite_note-96]

Under the new royal charter instituted in 2017, the corporation must publish an annual report to Ofcom, outlining its plans and public service obligations for the next year. In its 2017–18 report, released July 2017, the BBC announced plans to "re-invent" its output to better compete against commercial streaming services such as [Netflix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netflix). These plans included increasing the diversity of its content on television and radio, a major increase in investments towards digital children's content, and plans to make larger investments in Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland to "rise to the challenge of better reflecting and representing a changing UK".[^cite_note-97][^cite_note-98] Since 2017, the BBC has also funded the [Local Democracy Reporting Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Democracy_Reporting_Service), with up to 165 journalists employed by independent news organisations to report on [local democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_government_in_the_United_Kingdom) issues on a [pooled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Press_pool) basis.[^cite_note-Linford-99]

In 2016 the BBC Director General [Tony Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Hall,_Baron_Hall_of_Birkenhead) announced a savings target of £800 million per year by 2021, which is about 23% of annual licence fee revenue. Having to take on the £700 million cost for free TV licences for the over-75 pensioners, and rapid inflation in drama and sport coverage costs, was given as the reason. Duplication of management and content spending would be reduced, and there would be a review of [BBC News](1139893.md).[^cite_note-guardian-20160308-100][^cite_note-forbes-20160511-101]

In September 2019 the BBC launched the [Trusted News Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trusted_News_Initiative) to work with news and social media companies to combat [disinformation](142690.md) about national elections.[^cite_note-102][^cite_note-103]

In 2020 the BBC announced a BBC News savings target of £80 million per year by 2022, involving about 520 staff reductions. The BBC's director of news and current affairs [Fran Unsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran_Unsworth) said there would be further moves toward digital broadcasting, in part to attract back a youth audience, and more pooling of reporters to stop separate teams covering the same news.[^cite_note-bbc-20200129-104][^cite_note-guardian-20200715-105] In 2020, the BBC reported a £119 million deficit because of delays to cost reduction plans, and the forthcoming ending of the remaining £253 million funding towards pensioner licence fees would increase financial pressures.[^cite_note-ft-20200915-106]

In March 2023 the BBC was at the centre of a political row with football pundit [Gary Lineker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Lineker), after he criticised the British government's asylum policy on social media. Lineker was suspended from his position on *Match of the Day* before being re-instated after receiving overwhelming support from his colleagues. The scandal was made worse due to the connections between BBC's chairman, Richard Sharp, and the Conservative Party.[^cite_note-107]

In April 2023 Richard Sharp resigned as chairman after a report found he did not disclose potential perceived conflicts of interest in his role in the facilitation of a loan to Prime Minister [Boris Johnson](19065069.md).[^cite_note-108][^cite_note-109] Dame [Elan Closs Stephens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elan_Closs_Stephens) was appointed as acting chairwoman on 27 June 2023, and she would lead the BBC board for a year or until a new permanent chair has been appointed.[^cite_note-Stephens-110] [Samir Shah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samir_Shah) was subsequently appointed with effect from 4 March 2024.[^cite_note-ShahAppointment-111] In October 2024 it was announced that the BBC along with [Sky Sports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_Sports) signed a deal to broadcast the 2025–26 season of the [Women's Super League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_Super_League) campaign.[^cite_note-112]

In May 2025, BBC director general [Tim Davie](1925670.md) said there were plans to switch off traditional broadcast transmissions in the 2030s to transition to a fully online delivery of programmes.[^cite_note-113]

In November 2025, following the leaking of [a report that alleged systemic bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_BBC_editorial_bias_controversy) within the BBC, Tim Davie resigned as Director-General along with Deborah Turness as CEO of News within the organisation.[^cite_note-114]

On 16 December 2025, the [UK government](77280613.md) considered a [green paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_paper) of new ways to fund the BBC, such as replacing the television licence with either advertising or a subscription model.[^cite_note-115]

On 25 March 2026, former Google executive [Matt Brittin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matt_Brittin) was appointed as the new Director-General.[^cite_note-116]

On 15 April 2026, the BBC announced cuts up to 2,000 jobs—its largest layoffs in over a decade—to reduce costs by about 10% (around £500 million) over the next two years.[^cite_note-117]

## Governance

Main article: [BBC independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_independence)

The BBC is a [chartered corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chartered_corporation), independent from direct government intervention, with its activities being overseen from April 2017 by the [BBC Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Board) and regulated by [Ofcom](411296.md).[^cite_note-Ofcom-118][^cite_note-Appointments-119] The chairman is [Samir Shah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samir_Shah).[^cite_note-ShahAppointment-111]

### Charter and Agreement

Main article: [BBC Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Charter)

The BBC is a [public broadcasting](178808.md) company that operates under a [royal charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_charter). The charter is the constitutional basis for the BBC, and sets out the BBC's object, mission and public purposes.[^cite_note-BBConCA-120] It emphasises [public service](1291782.md), (limited)[^cite_note-122] [editorial independence](6958049.md), prohibits advertising on domestic services and proclaims the BBC is to "seek to avoid adverse impacts on competition which are not necessary for the effective fulfilment of the Mission and the promotion of the Public Purposes".[^cite_note-123]

The charter additionally sets out that the BBC is subject to an additional agreement between it and the [Culture Secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_Secretary), and that its [operating licence](1768354.md) is to be set by Ofcom, an external [regulatory body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulatory_body). It used to be that the [Home Secretary](149104.md) be departmental to both the agreement as well as the licence, and regulatory duties fall to the [BBC Trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Trust), but the 2017 charter changed those 2007 arrangements.[^cite_note-bbchow-124]

The charter also outlines the BBC's governance and regulatory arrangements as a statutory corporation, including the role and composition of the BBC Board. The current charter took effect on 1 January 2017 and is set to expire on 31 December 2027; the agreement being coterminous.[^cite_note-BBConCA-120]

### BBC Board

Main article: [BBC Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Board)

The BBC Board was formed in April 2017. It replaced the previous governing body, the BBC Trust, which itself had replaced the [board of governors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_of_Governors_of_the_BBC) in 2007. The board sets the strategy for the corporation, assesses the performance of the BBC's executive board in delivering the BBC's services, and appoints the director-general. Ofcom is responsible for the regulation of the BBC. ### MI5 vetting policy

From as early as the 1930s until the 1990s, [MI5](100076.md), the British domestic intelligence service, engaged in the vetting of applicants for BBC jobs, a policy designed to keep out persons deemed subversive.[^cite_note-vetting-130][^cite_note-131] In 1933, BBC executive Colonel Alan Dawnay began to meet the head of MI5, Sir [Vernon Kell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernon_Kell), to informally trade information; from 1935, a formal arrangement was made whereby job applicants would be secretly vetted by MI5 for their political views (without their knowledge).[^cite_note-vetting-130] The BBC took up a policy of denying any suggestion by the press of such a relationship (the very existence of MI5 itself was not officially acknowledged until the [Security Service Act 1989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_Service_Act_1989)).[^cite_note-vetting-130]

This relationship garnered wider public attention after an article by [David Leigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Leigh_\(journalist\)) and Paul Lashmar appeared in *[The Observer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Observer)* in August 1985, revealing that MI5 had been vetting appointments, running operations from Room 105 in Broadcasting House.[^cite_note-vetting-130][^cite_note-132] At the time of the exposé, the operation was being run by [Ronnie Stonham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronnie_Stonham). A memo from 1984 revealed that blacklisted organisations included the far-left Communist Party of Great Britain, the [Socialist Workers Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Workers_Party_\(UK\)), the [Workers Revolutionary Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workers_Revolutionary_Party_\(UK\)) and the [Militant tendency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Militant_tendency), as well as the far-right [National Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Front_\(UK\)) and the [British National Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_National_Party). An association with one of these groups could result in a denial of a job application.[^cite_note-vetting-130]

In October 1985 the BBC announced that it would stop the vetting process, except for a few people in top roles, as well as those in charge of [Wartime Broadcasting Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wartime_Broadcasting_Service) emergency broadcasting (in the event of a nuclear war) and staff of the [BBC World Service](37627.md).[^cite_note-vetting-130] In 1990, following the Security Service Act 1989, vetting was further restricted to those responsible for wartime broadcasting and those with access to [secret government information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_clearance).[^cite_note-vetting-130] Michael Hodder, who succeeded Stonham, had the MI5 vetting files sent to the [BBC Archives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Archives) in [Reading, Berkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reading,_Berkshire).[^cite_note-vetting-130]

## Finances

The BBC has the second-largest budget of any UK-based broadcaster with an operating expenditure of £4.722 billion in 2013/14[^cite_note-2013-14-consolidated-income-statement-133] compared with £6.471 billion for [Sky UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_UK) in 2013/14[^cite_note-134] and £1.843 billion for ITV in the calendar year 2013.[^cite_note-135]

### Revenue

See also: [Television licence](48551.md) and [Television licensing in the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_licensing_in_the_United_Kingdom)

The principal means of funding the BBC is through the television licence, costing £169.50 per year per household as of April 2024.[^cite_note-136] Such a licence is required to legally receive broadcast television across the UK, the [Channel Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_Islands) and the [Isle of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_of_Man). No licence is required to own a television used for other means, or for sound only radio sets (though a separate licence for these was also required for non-TV households until 1971). The cost of a television licence is set by the government and enforced by the criminal law. A 50% discount is offered to people who are registered blind or severely visually impaired,[^cite_note-137] and the licence is completely free for any household containing anyone aged 75 or over and receiving pension credit.[^cite_note-TV_Licensing_Over_75_TV_Licences-138]

The BBC pursues its licence fee collection and enforcement under the trading name "TV Licensing". The revenue is collected privately by [Capita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capita), an outside agency, and is paid into the central government [Consolidated Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consolidated_Fund), a process defined in the [Communications Act 2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communications_Act_2003). Funds are then allocated by the [Department for Culture, Media and Sport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_for_Culture,_Media_and_Sport) (DCMS) and the [Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HM_Treasury) and approved by Parliament via legislation. Additional revenues are paid by the [Department for Work and Pensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_for_Work_and_Pensions) to compensate for subsidised licences for eligible over-75-year-olds.

The licence fee is classified as a tax,[^cite_note-Select_Committee_20060303-139] and its evasion is a criminal offence. Since 1991, collection and enforcement of the licence fee has been the responsibility of the BBC in its role as TV Licensing Authority.[^cite_note-KeyFacts-140] The BBC carries out surveillance (mostly using subcontractors) on properties (under the auspices of the [Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation_of_Investigatory_Powers_Act_2000)) and may conduct searches of a property using a search warrant.[^cite_note-BBC25AUG2006-141] According to TV Licensing, 216,900 people in the UK were caught watching TV without a licence in 2018/19.[^cite_note-142] Licence fee evasion makes up about one-tenth of all cases prosecuted in magistrates' courts, representing 0.3% of court time.[^cite_note-Perry_reference_BBC-143]

Income from commercial enterprises and from overseas sales of its catalogue of programmes has substantially increased over recent years,[^cite_note-144] with [BBC Worldwide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Worldwide) contributing some £243 million to the BBC's core public service business.[^cite_note-BBC_Annual_Report_2018/19-145]

According to the BBC's 2018/19 Annual Report, its total income was £4.889 billion a decrease from £5.062 billion in 2017/18 – partly owing to a 3.7% phased reduction in government funding for free over-75s TV licences,[^cite_note-BBC_Annual_Report_2018/19-145] which can be broken down as follows:

- £3.690 billion in licence fees collected from householders;

- £1.199 billion from the BBC's commercial businesses and government grants some of which will cease in 2020

But the licence fee has attracted criticism. It has been argued that in an age of multi-stream, multi-channel availability, an obligation to pay a licence fee is no longer appropriate. The BBC's use of private sector company Capita to send letters to premises not paying the licence fee has been criticised, especially as there have been cases where such letters have been sent to premises which are up to date with their payments, or do not require a TV licence.[^cite_note-146]

The BBC uses advertising campaigns to inform customers of the requirement to pay the licence fee. Past campaigns have been criticised by [Conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)) MP [Boris Johnson](19065069.md) and former MP [Ann Widdecombe](2422.md) for having a threatening nature and language used to scare evaders into paying.[^cite_note-147] Audio clips and television broadcasts are used to inform listeners of the BBC's comprehensive database.[^cite_note-148] There are a number of pressure groups campaigning on the issue of the licence fee.[^cite_note-149]

In 2023, about half a million UK households cancelled their TV licence, driven by shifting viewing habits and financial pressures. As a result, the BBC saw a decline in revenue, with the number of households paying the licence fee dropping to 23.9 million.[^cite_note-150]

The majority of the BBC's commercial output comes from its commercial arm BBC Worldwide which sell programmes abroad and exploit key brands for merchandise. Of their 2012/13 sales, 27% were centred on the five key "superbrands" of *[Doctor Who](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Who)*, *[Top Gear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top_Gear_\(2002_TV_series\))*, *[Strictly Come Dancing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strictly_Come_Dancing)* (known as *[Dancing with the Stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dancing_with_the_Stars)* internationally), the BBC's archive of natural history programming (collected under the umbrella of [BBC Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Earth)) and the (now sold) travel guide brand [Lonely Planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lonely_Planet).[^cite_note-BBC_Worldwide_2012/13_review-151]

In April 2026, the BBC announced that they would be cutting between 1,800 and 2,000 jobs, citing a need to save £500 million over the coming two years.[^cite_note-152]

Broadcasting House in [Portland Place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portland_Place), central London, is the official headquarters of the BBC. It is home to the BBC's national and international radio networks ([BBC Radio 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_1), [BBC Radio 1Xtra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_1Xtra), [BBC Asian Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Asian_Network), [BBC Radio 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_2), [BBC Radio 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_3), [BBC Radio 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_4), [BBC Radio 4 Extra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_4_Extra), [BBC Radio 6 Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_6_Music) and the [BBC World Service](37627.md)), with the exception of [BBC Radio 5 Live](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_5_Live) and [BBC Radio 5 Sports Extra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_5_Sports_Extra) which have broadcast from [MediaCityUK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MediaCityUK) in [Salford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salford) since 2011. It is also the home of [BBC News](1139893.md), which relocated to the building from BBC Television Centre in 2013. On the front of the building are statues of [Prospero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prospero) and [Ariel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariel_\(The_Tempest\)), characters from [William Shakespeare](32897.md)'s play *[The Tempest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tempest)*, sculpted by [Eric Gill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Gill). Renovation of Broadcasting House began in 2002, and was completed in 2012.[^cite_note-153]

Until it closed at the end of March 2013, [BBC Television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Television) was based at [Television Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_Centre,_London), a purpose-built television facility opened in 1960 located in [White City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_City,_London), four miles (6 km) west of central London.[^cite_note-154] This facility was host to a number of famous guests and programmes through the years, and its name and image is familiar with many British citizens. Nearby, the [White City Place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_City_Place) complex contains numerous programme offices, housed in Centre House, the Media Centre and Broadcast Centre. It is in this area around [Shepherd's Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shepherd's_Bush) that the majority of BBC employees worked.

As part of a major reorganisation of BBC property, the entire BBC News operation relocated from the News Centre at BBC Television Centre to the refurbished Broadcasting House to create what is being described as "one of the world's largest live broadcast centres".[^cite_note-155] The [BBC News Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_News_Channel) and [BBC News International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_News_\(international_TV_channel\)) relocated to the premises in early 2013.[^cite_note-156] Broadcasting House is now also home to most of the BBC's national radio stations, and the [BBC World Service](37627.md). The major part of this plan involved the demolition of the two post-war extensions to the building and construction of an extension[^cite_note-157] designed by [Sir Richard MacCormac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Richard_MacCormac) of [MJP Architects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MJP_Architects). This move concentrated the BBC's London operations, allowing them to sell Television Centre.[^cite_note-BBC_News_Article_-_TV_Centre_up_for_sale-158]

In addition to the scheme above, the BBC is in the process of making and producing more programmes outside London, involving production centres such as [Belfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Northern_Ireland), [Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Cymru_Wales), [Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Scotland), Newcastle and, most notably, in [Greater Manchester](79819.md) as part of the "BBC North Project" scheme where several major departments, including [BBC North West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_North_West), [BBC Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Manchester), [BBC Sport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Sport), [BBC Children's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBBC), [CBeebies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBeebies), Radio 5 Live, [BBC Radio 5 Sports Extra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_5_Sports_Extra), *[BBC Breakfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Breakfast)*, [BBC Learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Learning) and the [BBC Philharmonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Philharmonic) have all moved from their previous locations in either London or [New Broadcasting House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Broadcasting_House,_Manchester), Manchester to the new 200-acre (80ha) [MediaCityUK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MediaCityUK) production facilities in [Salford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Salford), that form part of the large [BBC North Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_North) division and will therefore become the biggest staffing operation outside London.[^cite_note-159][^cite_note-BBC_North_Website-160]



A [Dalek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalek) prop used in *[Doctor Who](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Who)* at [BBC Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Birmingham)

As well as the two main sites in London (Broadcasting House and White City), there are seven other important BBC production centres in the UK, mainly specialising in different productions. [Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Cymru_Wales_New_Broadcasting_House) is home to [BBC Cymru Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Cymru_Wales), which specialises in drama production. Open since 2012, and containing 7 new studios, Roath Lock[^cite_note-161] is notable as the home of productions such as *[Doctor Who](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Who)* and *[Casualty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casualty_\(TV_series\))*. [Broadcasting House Belfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcasting_House,_Belfast), home to [BBC Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Northern_Ireland), specialises in original drama and comedy, and has taken part in many co-productions with independent companies and notably with [RTÉ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89) in the Republic of Ireland. [BBC Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Scotland), based in [Pacific Quay, Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Pacific_Quay) is a large producer of programmes for the network, including several quiz shows. In England, the larger regions also produce some programming.

Previously the largest hub of BBC programming from the [regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_English_Regions) had been BBC North West. At present they produce all religious and ethical programmes on the BBC, as well as other programmes such as *[A Question of Sport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Question_of_Sport)*. However, this is to be merged and expanded under the [BBC North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_North) project, which involved the region moving from [New Broadcasting House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Broadcasting_House,_Manchester), Manchester, to [MediaCityUK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MediaCityUK). [BBC Midlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_West_Midlands), based at [Mailbox Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mailbox_Birmingham), also produces drama and contains the headquarters for the English regions and the BBC's daytime output. Other production centres include [Broadcasting House Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcasting_House_Bristol), home of [BBC West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_West) and famously the [BBC Studios Natural History Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Studios_Natural_History_Unit) and to a lesser extent, [Quarry Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarry_Hill,_Leeds) in Leeds, home of [BBC Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Yorkshire). There are also many smaller local and regional studios throughout the UK, operating the BBC regional television services and the [BBC Local Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Local_Radio) stations.

The BBC also operates several news gathering centres in various locations around the world, which provide news coverage of that region to the national and international news operations.

### Information technology service management

In 2004 the BBC contracted out its former BBC Technology division to the German engineering and electronics company [Siemens IT Solutions and Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens_IT_Solutions_and_Services) (SIS), outsourcing its [IT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IT), [telephony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephony) and [broadcast technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_technology) systems.[^cite_note-bbc-2204-74] When Atos Origin acquired the SIS division from [Siemens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens) in December 2010 for €850 million (£720m),[^cite_note-cw-siemens-162] the BBC support contract also passed to [Atos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atos), and in July 2011, the BBC announced to staff that its technology support would become an Atos service.[^cite_note-atos-ariel-75] Siemens staff working on the BBC contract were transferred to Atos; the BBC's [Information Technology systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_technology) are now managed by Atos.[^cite_note-163] In 2011, the BBC's chief financial officer [Zarin Patel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zarin_Patel) stated to the [House of Commons](13828.md) [Public Accounts Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Accounts_Committee_\(United_Kingdom\)) that, following criticism of the BBC's management of major IT projects with Siemens (such as the [Digital Media Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_Media_Initiative)), the BBC partnership with Atos would be instrumental in achieving cost savings of about £64 million as part of the BBC's "Delivering Quality First" programme.[^cite_note-publicaccounts-164] In 2012, the BBC's then-[chief technology officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_technology_officer) John Linwood, expressed confidence in service improvements to the BBC's technology provision brought about by Atos. He also stated that supplier accountability had been strengthened following some high-profile technology failures which had taken place during the partnership with Siemens.[^cite_note-cw-linwood-165]

### BBC Genome Project

Main article: [BBC Genome Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Genome_Project)

In December 2012 the BBC completed a digitisation exercise, scanning the listings of all BBC programmes from an entire run of about 4,500 copies of the *[Radio Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Times)* magazine from the first, 1923, issue to 2009 (later listings already being held electronically), the "BBC Genome project", with a view to creating an online database of its programme output. An earlier ten months of listings are to be obtained from other sources. They identified about five million programmes, involving 8.5 million actors, presenters, writers and technical staff.[^cite_note-Kelion-177] The Genome project was opened to public access on 15 October 2014, with corrections to OCR errors and changes to advertised schedules being [crowdsourced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crowdsourced).[^cite_note-BBC-RTANL-178]

### Radio



Weekly reach of the BBC's national radio stations, on both analogue and digital (2012)

[^cite_note-BBC_Annual_Report_2011-12_reach_pages-167]

Main articles: [BBC Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio) and [BBC Local Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Local_Radio)

The BBC has ten radio stations serving the whole of the UK, a further seven stations in the "national regions" ([Wales](69894.md), [Scotland](26994.md), and [Northern Ireland](21265.md)), and 39 other local stations serving defined areas of England. Of the ten national stations, five are major stations and are available on [FM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FM_broadcasting) or [AM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AM_broadcasting) as well as on [DAB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_radio_in_the_United_Kingdom) and online. These are [BBC Radio 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_1), offering new contemporary music and popular styles and being notable for its chart show; [BBC Radio 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_2), playing adult contemporary, country and soul music amongst many other genres; [BBC Radio 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_3), presenting classical and jazz music together with some spoken-word programming of a cultural nature in the evenings; [BBC Radio 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_4), focusing on news, factual and other speech-based programming, including drama and comedy; and [BBC Radio 5 Live](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_5_Live), broadcasting 24-hour news, sport and talk programming.[^cite_note-179]



Swedish actress [Ingrid Bergman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingrid_Bergman) being interviewed on BBC Radio in October 1954

In addition to these five stations, the BBC runs a further five stations that broadcast on DAB and online only. These stations supplement and expand on the big five stations, and were launched in 2002. [BBC Radio 1Xtra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_1Xtra) sisters Radio 1, and broadcasts new Black music and urban tracks. [BBC Radio 5 Sports Extra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_5_Sports_Extra) sisters 5 Live and offers extra sport analysis, including broadcasting sports that previously were not covered. [BBC Radio 6 Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_6_Music) offers alternative music genres and is notable as a platform for new artists. BBC Radio 7, later renamed [BBC Radio 4 Extra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_4_Extra), provides archive drama, comedy and children's programming. The final station is the [BBC Asian Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Asian_Network), providing music, talk and news to this section of the community. This station evolved out of Local radio stations serving certain areas, and as such this station is available on [medium wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium_wave) frequency in some areas of the Midlands.

As well as the national stations, the BBC also provides 40 [BBC Local Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Local_Radio) stations in England and the Channel Islands, each named for and covering a particular city and its surrounding area (e.g. [BBC Radio Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_Bristol)), county or region (e.g. [BBC Three Counties Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Three_Counties_Radio)), or geographical area (e.g. [BBC Radio Solent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_Solent) covering the central south coast). A further six stations broadcast in what the BBC terms "the national regions": Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland. These are [BBC Radio Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_Wales) (in English), [BBC Radio Cymru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_Cymru) (in [Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language)), [BBC Radio Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_Scotland) (in English), [BBC Radio nan Gàidheal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_nan_G%C3%A0idheal) (in [Scottish Gaelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Gaelic)), [BBC Radio Ulster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_Ulster), and [BBC Radio Foyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_Foyle), the latter being an opt-out station from Radio Ulster for the north-west of Northern Ireland.

The BBC's UK national channels are also broadcast in the Channel Islands and the Isle of Man (although these [Crown Dependencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_Dependencies) are outside the UK), and in the former there are two local stations – [BBC Radio Guernsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_Guernsey) and [BBC Radio Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_Jersey). There is no BBC local radio station, however, in the Isle of Man, partly because the island has long been served by the popular independent commercial station, [Manx Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manx_Radio), which predates the existence of BBC Local Radio. BBC services in the dependencies are financed from [television licence](48551.md) fees which are set at the same level as those payable in the UK, although collected locally. This is the subject of some controversy in the Isle of Man since, as well as having no BBC Local Radio service, the island also lacks a local television news service analogous to that provided by [BBC Channel Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Spotlight_\(BBC_South_West_TV_programme\)#BBC_Channel_Islands).[^cite_note-180]



[BBC World Service](37627.md), with [Jonathan Dimbleby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Dimbleby) (middle) broadcasting from Budapest, Hungary in 2016

For a worldwide audience the [BBC World Service](37627.md) provides news, current affairs and information in more than 40 languages, including English, around the world, and is available in over 150 capital cities, making it the world's largest external broadcaster in terms of reception area, language selection and audience reach.[^cite_note-181][^cite_note-182] It is broadcast worldwide on [shortwave radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortwave_radio), DAB and online and has an estimated weekly audience of 192 million, and its websites have an audience of 38 million people per week.[^cite_note-183] Since 2005, it is also available on DAB in the UK, a step not taken before, due to the way it is funded. Following the Government's spending review in 2011, the service was funded for the first time through the Licence fee.[^cite_note-BBC_World_Service_Annual_Review_2010-184][^cite_note-World_Service_Finance_details-185] In recent years, some services of the World Service have been reduced: the [Thai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thai_language) service ended in 2006,[^cite_note-186] as did the Eastern European languages. Resources were diverted instead into the new [BBC News Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_News_Arabic).[^cite_note-187]

Historically the BBC was the only legal radio broadcaster based in the UK mainland until 1967, when [University Radio York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_Radio_York), then under the name *Radio York*, was launched as the first, and now oldest, legal independent radio station in the country. However, the BBC did not enjoy a complete monopoly before this, as several Continental stations, such as [Radio Luxembourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Luxembourg), had broadcast programmes in English to Britain since the 1930s and the Isle of Man-based [Manx Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manx_Radio) began in 1964. Today, despite the advent of [commercial broadcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_broadcasting), BBC radio stations remain among the most listened-to in the country. Radio 2 has the largest audience share (up to 16.8% in 2011–12) and Radios 1 and 4 ranked second and third in terms of weekly [reach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reach_\(advertising\)).[^cite_note-188]

BBC programming is also available to other services and in other countries. Since 1943, the BBC has provided radio programming to the [British Forces Broadcasting Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Forces_Broadcasting_Service), which broadcasts in countries where British troops are stationed. BBC Radio 1 is also carried in Canada on [SiriusXM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SiriusXM) (online streaming only). The BBC is a patron of the [Radio Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Academy), a registered UK charity that promotes excellence in broadcasting.[^cite_note-189]

### News

Main article: [BBC News](1139893.md)



The new newsroom in Broadcasting House, central London, officially opened by [the Queen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_II) in 2013

BBC News is the largest broadcast news gathering operation in the world,[^cite_note-About_BBC_News_Website-190] providing services to BBC domestic radio as well as television networks such as the [BBC News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_News_\(British_TV_channel\)), BBC Parliament and [BBC News International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_News_\(international_TV_channel\)). In addition to this, news stories are available on the BBC Red Button service and [BBC News Online](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_News_Online). In addition to this, the BBC has been developing new ways to access BBC News and as a result, has launched the service on BBC Mobile, making it accessible to mobile phones and PDAs, as well as developing alerts by email, on digital television, and on computers through a [desktop alert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Alerts).

Ratings figures suggest that during major incidents such as the [7 July 2005 London bombings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7_July_2005_London_bombings) or royal events, the UK audience overwhelmingly turns to the BBC's coverage as opposed to its commercial rivals.[^cite_note-191] On 7 July 2005, the day there were a series of coordinated bomb blasts on London's public transport system, the BBC Online website recorded an all time [bandwidth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandwidth_\(computing\)) peak of 11 [Gb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit#Multiple_bits)/s at 12.00 on 7 July. BBC News received some 1 billion total hits on the day of the event (including all images, text, and [HTML](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTML)), serving some 5.5 [terabytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terabytes) of data. At peak times during the day, there were 40,000-page requests per second for the BBC News website. The previous day's announcement of the [2012 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics) being awarded to London caused a peak of about 5 Gbit/s. The previous all-time high at BBC Online was caused by the announcement of the [Michael Jackson](14995351.md) verdict, which used 7.2 Gbit/s.[^cite_note-192]

### Internet

Main article: [BBC Online](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Online)

The BBC's online presence includes a comprehensive [news website](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_News_Online) and archive. The BBC's first official online service was the BBC Networking Club, which was launched on 11 May 1994. The service was subsequently relaunched as BBC Online in 1997, before being renamed BBCi, then bbc.co.uk, before it was rebranded back as BBC Online. The website is funded by the Licence fee, but uses [GeoIP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geotargeting) technology, allowing advertisements to be carried on the site when viewed outside the UK.[^cite_note-193] The BBC claims the site to be "Europe's most popular content-based site"[^cite_note-194] and states that 13.2 million people in the UK visit the site's more than two million pages each day.[^cite_note-195]

The centre of the website is the Homepage, which features a modular layout. Users can choose which modules, and which information, is displayed on their homepage, allowing the user to customise it. This system was first launched in December 2007, becoming permanent in February 2008, and has undergone a few aesthetical changes since then.[^cite_note-196] The home page then has links to other micro-sites, such as [BBC News Online](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_News_Online), [Sport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Sport), [Weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Weather), TV, and Radio. As part of the site, every programme on BBC Television or Radio is given its own page, with bigger programmes getting their own micro-site, and as a result it is often common for viewers and listeners to be told [URLs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/URL) for the programme website.



2008 advertisement for [BBC iPlayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_iPlayer) at Old Street, London

Another large part of the site also allows users to watch and listen to most Television and Radio output live and for seven days after broadcast using the [BBC iPlayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_iPlayer) platform, which launched on 27 July 2007, and initially used [peer-to-peer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer-to-peer) and [DRM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_rights_management) technology to deliver both radio and TV content of the last seven days for offline use for up to 30 days, since then video is now streamed directly. Also, through participation in the [Creative Archive Licence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creative_Archive_Licence) group, bbc.co.uk allowed legal downloads of selected archive material via the internet.[^cite_note-197]

The BBC has often included learning as part of its online service, running services such as [BBC Jam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Jam), [Learning Zone Class Clips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Learning_Zone) and also runs services such as [BBC WebWise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_WebWise) and First Click which are designed to teach people how to use the internet. BBC Jam was a free online service, delivered through broadband and narrowband connections, providing high-quality interactive resources designed to stimulate learning at home and at school. Initial content was made available in January 2006; however, BBC Jam was suspended on 20 March 2007 due to allegations made to the [European Commission](9974.md) that it was damaging the interests of the commercial sector of the industry.[^cite_note-198]

In recent years some major on-line companies and politicians have complained that BBC Online receives too much funding from the television licence, meaning that other websites are unable to compete with the vast amount of advertising-free on-line content available on BBC Online.[^cite_note-199] Some have proposed that the amount of licence fee money spent on BBC Online should be reduced—either being replaced with funding from advertisements or subscriptions, or a reduction in the amount of content available on the site.[^cite_note-200] In response to this the BBC carried out an investigation, and has now set in motion a plan to change the way it provides its online services. BBC Online will now attempt to fill in gaps in the market, and will guide users to other websites for currently existing market provision. (For example, instead of providing local events information and timetables, users will be guided to outside websites already providing that information.) Part of this plan included the BBC closing some of its websites, and rediverting money to redevelop other parts.[^cite_note-201][^cite_note-202]

On 26 February 2010 *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times)* claimed that [Mark Thompson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Thompson_\(media_executive\)), Director General of the BBC, proposed that the BBC's web output should be cut by 50%, with online staff numbers and budgets reduced by 25% in a bid to scale back BBC operations and allow commercial rivals more room.[^cite_note-203] On 2 March 2010, the BBC reported that it would cut its website spending by 25% and close BBC 6 Music and Asian Network, as part of Mark Thompson's plans to make "a smaller, fitter BBC for the digital age".[^cite_note-204][^cite_note-205]

### Interactive television

Main article: [BBC Red Button](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Red_Button)

BBC Red Button is the brand name for the BBC's [interactive television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interactive_television) services, which are available through [Freeview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeview_\(UK\)) (digital terrestrial), as well as [Freesat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freesat), [Sky UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_UK) (satellite), and [Virgin Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_Media) (cable). Unlike Ceefax, the service's analogue counterpart, BBC Red Button is able to display full-colour graphics, photographs, and video, as well as programmes and can be accessed from any BBC channel. The service carries News, Weather and Sport 24 hours a day, but also provides extra features related to programmes specific at that time. Examples include viewers to play along at home to gameshows, to give, voice and vote on opinions to issues, as used alongside programmes such as *[Question Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Question_Time_\(TV_programme\))*. At some points in the year, when multiple sporting events occur, some coverage of less mainstream sports or games are frequently placed on the Red Button for viewers to watch. Frequently, other features are added unrelated to programmes being broadcast at that time, such as the broadcast of the *[Doctor Who](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Who)* animated episode *[Dreamland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dreamland_\(Doctor_Who\))* in November 2009.[^cite_note-206]

### Music



The [BBC Big Band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Big_Band)

The BBC employs 5 staff orchestras, a professional choir, and supports two amateur choruses, based in BBC venues across the UK;[^cite_note-207] the [BBC Symphony Orchestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Symphony_Orchestra), the [BBC Singers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Singers) and [BBC Symphony Chorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Symphony_Chorus) based in London, the [BBC Scottish Symphony Orchestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Scottish_Symphony_Orchestra) in Glasgow, the [BBC Philharmonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Philharmonic) in Salford, the [BBC Concert Orchestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Concert_Orchestra) based in Watford, and the [BBC National Orchestra of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_National_Orchestra_of_Wales) in Cardiff. It also buys a selected number of broadcasts from the [Ulster Orchestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Orchestra) in Belfast and the [BBC Big Band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Big_Band).



[BBC Proms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Proms) at the [Royal Albert Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Albert_Hall)

The [BBC Proms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Proms) have been produced by the BBC every year since 1927, stepping in to fund the popular eight-week summer classical music festival when music publishers Chappell and Co withdrew their support.[^cite_note-208] In 1930, the newly formed BBC Symphony Orchestra gave all 49 Proms, and have performed at every Last Night of the Proms since then. The Proms have been held at the [Royal Albert Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Albert_Hall) since 1941, and the BBC's orchestras and choirs are the backbone of the festival, giving about 40% to 50% of all performances each season.[^cite_note-209]

Many famous musicians of every genre have played at the BBC, such as [The Beatles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beatles) (*[Live at the BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_at_the_BBC_\(Beatles_album\))* is one of their many albums). The BBC is also responsible for the broadcast of the [Glastonbury Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glastonbury_Festival) and the [Reading and Leeds Festivals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reading_and_Leeds_Festivals), and it has participated in the [Eurovision Song Contest](9954.md) representing the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_in_the_Eurovision_Song_Contest) for over 60 years.[^cite_note-210] The BBC also operates the division of BBC Audiobooks sometimes found in association with Chivers Audiobooks.

Some of the [BBC Micro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Micro) team in 2008. Developed by [Acorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acorn_Computers), the Micro computer dominated the educational computer market in the UK during the 1980s.

### Ceefax

Main article: [Ceefax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceefax)



[Ceefax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceefax) page (showing football news) in 2009. A teletext service provided by the BBC from 1974 to 2012, it was a popular method to check score updates.

[^cite_note-216]

The BBC provided the world's first [teletext](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teletext) service called Ceefax (near-homophonous with "See Facts") from 23 September 1974 until 23 October 2012 on the BBC1 analogue channel, then later on BBC2.[^cite_note-217] It showed informational pages, such as news, sport, and the weather. From New Year's Eve, 1974, ITV's [Oracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ORACLE_\(teletext\)) tried to compete with Ceefax. Oracle closed on New Year's Eve, 1992. During its lifetime, Ceefax attracted millions of viewers, right up until 2012, prior to the [digital switchover in the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_terrestrial_television_in_the_United_Kingdom#Digital_switchover). Since then, the BBC's [Red Button Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Red_Button) has provided a digital information system that replaced Ceefax.[^cite_note-218]

### BritBox

Main article: [BritBox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BritBox)

In 2016 the BBC, in partnership with fellow UK broadcasters [ITV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITV_\(TV_network\)) and [Channel 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_4) (the latter later withdrew from the project), set up 'project kangaroo' to develop an international online streaming service to rival services such as Netflix and Hulu.[^cite_note-Variety-219][^cite_note-220] During the development stages 'Britflix' was touted as a potential name. However, the service eventually launched as [BritBox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BritBox) in March 2017. The online platform shows a catalogue of classic BBC and ITV shows, as well as making a number of programmes available shortly after their UK broadcast. As of 2021, BritBox is available in the UK, the US, Canada, Australia, and, more recently, South Africa, with the potential availability for new markets in the future.[^cite_note-221][^cite_note-222][^cite_note-223]

## Commercial activities

Main article: [BBC Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Studios)



[BBC Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Studios) is headquartered at [Television Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_Centre,_London) (BBC Television's former headquarters) in west London.

BBC Studios is the wholly owned commercial subsidiary of the BBC, responsible for the commercial exploitation of BBC programmes and other properties, including a number of television stations throughout the world.[^cite_note-224] It was formed in 2018 after the merger of the BBC's commercial production arm and the BBC's commercial international distribution arm, [BBC Worldwide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Worldwide), with the latter formed in 1995 following the restructuring of its predecessor, BBC Enterprises. Prior to this, the selling of BBC television programmes was at first handled in 1958 with the establishment of a business manager post.[^cite_note-BBC_70_years_book-225] This expanded until the establishment of the Television Promotions (renamed Television Enterprises) department in 1960 under a general manager.[^cite_note-BBC_70_years_book-225]

The company owns and administers a number of commercial stations around the world operating in a number of territories and on a number of different platforms. These include [BBC UKTV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_UKTV) for the Australasia region, and formerly [BBC America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_America) (now fully owned by [AMC Networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AMC_Networks)). The company airs two channels aimed at children, an international [CBeebies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBeebies) channel and [BBC Kids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Kids), a joint venture with [Knowledge Network Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_Network_Corporation). The company also runs [BBC Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Earth_\(international_TV_channel\)), which distributes the BBC's natural history content to countries outside the UK, and [BBC Lifestyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Lifestyle), broadcasting programmes based on themes of Food, Style and Wellbeing.[^cite_note-226] In addition to this, BBC Studios ran an international version of the channel [BBC HD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_HD_\(international\)).



The BBC's East Africa bureau in Nairobi, Kenya. It is the broadcaster's biggest bureau outside the UK.

[^cite_note-227]

BBC Studios also distributes the 24-hour international news channel [BBC News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_News_\(international_TV_channel\)). The station is separate from BBC Studios to maintain the station's neutral point of view, but is distributed by BBC Studios. The channel itself is the oldest surviving entity of its kind, and has 50 foreign news bureaus and correspondents in nearly all countries in the world.[^cite_note-Henery-2010-228] As officially surveyed, it is available to more than 294 million households, significantly more than [CNN](62028.md)'s estimated 200 million.[^cite_note-Henery-2010-228] In addition to these international channels, BBC Studios also owns the [UKTV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UKTV) network of seven channels. These channels contain BBC archive programming to be rebroadcast on their respective channels: [Alibi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alibi_\(TV_channel\)), crime dramas; [Dave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dave_\(TV_channel\)) (slogan: "The Home of Witty Banter"); [Drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama_\(British_TV_channel\)), drama, launched in 2013; [Eden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eden_\(British_TV_channel\)), nature; [Gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_\(British_TV_channel\)), comedy; [W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W_\(British_TV_channel\)), Entertainment; and [Yesterday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yesterday_\(TV_channel\)), history programming.

In addition to these channels, many BBC programmes are sold via BBC Studios to foreign television stations with comedy, documentaries, crime dramas (such as *[Luther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luther_\(TV_series\))* and *[Peaky Blinders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peaky_Blinders_\(TV_series\))*) and [historical drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_drama) productions being the most popular. The BBC's most successful reality television show format, *[Strictly Come Dancing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strictly_Come_Dancing)*—under the title *Dancing with the Stars*—has been exported to 60 other countries.[^cite_note-229][^cite_note-230] Shows commissioned and distributed by the BBC include the *[Wallace & Gromit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallace_&_Gromit)* animated comedy short films *[The Wrong Trousers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wrong_Trousers)* and *[A Close Shave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Close_Shave)*.[^cite_note-231] BBC television news also appears nightly on many [PBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PBS) stations in the US, as do reruns of BBC programmes such as *[EastEnders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EastEnders)*, and in New Zealand on [TVNZ 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TVNZ_1).[^cite_note-232]



The 1931 Christmas issue of the *[Radio Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Times)*.

In addition to programming, BBC Studios produces material to accompany programmes. The company maintained the publishing arm of the BBC, BBC Magazines, which published the *[Radio Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Times)*; first published by the BBC on 28 September 1923, it is the world's first broadcast listings magazine.[^cite_note-233] *Radio Times* covers all British television and radio programming schedules, and the 1988 Christmas edition sold 11,220,666 copies, which the *[Guinness World Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness_World_Records)* certified as the biggest-selling edition of any British magazine in history.[^cite_note-234] Other magazines that support BBC programming include *[BBC Top Gear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top_Gear_\(magazine\))*, *[BBC Good Food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Good_Food)*, *[BBC Sky at Night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sky_at_Night)*, *[BBC History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_History)*, *[BBC Wildlife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Wildlife)* and *[BBC Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Music_Magazine)*. BBC Magazines was sold to Exponent Private Equity in 2011, which merged it with Origin Publishing (previously owned by BBC Worldwide between 2004 and 2006) to form [Immediate Media Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immediate_Media_Company).[^cite_note-235]

BBC Studios also publishes books, to accompany programmes such as *Sherlock* and *Doctor Who* under the [BBC Books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Books) brand, a publishing imprint majority owned by [Random House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_House). Soundtrack albums, singles (which include two UK number one singles from BBC children's shows, "[Teletubbies say "Eh-oh!"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teletubbies_say_%22Eh-oh!%22)" from *[Teletubbies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teletubbies)* and "[Can We Fix It?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Can_We_Fix_It%3F)" from *[Bob the Builder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_the_Builder)*), talking books and sections of radio broadcasts are also sold under the brand [BBC Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Records), with DVDs also being sold and licensed in large quantities to consumers both in the UK and abroad under the [BBC Studios Home Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Studios_Home_Entertainment) brand.[^cite_note-236] Archive programming and classical music recordings are sold under the brand BBC Legends.[^cite_note-237]

## Cultural significance



[Blue plaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_plaque) at [Alexandra Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandra_Palace), commemorating the launch of the world's first high-definition television service, [BBC Television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Television), in 1936

Until the development, popularisation, and domination of television, radio was the broadcast medium upon which people in the United Kingdom relied. It "reached into every home in the land, and simultaneously united the nation, an important factor during the Second World War".[^cite_note-Perry-238] The BBC introduced the world's first "high-definition" 405-line television service in 1936. It suspended its television service during the Second World War and until 1946, but remained the only television broadcaster in the UK until 1955, when Independent Television (ITV) began operating.[^cite_note-Perry16-239] This heralded the transformation of television into a popular and dominant medium. Nevertheless, "throughout the 1950s radio still remained the dominant source of broadcast comedy".[^cite_note-Perry16-239] Further, the BBC was the only legal radio broadcaster until 1968 (when [University Radio York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_Radio_York) obtained its first licence).[^cite_note-240]



The nature documentaries of [David Attenborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Attenborough), such as *[The Blue Planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Blue_Planet)*, *[Planet Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet_Earth_\(2006_TV_series\))* and *[Life on Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_on_Earth_\(TV_series\))*, are produced by the [BBC Studios Natural History Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Studios_Natural_History_Unit), the largest wildlife documentary production house in the world.

[^cite_note-241]

Despite the advent of commercial television and radio, with competition from ITV, Channel 4 and [Sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_UK), the BBC has remained one of the main elements in British popular culture through its obligation to produce TV and radio programmes for mass audiences.[^cite_note-Public_service-242][^cite_note-243] However, the arrival of BBC2 allowed the BBC also to make programmes for minority interests in drama, documentaries, current affairs, entertainment, and sport. Examples cited include the television series *[Civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilisation_\(TV_series\))*, *[Doctor Who](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Who)*, *[I, Claudius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I,_Claudius_\(TV_series\))*, *[Monty Python's Flying Circus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monty_Python's_Flying_Circus)*, *[Pot Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pot_Black)*, and *[Tonight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonight_\(1957_TV_programme\))*, but other examples can be given in each of these fields as shown by the BBC's entries in the [British Film Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Film_Institute)'s 2000 list of the [BFI TV 100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BFI_TV_100), with the BBC's 1970s sitcom *[Fawlty Towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fawlty_Towers)* (featuring [John Cleese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cleese) as [Basil Fawlty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basil_Fawlty)) topping the list.[^cite_note-tv100-244] Described as television's first "watercooler moment" by *[The Telegraph](23797577.md)*, the BBC's 1954 [adaptation of *Nineteen Eighty-Four*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineteen_Eighty-Four_\(British_TV_programme\)) made a dramatic impact on a fledgling television audience who were horrified by the graphic depiction of a totalitarian regime controlling the population.[^cite_note-245][^cite_note-246] Making his BBC debut in 1949, [Benny Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benny_Hill) with *[The Benny Hill Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Benny_Hill_Show)* became the first British comedian to become famous via television.[^cite_note-247] Popular comedy duos on the BBC include [Morecambe and Wise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morecambe_and_Wise) (whose show debuted in 1968) and [The Two Ronnies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Two_Ronnies) (debuting in 1971).[^cite_note-248] Black comedy sitcom *[Blackadder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackadder)*, starring [Rowan Atkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rowan_Atkinson) as the title character, ran for four series between 1983 and 1989.[^cite_note-249]

*[Top of the Pops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top_of_the_Pops)*, the world's longest-running weekly music show, first aired in January 1964, the Rolling Stones being the first group to perform on it.[^cite_note-250] On air since 22 August 1964, *[Match of the Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Match_of_the_Day)* is broadcast on Saturday nights during the [Premier League](11250.md) season.[^cite_note-251] Some BBC shows have had a direct impact on society. For example, *[The Great British Bake Off](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_British_Bake_Off)* is credited with reinvigorating interest in baking throughout the UK, with stores reporting sharp rises in sales of baking ingredients and accessories.[^cite_note-252] The export of BBC programmes through services like the [BBC World Service](37627.md) and [BBC News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_News_\(international_TV_channel\)), as well as through the channels operated by [BBC Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Studios), means that audiences can consume BBC productions worldwide. Long-running BBC shows include: *[Desert Island Discs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert_Island_Discs)*, broadcast on radio since 1942,[^cite_note-253] *[Sports Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_Report)*, broadcast on radio from 5 pm on Saturday evenings during the football season since January 1948, and featuring the same theme tune by [Hubert Bath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubert_Bath), is the world's longest-running sports radio programme,[^cite_note-254][^cite_note-255] *[The Archers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Archers)*, broadcast on radio since 1951, is the world's longest-running drama,[^cite_note-256] and *[Panorama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panorama_\(British_TV_programme\))*, broadcast on BBC television since 1953, is the world's longest-running news television programme.[^cite_note-257] [Douglas Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Adams)' 1978 Radio 4 sci-fi comedy series *[The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hitchhiker's_Guide_to_the_Galaxy_\(radio_series\))*, which spawned a [media franchise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hitchhiker's_Guide_to_the_Galaxy), was the first radio comedy programme to be produced in stereo, and was innovative in its use of music and sound effects.[^cite_note-258] The BBC's broadcast of [Wimbledon](91195.md) is the longest-running partnership in sports broadcasting history, starting with radio in 1927 and television in 1937.[^cite_note-259]



[BAFTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Academy_of_Film_and_Television_Arts) (British Academy of Film and Television Arts) mask and the BBC logo. The BBC broadcasts BAFTA's [film](150200.md) and [television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Academy_Television_Awards) award ceremonies.

The [British Academy Film Awards](150200.md) (BAFTAs) was first broadcast on the BBC in 1956, with [Vivien Leigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivien_Leigh) as the host.[^cite_note-260] The television equivalent, the [British Academy Television Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Academy_Television_Awards), has been screened exclusively on the BBC since a [2007 awards ceremony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_British_Academy_Television_Awards) that included wins for [Jim Broadbent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Broadbent) (Best actor) and [Ricky Gervais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricky_Gervais) (Best comedy performance).[^cite_note-261]

The term "BBC English" was used as an alternative name for [Received Pronunciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Received_Pronunciation), and the *English Pronouncing Dictionary* uses the term "BBC Pronunciation" to label its recommendations.[^cite_note-262] However, the BBC itself now makes more use of [regional accents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_accents_of_English) to reflect the diversity of the UK, while continuing to expect clarity and fluency of its presenters.[^cite_note-accents-263] From its "starchy" beginnings, the BBC has also become more inclusive, and now attempts to accommodate the interests of all strata of society and all minorities, because they all pay the licence fee.[^cite_note-diversityPolicy-264]

### Colloquial terms

Older domestic UK audiences often refer to the BBC as "the Beeb", a nickname coined by [Peter Sellers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Sellers) during a [1972 reunion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_Goon_Show_of_All) of the 1950s BBC radio comedy *[The Goon Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Goon_Show)*, when he referred to the "Beeb Beeb Ceeb".[^cite_note-265] It was then shortened and popularised by radio DJ [Kenny Everett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenny_Everett).[^cite_note-266] [David Bowie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bowie)'s recording sessions at the BBC were released as *[Bowie at the Beeb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowie_at_the_Beeb)*, while [Queen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_\(band\))'s BBC recording sessions were released as *[At the Beeb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/At_the_Beeb)*.[^cite_note-267] Another nickname, now less commonly used, is "Auntie", said to originate from the old-fashioned "Auntie knows best" attitude, or the idea of aunties and uncles who are present in the background of one's life (but possibly a reference to the "aunties" and "uncles" who presented children's programmes in the early days) in the days when John Reith, the BBC's first director general, was in charge.[^cite_note-268] The term "Auntie" for the BBC is often credited to radio disc-jockey [Jack Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Jackson_\(radio_personality\)).[^cite_note-Jackson-11] To celebrate the fiftieth anniversary of the BBC the song "[Auntie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auntie_\(song\))" was released in 1972.[^cite_note-269] It also featured in the title of the BBC's blooper show, *[Auntie's Bloomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auntie's_Bloomers)*, which was presented by [Terry Wogan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_Wogan) from 1991 to 2001.[^cite_note-270] The two nicknames have also been used together as "Auntie Beeb".[^cite_note-271]

## Controversies

Main articles: [Criticism of the BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criticism_of_the_BBC) and [BBC controversies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_controversies)



[Pro-Palestinian protester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaza_war_protests_in_the_United_Kingdom) accusing the BBC of pro-Israel bias during the [Gaza war](74998519.md)

Throughout its existence the BBC has faced numerous accusations regarding many topics such as the [Iraq war](201936.md), the [Gaza war](74998519.md), [colonialism](7299.md), [politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics), [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics) and [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion), as well as funding and staffing. It also has been involved in numerous controversies because of its coverage of specific news stories, events and programming. The BBC has been accused repeatedly of anti-Israel, [anti-semitism](1078.md), and anti-Palestinian bias.[^cite_note-277][^cite_note-278][^cite_note-279]

### Claims of liberal and left-wing bias

See also: [2025 BBC editorial bias allegations](81564054.md)

The BBC has long faced accusations of liberal and left-wing bias.[^cite_note-280] Accusations of a bias against the [Premiership of Margaret Thatcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premiership_of_Margaret_Thatcher) and the [Conservative Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)) were often made against the BBC by members of that government, with [Margaret Thatcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Thatcher) herself considering the broadcaster's news coverage to be biased and irresponsible.[^cite_note-281] In 2011, [Peter Sissons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Sissons), a main news presenter at the BBC from 1989 to 2009, said that "at the core of the BBC, in its very [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA), is a way of thinking that is firmly of the Left".[^cite_note-282]

Another BBC presenter, [Andrew Marr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Marr), commented that "the BBC is not impartial or neutral. It has a liberal bias, not so much a party-political bias. It is better expressed as a cultural liberal bias." Former BBC director [Roger Mosey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Mosey) classified it as "liberal defensive".[^cite_note-283][^cite_note-284][^cite_note-285] In 2022, the BBC chairman, [Richard Sharp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Sharp_\(banker\)), said that the BBC "does have a liberal bias" and that the institution is "fighting against it".[^cite_note-286] In November 2025, [allegations](81564054.md) by *[The Daily Telegraph](23797577.md)* that the BBC had deceptively spliced a [Donald Trump](4848272.md) speech preceding the [January 6 United States Capitol attack](66307490.md) in a documentary led to several BBC executives resigning, including the head of the BBC.[^cite_note-287]

### Claims of right-wing bias

Writing for *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* in 2018, the left-wing columnist [Owen Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owen_Jones) stated "the truth is the BBC is stacked full of rightwingers,"[^cite_note-288] and cited as an example of bias its employment of "ultra-[Thatcherite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thatcherite)" [Andrew Neil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Neil) as a politics presenter.[^cite_note-289] In August 2022, former BBC presenter [Emily Maitlis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emily_Maitlis) stated that BBC board member [Robbie Gibb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robbie_Gibb), who had worked as an editorial advisor to the right-wing channel [GB News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GB_News), and former Prime Minister [Boris Johnson](19065069.md), who had appointed him to the board, was an "active agent of the Conservative party" who played a significant role in determining the nature of the corporation's news output.[^cite_note-290] In November 2025, [LibDem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LibDem) leader [Ed Davey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Davey) called for Gibb's removal from the BBC board because of Gibb's alleged role in the 2025 BBC editorial bias controversy.[^cite_note-291]

A 2018 opinion poll by BMG Research found that 40% of the British public thought that the BBC was politically partisan, with a nearly even split between those who believed it leaned to the left or right.[^cite_note-economist-20190109-292]

### Accusations of bias on transgender topics

The BBC has been accused by politicians, media sources, BBC staff, and trans rights advocates of creating a "moral panic" against trans rights and of wider transphobia in reporting,[^cite_note-293][^cite_note-294] citing examples such as the article ["We're being pressured into sex by some trans women"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%22We're_being_pressured_into_sex_by_some_trans_women%22), in which the BBC cited a social media poll from an anti-trans group to say that transgender women were pressuring lesbians to have sex with them under the threat of being labelled transphobic.[^cite_note-Hansford-295][^cite_note-296][^cite_note-297] Additionally oft-cited are its policy of "balancing" appearances by transgender people with activists from gender-critical groups, its manual of style which requires any allegations of transphobia to be challenged by the news presenter, and its policy against using the term "cisgender".[^cite_note-298][^cite_note-299]

The BBC has also been accused by [gender-critical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender-critical_feminism) groups and BBC staff of having a pro-trans bias, primarily for "failing to make it clear that transgender women are biological males".[^cite_note-300][^cite_note-301] Such accusations have alleged that the BBC's LGBT desk has been "captured by a small group of people" intent on promoting a pro-trans agenda.[^cite_note-Hansford-295][^cite_note-302][^cite_note-303] Shortly thereafter, the BBC began running articles referring to trans women as "biological males who identify as women".[^cite_note-304]

### Bullying and workplace culture

Concerns about bullying at the BBC have recurred for decades and have prompted several internal reviews.

After the [Jimmy Savile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Savile) revelations, the BBC commissioned the 2013 *Respect at Work* review led by barrister [Dinah Rose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinah_Rose). More than 930 staff took part. It found sexual harassment to be rare but bullying more common, and described "a strong undercurrent of fear" among staff who raised complaints.[^cite_note-305] The BBC pledged to overhaul its procedures and drop confidentiality clauses that discouraged staff from speaking out.[^cite_note-306]

A workplace culture review published in April 2025, carried out for the BBC by Change Associates, found no "toxic culture" but said a minority whose conduct damaged morale had not been dealt with adequately.[^cite_note-307][^cite_note-308] Over the same period the corporation removed several presenters after complaints were investigated and upheld.

In June 2026 the BBC dismissed five long-serving journalists at its Hong Kong bureau, citing redundancy. Some had earlier raised bullying concerns. The [Hong Kong Journalists Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong_Journalists_Association) sought an independent investigation into the bureau's management, and the [International Federation of Journalists](1178863.md) backed the call, urging the BBC to examine the dismissals and the treatment of staff who had raised concerns.[^cite_note-309] As of July 2026, no review had been commissioned.[^cite_note-310]
