[Australia](4689264.md) and its offshore islands and territories have 898 recorded [bird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird) species as of 2014.[^cite_note-DolbyClarke-1] Of the recorded birds, 165 are considered [vagrant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vagrancy_\(biology\)) or accidental visitors, of the remainder over 45% are classified as Australian endemics: found nowhere else on earth.[^cite_note-DolbyClarke-1] It has been suggested that up to 10% of Australian bird species may go extinct by the year 2100 as a result of climate change.[^cite_note-2] Recent comprehensive assessments show that the overall extinction risk for the nation's avifauna is accelerating, driven by both external environmental threats and intrinsic biological vulnerabilities.[^cite_note-\:0-3][^cite_note-\:1-4]

Australian species range from the tiny 8 cm (3.1 in) [weebill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weebill) to the huge, flightless [emu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emu). Many species of Australian birds will immediately seem familiar to visitors from the Northern Hemisphere: Australian [wrens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wren) look and act much like northern [wrens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wren), and Australian [robins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australasian_robin) seem to be close relatives of the northern [robins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_robin). However, the majority of Australian [passerines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passerine) are descended from the ancestors of the [crow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crow) family, and the close resemblance is misleading: the cause is not genetic relatedness but [convergent evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_evolution).

For example, almost any land habitat offers a nice home for a small bird that specialises in finding small insects: the form best fitted to that task is one with long legs for agility and obstacle clearance, moderately-sized wings optimised for quick, short flights, and a large, upright tail for rapid changes of direction. In consequence, the unrelated birds that fill that role in the [Americas](29833.md) and in Australia look and act as though they are close relatives.

Australian birds which show convergent evolution with Northern Hemisphere species:

- [honeyeaters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeyeater) (resemble [sunbirds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunbird))

- [sittellas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sittella) (resemble [nuthatches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuthatch))

- [Australasian babblers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australasian_babbler) (resemble [scimitar babblers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scimitar_babbler))

- [Australian robins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_robin) (resemble [Old World chats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_World_chat))

- [Scrub robins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drymodes) (resemble [thrushes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrush_\(bird\)))

## Kinds of birds

Australian birds can be classified into six categories:

- Old endemics: long-established non-[passerines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passerine) of ultimately [Gondwanan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gondwanan) origin, notably [emus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emu), [cassowaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassowary) and the huge [parrot group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psittaciformes)

  [^cite_note-5]

- Corvid radiation: [Passerines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passerine) peculiar to [Australasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australasia), descended from the [crow family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corvidae), and now occupying a vast range of roles and sizes; examples include [wrens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maluridae), [robins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroicidae), [magpies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_magpie), [thornbills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acanthiza), [pardalotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pardalote), the huge [honeyeater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeyeater) family, [treecreepers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australasian_treecreeper), [lyrebirds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyrebird), [birds-of-paradise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birds-of-paradise) and [bowerbirds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowerbird)

- Eurasian colonists: later colonists from [Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasia), including [plovers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plover), [swallows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hirundinidae), [larks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lark), [thrushes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrush_\(bird\)), [cisticolas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cisticola), sunbirds and some [raptors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_of_prey)

- Recent introductions: birds recently introduced by humans; some, such as the [European goldfinch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_goldfinch) and [greenfinch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_greenfinch), appear to coexist with native fauna; others, such as the [common starling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_starling), [blackbird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_blackbird), [house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_sparrow) and [tree sparrows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_sparrow), and the [common myna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_myna), are more destructive

- Migratory shorebirds: a suite of [waders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wader) in the [Scolopacidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scolopacidae) and [Charadriidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charadriidae) families which breed in northern [Asia](689.md) and [Alaska](624.md) and spend the non-breeding season in [Australasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australasia)

- [Seabirds](147019.md): a large and cosmopolitan group of [petrels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrel), [albatrosses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albatross), [sulids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulidae), [gulls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gull), [terns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tern) and [cormorants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cormorant), many of which either breed on islands within Australian territory or frequent its coast and [territorial waters](496983.md)

## Conservation

The conservation status of Australian birds is influenced by a combination of intrinsic biological traits and external environmental threats. Between 1990 and 2020, the overall extinction risk for Australian avifauna increased, with the steepest decline occurring in the most recent decade.[^cite_note-\:1-4] During this period, the number of avian taxa moved to higher extinction risk categories vastly exceeded those reassigned to lower categories.[^cite_note-\:1-4] The primary drivers of this increased risk included the 2019–2020 wildfires, rapid population declines in migratory shorebirds, and a loss of suitable habitat for upland rainforest species.[^cite_note-\:1-4] Furthermore, a comprehensive phylogenetic analysis revealed that intrinsic traits (specifically high evolutionary distinctiveness, island endemism, and an inability to utilise agricultural habitats) predispose certain Australian bird species to a disproportionately elevated extinction risk.[^cite_note-\:0-3] Historically, the taxa that have already gone extinct were characterised by large body mass and being restricted to islands.[^cite_note-\:0-3]

## Parasites

The country does not suffer from several [Apicomplexan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apicomplexa) parasites found throughout the rest of the world.[^cite_note-Atkinson-et-al-2009-7]<sup>: 14, 36 </sup> Several species of both avian *[haemoproteids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haemoproteus)* and avian *[Plasmodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium)* spp. are absent here.[^cite_note-Atkinson-et-al-2009-7]<sup>: 14, 36 </sup>

## Regional references and guides

Important regional references include:

- *[Australia Birds](http://www.waterfordpress.com/)*, a portable folding guide authored by zoologist James Kavanagh, features 140 of the most familiar species. Part of a four title series on Australia flora & fauna featuring ecoregions and major bird spotting sites around the country.

- *[Finding Australian Birds](http://www.publish.csiro.au/pid/6518.htm)*, authored by Tim Dolby and Rohan Clarke (2014), features the best places in Australia for finding birds.

- *The [Handbook of Australian, New Zealand and Antarctic Birds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handbook_of_Australian,_New_Zealand_and_Antarctic_Birds)* (*[HANZAB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HANZAB)*), the pre-eminent scientific reference, in seven volumes.

- *[The New Atlas of Australian Birds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_of_Australian_Birds)*, an extensive detailed survey of Australian bird distributions.

- *[The Action Plan for Australian Birds 2000](http://www.environment.gov.au/biodiversity/threatened/publications/action/birds2000/index.html)*, Garnett, Stephen T.; & Crowley, Gabriel M., Environment Australia, Canberra, 2000 [ISBN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISBN_\(identifier\)) [0-642-54683-5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special\:BookSources/0-642-54683-5), a comprehensive survey of the conservation status of Australian species, with costed conservation and recovery strategies.

- *[Reader's Digest Complete Book of Australian Birds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reader's_Digest_Complete_Book_of_Australian_Birds)* was once the standard general reference, but is now somewhat dated. The second edition (1986) remains in print.

- *[Where to See Birds in Victoria](http://www.allenandunwin.com/default.aspx?page=94\&book=9781741757361)*, edited by Tim Dolby (2009), features places in Victoria for seeing birds.
