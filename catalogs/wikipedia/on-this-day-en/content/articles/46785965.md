A **first-preference** is a voter's most-preferred candidate.[^cite_note-SBS-1][^cite_note-environ-2][^cite_note-Cameron-3][^cite_note-RTEResults2015-4] In certain ranked systems such as [first preference plurality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_preference_plurality), [ranked-choice voting (RCV)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant-runoff_voting), and the [single transferable vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_transferable_vote), first preferences for a candidate are considered most important and prioritized heavily. This incentivizes pandering to the [political base or "core support"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_base) as a result of the [center squeeze effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_squeeze_effect). Methods like [Condorcet voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet_voting), [rated voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rated_voting), and the [Borda count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borda_count) do not exhibit such effects. Methods like [anti-plurality voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-plurality_voting) and [Coombs' method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coombs'_method) have the opposite effect, being dominated by a voter's bottom rankings and so tending to elect the "least offensive" candidates.

First-preference votes are used by [psephologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psephologists) and the [print](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Print_media) and [broadcast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast) media to broadly describe the *state of the parties* at elections and the *[swing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swingometer)* between elections.[^cite_note-RTEResults2015-4][^cite_note-UWA-5][^cite_note-NSW2012-6] The term is much-used in Australian politics, where ranked voting has been universal at federal, state, and local levels since the 1920s.
