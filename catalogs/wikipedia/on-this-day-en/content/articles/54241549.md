The Bolivarian Republic of [Venezuela](32374.md) was a founding member of the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md) (IMF) in 1946, but its relationship with the institution has been marked by significant shifts, reflecting the country's turbulent economic and political landscape.[^cite_note-1]

## Background

Venezuela possesses the world's largest proven [oil reserves](19100139.md), making its economy exceptionally dependent on [petroleum](23195.md) production and exports.[^cite_note-2] As a prominent member of [OPEC](166346.md), the nation's fiscal health has historically been tied to the volatile [price of oil](5137675.md). The [precipitous drop in global oil prices in 2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010s_oil_glut) triggered a severe and ongoing economic crisis, causing state revenue to collapse, the economy to contract, and the [Central Bank of Venezuela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Bank_of_Venezuela)'s monetary reserves to dwindle.[^cite_note-3]

## Relationship with the IMF

### Departure and isolation

Under the government of President [Hugo Chávez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Ch%C3%A1vez), Venezuela pursued a policy of economic sovereignty and distanced itself from international financial institutions. In a symbolic move, the country paid off its debts to both the IMF and the [World Bank](36752.md) five years ahead of schedule in 2007, subsequently cutting off formal financial relations with them.[^cite_note-4] Despite this political stance, both the IMF and the World Bank have repeatedly expressed their readiness to provide technical and financial assistance to Venezuela if requested by its government.[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6]

### Economic context of the rupture

The Chávez administration and that of his successor, [Nicolás Maduro](7161476.md), pursued a policy of extensive [state takeovers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalization) of key industries, including oil production, [banking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banking), [telecommunications](33094374.md), and [mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining). During a period of high oil prices, the government funded ambitious social programs and public spending. These redistributive policies, according to official figures, successfully reduced the [poverty rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty_rate) from 50% in 1998 to 30% by 2013 and lowered income [inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_inequality).[^cite_note-7] However, this model proved unsustainable when oil revenues plummeted, leaving the economy exposed.

## Economic crisis and statistics

Following the oil price crash, Venezuela entered a deep economic depression. By 2015, the [per capita income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_capita_income) had fallen to \$7,808.[^cite_note-8] According to IMF estimates, the economy contracted by approximately 30% between 2013 and 2017. The country experienced [rampant inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation), which was projected to reach 720% in 2017, alongside a forecasted economic contraction of a further 7.4%.[^cite_note-9]

A critical symptom of the crisis was a severely overvalued official [exchange rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_rate). While the government maintained a fixed rate of 10 [bolivars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venezuelan_bol%C3%ADvar) to the US dollar, the currency's value on the [black market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_market) was a fraction of this, crippling the formal economy and leading to widespread shortages of essential goods, including food and medicine.[^cite_note-10] Despite its estrangement from the Fund, Venezuela retained an IMF quota allocation of 3,722,700 [SDR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_drawing_rights) as of 2016.[^cite_note-11]
