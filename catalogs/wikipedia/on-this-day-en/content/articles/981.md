The **Alps** ([/ælps/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English))[^cite_note-2] are some of the highest and most extensive [mountain ranges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_range) in [Europe](9239.md),[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4] stretching approximately 1,200 km (750 mi) across several [Alpine countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_states) (from west to east): [Monaco](19261.md), [France](5843419.md), [Switzerland](26748.md), [Italy](14532.md), [Liechtenstein](17810.md), [Germany](11867.md), [Austria](26964606.md), and [Slovenia](27338.md).[^cite_note-5]

The Alpine arch extends from [Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nice) on the western [Mediterranean](19006.md) to [Trieste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trieste) on the [Adriatic](23275478.md) and [Vienna](55866.md) at the beginning of the [Pannonian Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pannonian_Basin). The mountains were formed over tens of millions of years as the African and Eurasian [tectonic plates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tectonic_plate) collided. Extreme shortening  caused by the event resulted in marine [sedimentary rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary_rock) rising by [thrusting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust_fault) and [folding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fold_\(geology\)) into high mountain peaks such as [Mont Blanc](44768.md) and the [Matterhorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matterhorn). The Alpine region area contains 82 peaks higher than [4,000 m (13,000 ft)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Alpine_four-thousanders). The altitude and size of the range affect the climate in Europe, and in the mountains, precipitation levels vary greatly and climatic conditions consist of distinct zones.

Massif du Mont-Blanc seen from Tête du Vuargne in commune of Verchaix, Haute-Savoie, France

The [Mont Blanc massif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mont_Blanc_massif), the highest range of the Alps

Evidence of human habitation in the Alps goes back to the [Palaeolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaeolithic) era. A [mummified man ("Ötzi")](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%96tzi), determined to be 5,000 years old, was discovered on a glacier at the Austrian–Italian border in 1991.[^cite_note-otzi-6] By the 6th century BC, the Celtic [La Tène culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_T%C3%A8ne_culture) was well established. [Hannibal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannibal) notably crossed the Alps with a herd of elephants, and the [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) had settlements in the region. In 1800, [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon) crossed one of the mountain passes with an army of 40,000. The 18th and 19th centuries saw an influx of [naturalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_history), writers, and artists, in particular, the [Romanticists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism), followed by the [Golden Age of Alpinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_age_of_alpinism) as mountaineers began to ascend the peaks of the Alps.

The Alpine region has a strong cultural identity. Traditional practices such as farming, cheese making, and woodworking still thrive in Alpine villages. However, the tourist industry began to grow early in the 20th century and expanded significantly after World War II, eventually becoming the dominant industry by the end of the century. The [Winter Olympic Games](34069.md) have been hosted in the Swiss, French, Italian, Austrian and German Alps. As of 2010, the region is home to 14 million people and has 120 million annual visitors.[^cite_note-Chatré8-7]

## Etymology and toponymy

See also: [Albion § Etymology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albion#Etymology)



An "Alp" refers to a high elevation pasture frequented only in summer. It often includes several huts and small places of worship (here the Alpe Bardughè in [Ticino](84147.md)).

The English word *Alps* comes from the Latin *Alpes*.

The Latin word *[Alpes](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/en\:Alpes)* could possibly come from the adjective *[albus](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/en\:albus)*[^cite_note-etymonline.com-8] ("white"), or could possibly come from the Greek goddess [Alphito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphito), whose name is related to *alphita*, the "white flour"; *[alphos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphos)*, a dull white leprosy; and finally the [Proto-Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European) word [\*albʰós](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%F0%92%80%A0%F0%92%89%BA%F0%92%80%B8#Hittite). Similarly, the river god [Alpheus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpheus_\(deity\)) is also supposed to derive from the Greek *alphos* and means whitish.[^cite_note-9]

In his commentary on the *Aeneid* of [Virgil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil), the late fourth-century grammarian [Maurus Servius Honoratus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurus_Servius_Honoratus) says that all high mountains are called *Alpes* by Celts.[^cite_note-10]

According to the *Oxford English Dictionary*, the Latin *Alpes* might derive from a [pre-Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Indo-European_languages) word \**alb* "hill"; "Albania" is a related derivation. Albania, the name of the region known as the country of [Albania](738.md), has been used as a name for several mountainous areas across Europe.

In [Roman times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome), "Albania" was a name for the eastern [Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasus), while in the English languages "Albania" (or "Albany") was occasionally used as a name for [Scotland](26994.md),[^cite_note-11] although it is more likely derived from the Latin word *[albus](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/en\:albus)*,[^cite_note-etymonline.com-8] the colour white.

In modern languages the term *alp*, *alm*, *albe* or *alpe* refers to [grazing pastures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transhumance_in_the_Alps) in the alpine regions below the glaciers, not the peaks.[^cite_note-Schmid93-12]

An *alp* refers to a high mountain pasture, typically near or above the [tree line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_line), where cows and other livestock are taken to be grazed during the summer months and where huts and hay barns can be found, sometimes constituting tiny hamlets. Therefore, the term "the Alps", as a reference to the mountains, is a misnomer.[^cite_note-Reynolds43ff-13][^cite_note-Fleming_4-14] The term for the mountain peaks varies by nation and language: words such as *Horn*, *Kogel*, *Kopf*, *Gipfel*, *Spitze*, *Stock*, and *Berg* are used in German-speaking regions; *Mont*, *Pic*, *Tête*, *Pointe*, *Dent*, *Roche*, and *Aiguille* in French-speaking regions; and *Monte*, *Picco*, *Corno*, *Punta*, *Pizzo*, or *Cima* in Italian-speaking regions.[^cite_note-15]

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of the Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_the_Alps)



The Alps extend in an arc from France in the south and west to Slovenia in the east, and from Monaco in the south to Germany in the north.

The Alps are a crescent shaped geographic feature of central Europe that ranges in an 800 km (500 mi) arc (curved line) from east to west and is 200 km (120 mi) in width. The mean height of the mountain peaks is 2.5 km (1.6 mi).[^cite_note-Ceben_22–24-16] The range stretches from the [Mediterranean Sea](19006.md) north above the [Po](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Po_\(river\)) basin, extending through France from [Grenoble](50972.md), and stretching eastward through mid and southern Switzerland. The range continues onward toward [Vienna](55866.md), Austria, and southeast to the [Adriatic Sea](23275478.md) and [Slovenia](27338.md).[^cite_note-Chatré9-17][^cite_note-18][^cite_note-Beattie_xii–xiii-19] Depending on the definitions used, the [Alpokalja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpokalja) foothills in western [Hungary](13275.md) may also qualify as part of the Alps.[^cite_note-20]

To the south it dips into northern Italy and to the north extends to the southern border of [Bavaria](3764.md) in Germany.[^cite_note-Beattie_xii–xiii-19] In areas like [Chiasso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiasso), Switzerland, and [Allgäu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allg%C3%A4u), Bavaria, the demarcation between the mountain range and the flatlands is clear; in other places such as [Geneva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva), it is less clear.

The Alps are found in the following countries: Austria (28.7% of the range's area), Italy (27.2%), France (21.4%), Switzerland (13.2%), Germany (5.8%), Slovenia (3.6%), Liechtenstein (0.08%) and Monaco (0.001%).[^cite_note-21]

The highest portion of the range is divided by the glacial trough of the [Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rh%C3%B4ne) valley, from [Mont Blanc](44768.md) to the [Matterhorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matterhorn) and [Monte Rosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Rosa) on the southern side, and the [Bernese Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernese_Alps) on the northern. The peaks in the easterly portion of the range, in Austria and Slovenia, are smaller than those in the central and western portions.[^cite_note-Beattie_xii–xiii-19]

The variances in nomenclature in the region spanned by the Alps make classification of the mountains and subregions difficult, but a general classification is that of the [Eastern Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Alps) and [Western Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Alps) with the divide between the two occurring in eastern Switzerland according to geologist Stefan Schmid,[^cite_note-Schmid93-12] near the [Splügen Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spl%C3%BCgen_Pass).



A typical alpine village in the [Tuxertal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuxertal) valley of [Tyrol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrol_\(federal_state\)), Austria

The highest peaks of the Western Alps and Eastern Alps, respectively, are Mont Blanc, at 4,810 m (15,780 ft),[^cite_note-22] and [Piz Bernina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piz_Bernina), at 4,049 m (13,284 ft). The second-highest major peaks are the [Dufourspitze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dufourspitze) of the [Monte Rosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Rosa) massif, at 4,634 m (15,203 ft), and [Ortler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ortler),[^cite_note-23] at 3,905 m (12,810 ft), respectively.

A series of lower mountain ranges run parallel to the main chain of the Alps, including the [French Prealps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Prealps) in France and the [Jura Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jura_Mountains) in Switzerland and France. The secondary chain of the Alps follows the [watershed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_divide) from the Mediterranean Sea to the [Wienerwald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_Woods), passing over many of the highest and most well-known peaks in the Alps. From the Colle di Cadibona to [Col de Tende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Col_de_Tende) it runs westwards, before turning to the northwest and then, near the [Colle della Maddalena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maddalena_Pass), to the north. Upon reaching the Swiss border, the line of the main chain heads approximately east-northeast, a heading it follows until its end near Vienna.[^cite_note-24]

The northeast end of the Alpine arc, directly on the [Danube](21209639.md), which flows into the Black Sea, is the [Leopoldsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopoldsberg) near Vienna. In contrast, the southeastern part of the Alps ends on the [Adriatic Sea](23275478.md) in the area around Trieste towards [Duino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duino) and [Barcola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barcola).[^cite_note-25]

### Passes

Main article: [Principal passes of the Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_passes_of_the_Alps)



*[Teufelsbrücke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teufelsbr%C3%BCcke)* (Devil's Bridge) on the route to the [St Gotthard Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Gotthard_Pass); the currently used bridge from 1958 over the first drivable bridge from 1830

Crossing routes by foot, road, or train are known as *passes*, and usually consist of depressions in the mountains in which a valley leads from the plains and hilly pre-mountainous zones.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECoolidgeLakeKnox1911740-26]

The Alps have been crossed for explorations, commerce (historical: salt, wine, grain, textiles, and metals), military purposes, political reasons, religious and cultural motivations, and migrations for thousands of years. For a thousands of years these routes had been footpaths only. Many had been used by explorers of pre-civilised cultures at least since Stone Ages. The oldest known human mummy, the [Ötzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%96tzi), has been found 1991 on the [Tisenjoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tisenjoch?action=edit\&redlink=1) on the Austrian-Italian border; he has been killed there about 5300 years ago. The Romans built several Roman Roads over several mountain passes in the Alps in order to control Roman territory west, north, north-east of their home base. Usually, they were also used by animal drawn carts.

Merchant traffic was supported by [pack animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pack_animal) such as mules, horses, and even elephants (c.f. [Hannibal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannibal)) usually on footpaths only. Already during the Roman Empire, but again in the late Middle Ages heavy carts and [sleighs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleigh) were in use on drivable roads over Alpine passes.[^cite_note-The_Alps:_An_Environmental_History-27]

In the medieval period, [hospices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospices) were established by religious orders at the summits of many of the main passes.[^cite_note-Fleming_4-14]

Nowadays, the most important passes are the [Col de l'Iseran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Col_de_l'Iseran) (the highest), the [Col Agnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Col_Agnel), the [Brenner Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brenner_Pass), the [Mont Cenis Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mont_Cenis), the [Great St. Bernard Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_St._Bernard_Pass), the [Col de Tende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Col_de_Tende), the [Saint Gotthard Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Gotthard_Pass), the [Semmering Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semmering_Pass), the [Simplon Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplon_Pass), and the [Stelvio Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stelvio_Pass).[^cite_note-Britannica-28]

Crossing the Italian-Austrian border, the Brenner Pass separates the [Ötztal Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%96tztal_Alps) and [Zillertal Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zillertal_Alps) and has been in use as a regular trading route since the Roman Times, and again since 14th century. The lowest of the Alpine passes at 984 m (3,228 ft) is the Semmering Pass crossing from [Lower Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Austria) to [Styria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Styria). It has been in continuous use as a trade route since the 12th century, instead of the Roman Road over the Wechsel Pass. A railroad with a tunnel 1.434 km (0.891 mi) long was built along the route of the pass in the mid-19th century.&#x20;

With a summit of 2,469 m (8,100 ft), the Great St Bernard Pass is one of the highest in the Alps, crossing the Italian-Swiss border east of the Pennine Alps along the flanks of Mont Blanc. The pass was already used by the Romans to control north-western provinces, and later by [Napoleon Bonaparte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_Bonaparte) to cross 40,000 troops in 1800.[^cite_note-switz-29]

The [Mont Cenis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mont_Cenis) pass has been a major commercial and military road between Western Europe and Italy. The pass was crossed by many troops on their way to the Italian peninsula. From [Constantine I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_I_\(emperor\)), [Pepin the Short](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pepin_the_Short) and [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne) to [Henry IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_IV,_Holy_Roman_Emperor), Napoléon and more recently the German [Gebirgsjägers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gebirgsj%C3%A4ger) during [World War II](32927.md).[^cite_note-30]

In the 19th century, the principal passes of the Alps were modernised by engineers to speed up passenger and freight transport.[^cite_note-The_Alps:_An_Environmental_History-27] The Mont Cenis Pass has been supplanted by the [Fréjus Rail Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fr%C3%A9jus_Rail_Tunnel) opening in 1871, while the [Fréjus Road Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fr%C3%A9jus_Road_Tunnel) opened 1980.[^cite_note-31] Railway lines could not be built in the Alps without tunnels and bridges. Apart from the Mont Cenis Railway Tunnel the [Semmering Railway Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semmering_railway) and the [St Gotthard Railway Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gotthard_Tunnel) were built between 1872 and 1882. By the early 20th century eight trans-alpine railway lines had been put into operation.[^cite_note-The_Alps:_An_Environmental_History-27]

The Saint Gotthard Pass crosses from [Central Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Switzerland) to [Ticino](84147.md). In 1882 the 15 km-long (9.3 mi) [Saint Gotthard Railway Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gotthard_Tunnel) was opened connecting [Lucerne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucerne) in Switzerland, with [Milan](36511.md) in Italy. 98 years later followed the [Gotthard Road Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gotthard_Road_Tunnel) (16.9 km (10.5 mi) long) connecting the [A2 motorway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A2_motorway_\(Switzerland\)) in [Göschenen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6schenen) on the north side with [Airolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airolo) on the south side, exactly like the railway tunnel.

On 1 June 2016 the world's longest railway tunnel, the [Gotthard Base Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gotthard_Base_Tunnel), was opened, which connects [Erstfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erstfeld) in [canton of Uri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canton_of_Uri) with [Bodio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodio) in [canton of Ticino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canton_of_Ticino) by two single tubes of 57.1 km (35.5 mi).[^cite_note-32] It is the first tunnel that traverses the Alps on a flat route.[^cite_note-33] From 11 December 2016, it has been part of the regular railway timetable and used hourly as standard ride between [Basel](4911.md)/[Lucerne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucerne)/[Zurich](40334603.md) and [Bellinzona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellinzona)/[Lugano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lugano)/[Milan](36511.md).[^cite_note-34]



[Stelvio Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stelvio_Pass) (2,757 m (9,045 ft)) is the [highest paved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_highest_paved_roads_in_the_Alps) mountain pass in the [Eastern Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Alps), and the second highest in the Alps.

The highest drivable pass in the alps is the [Col de l'Iseran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Col_de_l'Iseran) in [Savoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savoy) (France) at 2,770 m (9,088 ft), followed by the Stelvio Pass in northern Italy at 2,757 m (9,045 ft); the road was built in the 1820s.[^cite_note-Britannica-28]

### Highest mountains

See also: [List of Alpine four-thousanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Alpine_four-thousanders)



The [Eiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eiger) (shown along with the [Mönch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%B6nch) and the [Jungfrau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungfrau)) has the tallest north face in the Alps.

The [Union Internationale des Associations d'Alpinisme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Internationale_des_Associations_d'Alpinisme) (UIAA) has defined a list of 82 "official" Alpine summits that reach at least 4,000 m (13,123 ft).[^cite_note-35] The list includes not only mountains, but also subpeaks with little [prominence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topographic_prominence) that are considered important [mountaineering](20341.md) objectives. Below are listed the 29 "four-thousanders" with at least 300 m (984 ft) of prominence.

While Mont Blanc was first climbed in 1786 and the Jungfrau in 1811, most of the Alpine four-thousanders were climbed during the second half of the 19th century, notably [Piz Bernina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piz_Bernina) (1850), the [Dom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dom_\(mountain\)) (1858), the [Grand Combin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Combin) (1859), the [Weisshorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weisshorn) (1861) and the [Barre des Écrins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barre_des_%C3%89crins) (1864); the ascent of the Matterhorn in 1865 marked the end of the [golden age of alpinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_age_of_alpinism). [Karl Blodig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Blodig) (1859–1956) was among the first to successfully climb all the major 4,000 m peaks. He completed his series of ascents in 1911.[^cite_note-36] Many of the big Alpine three-thousanders were climbed in the early 19th century, notably the [Grossglockner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grossglockner) (1800) and the [Ortler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ortler) (1804), although some of them were climbed only much later, such at [Mont Pelvoux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mont_Pelvoux) (1848), [Monte Viso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Viso) (1861) and [La Meije](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Meije) (1877).

The first British Mont Blanc ascent by a man was in 1788; the first ascent by a woman was in 1808. By the mid-1850s Swiss mountaineers had ascended most of the peaks and were eagerly sought as mountain guides. [Edward Whymper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Whymper) reached the top of the Matterhorn in 1865 (after seven attempts), and in 1938 the last of the six [great north faces of the Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_north_faces_of_the_Alps) was climbed with the first ascent of the Eiger *Nordwand* (north face of the Eiger).[^cite_note-Shoumatoff197ff-37]

### Rivers and lakes

For a more comprehensive list, see [List of valleys of the Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_valleys_of_the_Alps).



[Southern pre-alpine lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Lakes) like [Lake Garda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Garda) are characterised by warmer [microclimates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microclimate) than the surrounding areas.

The Alps provide lowland Europe with drinking water, irrigation, and hydroelectric power.[^cite_note-39] Although the area is only about 11% of the surface area of Europe, the Alps provide up to 90% of water to lowland Europe, particularly to arid areas and during the summer months. Cities such as Milan depend on 80% of water from Alpine runoff.[^cite_note-Chatré9-17][^cite_note-Benniston_et_al._2011,_1-40][^cite_note-41] Water from the rivers is used in at least 550 [hydroelectricity](381399.md) power plants, considering only those producing at least 10MW of electricity.[^cite_note-42]

Major European rivers flow from the Alps, such as the [Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhine), the [Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rh%C3%B4ne), the [Ticino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ticino_\(river\))->[Po](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Po_\(river\)), and the [Inn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inn_\(river\))->[Danube](21209639.md), all of which have headwaters in the Alps and flow into neighbouring countries, finally emptying into the [North Sea](21179.md), the Mediterranean Sea, the Adriatic Sea and the [Black Sea](3386.md). Other rivers such as the Danube have major tributaries flowing into them that originate in the Alps.[^cite_note-Chatré9-17]

The Rhône is second to the [Nile](21244.md) as a freshwater source to the Mediterranean Sea; the river begins as glacial meltwater, flows into [Lake Geneva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Geneva), and from there to France where one of its uses is to cool nuclear power plants.[^cite_note-Benniston_et_al._2011,_3-43] The Rhine originates in a 30 km<sup>2</sup> (12 sq mi) area in Switzerland and represents almost 60% of water exported from the country.[^cite_note-Benniston_et_al._2011,_3-43] Tributary valleys, some of which are complicated, channel water to the main valleys which can experience flooding during the snowmelt season when rapid runoff causes debris torrents and swollen rivers.[^cite_note-Ceben_1998,_31-44]

The rivers flow through lakes, such as Lake Geneva, a crescent-shaped lake crossing the Swiss border with Lausanne on the Swiss side and the town of [Evian-les-Bains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evian-les-Bains) on the French side. In Germany, the medieval [St. Bartholomew's chapel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Bartholomew's_Church,_Berchtesgaden) was built on the south side of the [Königssee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6nigssee), accessible only by boat or by climbing over the abutting peaks.[^cite_note-45]

Additionally, the Alps have led to the creation of large lakes in Italy. For instance, the [Sarca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarca), the [primary inflow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflow_\(hydrology\)) of [Lake Garda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Garda), originates in the Italian Alps.[^cite_note-EnBrit-46] The [Italian Lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Lakes) are a popular tourist destination since the Roman Era for their mild climate.

Scientists have been studying the [impact of climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_climate_change) and water use. For example, each year more water is diverted from rivers for [snowmaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowmaking) in the ski resorts, the effect of which is yet unknown. Furthermore, the steady decrease of glaciated areas for about 150 years—vastly accelerated during the last 30–50 years—combined with an increasingly succession of winters with lower-than-expected precipitation (snow) due to much warmer winters will have a non-negligible future impact on the water storage in the Alps, namely glacier, and consequently on rivers in the Alps as well as an effect on the water availability to the lowlands of many European countries.[^cite_note-Benniston_et_al._2011,_1-40][^cite_note-47]

### Climate

Main articles: [Climate of the Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_the_Alps) and [High Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Alps)

The Alps are a classic example of what happens when a [temperate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperateness) area at lower altitude gives way to higher-elevation terrain. Elevations around the world that have cold climates similar to those of the [polar regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_region) have been called [Alpine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_climate). A rise from sea level into the upper regions of the atmosphere causes the temperature to decrease (see [adiabatic lapse rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adiabatic_lapse_rate)). The effect of mountain chains on prevailing winds is to carry warm air belonging to the lower region into an upper zone, where it expands in volume at the cost of a proportionate loss of temperature, often accompanied by precipitation in the form of snow or rain.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECoolidgeLakeKnox1911737-48] The height of the Alps is sufficient to divide the weather patterns in Europe into a wet north and dry south because moisture is sucked from the air as it flows over the high peaks.[^cite_note-49]



The [Aletsch Glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aletsch_Glacier) with pine trees growing on the hillside (2007; the surface is 180 m (590 ft) lower than 150 years ago; and even 20 m (66 ft) lower ten years later!)

The severe weather in the Alps has been studied since the 18th century; particularly the weather patterns such as the seasonal [foehn wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foehn_wind). Numerous weather stations were placed in the mountains early in the early 20th century, providing continuous data for climatologists.[^cite_note-Ceben_22–24-16] Some of the valleys are quite arid such as the [Aosta Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aosta_Valley) in Italy, the [Maurienne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurienne) in France, the [Valais](198789.md) in Switzerland, and northern [Tyrol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrol_\(federal_state\)).[^cite_note-Ceben_22–24-16]

The areas that are not arid and receive high precipitation experience periodic flooding from rapid snowmelt and runoff.[^cite_note-Ceben_1998,_31-44] The mean precipitation in the Alps ranges from a low of 2,600 mm (100 in) per year to 3,600 mm (140 in) per year, with the higher levels occurring at high altitudes. At altitudes between 1,000 and 3,000 m (3,300 and 9,800 ft), snowfall begins in November and accumulates through to April or May when the melt begins. [Snow lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snow_line) vary from 2,400 to 3,000 m (7,900 to 9,800 ft), above which the snow is permanent and the temperatures hover around the freezing point even during July and August. High-water levels in streams and rivers peak in June and July when the snow is still melting at the higher altitudes.[^cite_note-50]

The Alps are split into five climatic zones, each with different vegetation. The climate, plant life, and animal life vary among the different sections or zones of the mountains. The lowest zone is the colline zone, which exists between 500 and 1,000 m (1,600 and 3,300 ft), depending on the location. The [montane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montane) zone extends from 800 to 1,700 m (2,600 to 5,600 ft), followed by the sub-Alpine zone from 1,600 to 2,400 m (5,200 to 7,900 ft). The Alpine zone, extending from [tree line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_line) to the snow line, is followed by the glacial zone, which covers the glaciated areas of the mountain. Climatic conditions show variances within the same zones; for example, weather conditions at the head of a mountain valley, extending directly from the peaks, are colder and more severe than those at the mouth of a valley which tend to be less severe and receive less snowfall.[^cite_note-51]

#### Climate change



Shrinkage of snow cover duration, starting \~end of the 19th century

[^cite_note-10.1038/s41558-022-01575-3-52]

Various models of [climate change](5042951.md) have been projected into the 22nd century for the Alps, with an expectation that a trend toward increased temperatures will have an effect on [snowfall, snowpack, glaciation, and river runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_climate_change#Ice_and_snow).[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54] Significant changes, of both natural and anthropogenic origins, have already been diagnosed from observations,[^cite_note-55][^cite_note-56][^cite_note-57] including a 5.6% reduction per decade in snow cover duration over the last 50 years, which also highlights [climate change adaptation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_adaptation) needs due to impacts on the climate and regional socio-economic activities.[^cite_note-10.1038/s41558-022-01575-3-52]

## Geology

Main articles: [Alpine orogeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_orogeny) and [Geology of the Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology_of_the_Alps)

Important geological concepts were established as naturalists began studying the rock formations of the Alps in the 18th century. In the mid-19th century, the now-defunct idea of [geosynclines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosyncline) was used to explain the presence of "folded" mountain chains. This theory was replaced in the mid-20th century by the theory of [plate tectonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics).[^cite_note-Graciansky1ff-58]



The geologic [folding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fold_\(geology\)) seen at the Arpenaz waterfall, shown here in a mid-18th-century drawing, was noted by 18th-century geologists.

[^cite_note-Graciansky5-59]

The formation of the Alps (the [Alpine orogeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_orogeny)) was an episodic process that began about 300 million years ago.[^cite_note-60] In the [Paleozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleozoic) Era the [Pangaean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pangaea) [supercontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercontinent) consisted of a single [tectonic plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tectonic_plate); it broke into separate plates during the [Mesozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesozoic) Era and the [Tethys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tethys_Ocean) sea developed between [Laurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurasia) and [Gondwana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gondwana) during the [Jurassic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurassic) Period.[^cite_note-Graciansky1ff-58] The Tethys was later squeezed between colliding plates causing the formation of mountain ranges called the [Alpide belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpide_belt), from [Gibraltar](7607314.md) through the [Himalayas](37966.md) to [Indonesia](14579.md)—a process that began at the end of the [Mesozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesozoic) and continues into the present. The formation of the Alps was a segment of this orogenic process,[^cite_note-Graciansky1ff-58] caused by the collision between the [African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Plate) and the [Eurasian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_Plate) plates[^cite_note-Gerrard9-61] that began in the late [Cretaceous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretaceous) Period.[^cite_note-Gerrard16-62]

Under extreme [compressive stresses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compressive_stress) and pressure, marine [sedimentary rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary_rock) were uplifted, forming characteristic recumbent [folds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fold_\(geology\)), and [thrust faults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust_fault).[^cite_note-63] As the rising peaks underwent erosion, a layer of marine [flysch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flysch) sediments was deposited in the [foreland basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreland_basin), and the sediments became involved in younger folds as the orogeny progressed. Coarse sediments from the continual uplift and erosion were later deposited in [foreland areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molasse_basin) north of the Alps.[^cite_note-Gerrard9-61] These regions in Switzerland and Bavaria are well-developed, containing classic examples of [flysch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flysch), which is [sedimentary rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary_rock) formed during mountain building.[^cite_note-Schmid102-64]

The Alpine orogeny occurred in ongoing cycles through to the Paleogene causing differences in folded structures, with a late-stage orogeny causing the development of the [Jura Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jura_Mountains).[^cite_note-Schmid97-65] A series of tectonic events in the Triassic, Jurassic and Cretaceous periods caused different [paleogeographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaeogeography) regions.[^cite_note-Schmid97-65] The Alps are subdivided by different [lithology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithology) (rock composition) and nappe structures according to the orogenic events that affected them.[^cite_note-Schmid93-12] The geological subdivision differentiates the Western, Eastern Alps, and Southern Alps: the [Helveticum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helvetic_nappes) in the north, the [Penninicum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penninic_nappes) and [Austroalpine system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austroalpine_nappes) in the centre and, south of the Periadriatic Seam, the [Southern Alpine system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Alps_\(geology\)).[^cite_note-Schmid99-66]



Compressed metamorphosed Tethyan sediments and their oceanic basement are sandwiched between the tip of the [Matterhorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matterhorn) (Italian-Swiss border), which consists of gneisses originally part of the African plate, and the base of the peak, which is part of the Eurasian plate.

[^cite_note-Graciansky5-59]

According to geologist Stefan Schmid, because the Western Alps underwent a metamorphic event in the [Cenozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cenozoic) Era while the Austroalpine peaks underwent an event in the Cretaceous Period, the two areas show distinct differences in nappe formations.[^cite_note-Schmid97-65] [Flysch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flysch) deposits in the Southern Alps of [Lombardy](43807.md) probably occurred in the Cretaceous or later.[^cite_note-Schmid97-65]

Peaks in France, Italy and Switzerland lie in the "Houillière zone", which consists of [basement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basement_\(geology\)) with sediments from the Mesozoic Era.[^cite_note-Schmid99-66] High "massifs" with external sedimentary cover are more common in the Western Alps and were affected by [Neogene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neogene) Period [thin-skinned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin-skinned_deformation) thrusting whereas the Eastern Alps have comparatively few high peaked massifs.[^cite_note-Schmid102-64] Similarly the peaks in eastern Switzerland extending to western Austria (Helvetic nappes) consist of thin-skinned sedimentary folding that [detached](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decollement) from former [basement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basement_\(geology\)) rock.[^cite_note-Schmid103-67]

In simple terms, the structure of the Alps consists of layers of rock of European, African, and oceanic (Tethyan) origin.[^cite_note-Graciansky29ff-68] The bottom nappe structure is of continental European origin, above which are stacked marine sediment nappes, topped off by nappes derived from the African plate.[^cite_note-Graciansky31-69] The Matterhorn is an example of the ongoing orogeny and shows evidence of great folding. The tip of the mountain consists of [gneisses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gneiss) from the African plate; the base of the peak, below the glaciated area, consists of European basement rock. The sequence of Tethyan marine sediments and their oceanic basement is sandwiched between rock derived from the African and European plates.[^cite_note-Graciansky5-59]



[Haute Maurienne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurienne) (Ambin and Vanoise massifs) and its exposed crystalline basement made of high-pressure subduction rocks such as [blueschist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blueschist) and [metaquartzite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartzite) (picture taken at 2,400 metres or 7,900 feet)

The core regions of the Alpine orogenic belt have been folded and fractured in such a manner that erosion produced the characteristic steep vertical peaks of the [Swiss Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_Alps) that rise seemingly straight out of the foreland areas.[^cite_note-Gerrard16-62] Peaks such as Mont Blanc, the Matterhorn, and high peaks in the Pennine Alps, the Briançonnais, and [Hohe Tauern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hohe_Tauern) consist of layers of rock from the various orogenies including exposures of basement rock.[^cite_note-70]

Due to the ever-present geologic instability, earthquakes continue in the Alps to this day.[^cite_note-71] Typically, the largest earthquakes in the alps have been between magnitude 6 and 7 on the Richter scale.[^cite_note-72] [Geodetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesy) measurements show ongoing topographic uplift at rates of up to about 2.5 mm per year in the North, Western and Central Alps, and at \~1 mm per year in the Eastern and South-Western Alps.[^cite_note-Sternai-2019-73] The underlying mechanisms that jointly drive the present-day uplift pattern are the isostatic rebound due to the melting of the last glacial maximum ice-cap or long-term erosion, detachment of the Western Alpine subducting slab, mantle convection as well as ongoing horizontal convergence between Africa and Europe, but their relative contributions to the uplift of the Alps are difficult to quantify and likely to vary significantly in space and time.[^cite_note-Sternai-2019-73]

### Minerals

The Alps are a source of minerals that have been mined for thousands of years. In the 8th to 6th centuries, BC during the [Hallstatt culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallstatt_culture), Celtic tribes mined copper; later the Romans mined gold for coins in the [Bad Gastein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Gastein) area. [Erzberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erzberg) in [Styria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Styria) furnishes high-quality iron ore for the steel industry. Crystals, such as [cinnabar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinnabar), [amethyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amethyst), and [quartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartz), are found throughout much of the Alpine region. The cinnabar deposits in Slovenia are a notable source of cinnabar pigments.[^cite_note-74]

Alpine crystals have been studied and collected for hundreds of years and began to be classified in the 18th century. [Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler) studied the shapes of crystals, and by the 19th-century crystal hunting was common in Alpine regions. David Friedrich Wiser amassed a collection of 8000 crystals that he studied and documented. In the 20th century Robert Parker wrote a well-known work about the rock crystals of the Swiss Alps; at the same period a commission was established to control and standardise the naming of Alpine minerals.[^cite_note-75]

### Glaciers

See also: [List of glaciers in Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_glaciers_in_Switzerland)



This illustration of the glacier systems of the [Mont Blanc massif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mont_Blanc_massif) by [Alexander Keith Johnston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Keith_Johnston_\(1804%E2%80%931871\)) was first published 1848 in *The Physical Atlas*.

In the [Miocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miocene) Epoch the mountains underwent severe erosion because of glaciation,[^cite_note-Gerrard16-62] which was noted in the mid-19th century by naturalist [Louis Agassiz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Agassiz) who presented a paper proclaiming the Alps were covered in ice at various intervals—a theory he formed when studying rocks near his [Neuchâtel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuch%C3%A2tel) home which he believed originated to the west in the Bernese Oberland. Because of his work he came to be known as the "father of the ice-age concept" although other naturalists before him put forth similar ideas.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff63ff-76]



[Louis Agassiz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Agassiz)'s studies of the [Unteraar Glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unteraar_Glacier) in the 1840s showed that it moved at 100 m (328 ft) per year.

[^cite_note-Shoumatoff63ff-76]

Agassiz studied glacier movement in the 1840s at the [Unteraar Glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unteraar_Glacier) where he found the glacier moved 100 m (328 ft) per year, more rapidly in the middle than at the edges. His work was continued by other scientists and now a permanent laboratory exists inside a glacier under the [Jungfraujoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungfraujoch), devoted exclusively to the study of Alpine glaciers.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff63ff-76]

Glaciers pick up rocks and sediment with them as they flow. This causes erosion and the formation of valleys over time. The [Inn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inn_\(river\)) valley is an example of a valley carved by glaciers during the [ice ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternary_glaciation) with a typical terraced structure caused by erosion. Eroded rocks from the most recent ice age lie at the bottom of the valley while the top of the valley consists of erosion from earlier ice ages.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff63ff-76] Glacial valleys have characteristically steep walls (reliefs); valleys with lower reliefs and [talus slopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scree) are remnants of glacial troughs or previously infilled valleys.[^cite_note-Gerrard132-77] [Moraines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moraine), piles of rock picked up during the movement of the glacier, accumulate at edges, centre, and the terminus of glaciers.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff63ff-76]



The Sphinx Tunnel connecting [Jungfraujoch railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungfraujoch_railway_station) to the [Sphinx Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphinx_Observatory), through a glacier at the [Jungfraujoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungfraujoch)

Alpine glaciers can be straight rivers of ice, long sweeping rivers, spread in a fan-like shape (Piedmont glaciers), and curtains of ice that hang from vertical slopes of the mountain peaks. The stress of the movement causes the ice to break and crack loudly, perhaps explaining why the mountains were believed to be home to dragons in the medieval period. The cracking creates unpredictable and dangerous [crevasses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crevasse), often invisible under new snowfall, which causes the greatest danger to mountaineers.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff71ff-78]

Glaciers end in ice caves (the [Rhône Glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rh%C3%B4ne_Glacier)), by trailing into a lake or river, or by shedding snowmelt on a meadow. Sometimes a piece of glacier will detach or break resulting in flooding, property damage, and loss of life.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff71ff-78]

High levels of precipitation cause the glaciers to descend to [permafrost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permafrost) levels in some areas whereas in other, more arid regions, glaciers remain above about the 3,500 m (11,483 ft) level.[^cite_note-Gerrard78-79] The 1,817 km<sup>2</sup> (702 sq mi) of the Alps covered by glaciers in 1876 had shrunk to 1,342 km<sup>2</sup> (518 sq mi) by 1973, resulting in decreased river run-off levels.[^cite_note-Gerrard108-80] Forty percent of the glaciation in Austria has disappeared since 1850, and 30% of that in Switzerland.[^cite_note-81]

Although the Alpine topography shows marked glacial morphologies,[^cite_note-82] the mechanisms by which glacial reshaping occurs are unclear. [Numerical modelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_simulation) suggests that glacial erosion propagates from low elevations to high elevations leading to an early increase of local relief followed by lowering of the mean orogen elevation.[^cite_note-83]

## Ecology

Main article: [Alps conifer and mixed forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alps_conifer_and_mixed_forests)

### Flora

Main article: [Flora of the Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flora_of_the_Alps)



Stemless gentian (*[Gentiana acaulis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentiana_acaulis)*)

Thirteen thousand species of plants have been identified in the Alpine regions.[^cite_note-Chatré8-7] Alpine plants are grouped by habitat and soil type which can be [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone) or non-calcareous. The habitats range from meadows, bogs, and woodland (deciduous and coniferous) areas to soil-less scree and [moraines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moraine), and rock faces and ridges.[^cite_note-Reynolds43ff-13] A natural vegetation limit with altitude is given by the presence of the chief [deciduous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deciduous) trees—oak, beech, [ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ash_tree) and [sycamore maple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sycamore_maple). These do not reach the same elevation, nor are they often found growing together, but their upper limit corresponds accurately enough to the change from a temperate to a colder climate that is further proved by a change in the presence of wild herbaceous vegetation.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECoolidgeLakeKnox1911738-85] This limit usually lies about 1,200 m (3,900 ft) above the sea on the north side of the Alps, but on the southern slopes it often rises to 1,500 m (4,900 ft), sometimes even to 1,700 m (5,600 ft).[^cite_note-86]



Edelweiss (*[Leontopodium alpinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edelweiss)*)

Above the forestry, there is often a band of dwarf pine trees (*[Pinus mugo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinus_mugo)*), which is in turn superseded by *Alpenrosen*, dwarf shrubs, typically *[Rhododendron ferrugineum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhododendron_ferrugineum)* (on acid soils) or *[Rhododendron hirsutum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhododendron_hirsutum)* (on alkaline soils).[^cite_note-87] Although Alpenrose prefers acidic soil, the plants are found throughout the region.[^cite_note-Reynolds43ff-13] Above the [tree line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_line) is the area defined as "alpine" where in the [alpine meadow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_meadow) plants are found that have adapted well to harsh conditions of cold temperatures, aridity, and [high altitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-altitude_adaptation). The alpine area fluctuates greatly because of regional fluctuations in tree lines.[^cite_note-88]

Alpine plants such as the [Alpine gentian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentiana_acaulis) grow in abundance in areas such as the meadows above the [Lauterbrunnental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lauterbrunnental). Gentians are named after the [Illyrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illyria) king [Gentius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentius), and 40 species of the early-spring blooming flower grow in the Alps, in a range of 1,500 to 2,400 m (4,900 to 7,900 ft).[^cite_note-89] Writing about the gentians in Switzerland [D. H. Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D._H._Lawrence) described them as "darkening the day-time, torch-like with the smoking blueness of Pluto's gloom."[^cite_note-90] Gentians tend to "appear" repeatedly as the spring blooming takes place at progressively later dates, moving from the lower altitude to the higher altitude meadows where the snow melts much later than in the valleys. On the highest rocky ledges, the spring flowers bloom in the summer.[^cite_note-Reynolds43ff-13]



Preserved internal alpine forest and meadow, [Vanoise National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanoise_National_Park)

At these higher altitudes, the plants tend to form isolated cushions. In the Alps, several species of flowering plants have been recorded above 4,000 m (13,000 ft), including *[Ranunculus glacialis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranunculus_glacialis)*, *[Androsace alpina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Androsace_alpina)* and *[Saxifraga biflora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxifraga_biflora?action=edit\&redlink=1)*. *[Eritrichium nanum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eritrichium_nanum)*, commonly known as the King of the Alps, is the most elusive of the alpine flowers, growing on rocky ridges at 2,600 to 3,750 m (8,530 to 12,300 ft).[^cite_note-91] Perhaps the best known of the alpine plants is [Edelweiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edelweiss) which grows in rocky areas and can be found at altitudes as low as 1,200 m (3,900 ft) and as high as 3,400 m (11,200 ft).[^cite_note-Reynolds43ff-13] The plants that grow at the highest altitudes have adapted to conditions by specialisation such as growing in rock screes that give protection from winds.[^cite_note-Sharp14ff-92]

The alpine meadows are also known for their distinct floral aromas during summer: the sensory landscape is often defined by the strong, spicy-sweet fragrance of [Alpine clover (*Trifolium alpinum*)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trifolium_alpinum), a characteristic scent of the high pastures.[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94]

The extreme and stressful climatic conditions give way to the growth of plant species with [secondary metabolites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_metabolites) important for medicinal purposes. *[Origanum vulgare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origanum_vulgare)*, *[Prunella vulgaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prunella_vulgaris)*, *[Solanum nigrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solanum_nigrum)*, and *[Urtica dioica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urtica_dioica)* are some of the more useful medicinal species found in the Alps.[^cite_note-95] Human interference has nearly exterminated the trees in many areas, and, except for the beech forests of the Austrian Alps, forests of deciduous trees are rarely found after the extreme deforestation between the 17th and 19th centuries.[^cite_note-96] The vegetation has changed since the second half of the 20th century, as the high alpine meadows cease to be harvested for hay or used for grazing which eventually might result in a regrowth of the forest. In some areas, the modern practice of building ski runs by mechanical means has destroyed the underlying tundra from which the plant life cannot recover during the non-skiing months, whereas areas that still practice a natural *piste* type of ski slope building preserve the fragile underlayers.[^cite_note-Sharp14ff-92]

### Fauna

The Alps are a habitat for 30,000 species of wildlife, ranging from the tiniest [snow fleas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snow_flea) to [brown bears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_bear), many of which have made adaptations to the harsh cold conditions and high altitudes to the point that some only survive in specific micro-climates either directly above or below the [snow line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snow_line).[^cite_note-Chatré8-7][^cite_note-97]



Young alpine ibex. When fully grown the horns of this male will be about one metre wide.

The largest mammal to live in the highest altitudes are the [alpine ibex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_ibex), which have been sighted as high as 3,000 m (9,800 ft). The ibex live in caves and descend to eat the succulent alpine grasses.[^cite_note-98] Classified as [antelopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antelopes),[^cite_note-Reynolds43ff-13] [chamois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamois) are smaller than ibex and found throughout the Alps, living above the tree line and are common in the entire alpine range.[^cite_note-99] Areas of the eastern Alps are still home to brown bears. In Switzerland the [canton of Bern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canton_of_Bern) was named for the bears but the last bear is recorded as having been killed in 1792 above [Kleine Scheidegg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleine_Scheidegg) by three hunters from [Grindelwald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grindelwald).[^cite_note-100]

Many rodents such as [voles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voles) live underground. [Marmots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marmots) live almost exclusively above the tree line as high as 2,700 m (8,900 ft). They hibernate in large groups to provide warmth,[^cite_note-Shoumatoff102ff-101] and can be found in all areas of the Alps, in large colonies they build beneath the alpine pastures.[^cite_note-Reynolds43ff-13] [Golden eagles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_eagle) and [bearded vultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bearded_vulture) are the largest birds to be found in the Alps; they nest high on rocky ledges and can be found at altitudes of 2,400 m (7,900 ft). The most common bird is the [alpine chough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_chough) which can be found scavenging at climber's huts or the [Jungfraujoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungfraujoch), a high-altitude tourist destination.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff97ff-102]



The alpine [Apollo butterfly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_butterfly) has adapted to alpine conditions.

Reptiles such as [adders and vipers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viperidae) live up to the snow line; because they cannot bear the cold temperatures they hibernate underground and soak up the warmth on rocky ledges.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff95ff-103] The high-altitude [Alpine salamanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_salamander) have adapted to living above the snow line by giving birth to fully developed young rather than laying eggs. [Brown trout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_trout) can be found in the streams up to the snow line.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff95ff-103] Molluscs such as the [wood snail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helicigona) live up the snow line. Popularly gathered as food, the [snails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snail) are now protected.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff88ff-104]

Several species of [moths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moth) live in the Alps, some of which are believed to have evolved in the same habitat up to 120 million years ago, long before the Alps were created. [Blue butterflies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycaenidae) can commonly be seen drinking from the snowmelt; some species of blues fly as high as 1,800 m (5,900 ft).[^cite_note-105] The butterflies tend to be large, such as those from the swallowtail *[Parnassius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parnassius)* family, with a habitat that ranges to 1,800 m (5,900 ft). Twelve species of beetles have habitats up to the snow line; the most beautiful and formerly collected for its colours but now protected is *[Rosalia alpina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosalia_alpina)*.[^cite_note-106] Spiders, such as the large [wolf spider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_spider), live above the snow line and can be seen as high as 400 m (1,300 ft). [Scorpions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scorpion) can be found in the Italian Alps.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff88ff-104]

Some of the species of moths and insects show evidence of having been indigenous to the area from as long ago as the Alpine orogeny. In [Émosson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lac_d'%C3%89mosson) in Valais, Switzerland, dinosaur tracks were found in the 1970s, dating probably from the [Triassic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triassic) Period.[^cite_note-107]

## History

Main article: [History of the Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Alps)

### Prehistory



[Petroglyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroglyphs), [Rock Drawings in Valcamonica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_Drawings_in_Valcamonica), Italy, which was recognised by [UNESCO](21786641.md) in 1979 and was Italy's first recognised [World Heritage Site](44940.md)



Reconstruction of [Ötzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%96tzi) mummy as shown in [Alpes-de-Haute-Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpes-de-Haute-Provence), France. The original mummy and his remains and personal belongings are on exhibit at the [South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Tyrol_Museum_of_Archaeology) in [Bolzano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolzano), [South Tyrol](169611.md), Italy.

When the ice melted after the [Würm glaciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W%C3%BCrm_glaciation), [Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleolithic) settlements were established along the lake shores and in cave systems. Evidence of human habitation has been found in caves near the [Vercors Cave System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vercors_Cave_System), close to Grenoble and Echirolles. In [Austria](26964606.md), the [Mondsee lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mondsee_\(lake\)) shows evidence of houses built on piles. Standing stones have been found in the Alpine areas of [France](5843419.md) and [Italy](14532.md). About 200,000 drawings and etchings have been documented, and are known as the [Rock Drawings in Valcamonica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_Drawings_in_Valcamonica).[^cite_note-Beattie25ff-108]

A mummy of a [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic) human, known as [Ötzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%96tzi), was discovered on the [Similaun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similaun). His clothing lets modern people assume that he was an alpine farmer, while the location and manner of his death suggests that Ötzi was travelling.[^cite_note-Beattie21ff-109] Analysis of the [mitochondrial DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondrial_DNA) of Ötzi, has shown that he belongs to the K1 [subclade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subclade).[^cite_note-110] His remains and personal belongings are on exhibit at the [South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Tyrol_Museum_of_Archaeology) in [Bolzano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolzano), [South Tyrol](169611.md), Italy.

From the 13th to the 6th century BC much of the Alps was settled by the [Germanic peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_peoples), [Lombards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lombards), [Alemanni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemanni), [Bavarii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bavarii), and [Franks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franks).[^cite_note-111] [Celt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celt) tribes settled in modern-day [Switzerland](26748.md) between 1500 and 1000 BC. The [Raeti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raeti) lived in the eastern regions, while the west was occupied by the [Helvetii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helvetii) and the [Allobroges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allobroges) settled in the Rhône valley and in [Savoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savoy). The [Ligures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligures) and [Adriatic Veneti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adriatic_Veneti) lived in [Northwest Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Italy) and [Triveneto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triveneto) respectively. The Celts mined [salt](1605200.md) in areas such as [Salzburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salzburg), where evidence was found of the [Hallstatt culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallstatt_culture).[^cite_note-Beattie25ff-108] By the 6th century BC the [La Tène culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_T%C3%A8ne_culture) was well established in the region,[^cite_note-112] and became known for high quality [Celtic art](2082902.md).[^cite_note-113]

Between 430 and 400 BC prolonged warfare in the Alps resulted in the devastation of agricultural land and human settlements, ultimately triggering the [enslavement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enslavement) of men, women, and children, goods had to be imported as a result. The [Etruscan civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscan_civilization) responded to raids by the [Massalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massalia) and acquired absolute control over the Alpine trade routes. Aggressors in modern-day Italy were dealt with and an alliance was formed with the Celts. The grip of the Etruscan settlements broke down, as the Roman political system expanded, so as to take control over Alpine trade routes that connected human settlements in the Alps with settlements in the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean).[^cite_note-114]

During the [Second Punic War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Punic_War) in 218 BC, the [Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carthage) general [Hannibal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannibal) initiated one of the most celebrated achievements of any military force in ancient warfare, recorded as [Hannibal crossing the Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannibal_crossing_the_Alps).[^cite_note-Lancel-115] The [Roman people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_people) built roads along the Alpine mountain passes, which continued to be used through the medieval period. Roman road markers can still be found on the Alpine mountain passes.[^cite_note-116]

During the [Gallic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallic_Wars) in 58 BC [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar) defeated the [Helvetii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helvetii). The [Rhaetian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhaetian_people) continued to resist but their territory was eventually conquered when the Romans crossed the [Danube](21209639.md) valley and defeated the [Brigantes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigantes).[^cite_note-Beattie27ff-117] The Romans built settlements in the Alps. In towns such as [Aosta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aosta), [Martigny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martigny), [Lausanne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lausanne), and [Partenkirchen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partenkirchen) remains of villas, arenas, and temples have been discovered.[^cite_note-118]

### Christianity, feudalism, and Napoleonic wars



Napoleon passing the Great St Bernard Pass, by [Edouard Castres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edouard_Castres)

Christianity was established in the Alps by the [Roman people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_people). Monasteries and churches were constructed, even at high Alpine altitudes. The [Franks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franks) expanded their [Carolingian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolingian_Empire), while the [Baiuvarii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baiuvarii) introduced [feudalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feudalism) in the eastern Alps. The construction of castles in the Alps supported the growing number of dukedoms and kingdoms. Castello del Buonconsiglio in [Trento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trento), still has intricate frescoes, and excellent examples of [Gothic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_art). The [Château de Chillon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C3%A2teau_de_Chillon) is preserved as an example of medieval architecture.[^cite_note-119] There are several important alpine saints and one such one is [Saint Maurice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Maurice).[^cite_note-120] Much of the medieval period was a time of power struggles between competing dynasties such as the [House of Savoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Savoy), the [Visconti of Milan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visconti_of_Milan), and the [House of Habsburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Habsburg).[^cite_note-121]

The [Great St Bernard Hospice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_St_Bernard_Hospice), built in the 9th or 10th centuries, at the summit of the [Great Saint Bernard Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Saint_Bernard_Pass) was a shelter for humans and destination for pilgrims.[^cite_note-122] In 1291, to protect themselves from incursions by the House of Habsburg, four Alpine [cantons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantons) drew up the [Federal Charter of 1291](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Charter_of_1291), which is considered to be a declaration of independence from neighbouring kingdoms. After a series of battles fought in the 13th, 14th, and 15th centuries, more cantons joined the confederacy and by the 16th century, [Switzerland](26748.md) was established as a [sovereign state](23604120.md).[^cite_note-123]

In the Alps, the [War of the Spanish Succession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Spanish_Succession) fallout resulted in a 1713 [treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty), part of the [Peace of Utrecht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_of_Utrecht), which relocated the Western Alps border along the watersheds. Historically, the Alps were used to determine the borders of political and administrative gangs, but the Peace of Utrecht was the first significant body of treaty that considered geographical conditions. The Alps were carved up and borders were agreed, so that [enclaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enclave) in the Alps could be eliminated.[^cite_note-124]

During the [Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars) in the late 18th century and early 19th century, [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon) annexed territory formerly controlled by the House of Habsburg, and the House of Savoy. In 1798, the [Helvetic Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helvetic_Republic) was established, two years later an army across the [Great St Bernard Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_St_Bernard_Pass).[^cite_note-125] In 1799 the Russian imperial military engaged the revolutionary French army in the Alps, this episode has been recorded as significant achievement in [mountain warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_warfare).[^cite_note-126] In October 1799 the troops commanded by [Alexander Suvorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Suvorov) were surrounded in the Alps by much larger French troops. The Russian troops broke out, mauled the French troops, and retreated through the [Panix Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panix_Pass).[^cite_note-127]

After the fall of Napoleon, many alpine countries developed heavy protections to prevent further invasion. Thus, [Savoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savoy) built a series of fortifications to protect the major alpine passes, such as the [col du Mont-Cenis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Col_du_Mont-Cenis), which was crossed by [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne) to obliterate the [Lombards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lombards).

In the 19th century, the monasteries built in the Alps to shelter humans became tourist destinations. The [Benedictines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedictine) had built monasteries in [Lucerne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucerne), and [Oberammergau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oberammergau). The [Cistercians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cistercian) built their temple at [Lake Constance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Constance). Meanwhile, the [Augustinians](144980.md) maintained abbeys in [Savoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savoy) and one in [Interlaken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlaken).[^cite_note-128]

### Exploration

Main article: [Exploration of the High Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration_of_the_High_Alps)

[Radiocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiocarbon_dating)-dated charcoal placed around 50,000 years ago was found in the *Drachloch* (Dragon's Hole) cave above the village of Vattis in the [canton of St. Gallen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canton_of_St._Gallen), proving that the high peaks were visited by prehistoric people. Seven bear skulls from the cave may have been buried by the same prehistoric people.[^cite_note-129] The peaks, however, were mostly ignored except for a few notable examples, and long left to the exclusive attention of the people of the adjoining valleys.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECoolidgeLakeKnox1911748-130][^cite_note-Shoumatoff188ff-131] The mountain peaks were seen as terrifying, the abode of dragons and demons, to the point that people blindfolded themselves to cross the Alpine passes.[^cite_note-132] The glaciers remained a mystery and many still believed the highest areas to be inhabited by dragons.[^cite_note-133]

[Charles VII of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_VII_of_France) ordered his [chamberlain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamberlain_\(office\)) to climb [Mont Aiguille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mont_Aiguille) in 1356. The knight reached the summit of [Rocciamelone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocciamelone) where he left a bronze triptych of three crosses, a feat which he conducted with the use of ladders to traverse the ice.[^cite_note-134] In 1492, Antoine de Ville climbed Mont Aiguille, without reaching the summit, an experience he described as "horrifying and terrifying."[^cite_note-Shoumatoff188ff-131] [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci) was fascinated by variations of light in the higher altitudes, and climbed a mountain—scholars are uncertain which one; some believe it may have been [Monte Rosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Rosa). From his description of a "blue like that of a gentian" sky it is thought that he reached a significantly high altitude.[^cite_note-135] In the 18th century four [Chamonix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamonix) men almost made the summit of Mont Blanc but were overcome by altitude sickness and snowblindness.[^cite_note-136]



[Horace Bénédict de Saussure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace_B%C3%A9n%C3%A9dict_de_Saussure) shown in, Descent from Mont-Blanc, by [Christian von Mechel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_von_Mechel)

[Conrad Gessner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conrad_Gessner) was the first naturalist to ascend the mountains in the 16th century, to study them, writing that in the mountains he found the "theatre of the Lord".[^cite_note-137] By the 19th century more naturalists began to arrive to explore, study and conquer the high peaks.[^cite_note-Fleming_vii-138] Two men who first explored the regions of ice and snow were [Horace-Bénédict de Saussure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace-B%C3%A9n%C3%A9dict_de_Saussure) (1740–1799) in the Pennine Alps,[^cite_note-139] and the Benedictine monk of [Disentis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disentis) [Placidus a Spescha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placidus_a_Spescha) (1752–1833).[^cite_note-Fleming_vii-138] Born in Geneva, Saussure was enamoured with the mountains from an early age; he left a law career to become a naturalist and spent many years trekking through the Bernese Oberland, the Savoy, the Piedmont and Valais, studying the glaciers and geology, as he became an early proponent of the theory of rock upheaval.[^cite_note-140] Saussure, in 1787, was a member of the third ascent of Mont Blanc—today the summits of all the peaks have been climbed.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff197ff-37]

### The Romantics and Alpinists



*[Wanderer above the Sea of Fog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wanderer_above_the_Sea_of_Fog)* by [Caspar David Friedrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caspar_David_Friedrich)

[Albrecht von Haller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albrecht_von_Haller)'s poem *Die Alpen*, published in 1732 described the mountains as an area of mythical purity.[^cite_note-141] [Jean-Jacques Rousseau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau) presented the Alps as a place of allure and beauty, in his novel *[Julie, or the New Heloise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julie,_or_the_New_Heloise)*, published in 1761. Later the first wave of [Romanticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism) such as [Johann Wolfgang von Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_von_Goethe), and [J. M. W. Turner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._M._W._Turner) came to admire the Alpine scenery;[^cite_note-142] [Wordsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Wordsworth) visited the area in 1790, writing of his experiences in *[The Prelude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prelude)* (1799). Schiller later wrote the play *[William Tell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Tell_\(play\))* (1804), which tells the story of the legendary Swiss marksman [William Tell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Tell) as part of the greater Swiss struggle for independence from the [Habsburg Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Habsburg) in the early 14th century. At the end of the [Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars), the Alpine countries began to see an influx of poets, artists, and musicians,[^cite_note-Fleming_2000,_83-143] as visitors came to experience the [sublime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublime_\(philosophy\)) effects of monumental nature.[^cite_note-144]

In 1816, [Byron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Byron), [Percy Bysshe Shelley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_Bysshe_Shelley) and his wife [Mary Shelley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Shelley) visited Geneva and all three were inspired by the scenery in their writings.[^cite_note-Fleming_2000,_83-143] During these visits, Shelley wrote the poem "[Mont Blanc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mont_Blanc_\(poem\))", Byron wrote "[The Prisoner of Chillon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prisoner_of_Chillon)" and the dramatic poem *[Manfred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manfred_\(drama\))*, and Mary Shelley, who found the scenery overwhelming, conceived the idea for the novel *[Frankenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankenstein)* in her villa on the shores of Lake Geneva amid a thunderstorm. When [Coleridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Taylor_Coleridge) travelled to [Chamonix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamonix), he declaimed, in defiance of Shelley, who had signed himself "Atheos" in the guestbook of the Hotel de Londres near Montenvers,[^cite_note-145] "Who would be, who could be an atheist in this valley of wonders".[^cite_note-146]

By the mid-19th century, scientists began to arrive en masse to study the geology and ecology of the region.[^cite_note-147]

The tourism and mountaineering development of the Alps began at the beginning of the 19th century. In the early years of the [golden age of alpinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_age_of_alpinism), scientific activities were mixed with sport, for example by the physicist [John Tyndall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tyndall), with the first ascent of the Matterhorn by [Edward Whymper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Whymper) being the highlight. In the later years, the [silver age of alpinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_age_of_alpinism), the focus was on mountain sports and climbing. The first president of the [Alpine Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_Club_\(UK\)), [John Ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ball_\(naturalist\)), is considered the discoverer of the Dolomites, which for decades were the focus of climbers like [Paul Grohmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Grohmann), [Michael Innerkofler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Innerkofler) and [Angelo Dibona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angelo_Dibona).[^cite_note-148][^cite_note-149][^cite_note-150]

### The Nazis



The Nazis hid looted art in salt mines at [Altaussee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altaussee), such as the [Early Netherlandish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Netherlandish_art) *[Ghent Altarpiece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghent_Altarpiece)* which sustained significant damage.

In autumn 1932, [Adolf Hitler](2731583.md) commissioned the first of a series of refurbishments, which eventually turned a mountain cottage, later named [Berghof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berghof_\(residence\)), into a fortified [citadel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citadel). This domestic, but representative, fortification had two small bedrooms, and a full bathroom, planned by the Munich architect and NSDAP member [Josef Neumaier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josef_Neumaier?action=edit\&redlink=1). Guests, such as [Rudolf Hess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Hess), stayed over, sleeping in tents or over the garage.[^cite_note-151]

The Alps, Adolf Hitler, and improbable powerful organisations have been subject to [crime fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_fiction).[^cite_note-152] The Alps acted as a geographical barrier to [Italy](14532.md), and the Alps for centuries were permeated with established smuggling routes, known as *green line*. After World War II, members of the [Schutzstaffel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schutzstaffel) that feared prosecution as [war criminals](48626.md), known in modern English only as *SS*, disappeared into a crowd of [refugees](45547.md).[^cite_note-153] Massive numbers of refugees entered Italy illegally, by navigating the Alps.[^cite_note-154]

There were [Ratlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratlines_\(World_War_II\)) (i.e. one through Spain and the second through Rome and Genoa) used by German Nazis and other fascists to flee Europe from 1945 onwards.

### Undocumented migrants

[Smugglers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smugglers) of humans have claimed that crossing the Alps is less dangerous, or deadly, than travelling 355 km on water between [Tripoli](31232.md) and [Lampedusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lampedusa) with a [tramp ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tramp_ship) (*carretta del mare*) or a [dinghy](135131.md). Some undocumented migrants, visa overstayers, false tourists, asylum seekers, and other clandestine humans, have died crossing the Alps. Their exact number can only be estimated.[^cite_note-155] Today, people with smartphones can plan their travel routes, find their way around with Google Maps, and find information about travelling routes on social media.

### Alpine people and culture

Further information: [Transhumance in the Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transhumance_in_the_Alps) and [Swiss folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_folklore)

The population of the region is 14 million spread across eight countries.[^cite_note-Chatré8-7] On the rim of the mountains, on the plateaus, and on the plains the economy consists of manufacturing and service jobs whereas in the higher altitudes and the mountains farming is still essential to the economy.[^cite_note-156] Farming and forestry continue to be mainstays of Alpine culture, industries that provide for export to the cities and maintain the mountain ecology.[^cite_note-Chartes_et._el._2010,_5-157]

The Alpine regions are multicultural and linguistically diverse. Dialects are common and vary from valley to valley and region to region. In the Slavic Alps alone 19 dialects have been identified. Some of the Romance dialects spoken in the French, Swiss and Italian alps of [Aosta Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aosta_Valley) derive from [Arpitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arpitan), while the southern part of the western range is related to [Occitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occitan_language); the German dialects derive from Germanic tribal languages.[^cite_note-Shoumataff_2001,_114–166-158] [Romansh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romansh_language), spoken by two percent of the population in southeast Switzerland, is an ancient [Rhaeto-Romanic language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhaeto-Romance_languages) derived from Latin, remnants of ancient Celtic languages and perhaps [Etruscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscan_language).[^cite_note-Shoumataff_2001,_114–166-158]



[Hallstatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallstatt) is known for its production of salt, dating back to prehistoric times ([Hallstatt culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallstatt_culture))&#x20;

Much of the Alpine culture is unchanged since hundreds of years when skills that guaranteed survival in the mountain valleys and the highest villages became mainstays, leading to strong traditions of carpentry, woodcarving, baking, pastry-making, and cheesemaking.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff123ff-159]

Farming has been a traditional occupation for centuries, although it became less dominant in the 20th century with the advent of tourism. Grazing and pasture land are limited because of the steep and rocky topography of the Alps. In mid-June, cows are moved to the highest pastures close to the snowline, where they are watched by herdsmen who stay in the high altitudes often living in stone huts or wooden barns during the summers.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff123ff-159] Villagers celebrate the day the cows are herded up to the pastures and again when they return in mid-September. The [Almabtrieb, Alpabzug, Alpabfahrt, Désalpes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almabtrieb) ("coming down from the alps") is celebrated by decorating the cows with garlands and enormous [cowbells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cowbell) while the farmers dress in traditional costumes.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff123ff-159]



In the summers the cows are brought up to the high mountain meadows for grazing. Small summer villages such as the one shown in this photograph taken in [Savoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savoy) are used.

Cheesemaking is an ancient tradition in most Alpine countries. A wheel of cheese from the [Emmental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmental) in Switzerland can weigh up to 45 kg (100 lb), and the [Beaufort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaufort_cheese) in [Savoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savoy) can weigh up to 70 kg (150 lb). Owners of the cows traditionally receive from the cheesemakers a portion about the proportion of the cows' milk from the summer months in the high alps. Haymaking is an important farming activity in mountain villages that have become somewhat mechanised in recent years, although the slopes are so steep that scythes are usually necessary to cut the grass. Hay is normally brought in twice a year, often also on festival days.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff123ff-159]

In the high villages, people live in homes built according to medieval designs that withstand cold winters. The kitchen is separated from the living area (called the *stube*, the area of the home heated by a stove), and second-floor bedrooms benefit from rising heat. The typical Swiss [chalet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalet) originated in the [Bernese Oberland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernese_Oberland). Chalets often face south or downhill and are built of solid wood, with a steeply gabled roof to allow accumulated snow to slide off easily. Stairs leading to upper levels are sometimes built on the outside, and balconies are sometimes enclosed.[^cite_note-Shoumatoff123ff-159][^cite_note-160]



Herding sheep in Austria

Food is passed from the kitchen to the stube, where the dining room table is placed. Some meals are communal, such as [fondue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fondue), where a pot is set in the middle of the table for each person to dip into. Other meals are still served traditionally on carved wooden plates. Furniture has been traditionally elaborately carved and in many Alpine countries, carpentry skills are passed from generation to generation.



Alpine chalet being built in Haute-[Maurienne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurienne) (Savoy). The use of thick pieces of [orthogneiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogneiss) (4–7 cm) is by the strict architectural regulations in the region bordering the national parks of Vanoise-Grand Paradis.

Roofs are traditionally constructed from Alpine rocks such as pieces of [schist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schist), gneiss, or [slate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slate).[^cite_note-161] Such chalets are typically found in the higher parts of the valleys, as in the [Maurienne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurienne) valley in [Savoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savoy), where the amount of snow during the cold months is important. The inclination of the roof cannot exceed 40%, allowing the snow to stay on top, thereby functioning as insulation from the cold.[^cite_note-162] In the lower areas where the forests are widespread, wooden tiles are traditionally used. Commonly made of [Norway spruce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway_spruce), they are called "tavaillon".

In the German-speaking parts of the Alps ([Austria](26964606.md), [Bavaria](3764.md), [South Tyrol](169611.md), [Liechtenstein](17810.md) and [Switzerland](26748.md)) and also [Slovenia](27338.md), there is a strong tradition of Alpine [folk culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_culture). Old traditions are carefully maintained among inhabitants of Alpine areas, even though this is seldom obvious to the visitor: many people are members of cultural associations where the Alpine folk culture is cultivated. At cultural events, traditional [folk costume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_costume) (in German *[Tracht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tracht)*) is expected: typically [lederhosen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lederhosen) for men and [dirndls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirndl) for women. Visitors can get a glimpse of the rich customs of the Alps at public [Volksfeste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volksfest). Even when large events feature only a little folk culture, all participants take part with gusto. Good opportunities to see local people celebrating the traditional culture occur at the many fairs, wine festivals, and firefighting festivals which fill weekends in the countryside from spring to autumn. Alpine festivals vary from country to country. Frequently they include music (e.g. the playing of [Alpenhorns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpenhorn)), dance (e.g. [Schuhplattler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schuhplattler)), sports (e.g. wrestling marches and archery), as well as traditions with pagan roots such as the lighting of fires on [Walpurgis Night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walpurgis_Night) and [Saint John's Eve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_John's_Eve). Many areas celebrate [Fastnacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swabian-Alemannic_Fastnacht) in the weeks before [Lent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lent). Folk costume also continues to be worn for most weddings and festivals.[^cite_note-163][^cite_note-marcopolo1-164]

### Tourism

Further information: [List of national parks in the Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_national_parks_in_the_Alps), [Tourism in Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_Austria), [Tourism in France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_France), [Tourism in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_Germany), [Tourism in Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_Italy), and [Tourism in Switzerland](9023823.md)



[Karl Schranz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Schranz) running the [Lauberhorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lauberhorn) in 1966

The Alps are one of the more popular tourist destinations in the world with many resorts such as [Oberstdorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oberstdorf), in Bavaria, [Saalbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saalbach-Hinterglemm) in Austria, [Davos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davos) in Switzerland, [Chamonix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamonix) in France, and [Cortina d'Ampezzo](568829.md) in Italy recording more than a million annual visitors. With over 120 million visitors a year, tourism is integral to the Alpine economy with much of it coming from winter sports, although summer visitors are also an important component.[^cite_note-Bart-165]

The tourism industry began in the early 19th century when foreigners visited the Alps, travelled to the bases of the mountains to enjoy the scenery, and stayed at the spa-resorts. Large hotels were built during the [Belle Époque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belle_%C3%89poque); [cog-railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rack_railway), built early in the 20th century, brought tourists to ever-higher elevations, with the [Jungfraubahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungfraubahn) terminating at the Jungfraujoch, well above the eternal snow-line, after going through a tunnel in Eiger. During this period winter sports were slowly introduced: in 1882 the first [figure skating](11152.md) championship was held in [St. Moritz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Moritz), and downhill skiing became a popular sport with English visitors early in the 20th century,[^cite_note-Bart-165] as the first ski-lift was installed in 1908 above Grindelwald.[^cite_note-166]

In the first half of the 20th century the Olympic Winter Games were held three times in Alpine venues: the [1924 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1924_Winter_Olympics) in Chamonix, France; the [1928 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1928_Winter_Olympics) in St. Moritz, Switzerland; and the [1936 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_Winter_Olympics) in [Garmisch-Partenkirchen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garmisch-Partenkirchen), Germany. During World War II the winter games were cancelled but after that time the Winter Games have been held in [St. Moritz (1948)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_Winter_Olympics), [Cortina d'Ampezzo (1956)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1956_Winter_Olympics), [Innsbruck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innsbruck), Austria (1964 and 1976), [Grenoble](50972.md), France, (1968), [Albertville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albertville), France, (1992), and [Turin (2006)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Winter_Olympics).[^cite_note-167] In 1930, the *Lauberhorn Rennen* (Lauberhorn Race), was run for the first time on the [Lauberhorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lauberhorn) above [Wengen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wengen);[^cite_note-168] the equally demanding [Hahnenkamm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hahnenkamm,_Kitzb%C3%BChel) was first run in the same year in [Kitzbühel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitzb%C3%BChel), Austria.[^cite_note-169] Both races continue to be held each January on successive weekends. The Lauberhorn is the more strenuous downhill race at 4.5 km (2.8 mi) and poses danger to racers who reach 130 km/h (81 mph) within seconds of leaving the start gate.[^cite_note-170]

During the post-World War I period, ski lifts were built in Swiss and Austrian towns to accommodate winter visitors, and summer tourism continued to be important. By the mid-20th century the popularity of downhill skiing increased greatly as it became more accessible and in the 1970s several new villages were built in France devoted almost exclusively to skiing, such as [Les Menuires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Menuires). Until this point, Austria and Switzerland had been the traditional and more popular destinations for winter sports, and by the end of the 20th century and into the early 21st century, France, Italy, and Tyrol began to see increases in winter visitors.[^cite_note-Bart-165] Since the 1980s tourism expansion and easy global access generate grave concerns regarding the loss of traditional Alpine culture and many uncertainties about sustainable development.[^cite_note-171] As a likely result of climatic change, the number of high altitude ski resorts and piste km is in decline since 2015, with snow-making machines installed at many sites.[^cite_note-172]

### Transportation



[Zentralbahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zentralbahn) Interregio train following the [Lake Brienz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Brienz) shoreline, near [Niederried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niederried_bei_Interlaken) in Switzerland

The region is serviced by 4,200 km (2,600 mi) of roads used by six million vehicles per year.[^cite_note-Chatré8-7] Train travel is well established in the Alps, with, for instance 120 km (75 mi) of track for every 1,000 km<sup>2</sup> (390 sq mi) in a country such as Switzerland.[^cite_note-173] Most of [Europe's highest railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_highest_railways_in_Europe) are located there. In 2007, the new 34.57 km-long (21.48 mi) [Lötschberg Base Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%B6tschberg_Base_Tunnel) was opened, which circumvents the 100 years older [Lötschberg Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%B6tschberg_Tunnel). With the opening of the 57.1 km-long (35.5 mi) [Gotthard Base Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gotthard_Base_Tunnel) on 1 June 2016, it bypasses the [Gotthard Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gotthard_Tunnel) built in the 19th century and realises the first flat route through the Alps.[^cite_note-174]

Some high mountain villages are [car-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedestrian_zone) either because of inaccessibility or by choice. [Wengen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wengen), and [Zermatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermatt) (in Switzerland) are accessible only by [cable car](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerial_lift) or [cog-rail trains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rack_railway). [Avoriaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avoriaz) (in France), is car-free, with other Alpine villages considering becoming car-free zones or limiting the number of cars for reasons of sustainability of the fragile Alpine terrain.[^cite_note-175]

The lower regions and larger towns of the Alps are well-served by motorways and main roads, but higher mountain passes and byroads, which are amongst the [highest in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_highest_paved_roads_in_Europe), can be treacherous even in summer due to steep slopes. Many passes are closed in winter. Several airports around the Alps (and some within), as well as long-distance rail links from all neighbouring countries, afford large numbers of travellers easy access.[^cite_note-Chatré8-7]
