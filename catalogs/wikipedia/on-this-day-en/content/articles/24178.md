**Palm Sunday** is the Christian [moveable feast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moveable_feast) that falls on the [Sunday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunday) before [Easter](9325.md). The feast commemorates [Jesus Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_Christ)'s [triumphal entry into Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triumphal_entry_into_Jerusalem), as described in all four [canonical Gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_Gospels),[^cite_note-3] and takes its name from the [palm branches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm_branch) waved by the crowd to greet and honor Jesus as he entered the city. Palm Sunday marks the first day of [Holy Week](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Week); in [Western Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Christianity), this is the beginning of the last week of the solemn season of [Lent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lent), preceding [Eastertide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastertide),[^cite_note-Cooper2013-4][^cite_note-Brewer1896-5][^cite_note-Blackwell2009-6][^cite_note-Melton2011-7] while in [Eastern Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Christianity), Holy Week commences after the conclusion of [Great Lent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lent).[^cite_note-8]

In most [Christian rites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_rite), Palm Sunday is celebrated by the blessing and distribution of palm branches (or the branches of other native trees), representing the palm branches that the crowd scattered before Christ as he rode into [Jerusalem](16043.md). These palms are sometimes woven into [crosses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_cross). The difficulty of procuring palms in unfavorable climates led to the substitution of branches of native trees, including [box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buxus_sempervirens), [olive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive), [willow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willow), and [yew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxus).

Many churches of mainstream [Christian denominations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_denominations), including the [Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy), [Catholic](606848.md), [Lutheran](23371382.md), [Moravian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravian_Church), [Reformed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformed_Christianity), [Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglicanism) and [Methodist](20119.md) traditions, distribute palm branches to their congregations during their Palm Sunday liturgies. Christians take these palms, which are often blessed by clergy, to their homes, where they hang them alongside [Christian art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_art) (especially [crosses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_cross) and [crucifixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crucifix)) or keep them in their Bibles and [daily devotional books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daily_devotional).[^cite_note-Kirk2018-9] In the days preceding the next year's Lent in Western Christianity, known as [Carnival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnival) or Shrovetide, churches often place a basket in their [narthex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narthex) to collect these palms, which are then ritually burned on [Shrove Tuesday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrove_Tuesday) to make the ashes to be used on the following day, [Ash Wednesday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ash_Wednesday), which is the first day of Lent.[^cite_note-GEC2018-10][^cite_note-11] In Eastern Christianity, where Ash Wednesday is non-existent, it is typical to return the Palms the following Palm Sunday prior to receiving new Palms.

Main article: [Triumphal entry into Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triumphal_entry_into_Jerusalem)



*[Triumphal entry into Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triumphal_entry_into_Jerusalem)*, Russian icon ([Cathedral of the Annunciation, Moscow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_the_Annunciation,_Moscow))

In the accounts of the four [canonical Gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_Gospel) in the Bible,[^cite_note-12] Christ's [triumphal entry into Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triumphal_entry_into_Jerusalem) takes place a week before his [resurrection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resurrection_of_Jesus).[^cite_note-Boring256-13][^cite_note-CEvans381-14][^cite_note-M133-15][^cite_note-CAEvans114-16] Only the Gospel of John shows a timeline of the event, dated six days before the [Passover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passover).[^cite_note-17]

The [raising of Lazarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raising_of_Lazarus) is mentioned only by the Gospel of John, in the previous chapter. The [Eastern Orthodox Church](10186.md) and the [Eastern Catholic Churches](214903.md) which follows the [Byzantine Rite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Rite), commemorate it on [Lazarus Saturday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lazarus_Saturday), following the text of the Gospel. In fact, the Jewish calendar dates begin at [sundown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunset) of the night beforehand, and conclude at [nightfall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dusk).[^cite_note-18]

The [Gospel of Matthew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Matthew) states that this happened that the prophecy might be fulfilled of: Zechariah 9\:9[^cite_note-19] "The Coming of Zion's King – See, your king comes to you, righteous and victorious, lowly and riding on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey".[^cite_note-20] It suggests that Jesus was declaring he was the [King of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus,_King_of_the_Jews).

According to the Gospels, Jesus Christ rode on [a donkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Messiah's_Donkey) into Jerusalem, and the celebrating people there laid down their cloaks and small branches of trees in front of him, singing part of Psalm 118: 25–26[^cite_note-21] – Blessed is He who comes in the name of the Lord. We bless you from the house of the Lord.[^cite_note-MacArthur17-2][^cite_note-Boring256-13][^cite_note-CEvans381-14][^cite_note-M133-15]

The symbolism of the donkey may refer to the Eastern tradition that it is an animal of peace, unlike the horse which is the animal of war.[^cite_note-Allison120-1] A king would have ridden a horse when he was bent on war and ridden a donkey to symbolize his arrival in peace. Christ's entry to Jerusalem would have thus symbolized his entry as the [Prince of Peace](1095706.md), not as a war-waging king.[^cite_note-Allison120-1][^cite_note-MacArthur17-2] Thus, there have been two different meanings (or more levels of [biblical hermeneutics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_hermeneutics)): a historical meaning, truly happening according to the Gospels, and a secondary meaning in the symbolism.



"Flevit super illam" (He wept over it); by [Enrique Simonet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrique_Simonet), 1892

In [Luke 19\:41](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luke_19#Verses_41%E2%80%9344) as Jesus approaches Jerusalem, he looks at the city and weeps over it (an event known as *Flevit super illam* in [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language)), foretelling his coming Passion and the suffering that awaits the city in the events of the destruction of the [Second Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Temple).[^cite_note-22]

In many lands in the ancient [Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_East), it was customary to cover in some way the path of someone thought worthy of the highest honour. The [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible)[^cite_note-23] reports that [Jehu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jehu), son of [Jehoshaphat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jehoshaphat), was treated this way. Both the [Synoptic Gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synoptic_Gospels) and the [Gospel of John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_John) report that people gave Jesus this form of honour. In the synoptics the people are described as laying their garments and cut [rushes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juncus) on the street, whereas John specifies fronds of palm (Greek *phoinix)*. In Jewish tradition, the palm is one of the [Four Species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Species) carried for [Sukkot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sukkot), as prescribed for rejoicing in Leviticus 23\:40.[^cite_note-24]

In the [Greco-Roman culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Roman_world) of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire), which strongly influenced Christian tradition, the [palm branch was a symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm_branch_\(symbol\)) of triumph and victory. It became the most common attribute of the [goddess Nike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nike_\(mythology\)) or [Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_\(mythology\)).[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-Vioque-26][^cite_note-27] For contemporary Roman observers, the procession would have evoked the [Roman triumph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_triumph),[^cite_note-28] when the *triumphator* laid down his arms and wore the [toga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toga), the civilian garment of peace that might be ornamented with emblems of the palm.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEVioque200261-29]

Although the [Epistles of Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistles_of_Paul) refer to Jesus as "triumphing", the entry into Jerusalem may not have been regularly pictured as a triumphal procession in this sense before the 13th century.[^cite_note-30] In [ancient Egyptian religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_religion), the palm was carried in funeral processions and represented eternal life. The [martyr's palm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martyr's_palm) was later used as a symbol of Christian martyrs and their spiritual victory or triumph over death.[^cite_note-31] In Revelation 7\:9, the white-clad multitude stand before the throne and [Lamb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamb_of_God) holding palm branches.[^cite_note-32]

### Eastern and Oriental Christianity

Palm Sunday, or the *Entry of the Lord into Jerusalem* as it may be called in [Orthodox Churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodoxy#Christianity), is one of the [Twelve Great Feasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_Great_Feasts) of the [liturgical year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liturgical_year). The day before Palm Sunday, [Lazarus Saturday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lazarus_Saturday), believers often prepare palm fronds by knotting them into crosses in preparation for the procession on Sunday. The [hangings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antependia) and [vestments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vestments) in the church are changed to a festive color – most commonly green.

The [Troparion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troparion) of the Feast (a short hymn) indicates that the resurrection of [Lazarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lazarus_of_Bethany) is a prefiguration of Christ's Resurrection:

> O Christ our God\
> &#x20;When Thou didst raise Lazarus from the dead before Thy Passion,\
> &#x20;Thou didst confirm the resurrection of the universe.\
> &#x20;Wherefore, we, like children,\
> &#x20;carry the banner of triumph and victory,\
> &#x20;and we cry to Thee, O Conqueror of death,\
> &#x20;Hosanna in the highest!\
> &#x20;Blessed is He that cometh\
> &#x20;in the Name of the Lord.

In the [Russian Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church), [Ukrainian Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Orthodox_Church_\(Moscow_Patriarchate\)), [Ukrainian Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Greek_Catholic_Church), [Eastern Lutheran Churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Lutheranism), [Ruthenian Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruthenian_Greek_Catholic_Church), [Polish](22936.md), [Bavarian](3764.md) and [Austrian](26964606.md) [Roman Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church), and various other [Eastern European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe) peoples, the custom developed of using [pussy willow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pussy_willow) and other twigs like box tree instead of palm fronds because the latter are not readily available that far north. There is no canonical requirement as to what kind of branches must be used, so some Orthodox believers use [olive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive) branches.

Whatever the kind, these branches are [blessed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blessing) and distributed together with candles either during the [All-Night Vigil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-Night_Vigil) on the Eve of the Feast (Saturday night), or before the [Divine Liturgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Liturgy) on Sunday morning. The [Great Entrance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Entrance) of the Divine Liturgy commemorates the "Entry of the Lord into Jerusalem", so the meaningfulness of this moment is punctuated on Palm Sunday as everyone stands, holding their branches and lit candles. The faithful take these branches and candles home with them after the liturgy, and keep them in their [icon corner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icon_corner) as an *evloghia* (blessing).

In Russia, [donkey walk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donkey_walk) processions took place in different cities, but most importantly in [Novgorod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novgorod) and, from 1558 until 1693, in Moscow. These were prominently featured in testimonies by foreign witnesses and mentioned in contemporary Western maps of the city. The [Patriarch of Moscow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_of_Moscow), representing Christ, rode on a "donkey", actually a horse draped in white cloth. The [Tsar of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsar_of_Russia) humbly led the procession on foot. Originally, Moscow processions began inside the [Kremlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_Kremlin) and terminated at Trinity Church, now known as [Saint Basil's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Basil's_Cathedral). In 1658 [Patriarch Nikon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_Nikon) reversed the order of procession. [Peter I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_I_of_Russia) in the 1720s, as a part of his [nationalisation of the church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_reform_of_Peter_I), terminated the custom. It has been occasionally recreated in the 21st century.

In [Oriental Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Orthodox) churches, palm fronds are distributed at the front of the church at the sanctuary steps. In India the sanctuary itself is strewn with marigolds, and the congregation proceeds through and outside the church.

### Western Christianity



An [Evangelical-Lutheran](23371382.md) Palm Sunday Mass in a congregation of the [Church of Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Sweden). Due to the cold climate in the Nordic countries, [Pussy Willow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pussy_willow) has traditionally been used as a substitute for palm fronds for decoration and processions.

In ancient times, palm branches symbolized goodness and victory. They were often depicted on coins and important buildings. [Solomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon) had palm branches carved into the walls and doors of the temple.[^cite_note-33] Again at the end of the Bible, people from every nation raise palm branches to honor Jesus.[^cite_note-34]

Palm Sunday commemorates the entrance of Christ into Jerusalem,[^cite_note-35] during which palm branches were placed in his path, before his arrest on Holy Thursday and his crucifixion on Good Friday. It thus marks the beginning of Holy Week, the final week of Lent.

In churches of many [Christian denominations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_denomination), members of the congregation, oftentimes children, are given palms that they carry as they walk in a procession around the inside of the church.[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37] In the [Church of Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Pakistan), a [united Protestant Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_and_uniting_churches), the faithful on Palm Sunday carry palm branches into the church as they sing Psalm 24.[^cite_note-38] A robust hymnody has developed for Palm Sunday, including "[Hosanna, Loud Hosanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeanette_Threlfall)", "[Ride On, Ride On in Majesty!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ride_On,_Ride_On_in_Majesty!)", "[All Glory, Laud and Honour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Glory,_Laud_and_Honour)", "[Palms of Victory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palms_of_Victory)", and "[Hark the glad sound! the Saviour comes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Ravenscroft)".[^cite_note-39]

In the [Roman Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church), as well as among many [Lutheran](23371382.md) and [Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican) congregations, palm fronds (or in colder climates some kind of substitutes) are [blessed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blessing) with [holy water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_water) outside the church building (or in cold climates in the [narthex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narthex) when Easter falls early in the year) in an event called the Blessing of the Palms.[^cite_note-Usalis2018-40] A solemn procession of the entire congregation takes place immediately after the blessing of the palms, called the Palm procession.

&#x20;In the Catholic, Lutheran, and Anglican traditions, this feast now coincides with that of [Passion Sunday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passion_Sunday), which is the focus of the [Mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_\(liturgy\)) which follows the procession.[^cite_note-Falk2014-41][^cite_note-Stephens2022-42] The Catholic Church considers the blessed palms to be [sacramentals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacramentals). The vestments for the day are deep scarlet red, the colour of blood, indicating the supreme redemptive sacrifice Christ was entering the city to make, to fulfill his passion and resurrection in Jerusalem.



*Palm Sunday* and other named days and day ranges around Lent and Easter in Western Christianity, with the fasting days of Lent numbered

In the Lutheran Churches, as well as in the [Episcopal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_Church_\(United_States\))/Anglican churches, the day is officially called *The Sunday of the Passion: Palm Sunday*; in practice, though, it is usually termed *Palm Sunday* as in the 1928 American [Book of Common Prayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Common_Prayer) and in earlier Lutheran liturgies and calendars, to avoid undue confusion with the penultimate Sunday of Lent in the traditional calendar, which was *Passion Sunday*.

&#x20;In traditional usage of the [Methodist Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodist_Church_\(USA\)), *[The Book of Worship for Church and Home](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Worship_for_Church_and_Home_\(1965\))* (1965) provides the following [Collect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collect) for Palm Sunday:[^cite_note-BOW1964-43]

> Almighty and everlasting God, who, of thy tender love toward mankind hast sent thy Son our Savior Jesus Christ to take upon him our flesh, and to suffer death upon the cross, that all mankind should follow the example of his great humility: Mercifully grant that we may both follow the example of his patience and also be made partakers of his resurrection; through the same Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.[^cite_note-BOW1964-43]

It is customary in many churches for worshippers to receive fresh palm leaves on Palm Sunday. In parts of the world where this has historically been impractical, substitute traditions have arisen.

### Belgium

In [Hoegaarden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoegaarden), one of the last remaining Palm Sunday processions takes place every year. A fellowship of Twelve Apostles carries a wooden statue of Christ around the town, while children go door to door offering the palms ([box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buxus)) for coins.[^cite_note-44]

### Bulgaria

In [Bulgaria](3415.md), Palm Sunday is known as *Tsvetnitsa* (*tsvete*, "flower") or *Vrabnitsa* (*varba*, "willow"), or [Flower's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower's_Day). People with flower-related names (e.g., Bilyan(a), Liliya, Margarita, Nevena, Ralitsa, Rosa, Temenuzhka, Tsvetan(a), Tsvetelin(a), Tsvetin(a), Tsvetko, Violeta, Yavor, Zdravko, Zjumbjul, etc.) celebrate this day as their [name day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Name_day).[^cite_note-45]

### England

In the 15th through the 17th centuries in England, Palm Sunday was frequently marked by the burning of [Jack o' Lent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_o'_Lent) figures. This was a straw [effigy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effigy) which would be stoned and abused on Ash Wednesday, and kept in the parish for burning on Palm Sunday. The symbolism was believed to be a kind of revenge on [Judas Iscariot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judas_Iscariot), who had betrayed Christ. The effigy could also have represented the hated figure of Winter, whose destruction prepares the way for Spring.[^cite_note-46]

### Egypt and Ethiopia

In the [Coptic Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_Orthodox_Church) and [Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Orthodox_Tewahedo_Church) [Ethiopia](187749.md), this holiday is referred to as Hosanna. Palm leaves will be blessed and distributed, they are used to create crucifixes, rings and other ornaments.

### Finland



[Easter witches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_witch) in Finland

In [Finland](10577.md), it is popular for children to dress up as [Easter witches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_witch) and go door to door in neighborhoods and trade decorated [pussy willow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pussy_willow) branches for coins and candy. This is an old [Karelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karelia) custom called *[virpominen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virpominen)*.

It is customary for the children to chant, with some variation, "Virvon varvon tuoreeks, terveeks, tulevaks vuodeks, vitsa sulle, palkka mulle!"[^cite_note-47] which very roughly translates as "I'm wishing you a fresh, healthy upcoming year, a branch for you, a prize for me!" The chant has been translated in [Juha Vuorinen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juha_Vuorinen)'s novel *Totally Smashed!* as "Willow switch, I'm the Easter witch! I wish you health and a love that's rich! From me I bring some luck today, for this branch what will you pay?"[^cite_note-48]

### Germany



*Palmesel*

In some regions of Germany, long stakes with pussy willow, box and other twigs are taken for the Palm procession rather than [nosegays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nosegays). In some Southern regions either the priest leads the palm procession, riding on a donkey, or a wooden donkey (called *Palmesel*) with a figure of Christ is traditionally trundled with the procession of the faithful.

### India

In most of the Christian churches in India, palms are blessed by the priest on Palm Sunday and are then distributed among the people after the celebration of the [Holy Mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_\(liturgy\)). There is a tradition of folding palm [fronds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fronds) into palm crosses.



Flowers (in this instance [marigolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagetes)) strewn about the sanctuary in an [Oriental Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Orthodox) church in [Mumbai](19189.md), India, on Palm Sunday

In the [South Indian](678583.md) state of [Kerala](4349459.md) (and in [Indian Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Orthodox), [Church of South India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_South_India) (CSI), [Syro-Malankara Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syro-Malankara_Catholic_Church), and [Syriac Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox_Church) (Jacobite) congregations elsewhere in India and throughout the world), flowers are strewn about the sanctuary on Palm Sunday during the reading of the Gospel, at the words uttered by the crowd welcoming Jesus, "Hosanna! Blessed is he who is come and is to come in the name of the Lord God". These words are read to the congregation thrice. The congregation then repeats, "Hosanna!", and the flowers are scattered, a common custom in Indian celebrations. This symbolizes Jesus' triumphant entry into Jerusalem.



Palm Sunday in [Malankara Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malankara_Orthodox_Church) ([Oriental Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Orthodox)) of [Kerala](4349459.md) – People holding tender leaves of coconut palms (kuruthola) and flowers are thrown upwards during Gospel reading

Indian Orthodoxy traces its roots to the arrival in India of Saint [Thomas the Apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_the_Apostle) (traditionally dated to AD 52) and his evangelism among both the Brahmans of the [Malabar Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malabar_Coast) and the ancient Jewish community there. Its rites and ceremonies are Jewish, Indian and Levantine Christian, in origin. In [Syro-Malabar Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syro-Malabar_Catholic_Church)'s palm leaves are blessed during Palm Sunday ceremony and a [Procession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procession) takes place holding the palms.[^cite_note-49]

### Ireland

In [Ireland](147575.md)'s cold climate, real palm leaves are generally not available, so [yew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxus_baccata), [silver fir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abies_alba), [spruce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picea_abies) or [cypress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupressus_sempervirens) are used instead; it is known in [Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language) as *Domhnach an Iúir*, "Yew Sunday".[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-auto-51] The historian [Patrick Weston Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Weston_Joyce) noted that yew was always called "palm" in his 1830s childhood, and he only later learned the tree's correct name. "Palm" branches were often worn in the [buttonhole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttonhole) or hung on the wall; the second practise is still common, and palm branches are blessed with [holy water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_water) at [Catholic Masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Mass).[^cite_note-52]

In the past in some areas, a palm stem was charred and a cross was marked on eggs set for hatching, while in parts of Counties Galway and Mayo, shredded palm was mixed through the seed grain. The coincidence of Palm Sunday and [Saint Patrick's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Patrick's_Day) (March 17), "when the [shamrock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamrock) and the palm are worn together", was said to presage a great event; this last happened in 1940, at the beginning of the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War), and will not reoccur until 2391.[^cite_note-auto-51][^cite_note-53]

### Italy



Palm Sunday in [Sturno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturno), Italy

In [Italy](14532.md), palm leaves are used along with small olive branches, readily available in the Mediterranean climate. These are placed at house entrances (for instance, hanging above the door) to last until the following year's Palm Sunday. For this reason, usually palm leaves are not used whole, due to their size; instead, leaf strips are braided into smaller shapes. Small olive branches are also often used to decorate traditional Easter cakes, along with other symbols of birth, like eggs.

### Latvia

In Latvia, Palm Sunday is called "Pussy Willow Sunday", and pussy willows – symbolizing new life – are blessed and distributed to the faithful.[^cite_note-54] Children are often awakened that morning with ritualistic swats of a willow branch.[^cite_note-55]

### Lithuania

When Christianity came to Lithuania, the plants which sprouted earliest were honored during spring feasts. The name "Palm Sunday" is a misnomer; the "verba" or "dwarfed spruce" is used instead. According to tradition, on the Saturday before Palm Sunday the Lithuanians take special care in choosing and cutting well-formed branches, which the women-folk decorate with flowers. The flowers are meticulously tied onto the branches, making the "Verba".[^cite_note-56]

### The Levant

In [Israel](9282173.md), [Jordan](7515964.md), [Lebanon](17771.md), [Palestine](241405.md), and [Syria](7515849.md), Palm Sunday (*Shaa'nineh* in Arabic) is perhaps the best-attended liturgy in the Christian Calendar, among the [Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodoxy#Christianity), Catholic ([Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Church) and [Eastern](214903.md)), and [Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican) Churches, perhaps because it is notably a family occasion.[^cite_note-57] On this day, children attend church with branches from [olive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive) and palm trees. Also, there will be carefully woven [crosses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross) and other symbols made from palm fronds and [roses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rose) and a procession at the beginning of the liturgy, during which at some point, the priest will take an [olive branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive_branch) and splash [holy water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_water) on the faithful.

### Malta

All the parishes of [Malta](19137.md) and [Gozo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gozo) on Palm Sunday ([Maltese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltese_language): *Ħadd il-Palm*) bless the palm leaves and the olive leaves. Those parishes that have the statues of [Good Friday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Friday) bless the olive tree they put on the statues of "Jesus prays in the Olive Garden" (*Ġesù fl-Ort*) and the "Betrayal of Judas" (*il-Bewsa ta' Ġuda*). Also, many people take a small olive branch to their homes because it is a [sacramental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacramentals).

### Netherlands

In the [Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Low_Saxon) regions of the Netherlands, crosses are decorated with candy and bread, made in the form of a [rooster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rooster). In the [Diocese of Groningen-Leeuwarden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Groningen-Leeuwarden), a great [procession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procession) with oil lamps is held the night before Palm Sunday in honour of the [Sorrowful Mother of Warfhuizen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorrowful_Mother_of_Warfhuizen).

### Philippines

See also: [Holy Week in the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Week_in_the_Philippines)



Palm fronds being blessed in [San Carlos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Carlos,_Pangasinan), [Pangasinan](1691800.md), Philippines

In the [Philippines](23440.md), a statue of Christ riding a donkey (the *Humenta*), or the presiding priest on horseback, is brought to the local church in a morning procession. Congregants line the route, waving *palaspás* (ornately woven palm branches) and spreading *tapis* (heirloom "aprons" made for this ritual) in imitation of the excited Jerusalemites. At the church parvise, a house, or the town plaza, children dressed as angels scatter flowers as they sing the day's [antiphon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiphon) *[Hosanna filio David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hosanna_filio_David)* in the vernacular and to traditional tunes. The first Mass of the day then follows.

Once blessed, the *palaspás* are brought home and placed on altars, doorways, and windows. The Church teaches that this is a sign of welcoming Christ into the home, but folk belief holds that the blessed *palaspás* are [apotropaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apotropaic), deterring evil spirits, lightning, and fires. Another folk custom is to feed pieces of blessed *palaspás* to [roosters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rooster) used in [*sabong*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cockfight#Philippines) (cockfighting); this was strongly discouraged by the [Archbishop of Manila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_Manila), Cardinal [Luis Antonio Tagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Antonio_Tagle).[^cite_note-58] In other provinces, the flowers strewn by the angels during the procession are added to the rice seeds being planted, in the belief that these will ensure a bountiful harvest.

### Poland



A palm in [Łyse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%81yse,_Masovian_Voivodeship), [Poland](22936.md)

Many [Polish](22936.md) towns and villages organize decorated [Easter palm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_palm) competitions: the best known are [Lipnica Murowana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipnica_Murowana) in [Lesser Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser_Poland) and [Łyse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%81yse,_Masovian_Voivodeship) in [Mazovia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazovia). The biggest of those reach above 30 meters (98 ft) in length; for example, the highest palm in 2008 was 33.39 meters (109.5 ft).[^cite_note-59]

### Romania and Moldova

In [Romania](25445.md) and [Moldova](19260.md), Palm Sunday is known as *Duminica Floriilor* or simply *Florii*, translating *Flowers' Sunday*.[^cite_note-60][^cite_note-61] This name has its roots in a pre-Christian fertility festival, where flowers played a prominent role.[^cite_note-62]

### Spain

See also: [Holy Week in Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Week_in_Spain)

In Spain, there is a tradition at the Palmeral of Elche (Europe's largest palm grove) in which local people cover palm leaves from the sun to allow them to whiten, and then they tie and braid them into intricate shapes.[^cite_note-63]

A [Spanish rhyming proverb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_proverbs) states: *Domingo de Ramos, quien no estrena algo, se le caen las manos* ("On Palm Sunday, the hands drop off of those who fail to wear something new"). On Palm Sunday, it is customary to don new clothing or shoes.[^cite_note-64]

### Syria

In [Syria](7515849.md), it is popular for children to go door to door in neighborhoods, chanting about Lazarus' rising from the dead, for which they are given coins or eggs.[^cite_note-65]

### Wales

Main article: [Flowering Sunday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowering_Sunday)



These Flowering Sunday grave decorations were photographed in South Wales c. 1907.

In southern [Wales](69894.md) and nearby portions of England, '*Sul y Blodau*' or 'Flowering Sunday' is a grave decoration tradition commonly observed on Palm Sunday, although historically Flowering Sunday grave decoration was also observed on other days as well. Today, the names Palm Sunday and Flowering Sunday are used interchangeably in those regions. In 1829, Thomas Wallace of [Llanbadoc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llanbadoc), [Monmouthshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monmouthshire) published a poem which contains the first known reference to the custom being practiced only on Palm Sunday.

Welsh cemetery cleaning and decoration traditions may have begun as an Easter celebration before becoming more commonly associated with Palm Sunday. As early as 1786, cleaning and flower decorations were attested by William Matthews during a tour of South Wales.[^cite_note-66] Richard Warner attested in 1797 "the ornamenting of the graves of the deceased with various plants and flowers, at certain seasons, by the surviving relatives" and noted that Easter was the most popular time for this tradition. By 1803, Malkin's observations in "The Scenery, Antiquities, and Biography of South Wales from materials collected during two excursions in the year 1803" reflect the shift away predominantly associating the custom with Easter.[^cite_note-67]

The last week of Lent is called *Holy Week* in the Western Churches, and *Great and Holy Week* in the Eastern. During this week, believers remember the events in the last week of Jesus' life. These include Jesus' entrance into Jerusalem and his suffering on the way to crucifixion, which are sometimes called the "Passion of Jesus Christ," or "Passion of Christ."

It includes "Holy Week," the week before Easter. For six weeks preceding Easter, it is a time of penitential prayer, fasting, and almsgiving to prepare for the celebration of the resurrection of Jesus on Easter Sunday. This season of Lent originally was also a time of preparation for baptismal candidates and those separated from the Church who were rejoining the community. Holy Week, the last week of Lent, commemorates the last week of the earthly life of Jesus Christ. It covers the events of his triumphal entry into Jerusalem, the last supper, the arrest, and his death by crucifixion. Beginning with the sixth Sunday of Lent, Holy Week includes Palm Sunday, Spy Wednesday, Maundy Thursday, Good Friday, and Holy Saturday.

Holy Week in the Orthodox Church (for Greeks, Russians and any other nationalities that are Orthodox Christian) takes place in the week after Great Lent and just before Pascha, or Orthodox Easter. The last day of lent is the Saturday of Lazarus, which celebrates when Jesus raised Lazarus from the dead. Holy Week officially begins with Palm Sunday and follows the last week of Christ's life before His death and resurrection.
