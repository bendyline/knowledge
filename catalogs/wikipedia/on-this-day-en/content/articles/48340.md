# Pesticide



A self-propelled crop sprayer spraying pesticide on a field



A [crop-duster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop-duster) spraying pesticide on a field

**Pesticides** are substances that are used to [control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pest_control) [pests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pest_\(organism\)).[^cite_note-1] They include [herbicides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbicide), [insecticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insecticide), [nematicides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nematicide), [fungicides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungicide), and many others (see table).[^cite_note-2] The most common of these are herbicides, which account for approximately 50% of all pesticide use globally.[^cite_note-3] Most pesticides are used as plant protection products (also known as crop protection products), which in general protect plants from [weeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weed), fungi, or [insects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insect).&#x20;

In general, a pesticide is a [chemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_substance) or [biological agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_agent) (such as a [virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus), [bacterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacterium), or [fungus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entomopathogenic_fungus)) that deters, incapacitates, kills, or otherwise discourages pests. Target pests can include insects, plant [pathogens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathogen), weeds, [molluscs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mollusca), [birds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird), [mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammal), [fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish), [nematodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nematode) (roundworms), and [microbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microbe) that destroy property, cause nuisance, spread disease, or are disease [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_\(epidemiology\)). Pesticides thus increase [agricultural yields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_yield). Along with these benefits, pesticides also have drawbacks, such as [potential toxicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_poisoning) to humans and other species.

The word pesticide derives from the Latin *pestis* (plague) and *caedere* (kill).[^cite_note-5]

The [Food and Agriculture Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_and_Agriculture_Organization) (FAO) has defined *pesticide* as:

any substance or mixture of substances intended for preventing, destroying, or controlling any pest, including vectors of human or animal disease, unwanted species of plants or animals, causing harm during or otherwise interfering with the production, processing, storage, transport, or marketing of food, agricultural commodities, wood and wood products or animal feedstuffs, or substances that may be administered to animals for the control of insects, arachnids, or other pests in or on their bodies. The term includes substances intended for use as a plant growth regulator, defoliant, desiccant, or agent for thinning fruit or preventing the premature fall of fruit. Also used as substances applied to crops either before or after harvest to protect the commodity from deterioration during storage and transport.

[^cite_note-code-6]

### Classifications

Pesticides can be classified by target [organism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism) (e.g., [herbicides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbicide), [insecticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insecticide), [fungicides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungicide), [rodenticides](991169.md), and [pediculicides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pediculicide) – see table),[^cite_note-7]

[Biopesticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biopesticide) according to the [EPA](58666.md) include microbial pesticides, biochemical pesticides, and plant-incorporated protectants.[^cite_note-\:11-8]

Pesticides can be classified into structural classes, with many structural classes developed for each of the target organisms listed in the table. A structural class is usually associated with a single [mode of action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_of_action), whereas a mode of action may encompass more than one structural class.

The pesticidal chemical ([active ingredient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_ingredient)) is mixed ([formulated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_formulation)) with other components to form the product that is sold, and which is [applied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_application) in various ways. Pesticides in gas form are [fumigants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fumigation).

Pesticides can be classified based upon their [mode of action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_of_action), which indicates the exact biological mechanism which the pesticide disrupts. The modes of action are important for [resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_resistance) management, and are categorized and administered by the [insecticide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insecticide_Resistance_Action_Committee), [herbicide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbicide_Resistance_Action_Committee), and [fungicide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungicide_Resistance_Action_Committee) resistance action committees.

Pesticides may be systemic or non-systemic.[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10] A systemic pesticide moves (translocates) inside the plant. Translocation may be upward in the [xylem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylem), or downward in the [phloem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phloem) or both. Non-systemic pesticides (contact pesticides) remain on the surface and act through direct contact with the target organism. Pesticides are more effective if they are systemic. Systemicity is a prerequisite for the pesticide to be used as a seed-treatment.

Pesticides can be classified as [persistent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistent_organic_pollutant) (non-biodegradable) or non-persistent ([biodegradable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodegradation)). A pesticide must be persistent enough to kill or control its target but must degrade fast enough not to accumulate in the [environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_pesticides) or the [food chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomagnification) in order to be approved by the authorities.[^cite_note-\:7-11][^cite_note-\:8-12] Persistent pesticides, including [DDT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DDT), were [banned many years ago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_Convention_on_Persistent_Organic_Pollutants), an exception being spraying in houses to combat [malaria](20423.md) [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_control).[^cite_note-\:9-13]

## History

From ancient times until the 1950s the pesticides used were [inorganic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inorganic_compound) compounds and [plant extracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extract).[^cite_note-\:4-14][^cite_note-\:18-15] The inorganic compounds were derivatives of [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bordeaux_mixture), [arsenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_green), mercury, [sulfur](27127.md), among others, and the plant extracts contained [pyrethrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrethrum), [nicotine](38272.md), and [rotenone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotenone) among others. The less toxic of these are still in use in [organic farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_farming). In the 1940s the insecticide [DDT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DDT), and the herbicide [2,4-D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2,4-Dichlorophenoxyacetic_acid), were introduced. These synthetic [organic compounds](22203.md) were widely used and were very profitable. They were followed in the 1950s and 1960s by numerous other synthetic pesticides, which led to the growth of the pesticide industry.[^cite_note-\:4-14][^cite_note-\:18-15] During this period, it became increasingly evident that DDT, which had been sprayed widely in the environment to combat the vector, had [accumulated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomagnification) in the [food chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_chain). It had become a global pollutant, as summarized in the well-known book [Silent Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_Spring). Finally, DDT was banned in the 1970s in several countries, and subsequently all persistent pesticides were banned worldwide, an exception being spraying on interior walls for vector control.[^cite_note-\:9-13]

Resistance to a pesticide was first seen in the 1920s with inorganic pesticides,[^cite_note-\:4-14] and later it was found that development of resistance is to be expected, and measures to delay it are important. [Integrated pest management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_pest_management) (IPM) was introduced in the 1950s. By careful analysis and spraying only when an economical or biological threshold of crop damage is reached, pesticide application is reduced. This became in the 2020s the official policy of international organisations, industry, and many governments.[^cite_note-\:18-15] With the introduction of high yielding varieties in the 1960s in the [green revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Revolution), more pesticides were used.[^cite_note-\:18-15] Since the 1980s [genetically modified crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetically_modified_crops) were introduced, which resulted in lower amounts of insecticides used on them.[^cite_note-\:18-15] [Organic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_farming) agriculture, which uses only non-synthetic pesticides, has grown and in 2020 represents about 1.5 per cent of the world's total agricultural land.[^cite_note-\:18-15]

Pesticides have become more effective. Application rates fell from 1,000 to 2,500 grams of active ingredient per hectare (g/ha) in the 1950s to 40–100 g/ha in the 2000s.[^cite_note-\:18-15] Despite this, amounts used have increased. In high income countries over 20 years between the 1990s and 2010s amounts used increased 20%, while in the low income countries amounts increased 1623%.[^cite_note-\:18-15][^cite_note-16]

## Development of new pesticides

The aim is to find new compounds or agents with improved properties such as a new [mode of action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_of_action) or lower application rate. Another aim is to replace older pesticides which have been banned for reasons of [toxicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_effects_of_pesticides) or [environmental harm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_pesticides) or have become less effective due to development of [resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_resistance).[^cite_note-\:15-17][^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19][^cite_note-Bebber-Gurr-2015-20]

The process starts with testing (screening) against target organisms such as [insects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insecticide), [fungi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungicide) or [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbicide). Inputs are typically random compounds, [natural products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_product),[^cite_note-21] compounds designed to disrupt a biochemical target, compounds described in patents or literature, or [biocontrol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biopesticide) organisms.

Compounds that are active in the screening process, known as hits or leads, cannot be used as pesticides, except for biocontrol organisms and some potent natural products. These lead compounds need to be [optimised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_optimization) by a series of cycles of synthesis and testing of analogs. For approval by [regulatory authorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_regulation_in_the_United_States) for use as pesticides, the optimized compounds must meet several requirements.[^cite_note-\:7-11][^cite_note-\:8-12] In addition to being potent (low application rate), they must show low toxicity to non-target organisms, low environmental impact, and viable manufacturing cost. The cost of developing a pesticide in 2024 was estimated to be 301 million US dollars.[^cite_note-22] It has become more difficult to find new pesticides. More than 100 new active ingredients were introduced in the 2000s and less than 40 in the 2010s.[^cite_note-\:18-15] [Biopesticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biopesticide) are cheaper to develop, since the authorities require less toxicological and environmental study. Since 2000 the rate of new biological product introduction has frequently exceeded that of conventional products.[^cite_note-\:18-15]

More than 25% of existing chemical pesticides contain one or more [chiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality_\(chemistry\)) centres (stereogenic centres).[^cite_note-Ulrich-et-al-2012-23] Newer pesticides with lower application rates tend to have more complex structures, and thus more often contain chiral centres.[^cite_note-Ulrich-et-al-2012-23] In cases when most or all of the pesticidal activity in a new compound is found in one [enantiomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enantiomer) (the [eutomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eudysmic_ratio)), the registration and use of the compound as this single enantiomer is preferred. This reduces the total application rate and avoids the tedious environmental testing required when registering a racemate.[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25] However, if a [viable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing_cost) [enantioselective manufacturing route](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enantioselective_synthesis) cannot be found, then the [racemate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racemic_mixture) is registered and used.

## Uses



Pesticide use worldwide, by country in 2023

[^cite_note-26]

In addition to their main use in [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture), pesticides have a number of other applications. Pesticides are used to control organisms that are considered to be harmful, or pernicious to their surroundings.[^cite_note-purdue-27] For example, they are used to kill [mosquitoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosquitoes) that can transmit potentially deadly diseases like [West Nile virus](50642063.md), [yellow fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_fever), and [malaria](20423.md). They can also kill [bees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bee), [wasps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasp) or [ants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ant) that can cause allergic reactions. Insecticides can protect animals from illnesses that can be caused by [parasites](43937.md) such as [fleas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flea).[^cite_note-purdue-27] Pesticides can prevent sickness in humans that could be caused by [moldy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mold_\(fungus\)) food or diseased produce. Herbicides can be used to clear roadside weeds, trees, and brush. They can also kill invasive [weeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weed) that may cause environmental damage. Herbicides are commonly applied in ponds and lakes to control [algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae) and plants such as water grasses that can interfere with activities like swimming and fishing and cause the water to look or smell unpleasant.[^cite_note-VCE-28] Uncontrolled pests such as termites and mold can damage structures such as houses.[^cite_note-purdue-27] Pesticides are used in grocery stores and food storage facilities to manage [rodents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodents) and insects that infest food such as grain. Pesticides are used on [lawns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawn) and [golf courses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golf_course), partly for cosmetic reasons.[^cite_note-\:17-29]

[Integrated pest management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_pest_management), the use of multiple approaches to control pests, is becoming widespread and has been used with success in countries such as [Indonesia](14579.md), [China](5405.md), [Bangladesh](3454.md), the U.S., [Australia](4689264.md), and [Mexico](3966054.md).[^cite_note-sustaining-30] IPM attempts to recognize the more widespread impacts of an action on an [ecosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem), so that natural balances are not upset.[^cite_note-\:1-31]

Each use of a pesticide carries some associated risk. Proper pesticide use decreases these associated risks to a level deemed acceptable by pesticide regulatory agencies such as the [United States Environmental Protection Agency](58666.md) (EPA) and the [Pest Management Regulatory Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pest_Management_Regulatory_Agency) (PMRA) of Canada.

[DDT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DDT), sprayed on the walls of houses, is an organochlorine that has been used to fight [malaria](20423.md) vectors (mosquitos) since the 1940s. The [World Health Organization](33583.md) recommend this approach.[^cite_note-32] It and other organochlorine pesticides have been [banned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_Convention_on_Persistent_Organic_Pollutants) in most countries worldwide because of their persistence in the environment and human toxicity. DDT has become less effective, as [resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_resistance) was identified in Africa as early as 1955, and by 1972 nineteen species of mosquito worldwide were resistant to DDT.[^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34]

Total pesticides use in agriculture in 2023 was 3.73 million tonnes of active ingredients (Mt), a 2% decrease compared with 2022, a 14 percent increase in a decade, and a doubling since 1990.[^cite_note-\:0-36] Pesticides use per area of cropland in 2023 was 2.40 kg per hectare (kg/ha), a decrease of 2 percent compared with 2022; use per value of agricultural production was 0.88 g per international dollar (g/I\$) (−4%); and use per person was 0.45 kg per capita (kg/cap) (−3%).[^cite_note-\:0-36] Brazil was the world’s largest user of pesticides in 2023, with 801 kt of pesticides applications for agricultural use. ## Benefits

Pesticides increase agricultural yields.[^cite_note-38] Median yield increases range between 12% and 27% when pesticides are used, depending on the crop.[^cite_note-39] Another study found that not using pesticides reduced crop yields by about 10%.[^cite_note-40] A study conducted in 1999, found that a ban on pesticides in the United States may result in a rise of [food prices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_prices), loss of jobs, and an increase in world hunger.[^cite_note-41]

There are two levels of benefits for pesticide use, primary and secondary. Primary benefits are direct gains from the use of pesticides and secondary benefits are effects that are more long-term.[^cite_note-Cooper-42]

### Economics

In 2018 world pesticide sales were estimated to be \$65 billion, of which 88% was used for agriculture.[^cite_note-\:18-15] Generic accounted for 85% of sales in 2018.[^cite_note-44] In one study, it was estimated that for every dollar (\$1) that is spent on pesticides for crops results in up to four dollars (\$4) in crops which would otherwise be lost to insects, fungi and weeds.[^cite_note-Pimental1992-45] In general, farmers benefit from having an increase in crop yield and from being able to grow a variety of crops throughout the year. Consumers of agricultural products also benefit from being able to afford the vast quantities of produce available year-round.[^cite_note-Cooper-42]

## Disadvantages

On the cost side of pesticide use there can be [costs to the environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_damage) and costs to human health.[^cite_note-46] Pesticides safety education and pesticide applicator regulation are designed to protect the public from [pesticide misuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_misuse), but do not eliminate all misuse. Reducing the use of pesticides and choosing less toxic pesticides may reduce risks placed on society and the environment from pesticide use.[^cite_note-VCE-28]

### Health effects

Main article: [Health effects of pesticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_effects_of_pesticides)

Further information: [Pesticide poisoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_poisoning) and [Environmental impact of pesticides § Humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_pesticides#Humans)



A sign warning about potential pesticide exposure

Most health concerns related to pesticides stem from direct use, whether in occupational or non-occupational settings. In contrast, health risks from [pesticide residues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_residue) in fruits and vegetables are considered minimal.

Occupational use of pesticides may affect health negatively.[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48] mimicking hormones causing reproductive problems, and also causing cancer.[^cite_note-49] A 2007 [systematic review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematic_review) found that "most studies on [non-Hodgkin lymphoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Hodgkin_lymphoma) and [leukemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leukemia) showed positive associations with pesticide exposure" and thus concluded that cosmetic use of pesticides should be decreased.[^cite_note-50] There is substantial evidence of associations between organophosphate insecticide exposures and neurobehavioral alterations.[^cite_note-\:32-51][^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54] Limited evidence also exists for other negative outcomes from pesticide exposure including neurological, [birth defects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_defects), and [fetal death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fetal_death).[^cite_note-55]

2014 epidemiological review found associations between autism and exposure to certain pesticides, but noted that the available evidence was insufficient to conclude that the relationship was causal.[^cite_note-56]

Owing to inadequate regulation and safety precautions, 99% of pesticide-related deaths occur in developing countries that account for only 25% of pesticide usage.[^cite_note-\:022-57]

#### Exposure to pesticide residues from eating food

Main article: [Pesticide residue § Health impacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_residue#Health_impacts)

According to the [American Cancer Society](522681.md) there is no evidence that pesticide residues in food increase the risk of people getting cancer.[^cite_note-acs-58] A 2009 study estimated that lifetime exposure to pesticide residues from eating fruits and vegetables results in only 4.2 and 3.2 minutes of lost life per person in Switzerland and the United States, respectively.[^cite_note-59]

#### Non-occupational pesticide use

Pesticides are also found in majority of U.S. households with 88 million out of the 121.1 million households indicating that they use some form of pesticide in 2012.[^cite_note-EPAPesticides-60][^cite_note-61] As of 2007, there were more than 1,055 active ingredients registered as pesticides,[^cite_note-Goldman_2007-62] which yield over 20,000 pesticide products that are marketed in the United States.[^cite_note-\:2-63]

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends limiting exposure of children to pesticides and using safer alternatives:[^cite_note-64]

One study found pesticide self-poisoning the method of choice in one third of suicides worldwide, and recommended, among other things, more restrictions on the types of pesticides that are most harmful to humans.[^cite_note-65]

#### Pesticide use among agricultural workers

The World Health Organization and the [UN Environment Programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UN_Environment_Programme) estimate that 3 million agricultural workers in the developing world experience severe [poisoning from pesticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_poisoning) each year, resulting in 18,000 deaths.[^cite_note-sustaining-30] According to one study, as many as 25 million workers in developing countries may suffer mild pesticide poisoning yearly.[^cite_note-66] Other occupational exposures besides agricultural workers, including pet groomers, [groundskeepers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundskeeping), and [fumigators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fumigation), may also put individuals at risk of health effects from pesticides.[^cite_note-\:2-63]

Pesticide use is widespread in [Latin America](18524.md), as around US\$3 billion are spent each year in the region. Records indicate an increase in the frequency of pesticide poisonings over the past two decades. The most common incidents of pesticide poisoning is thought to result from exposure to organophosphate and carbamate insecticides.[^cite_note-\:12-67] At-home pesticide use, use of unregulated products, and the role of undocumented workers within the agricultural industry makes characterizing true pesticide exposure a challenge. It is estimated that 50–80% of pesticide poisoning cases are unreported.

Underreporting of pesticide poisoning is especially common in areas where agricultural workers are less likely to seek care from a healthcare facility that may be monitoring or tracking the incidence of acute poisoning. The extent of unintentional pesticide poisoning may be much greater than available data suggest, particularly among developing countries. Globally, agriculture and food production remain one of the largest industries. In East Africa, the agricultural industry represents one of the largest sectors of the economy, with nearly 80% of its population relying on agriculture for income.[^cite_note-68] Farmers in these communities rely on pesticide products to maintain high crop yields.

Some East Africa governments are shifting to [corporate farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_farming), and opportunities for foreign conglomerates to operate commercial farms have led to more accessible research on pesticide use and exposure among workers. In other areas where large proportions of the population rely on subsistence, small-scale farming, estimating pesticide use and exposure is more difficult.

#### Pesticide poisoning



The cholinergic synapse and the breakdown of [acetylcholine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetylcholine) into choline and acetate by acetylcholinesterase

Pesticides may exhibit toxic effects on [humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_pesticides#Humans) and other non-target species, the severity of which depends on the frequency and magnitude of exposure. Toxicity also depends on the rate of absorption, distribution within the body, metabolism, and elimination of compounds from the body. Commonly used pesticides like organophosphates and carbamates act by inhibiting [acetylcholinesterase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetylcholinesterase) activity, which prevents the breakdown of [acetylcholine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetylcholine) at the neural [synapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synapse). Excess acetylcholine can lead to [symptoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organophosphate_poisoning#Signs_and_symptoms) like muscle cramps or tremors, confusion, dizziness and nausea. Studies show that farm workers in Ethiopia, Kenya, and Zimbabwe have decreased concentrations of plasma acetylcholinesterase, the enzyme responsible for breaking down acetylcholine acting on synapses throughout the [nervous system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nervous_system).[^cite_note-\:13-69][^cite_note-\:33-70][^cite_note-\:23-71] Other studies in Ethiopia have observed reduced respiratory function among farm workers who spray crops with pesticides.[^cite_note-72] Numerous exposure pathways for farm workers increase the risk of pesticide poisoning, including dermal absorption walking through fields and applying products, as well as inhalation exposure.

#### Measuring exposure to pesticides

There are multiple approaches to measuring a person's exposure to pesticides, each of which provides an estimate of an individual's internal dose. Two broad approaches include measuring biomarkers and markers of biological effect.[^cite_note-\:03-73] The former involves taking direct measurement of the parent compound or its metabolites in various types of media: urine, blood, serum. Biomarkers may include a direct measurement of the compound in the body before it's been biotransformed during metabolism. Other suitable biomarkers may include the metabolites of the parent compound after they've been biotransformed during metabolism.[^cite_note-\:03-73] Toxicokinetic data can provide more detailed information on how quickly the compound is metabolized and eliminated from the body, and provide insights into the timing of exposure.

Markers of biological effect provide an estimation of exposure based on cellular activities related to the mechanism of action. For example, many studies investigating exposure to pesticides often involve the quantification of the acetylcholinesterase enzyme at the neural synapse to determine the magnitude of the inhibitory effect of organophosphate and carbamate pesticides.[^cite_note-\:13-69][^cite_note-\:33-70][^cite_note-\:23-71][^cite_note-\:03-73]

Another method of quantifying exposure involves measuring, at the molecular level, the amount of pesticide interacting with the site of action. These methods are more commonly used for occupational exposures where the mechanism of action is better understood, as described by WHO guidelines published in "Biological Monitoring of Chemical Exposure in the Workplace".[^cite_note-74] Better understanding of how pesticides elicit their toxic effects is needed before this method of exposure assessment can be applied to occupational exposure of agricultural workers.

Alternative methods to assess exposure include questionnaires to discern from participants whether they are experiencing symptoms associated with pesticide poisoning. Self-reported symptoms may include headaches, dizziness, nausea, joint pain, or respiratory symptoms.[^cite_note-\:33-70]

##### Challenges in assessing pesticide exposure

Multiple challenges exist in assessing exposure to pesticides in the general population, and many others that are specific to occupational exposures of agricultural workers. Beyond farm workers, estimating exposure to family members and children presents additional challenges, and may occur through "take-home" exposure from pesticide residues collected on clothing or equipment belonging to parent farm workers and inadvertently brought into the home. Children may also be exposed to pesticides prenatally from mothers who are exposed to pesticides during pregnancy.[^cite_note-\:32-51] Characterizing children's exposure resulting from drift of airborne and spray application of pesticides is similarly challenging, yet well documented in developing countries.[^cite_note-75] Because of critical development periods of the fetus and newborn children, these non-working populations are more vulnerable to the effects of pesticides, and may be at increased risk of developing neurocognitive effects and impaired development.[^cite_note-\:32-51][^cite_note-\:022-57]

While measuring biomarkers or markers of biological effects may provide more accurate estimates of exposure, collecting these data in the field is often impractical and many methods are not sensitive enough to detect low-level concentrations. Rapid cholinesterase test kits exist to collect blood samples in the field. Conducting large scale assessments of agricultural workers in remote regions of developing countries makes the implementation of these kits a challenge.[^cite_note-\:03-73] The cholinesterase assay is a useful clinical tool to assess individual exposure and acute toxicity. Considerable variability in baseline enzyme activity among individuals makes it difficult to compare field measurements of cholinesterase activity to a [reference dose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_dose) to determine health risk associated with exposure.[^cite_note-\:03-73] Another challenge in deriving a reference dose is identifying health endpoints that are relevant to exposure. More epidemiological research is needed to identify critical health endpoints, particularly among populations who are occupationally exposed.

#### Prevention

Minimizing harmful exposure to pesticides can be achieved by proper use of personal protective equipment, adequate reentry times into recently sprayed areas, and effective product labeling for hazardous substances as per [FIFRA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Insecticide,_Fungicide,_and_Rodenticide_Act) regulations. Training high-risk populations, including agricultural workers, on the proper use and storage of pesticides, can reduce the incidence of acute pesticide poisoning and potential chronic health effects associated with exposure. Continued research into the human toxic health effects of pesticides serves as a basis for relevant policies and enforceable [standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_regulation_in_the_United_States) that are health protective to all populations.

### Environmental effects

Main article: [Environmental impact of pesticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_pesticides)

Pesticide use raises a number of environmental concerns. Over 98% of sprayed insecticides and 95% of herbicides reach a destination other than their target species, including non-target species, air, water and soil.[^cite_note-sustaining-30] [Pesticide drift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_drift) occurs when pesticides suspended in the air as particles are carried by wind to other areas, potentially contaminating them. Pesticides are one of the causes of [water pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution), and some pesticides were [persistent organic pollutants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistent_organic_pollutants) (now banned), which contribute to [soil](4229946.md) and flower (pollen, nectar) contamination.[^cite_note-\:6-76] Furthermore, pesticide use can adversely impact neighboring agricultural activity, as pests themselves drift to and harm nearby crops that have no pesticide used on them.[^cite_note-77]

In addition, pesticide use reduces invertebrate [biodiversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity) in streams,[^cite_note-78] contributes to [pollinator decline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollinator_decline),[^cite_note-79][^cite_note-80][^cite_note-81] destroys habitat (especially for birds),[^cite_note-palmerw-82] and threatens [endangered species](20110668.md).[^cite_note-sustaining-30] Pests can develop a resistance to the pesticide ([pesticide resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_resistance)), necessitating a new pesticide. Alternatively a greater dose of the pesticide can be used to counteract the resistance, although this will cause a worsening of the ambient pollution problem.

The [Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_Convention_on_Persistent_Organic_Pollutants) banned all [persistent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistent_organic_pollutant) pesticides,[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84] in particular [DDT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DDT) and other organochlorine pesticides, which were stable and [lipophilic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipophilicity), and thus able to [bioaccumulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioaccumulation)[^cite_note-Castro-85] in the body and the [food chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomagnification). and which [spread throughout the planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_distillation).[^cite_note-pubs.caes.uga.edu-86][^cite_note-87] Persistent pesticides are no longer used for agriculture, and will not be approved by the authorities.[^cite_note-\:7-11][^cite_note-\:8-12] Because the half life in soil is long (for DDT 2–15 years[^cite_note-National_Biomonitoring_Program-88]) residues can still be detected in humans at levels 5 to 10 times lower than found in the 1970s.[^cite_note-cdc.gov-89]

Pesticides now have to be [degradable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_degradation) in the environment. Such degradation of pesticides is due to both innate chemical properties of the compounds and environmental processes or conditions.[^cite_note-90] For example, the presence of [halogens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halogens) within a chemical structure often slows down degradation in an aerobic environment.[^cite_note-91] [Adsorption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adsorption) to soil may retard pesticide movement, but also may reduce [bioavailability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioavailability) to microbial degraders.[^cite_note-92]

Pesticide contamination in the environment can be monitored through [bioindicators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioindicator) such as [bee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bee) [pollinators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollinator).[^cite_note-\:6-76]

In one study, the human health and environmental costs due to pesticides in the United States was estimated to be \$9.6 billion: offset by about \$40 billion in increased agricultural production.[^cite_note-Pimental-93]

Additional costs include the registration process and the cost of purchasing pesticides: which are typically borne by agrichemical companies and farmers respectively. The registration process can take several years to complete (there are 70 types of field tests) and can cost \$50–70 million for a single pesticide.[^cite_note-Pimental-93] At the beginning of the 21st century, the United States spent approximately \$10 billion on pesticides annually.[^cite_note-Pimental-93]

## Resistance

Main article: [Pesticide resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_resistance)

The use of pesticides inherently entails the risk of resistance developing. Various techniques and procedures of [pesticide application](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_application) can slow the development of resistance, as can some natural features of the target population and surrounding environment.[^cite_note-Dunlop-et-al-2018-4]

Globally, as of 2024, 275 weed species have evolved resistance to at least one herbicide.[^cite_note-94]

## Alternatives

Alternatives to pesticides are available and include [food irradiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_irradiation), [phytosanitary irradiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phytosanitary_irradiation), methods of cultivation, use of [biological pest controls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_pest_control) (such as pheromones and microbial pesticides), [genetic engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_engineering) (mostly [of crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetically_modified_crops)), and methods of interfering with insect breeding.[^cite_note-sustaining-30] Application of composted yard waste has also been used as a way of controlling pests.[^cite_note-nematodes-95] These methods are becoming increasingly popular and often are safer than traditional chemical pesticides.

### Cultivation practices

Cultivation practices include [polyculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyculture) (growing multiple types of plants), [crop rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_rotation), planting crops in areas where the pests that damage them do not live, timing planting according to when pests will be least problematic, and use of [trap crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trap_crop) that attract pests away from the real crop.[^cite_note-sustaining-30] Trap crops have successfully controlled pests in some commercial agricultural systems while reducing pesticide usage.[^cite_note-96] In other systems, trap crops can fail to reduce pest densities at a commercial scale, even when the trap crop works in controlled experiments.[^cite_note-97]

### Use of other organisms

Release of other organisms that fight the pest is another example of an alternative to pesticide use. These organisms can include natural [predators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predator) or [parasites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasite) of the pests.[^cite_note-sustaining-30] [Biological pesticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_pesticides) based on [entomopathogenic fungi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entomopathogenic_fungi), [bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteria) and [viruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus) causing disease in the pest species can also be used.[^cite_note-sustaining-30]

### Biological control engineering

Interfering with insects' reproduction can be accomplished by [sterilizing males](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sterile_insect_technique) of the target species and releasing them, so that they [mate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mating) with females but do not produce offspring.[^cite_note-sustaining-30] This technique was first used on the [screwworm fly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochliomyia_hominivorax) in 1958 and has since been used with the [medfly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medfly), the [tsetse fly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsetse_fly),[^cite_note-98] and the [gypsy moth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gypsy_moth).[^cite_note-99] This is a costly and slow approach that only works on some types of insects.[^cite_note-sustaining-30]

### Other alternatives

Other alternatives include "laserweeding" – the use of novel [agricultural robots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_robot) for [weed control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weed_control) [using lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_technology).[^cite_note-interestingengineeringPapadopoulos-100]

### Push pull strategy

[Push-pull technique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Push%E2%80%93pull_agricultural_pest_management): intercropping with a "push" crop that repels the pest, and planting a "pull" crop on the boundary that attracts and traps it.[^cite_note-PushPull-101]

### Effectiveness

Some evidence shows that alternatives to pesticides can be equally effective as the use of chemicals. A study of [Maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize) fields in northern Florida found that the application of composted yard waste with high [carbon to nitrogen ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_to_nitrogen_ratio) to agricultural fields was highly effective at reducing the population of plant-parasitic [nematodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nematode) and increasing crop yield, with yield increases ranging from 10% to 212%; the observed effects were long-term, often not appearing until the third season of the study.[^cite_note-nematodes-95] Additional [silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon#Biological_role) nutrition protects some [horticultural crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horticultural_crop) against [fungal diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungal_plant_pathogen) almost completely, while insufficient silicon sometimes leads to severe infection even when fungicides are used.[^cite_note-Epstein-1999-102]

Pesticide resistance is increasing and that may make alternatives more attractive.

## Types

### Biopesticides

Main article: [Biopesticide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biopesticide)

[Biopesticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biopesticides) are certain types of pesticides derived from such natural materials as animals, plants, bacteria, and certain minerals. For example, canola oil and baking soda have pesticidal applications and are considered biopesticides. Biopesticides fall into three major classes:

- [Microbial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microbial) pesticides which consist of bacteria, [entomopathogenic fungi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entomopathogenic_fungi) or viruses (and sometimes includes the metabolites that bacteria or fungi produce). Entomopathogenic [nematodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nematode) are also often classed as microbial pesticides, even though they are multi-cellular.

  [^cite_note-Coombs-103]

  [^cite_note-104]

- Biochemical pesticides or herbal pesticides

  [^cite_note-105]

  &#x20;are naturally occurring substances that control (or monitor in the case of [pheromones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pheromones)) pests and microbial diseases.

- Plant-incorporated protectants (PIPs) have genetic material from other species incorporated into their genetic material (*i.e.* [GM crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GM_crop)). Their use is controversial, especially in many European countries.

## Regulation

### International

In many countries, pesticides must be approved for sale and use by a government agency.[^cite_note-\:3-110][^cite_note-111]

Worldwide, 85% of countries have pesticide legislation for the proper storage of pesticides and 51% include provisions to ensure proper disposal of all obsolete pesticides.[^cite_note-NationsOrganization2019-112]

Though pesticide regulations differ from country to country, pesticides, and products on which they were used are traded across international borders. To deal with inconsistencies in regulations among countries, delegates to a conference of the United Nations [Food and Agriculture Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_and_Agriculture_Organization) adopted an International Code of Conduct on the Distribution and Use of Pesticides in 1985 to create voluntary standards of pesticide regulation for many countries.[^cite_note-\:3-110] The Code was updated in 1998 and 2002.[^cite_note-113] The FAO claims that the code has raised awareness about pesticide hazards and decreased the number of countries without restrictions on pesticide use.[^cite_note-code-6]

Three other efforts to improve regulation of international pesticide trade are the [United Nations London Guidelines for the Exchange of Information on Chemicals in International Trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_London_Guidelines_for_the_Exchange_of_Information_on_Chemicals_in_International_Trade?action=edit\&redlink=1) and the [United Nations Codex Alimentarius Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex_Alimentarius). The former seeks to implement procedures for ensuring that prior informed consent exists between countries buying and selling pesticides, while the latter seeks to create uniform standards for maximum levels of pesticide residues among participating countries.[^cite_note-114]

### United States

Main article: [Pesticide regulation in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_regulation_in_the_United_States)



Preparation for an [application](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_application) of hazardous herbicide in the US

In the [United States](3434750.md), the [Environmental Protection Agency](58666.md) (EPA) is responsible for regulating pesticides under the [Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Insecticide,_Fungicide,_and_Rodenticide_Act) (FIFRA) and the [Food Quality Protection Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_Quality_Protection_Act) (FQPA).[^cite_note-epa.gov-115]

Studies must be conducted to establish the conditions in which the material is safe to use and the effectiveness against the intended pest(s).[^cite_note-116] The EPA regulates pesticides to ensure that these products do not pose adverse effects to humans or the environment, with an emphasis on the health and safety of children.[^cite_note-\:5-117] Pesticides produced before November 1984 continue to be reassessed in order to meet the current scientific and regulatory standards. All registered pesticides are reviewed every 15 years to ensure they meet the proper standards.[^cite_note-epa.gov-115] During the registration process, a label is created. The label contains directions for proper use of the material in addition to safety restrictions. Based on acute toxicity, pesticides are assigned to a [Toxicity Class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxicity_Class). Pesticides are the most thoroughly tested chemicals after drugs in the United States; those used on food require more than 100 tests to determine a range of potential impacts.[^cite_note-\:5-117]

Some pesticides are considered too hazardous for sale to the general public and are designated [restricted use pesticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restricted_use_pesticide). Only certified applicators, who have passed an exam, may purchase or supervise the application of restricted use pesticides.[^cite_note-\:3-110] Records of sales and use are required to be maintained and may be audited by government agencies charged with the enforcement of pesticide regulations.[^cite_note-118][^cite_note-119] These records must be made available to employees and state or territorial environmental regulatory agencies.[^cite_note-120][^cite_note-121]

In addition to the EPA, the [United States Department of Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Agriculture) (USDA) and the United States [Food and Drug Administration](11632.md) (FDA) set standards for the level of pesticide residue that is allowed on or in crops.[^cite_note-Regulations-122] The EPA looks at what the potential human health and environmental effects might be associated with the use of the pesticide.[^cite_note-EPA-123]

In addition, the U.S. EPA uses the National Research Council's four-step process for human health risk assessment: (1) Hazard Identification, (2) Dose-Response Assessment, (3) Exposure Assessment, and (4) Risk Characterization.[^cite_note-124]

In 2013 Kaua'i County (Hawaiʻi) passed Bill No. 2491 to add an article to Chapter 22 of the county's code relating to pesticides and GMOs. The bill strengthens protections of local communities in Kaua'i where many large pesticide companies test their products.[^cite_note-125]

The first legislation providing federal authority for regulating pesticides was enacted in 1910.[^cite_note-Goldman_2007-62]

In the United States, the EPA gives priority to registering conventional, "Reduced-Risk Pesticides" that "pose less risk to human health and the environment than existing conventional alternatives".[^cite_note-126] Their benefits include lower impact on human health and other living organisms, reduced groundwater contamination, and lower dose rates.[^cite_note-fishel-reduced-risk-127]

### EU

Main article: [Regulation of pesticides in the European Union](56351395.md)

EU legislation has been approved banning the use of highly toxic pesticides including those that are [carcinogenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcinogenic), [mutagenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutagenic) or toxic to reproduction, those that are endocrine-disrupting, and those that are [persistent, bioaccumulative and toxic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistent,_bioaccumulative_and_toxic_substances) (PBT) or very persistent and very bioaccumulative (vPvB) and measures have been approved to improve the general safety of pesticides across all EU member states.[^cite_note-128]

In 2023 The Environment Committee of European Parliament approved a decision aiming to reduce pesticide use by 50% (the most hazardous by 65%) by the year 2030 and ensure sustainable use of pesticides (for example use them only as a last resort). The decision also includes measures for providing farmers with alternatives.[^cite_note-129]

## Residue

Main article: [Pesticide residue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_residue)

Pesticide residue refers to the pesticides that may remain on or in food after they are applied to food crops.[^cite_note-130] The [maximum residue limits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_residue_limit) (MRL) of pesticides in food are carefully set by the regulatory authorities to ensure, to their best judgement, no health impacts. Regulations such as pre-harvest intervals also often prevent harvest of crop or livestock products if recently treated in order to allow residue concentrations to decrease over time to safe levels before harvest. Exposure of the general population to these residues most commonly occurs through consumption of treated food sources, or being in close contact to areas treated with pesticides such as farms or lawns.[^cite_note-EPAres-131]

[Persistent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistent_organic_pollutant) pesticides are no longer used for agriculture, and will not be approved by the authorities.[^cite_note-132][^cite_note-133] Because the half life in soil is long (for DDT 2–15 years[^cite_note-National_Biomonitoring_Program-88]) residues can still be detected in humans at levels 5 to 10 times lower than found in the 1970s.[^cite_note-cdc.gov-89]

Residues are monitored by the authorities. In 2016, over 99% of samples of US produce had no pesticide residue or had residue levels well below the [EPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EPA) tolerance levels for each pesticide.[^cite_note-PDP-134]
