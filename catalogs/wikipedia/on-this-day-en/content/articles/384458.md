The **Romanian Orthodox Church** (**ROC**; [Romanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_language): *Biserica Ortodoxă Română*, *BOR*), or **Romanian** **Patriarchate**, is an [autocephalous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocephalous) [Eastern Orthodox church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_church) in [full communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_communion) with the other Eastern Orthodox churches and is one of the nine [patriarchates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarchates) of the Eastern Orthodox Church. Since 1925, the church's [primate](24672.md) has borne the title of [patriarch](175409.md). Its jurisdiction covers the territories of [Romania](25445.md) and [Moldova](19260.md), with additional [dioceses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese) for Romanians living in nearby [Ukraine](31750.md), [Serbia](29265.md) and [Hungary](13275.md), as well as for diaspora communities in [Central](5188.md) and [Western Europe](33800.md), [North America](21139.md) and [Oceania](22621.md). It is the only autocephalous church within Eastern Orthodoxy to have a [Romance language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_languages) for liturgical use.

According to the [2021 Romanian census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Romanian_census), 14,025,064 people (73.42% of the population) declared affiliation with the Romanian Orthodox Church, a figure that the church cites as its official membership in Romania.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13] Additionally, four million Romanian Orthodox believers live abroad, particularly in [Western Europe](33800.md).[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15] In 2020, the *World Christian Database*, the online version of the *[World Christian Encyclopedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Christian_Encyclopedia)*, published by *[Edinburgh University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Press)*, reported 16 million members of the church.[^cite_note-WCD-9]

Members of the ROC sometimes refer to [Orthodox Christian doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Christian_theology) as *Dreapta credință* ("right/correct belief" or "true faith"; compare to Greek ὀρθὴ δόξα, "straight/correct belief").[^cite_note-16]

### In the Principalities and the Kingdom of Romania

The Orthodox hierarchy in the territory of modern Romania had existed within the ecclesiastical jurisdiction of the [Ecumenical Patriarchate of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarchate_of_Constantinople) until 1865 when the churches in the Romanian principalities of [Wallachia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallachia) and [Moldavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moldavia) embarked on the path of ecclesiastical independence by nominating [Nifon Rusailă](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nifon_Rusail%C4%83?action=edit\&redlink=1), Metropolitan of Ungro-Wallachia, as the first Romanian primate. [Prince](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domnitor) [Alexandru Ioan Cuza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandru_Ioan_Cuza), who had in 1863 carried out a mass [confiscation of monastic estates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secularization_of_monastic_estates_in_Romania) in the face of stiff opposition from the Greek hierarchy in Constantinople, in 1865 pushed through a legislation that proclaimed complete independence of the church in the principalities from the patriarchate.

In 1872, the Orthodox churches in the principalities, the [Metropolis of Ungro-Wallachia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_of_Ungro-Wallachia) and the [Metropolis of Moldavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolis_of_Moldavia_and_Bukovina), merged to form the ROC.

Following the [international recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Berlin_\(1878\)) of the independence of the [United Principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Principalities) (later [Kingdom of Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Romania)) in 1878, after a long period of negotiations with the Ecumenical Patriarchate, Patriarch [Joachim IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joachim_IV_of_Constantinople) granted recognition to the autocephalous Metropolis of Romania in 1885, which was raised to the rank of patriarchate in 1925.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHitchins199492-17]

Romanian Orthodox theological education was underdeveloped at the end of the nineteenth century. The theological institute at [Sibiu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sibiu), for example, had only one theologian as part of its faculty; the rest were historians, journalists, naturalists, and agronomists. The focus of priestly education was practical and general rather than specialized. In the early twentieth century, the curriculum of a priest included subjects such as hygiene, calligraphy, accountancy, psychology, Romanian literature, geometry, chemistry, botany, and gymnastics. A strong emphasis was placed on church music, canon law, church history, and exegesis.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEClark2009-18]

After [World War I](4764461.md), the Kingdom of Romania significantly increased its territory. Consequently, the ROC needed massive reorganization in order to incorporate congregations from these new provinces. This led to shortages and difficulties. The church had to establish a uniform interpretation of canon law. It had to handle public funds for paying clergymen in the newly acquired territories and, generally speaking, manage the relationship with the state. The legislation was intricate. The *Statute on the organization of the Romanian Orthodox Church* adopted by the Romanian parliament on May 6, 1925, counted 178 articles. The law on the functioning of the ROC counted 46 articles. Legislators adopted the Transylvanian tradition of mixing clergymen and laymen in administrative assemblies and granted bishops seats in the Romanian Senate.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBiliuță2018-19] However, the context also allowed a number of young theologians like [Nichifor Crainic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nichifor_Crainic), [Ioan Savin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ioan_Savin), or [Dumitru Stăniloae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumitru_St%C4%83niloae) to study abroad. These theologians proved extremely influential after their return to Romania and helped shape theological academies. With a few rare exceptions, like [Gala Galaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gala_Galaction), the Romanian Orthodox theologians of this period embraced [nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism). Their scholarly works are thus imbued with nationalist ideology.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEClark2009-18]

The second half of the 1920s is marked by the rise of antisemitism in Romanian politics with figures such as [A.C. Cuza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A.C._Cuza) or [Iron Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Guard) founding father [Codreanu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codreanu). Antisemitism also became apparent in church publications. In 1925, for instance, church journal *Revista Teologică (The Theological Review)* published an antisemitic article by Sibiu professor priest Pompiliu Morușca. Morușca's article blamed the Jews for the economic situation of Romanians in [Bukovina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bukovina). It is a testimony of an older form of antisemitism going back to the 19th century. The ROC would evolve different forms of antisemitism in the 1930s.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBiliuță2016-20] The [Concordat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concordat) of 1927 also triggered anti-Catholic reactions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBiliuță2018-19]

### Patriarchate of Miron Cristea (1919–1939)

See also: [Relationship between the Romanian Orthodox Church and the Iron Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relationship_between_the_Romanian_Orthodox_Church_and_the_Iron_Guard) and [Patriarch Miron of Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_Miron_of_Romania)

The rise of [Nazi Germany](21212.md) exposed Romania to the Reich's theological ideas. This mixture of nationalism, racism and theological thought found fertile ground in a ROC that was already no stranger to antisemitism. It became particularly evident in the second half of the 1930s in the writings of theologians such as [Nichifor Crainic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nichifor_Crainic), Nicolae Neaga or [Liviu Stan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liviu_Stan?action=edit\&redlink=1).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBiliuță2016-20]

In 1936, Crainic published a seminal text titled *Rasă și religiune (Race and Religion)*. While rejecting the Nazi idea of a superior Germanic race, as well as the fascination with Germanic paganism, Crainic argued that some races are indeed superior based on their accomplishment of the Christian essence. Crainic also denied the Jews the moral right to use the books of the Old Testament since, according to him, those prophesies had been fulfilled by the coming of Christ who had abolished the Jewish religion.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBiliuță2016-20]

The deaths of prominent [Iron Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Guard) members [Ion Moța](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion_Mo%C8%9Ba) and [Vasile Marin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasile_Marin) on the same day, January 13, 1937, at [Majadahonda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majadahonda) during the [Spanish Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Civil_War) while fighting for the [Nationalist faction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalist_faction_\(Spanish_Civil_War\)) led to the organization of [massive processions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funerals_of_Ion_Mo%C8%9Ba_and_Vasile_Marin) in Romania, particularly in Bucharest where they were interred. Hundreds of Orthodox priests participated and Metropolitans [Nicolae Bălan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolae_B%C4%83lan) of [Transylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transylvania) and [Visarion Puiu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visarion_Puiu) of [Bukovina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bukovina) held special services.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201726-21][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBiliuță2016-20] Shortly after the funeral, Orthodox theologian Gheorghe Racoveanu and priest Grigore Cristescu founded the theological journal *Predania (The Tradinion)*. The first issue featured a glorification of Moța and Marin and their sacrifice and reflected the Guard's obsession for martyrdom. Intended as a bi-monthly *Predania* printed a total of twelve issues before being banned by the authorities. It stood out for its profoundly anti-ecumenical editorial line, publishing attacks against Catholics, Protestants, Evangelicals.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBiliuță2018-19]

Also in the aftermath of Moța and Marin's grandiose funeral, the Holy Synod issued a condemnation of [Freemasonry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freemasonry). Moreover, following the lead of Metropolitan Bălan who wrote the anti-Masonic manifest, the Synod issued a "Christian point of view" against political secularism stating that the church was in its right to choose which party was worthy of support, based on its moral principles. Iron Guard leader [Codreanu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codreanu) saluted the Synod's position and instructed that the Synod's proclamation should be read by Guard members in their respective *nests* (i.e. chapters).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBiliuță2016-20]

In 1937, the [Goga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octavian_Goga)-[Cuza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._C._Cuza) government was the first to adopt and enact antisemitic legislation in the Kingdom of Romania, stripping over two hundred thousand Jews of their citizenship. That very same year, the head of the ROC, Patriarch [Cristea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miron_Cristea) made an infamous speech in which he described the Jews as parasites who suck the bone marrow of the Romanian people and who should leave the country.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201727-22] The Orthodox church directly or indirectly supported far-right parties and antisemitic intellectuals in their anti-Jewish rhetoric.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201720-23] At the time many Orthodox priests had become active in far-right politics, thus in the 1937 parliamentary elections 33 out of 103 [Iron Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Guard) candidates were Orthodox priests.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201727-22]



Patriarch Miron Cristea as Prime-Minister in 1938

Overall, the church became increasingly involved in politics and, after [King Carol II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Carol_II) assumed emergency powers, Patriarch Miron Cristea became [prime-minister in February 1938](266262.md). In March 1938, the Holy Synod banned the conversion of Jews who were unable to prove their Romanian citizenship.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201753-24] Cristea continued the policies of the Goga-Cuza government but also advocated more radical antisemitic measures including deportation and exclusion from employment. Cristea referred to this last measure as "Romanianization". The church newspaper *Apostolul* was instrumental in propagating Cristea's antisemitic ideas throughout his premiership but church press as a whole became flooded with antisemitic materials.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201733-25] Miron Cristea died in March 1939. Soon after, the Holy Synod voted to uphold regulations adopted under Cristea banning the baptism of Jews who were not Romanian citizens.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201733-25]

Cristea's death led to elections being held in order to select a new patriarch. Metropolitans [Visarion Puiu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visarion_Puiu) and the highly influential [Nicolae Bălan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolae_B%C4%83lan) publicly declared their refusal to enter the race. Both of these bishops held pro-German, pro-Iron-Guard and antisemitic views and it is reasonable to assume that King Carol II's opposition was instrumental in their refusal. Thus, the patriarchal office passed to a reluctant [Nicodim Munteanu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicodim_Munteanu).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201734-26]

### 1940s – World War II

See also: [Romanian Orthodox Mission in Transnistria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_Orthodox_Mission_in_Transnistria)



Metropolitan [Visarion Puiu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visarion_Puiu) and King [Carol II of Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_II_of_Romania)

King Carol II abdicated on September 6, 1940. An openly pro-German coalition of the military headed by marshal [Ion Antonescu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion_Antonescu) and the [Iron Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Guard) took over. Patriarch Nicodim Munteanu's reaction was cautious and his September 1940 address was unenthusiastic. Munteanu, like Cristea before him, feared the anti-establishment nature of the Guard. But the Iron Guard was highly influential on the church's grassroots. In January 1941, seeking full control of the country, the Iron Guard attempted a violent insurrection known as [the Legionary Rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legionnaires'_rebellion_and_Bucharest_pogrom). The putsch failed and out of the 9000 people arrested, 422 were Orthodox priests.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201736-27]

Some particularly violent episodes during the insurrection directly involved the Orthodox clergy. Students and staff of the Theological Academy in Sibiu, led by Professor Spiridon Cândea and assisted by Iron Guard militiamen rounded up Jews in the courtyard of the academy and forced them to hand over their valuables at gunpoint. Monks from the Antim Monastery in Bucharest, led by their abbot, armed themselves and, using explosives, blew up a Synagogue on Antim Street. The numerous Jewish inhabitants of the neighborhood hid in terror.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBiliuță2020-28]

After Antonescu and the Army crushed the insurrection, the Holy Synod was quick to condemn the Legionary Rebellion and publicly paint it as a diabolical temptation that had led the Iron Guard to undermine the state and the [Conducător](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion_Antonescu). Many of the clergymen who had participated in the Rebellion were, however, shielded by their bishops and continued parish work in remote villages. Romania's participation in [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romania_in_World_War_II) on the Axis side after June 1941 would provide them with opportunities for rehabilitation.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBiliuță2020-28]

By the early 1940s, Orthodox theologians such as [Nichifor Crainic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nichifor_Crainic) already had a lengthy record of producing propaganda supporting the concept of [Judeo-Bolshevism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judeo-Bolshevism). After 1941 the idea became commonplace in central church newspapers such as *Apostolul* or *BOR*. A particularly infamous article was signed by Patriarch Nicodim himself and published in *BOR* in April 1942. It referred to the danger of domestic enemies whom he identified as mostly being Jewish.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201745-29] In 1943 *BOR* published a 13-page laudatory review of Nichifor Crainic's infamous antisemitic book *Transfigurarea Românismului* *(The Transfiguration of Romanianism)*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201746-30] Antisemitism was also present in regional journals,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201749-31] a leading example being [Dumitru Stăniloae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumitru_St%C4%83niloae)'s *Telegraful român* (*The Romanian Telegraph*).[^cite_note-Andreescu-32] Orthodox chaplains in the Romanian army cultivated the Judeo-Bolshevik myth.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201751-33]



[Curtea de Argeș Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtea_de_Arge%C8%99_Cathedral) in 1942

A particular case was Romanian-occupied [Transnistria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transnistria_Governorate). On August 15, 1941, The Holy Synod established a mission, rather than a new bishopric, in Romanian-occupied territories across the [Dniester](376633.md). The assumption was that Soviet atheist rule had destroyed the [Russian Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church) and the ROC took it upon itself to "re-evangelize" the locals. The main architect of the enterprise was [Archimandrite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimandrite) [Iuliu Scriban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iuliu_Scriban?action=edit\&redlink=1). In 1942 the Mission evolved into an Exarchate and was taken over by [Visarion Puiu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visarion_Puiu). Many of the missionaries were former affiliates of the Iron Guard, some were seeking rehabilitation after the 1941 insurrection. Abuse against the Jewish population was widespread and numerous reports of Orthodox priests partaking and profiting from the abuse exist.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBiliuță2020-28] In 1944, Visarion Puiu fled to Nazi Germany, then, after the war, in the West. In Romania he was tried and convicted *in absentia* after the war. Many priests active in Transnistria also faced prosecution after the war, although communist prosecutors were mostly looking for connections to the Iron Guard, rather than explicitly investigating the persecution of Jews.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201750-34]

Historical evidence regarding the ROC's role in World War II is overwhelmingly incriminating but there are a few exceptions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201757-35] [Tit Simedrea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tit_Simedrea?action=edit\&redlink=1), metropolitan of Bukovina is one two high-ranking bishops known to have interceded in favor of the Jewish population, the other being the metropolitan [Nicolae Bălan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolae_B%C4%83lan) of Transylvania. Evidence also surfaced that Simedrea personally sheltered a Jewish family in the metropolitanate compound.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201758-36] Priest Gheorghe Petre was recognized as [Righteous Among the Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Righteous_Among_the_Nations) for having saved Jews in [Kryve Ozero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryve_Ozero). Petre was arrested in 1943 and court-martialed but was released in 1944 for lack of evidence.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201761-37]

After [King Michael's Coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Michael's_Coup) on August 23, 1944, Romania switched sides. The coup had been backed by the communists; the church, known for its long-term record of anti-Soviet and anti-communist rhetoric now found itself in an awkward position.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201783-38] Patriarch Nicodim was quick to write a pastoral letter denouncing the previous dictatorship, blaming the Germans for the events that had taken place in Romania during the 30s and during the war and praising "the powerful neighbor from the East" with whom Romania had, supposedly, always had "the best political, cultural, and religious relations."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201783–84-39]

Starting in 1944, and even more after [Petru Groza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petru_Groza) became Prime-minister with Soviet support in 1945, the church tried to adapt to the new political situation. In August 1945 a letter of the Holy Synod was published in *BOR*. Again, it blamed the Germans for the horrors of the war and claimed that the Orthodox Church had always promoted democracy. The Romania Army was also praised for having joined forces with "the brave Soviet armies in the war against the true adversaries of our country." Finally, the Orthodox faithful were asked to fully support the new government.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201786–87-40] Later that year *BOR* published two relatively long articles authored by Bishop Antim Nica and, respectively, by Teodor Manolache. Both articles dealt with the Holocaust and painted the ROC as a savior of Jews.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopa201788–95-41]

### Communist period (1947–1989)

Main articles: [Romanian Orthodox Church in Communist Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_Orthodox_Church_in_Communist_Romania) and [Anti-religious campaign of Communist Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-religious_campaign_of_Communist_Romania)

Romania officially became a communist state in 1947. Restricted access to ecclesiastical and relevant state archives[^cite_note-presidency.ro-42]<sup>: 446–447 </sup>[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENeamțu2007-43] makes an accurate assessment of the ROC's attitude towards the [Communist regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Romania) a difficult proposition. Nevertheless, the activity of the Orthodox Church as an institution was more or less tolerated by the [Marxist–Leninist atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxist%E2%80%93Leninist_atheism) regime, although it was controlled through "special delegates" and its access to the public sphere was severely limited; the regime's attempts at repression generally focused on individual believers.[^cite_note-presidency.ro-42]<sup>: 453 </sup> The attitudes of the church's members, both laity and clergy, towards the communist regime, range broadly from opposition and [martyrdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martyrdom), to silent consent, collaboration or subservience aimed at ensuring survival. Beyond limited access to the [Securitate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Securitate) and Party archives as well as the short time elapsed since these events unfolded, such an assessment is complicated by the particularities of each individual and situation, the understanding each had about how their own relationship with the regime could influence others and how it actually did.[^cite_note-presidency.ro-42]<sup>: 455–456 </sup>[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEnache2006-44]



[Patriarch Justinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_Justinian_of_Romania) of Romania and [Patriarch Alexy I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_Alexy_I_of_Moscow) of Russia in Bucharest, 1962

The [Romanian Workers' Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_Workers'_Party), which assumed political power at the end of 1947, initiated mass purges that affected the Orthodox hierarchy. Thirteen bishops and archbishops who resisted cooperation with the regime were arrested.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERamet198919–20-45] A May 1947 decree imposed a mandatory retirement age for clergy, thus providing authorities with a convenient way to pension off old-guard holdouts. The 4 August 1948 Law on Cults institutionalised state control over episcopal elections and packed the Holy Synod with Communist supporters.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStanTurcescu2007-46] The [evangelical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical) wing of the ROC, known as the [Army of the Lord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_of_the_Lord), was suppressed by communist authorities in 1948.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMaclear1995485-47] In exchange for subservience and enthusiastic support for state policies, the property rights over as many as 2,500 church buildings and other assets belonging to the (by then-outlawed) [Romanian Greek-Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_Church_United_with_Rome,_Greek-Catholic) were transferred to the ROC; the government continued providing salaries for bishops and priests, as well as financial subsidies for the publication of religious books, calendars and theological journals.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERamet2004278-48] By weeding out the anti-communists from among the Orthodox clergy and setting up a pro-regime, secret police-infiltrated Union of Democratic Priests (1945), the party endeavoured to secure the hierarchy's cooperation. By January 1953 some 300–500 Orthodox priests were being held in concentration camps, and following Patriarch [Nicodim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicodim_Munteanu)'s death in May 1948, the party succeeded in having the ostensibly docile [Justinian Marina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian_Marina) elected to succeed him.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERamet198919–20-45]

As a result of measures passed in 1947–48, the state took over the 2,300 elementary schools and 24 high schools operated by the Orthodox Church. A new campaign struck the church in 1958–1962 when more than half of its remaining monasteries were closed, more than 2,000 monks were forced to take secular jobs, and about 1,500 clergy and lay activists were arrested (out of a total of up to 6,000 in the 1946–1964 period[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERamet2004278-48]). Throughout this period Patriarch Justinian took great care that his public statements met the regime's standards of political correctness and to avoid giving offence to the government;[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERamet198920-49] indeed the hierarchy at the time claimed that the arrests of clergy members were not due to religious persecution.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStanTurcescu2007-46]



A 1971 postage stamp from the Socialist Republic of Romania, part of the "Frescoes from Romanian Monasteries" series, depicting a fresco from the [Moldovița Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moldovi%C8%9Ba_Monastery).

The church's situation began to improve in 1962, when relations with the state suddenly thawed, an event that coincided with the beginning of Romania's pursuit of an independent foreign policy course that saw the political elite encourage nationalism as a means to strengthen its position against Soviet pressure. The ROC, an intensely national body that had made significant contributions to Romanian culture from the 14th century on, came to be regarded by the regime as a natural partner. As a result of this second co-optation, this time as an ally, the church entered a period of dramatic recovery. By 1975, its diocesan clergy was numbering about 12,000, and the church was already publishing by then eight high-quality theological reviews, including *Ortodoxia* and *Studii Teologice*. Orthodox clergymen consistently supported the [Ceaușescu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolae_Ceau%C5%9Fescu) regime's foreign policy, refrained from criticizing domestic policy, and upheld the Romanian government's line against the Soviets (over Bessarabia) and the Hungarians (over Transylvania). As of 1989, two metropolitan bishops even sat in the [Great National Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_National_Assembly_\(Socialist_Republic_of_Romania\)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERamet198920-49] The members of the church's hierarchy and clergy remained mostly silent as some two dozen historic Bucharest churches were demolished in the 1980s, and as plans for [systematization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematization_\(Romania\)) (including the destruction of village churches) were announced.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERamet2004279-50] A notable dissenter was [Gheorghe Calciu-Dumitreasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gheorghe_Calciu-Dumitreasa), imprisoned for a number of years and eventually expelled from Romania in June 1985, after signing an open letter criticizing and demanding an end to the regime's violations of human rights.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERamet2004278-48]

In an attempt to adapt to the newly created circumstances, the Eastern Orthodox Church proposed a new ecclesiology designed to justify its subservience to the state in supposedly theological terms. This so-called "Social Apostolate" doctrine, developed by Patriarch Justinian, asserted that the church owed allegiance to the secular government and should put itself at its service. This notion inflamed conservatives, who were consequently purged by [Gheorghe Gheorghiu-Dej](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gheorghe_Gheorghiu-Dej), Ceaușescu's predecessor and a friend of Justinian's. The Social Apostolate called on clerics to become active in the [People's Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_People's_Republic), thus laying the foundation for the church's submission to and collaboration with the state. Fr. Vasilescu, an Orthodox priest, attempted to find grounds in support of the Social Apostolate doctrine in the Christian tradition, citing [Augustine of Hippo](2030.md), [John Chrysostom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Chrysostom), [Maximus the Confessor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximus_the_Confessor), [Origen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origen) and [Tertullian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertullian). Based on this alleged grounding in tradition, Vasilescu concluded that Christians owed submission to their secular rulers as if it were the will of God. Once recalcitrants were removed from office, the remaining bishops adopted a servile attitude, endorsing Ceauşescu's concept of nation, supporting his policies, and applauding his peculiar ideas about peace.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERamet2004280-51]

#### Collaboration with the Securitate

Main article: [Clerical collaboration with communist secret services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clerical_collaboration_with_communist_secret_services)

In the wake of the [Romanian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_Revolution), the church never admitted to having ever willingly collaborated with the regime, although several Romanian Orthodox priests have publicly admitted after 1989 that they had collaborated with and/or served as informers for the [Securitate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Securitate), the [secret police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_police). A prime example was Bishop [Nicolae Corneanu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolae_Corneanu), the [Metropolitan of Banat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolis_of_Banat), who admitted to his efforts on behalf of the [Romanian Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_Communist_Party), and denounced activities of clerics in support of the Communists, including his own, as "the Church's \[act of] prostitution with the Communist regime".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStanTurcescu2007-46]

In 1986, Metropolitan [Antonie Plămădeală](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonie_Pl%C4%83m%C4%83deal%C4%83) defended Ceaușescu's church demolition programme as part of the need for [urbanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanization) and [modernisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernisation) in Romania.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStanTurcescu2000-52] The church hierarchy refused to try to inform the international community about what was happening.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStanTurcescu2006-53]

Widespread dissent from religious groups in Romania did not appear until revolution was sweeping across [Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe) in 1989. The Patriarch of the ROC [Teoctist Arăpașu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teoctist_Ar%C4%83pa%C8%99u) supported Ceaușescu up until the end of the regime, and even congratulated him after the state murdered one hundred demonstrators in [Timișoara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timi%C8%99oara).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEdiger2005-54] It was not until the day before Ceaușescu's execution on 24 December 1989 that the patriarch condemned him as "a new child-murdering [Herod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_the_Great)".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEdiger2005-54]

Following the removal of Communism, the patriarch resigned (only to return a few months after) and the Holy Synod apologised for those "who did not have the courage of the [martyrs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martyr)".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStanTurcescu2000-52]

### Post-revolution (1989–present)



[Romanian icon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_Orthodox_icons) of [Saint Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Peter)

As Romania made the transition to democracy, the church was freed from most of its state control, although the State Secretariat for Religious Denominations still maintains control over a number of aspects of the church's management of property, finances and administration. The state provides funding for the church in proportion to the number of its members, based on census returns[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFox2008167-55] and "the religion's needs" which is considered to be an "ambiguous provision".[^cite_note-56] Currently, the state provides the funds necessary for paying the salaries of priests, deacons and other prelates and the pensions of retired clergy, as well as for expenses related to lay church personnel. For the Orthodox church this is over 100 million euros for salaries,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDunlop2013-57] with additional millions for construction and renovation of church property. The same applies to all state-recognised religions in Romania.

The state also provides support for church construction and structural maintenance, with a preferential treatment of Orthodox parishes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEIordache2003253-58] The state funds all the expenses of Orthodox seminaries and colleges, including teachers' and professors' salaries who, for compensation purposes, are regarded as civil servants.

Since the fall of communism, [Greek-Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_Greek_Catholic_Church) leaders have claimed that the Eastern Catholic community is facing cultural and religious eradication. They cite opposition from the Romanian Orthodox Church to the restitution of churches that historically belonged to the Greek Catholic Church, as well as what they describe as support from the Romanian government for this position.[^cite_note-59] In the context of the universality of [Eastern Orthodoxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy), [Mihail Neamțu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihail_Neam%C8%9Bu) argued in 2006 that by calling itself "Romanian", the ROC "is laying claim to a perfect match between [religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_identity) and [national identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_identity) that is not borne out by real life".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEClark2009-18][^cite_note-60]

The church openly supported [banning same-sex marriage in a referendum in 2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Romanian_constitutional_referendum).[^cite_note-61][^cite_note-62] The church believes that [homosexuality](5488304.md) is a [sin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sin) and unnatural.[^cite_note-63]

According to the preliminary results of the 2021 census, the number of Orthodox Christians in Romania was approximately 2.3 million lower than a decade earlier. This decline may be partly explained by the decrease in the country's total population.[^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65]

### In Moldova

See also: [History of the Orthodox Church in Moldova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Orthodox_Church_in_Moldova)



Eparchies of the [Metropolis of Bessarabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolis_of_Bessarabia)

The ROC also has jurisdiction over a minority of believers in [Moldova](19260.md), who belong to the [Metropolis of Bessarabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolis_of_Bessarabia), as opposed to the majority, who belong to the [Metropolis of Chișinău and All Moldova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolis_of_Chi%C8%99in%C4%83u_and_All_Moldova), under the [Moscow Patriarchate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_of_Moscow_and_all_Rus'). In 2001 it won a landmark legal victory against the Government of Moldova at the [Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strasbourg)-based [European Court of Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Court_of_Human_Rights).

This means that despite current political issues, the Metropolis of Bessarabia is now recognized as "the rightful successor" to the Metropolitan Church of Bessarabia and Hotin, which existed from 1927 until its dissolution in 1944, when its canonical territory was put under the jurisdiction of the [Russian Orthodox Church's Moscow Patriarchate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church) in 1947.

After the debut of the ongoing [Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine), the ROC in Moldova has seen a significant number of parishes switching affiliation from the Moscow controlled Metropolis of Chișinău and All Moldova to the Metropolis of Bessarabia, sometimes smoothly, otherwise through intense debates and highly polemicized switches.[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69]

## Organization and leadership

See also: [List of hierarchs of the Romanian Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_hierarchs_of_the_Romanian_Orthodox_Church)



Romanian Orthodox Church organization after 2009

The ROC is organized in the form of the Romanian [Patriarchate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarchate). The highest hierarchical, canonical and dogmatical authority of the ROC is the [Holy Synod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Synod).

There are ten Orthodox [Metropolitanates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitanate), twenty [archbishoprics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishopric), twenty-three [bishoprics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishopric) in total, of which four metropolitans and nine bishops administer the church services for the Romanian Diaspora in Europe, the Americas, Asia and Oceania. An estimated number of over twelve thousand altar servers in parishes, monasteries and social centres of the church. Almost 400 monasteries exist inside the country, staffed by some 3,500 monks and 5,000 nuns. As of 2004, there are, inside [Romania](25445.md), fifteen theological universities where more than ten thousand students (some of them from [Bessarabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessarabia), [Bukovina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bukovina) and [Serbia](29265.md) benefiting from a few Romanian fellowships) currently study for a theological degree. More than 14,500 churches (traditionally named "lăcașe de cult", or houses of worship) exist in Romania for the Romanian Orthodox believers. As of 2002, almost 1,000 of those were either in the process of being built or rebuilt.[^cite_note-70]

## Notable theologians



Icon of the [Deesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deesis) from Romania, 17th century

[Dumitru Stăniloae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumitru_St%C4%83niloae) (1903–1993) is considered one of the greatest Orthodox theologians of the 20th century, having written extensively in all major fields of Eastern Christian [systematic theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematic_theology). One of his other major achievements in theology is the 45-year-long comprehensive series on Orthodox spirituality known as the Romanian [Philokalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philokalia), a collection of texts written by classical Byzantine writers, that he edited and translated from Greek. The ROC [canonized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonization) Dumitru Stăniloae as a saint in 2025.[^cite_note-synod2024-84][^cite_note-85]

[Archimandrite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimandrite) [Cleopa Ilie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleopa_Ilie) (1912–1998), elder of the *Sihăstria Monastery*, is considered one of the most representative fathers of contemporary Romanian Orthodox monastic spirituality.[^cite_note-86]

[Metropolitan Bartolomeu Anania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartolomeu_Anania) (1921–2011) was the Metropolitan of Cluj, Alba, Crișana and Maramureș from 1993 until his death.

## Jubilee and commemorative years

An initiative of Patriarch Daniel, with a deep missionary impact on the church and society, has been the proclamation of jubilee and commemorative years in the Romanian Patriarchate, accompanied by solemn sessions of the Holy Synod, conferences, congresses, monastic synaxes, debates, catechetical programmes, processions, and other church activities dedicated to each annual theme.

- 2008 – **The Jubilee Year** of the Holy Scripture and the Holy Liturgy;

- 2009 – **The Jubilee-Commemorative year** of Saint Basil the Great, Archbishop of Cæsarea in Cappadocia;

- 2010 – **The Jubilee Year** of the Orthodox Creed and of Romanian Autocephaly;

- 2011 – **The Jubilee Year** of Holy Baptism and Holy Matrimony;

- 2012 – **The Jubilee Year** of Holy Unction and of the care for the sick;

- 2013 – **The Jubilee Year** of the Holy Emperors Constantine and Helena;

- 2014 – **The Jubilee Year** of the Eucharist (of the Holy Confession and of the Holy Communion) and the **Commemorative Year** of the Martyr Saints of the Brancoveanu family;

- 2015 – **The Jubilee Year** of the Mission of Parish and Monastery Today and the **Commemorative Year** of Saint John Chrysostom and of the great spiritual shepherds in the eparchies;

- 2016 – **The Jubilee Year** of Religious Education for Orthodox Youth and the **Commemorative Year** of the Holy Hierarch and Martyr Antim of Iveria and of all the printing houses of the church;

- 2017 – **The Jubilee Year** of the Holy Icons and of church painters and the **Commemorative Year** of Patriarch Justin and of all defenders of Orthodoxy during communism;

- 2018 – **The Jubilee Year** of Unity of Faith and Nation, and the **Commemorative Year** of the 1918 Great Union Founders;

- 2019 – **Solemn Year** of church singers and of the **Commemorative Year** of Patriarch Nicodim and of the translators of church books;

- 2020 – **Solemn Year** of Ministry to Parents and Children and the **Commemorative Year** of Romanian Orthodox Philanthropists;

- 2021 – **Solemn Year** of pastoral care of Romanians abroad and the **Commemorative Year** of the reposed in the Lord;

- 2022 – **Solemn Year** of Prayer in the church's life and the Christian's life and the **Commemorative Year** of the Hesychast Saints Symeon the New Theologian, Gregory Palamas and Paisius of Neamț;

- 2023 – **Solemn Year** of the Pastoral Care of the Elderly and the **Commemorative Year** of the Hymnographers and Church Chanters;

- 2024 – **Solemn Year** of the pastoral care of the sick and the **Commemorative Year** of all the holy unmercenary healers;

- 2025 – **The Jubilee Year** of the Centennial of the Romanian Patriarchate and the **Commemorative Year** of the Romanian Orthodox confessors of the twentieth century.
