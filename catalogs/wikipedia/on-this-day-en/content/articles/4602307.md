# Moro conflict

This article is about the post-colonial era insurgency. For the conflict with the United States of 1899–1913, see [Moro Rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_Rebellion). For the pre-1899 conflict, see [Spanish–Moro conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93Moro_conflict). For the series of attacks over Sabah, see [Cross border attacks in Sabah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_border_attacks_in_Sabah).

Not to be confused with [Kidnapping and murder of Aldo Moro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidnapping_and_murder_of_Aldo_Moro).

The **Moro conflict**[^cite_note-IcaFernandez2017-44][^cite_note-StevenRood2005-45][^cite_note-SamChittick2019-46] was an insurgency in the [Mindanao](20546.md) region in southern [Philippines](23440.md) which involved multiple armed groups.[^cite_note-GutierrezLandlessness-47][^cite_note-books.google.com-1] A [decades-long peace process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangsamoro_peace_process)[^cite_note-IcaFernandez2017-44][^cite_note-MattStephens2019-48] has resulted in peace deals between the [Philippine government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_the_Philippines) and two major armed groups, the [Moro National Liberation Front](2821886.md) (MNLF)[^cite_note-Timeline-49] and the [Moro Islamic Liberation Front](51673.md) (MILF),[^cite_note-PhilippinePeaceBreakthrough2014-50] but other smaller armed groups continue to exist. In 2017, the peace council settled around 138 clan conflicts.[^cite_note-51]

The root cause of the Moro conflict is associated in a long history of resistance by the [Moro people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_people) against foreign rule, for centuries the region was under the control of the [Spanish Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Empire). The U.S. had a [brief war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93American_War) with Spain in 1898 which ended in the transfer of the Philippines to the United States, and this led to [American occupation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_\(1898%E2%80%931946\)) until 1946.

During the [administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_Ferdinand_Marcos) of President [Ferdinand Marcos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Marcos), political tensions and open hostilities developed between the government and Moro rebel groups.[^cite_note-GutierrezLandlessness-47][^cite_note-scribd.com-52] The Moro insurgency was triggered[^cite_note-MoroStory-53] by the [Jabidah massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabidah_massacre) on March 18, 1968, during which 60 Filipino Muslim commandos on a planned [operation to reclaim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Borneo_dispute) the eastern part of the Malaysian state of [Sabah](28678.md) were killed. To conserve the secrecy of the operation, the Malaysian government supported and funded the rebels which devastated the southern Philippines, until support ceased in 2001.[^cite_note-Misuari_arrested-54]

Various organizations pushing for Moro self-determination, either through autonomy or independence, were almost immediately formed in response.[^cite_note-RevoltinMindanao:-55][^cite_note-BetweenIntegrationandSecession-56] Although these generally did not last long until [University of the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_the_Philippines) professor [Nur Misuari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nur_Misuari) established the Moro National Liberation Front, an armed insurgent group committed to establishing an independent Mindanao, in 1972. In the following years, the MNLF splintered into several different groups including the Moro Islamic Liberation Front founded by [Hashim Salamat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salamat_Hashim) in 1977, which sought to establish an Islamic state within the Philippines. In 1991, [Abdurajak Abubakar Janjalani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdurajak_Abubakar_Janjalani) gathered radical members of the old MNLF who wanted to resume armed struggle and established the [Abu Sayyaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Sayyaf) (ASG). When the MILF modified its demands from independence to autonomy, a faction led by [Ameril Umbra Kato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ameril_Umbra_Kato) disagreed, eventually forming the [Bangsamoro Islamic Freedom Fighters](39963003.md) (BIFF) in 2008.[^cite_note-InqFeb2015-57][^cite_note-58]

Casualty statistics vary for the conflict, though the conservative estimates of the [Uppsala Conflict Data Program](18537936.md) indicate that at least 6,015 people were killed in armed conflict between the government and ASG, BIFF, MILF, MNLF between 1989 and 2012.[^cite_note-59]

The [Moro people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_people) have had a history of resistance against foreign rule for more than 400 years. During the [Spanish–Moro conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93Moro_conflict), Spain repeatedly tried to conquer the Moro [Sultanate of Sulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Sulu), [Sultanate of Maguindanao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Maguindanao), and the Confederation of sultanates in Lanao like what it did with the former Muslim [Rajahnate of Maynila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajahnate_of_Maynila) (As the Rajah of Manila, [Rajah Matanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajah_Matanda) was a grandson of the Sultan of Brunei, "Sultan Bolkiah" and a Princess of Sulu "Dayang-dayang Laila Machanai"). Although Spain succeeded in supplanting Islam in Manila and Mindoro, they failed against the Muslims in the South. The armed struggle against the Spanish, Americans, Japanese, and Christian Filipinos is considered by current Moro Muslim leaders to be part of a four-century-long "national liberation movement" of the Bangsamoro (Moro Nation).[^cite_note-60]

The foundations of the modern conflict can be traced to the Spanish and American wars against the Moros.[^cite_note-61] Following the [Spanish–American War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93American_War) in 1898, another conflict sparked in the southern Philippines between the revolutionary [Muslims in the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_people) and the [United States military](32212.md) that took place between 1899 and 1913. On August 14, 1898, after defeating Spanish forces, the United States claimed the Philippines as its territory under the [Treaty of Paris of 1898](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_of_1898), establishing a military government under General [Wesley Merritt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wesley_Merritt) as [Military Governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Governor).[^cite_note-62] Filipinos immediately opposed foreign rule by the United States.

American forces took control from the Spanish government in [Jolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jolo) on May 18, 1899, and at [Zamboanga](25167165.md) in December 1899.[^cite_note-63] Brigadier General [John C. Bates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_C._Bates) was sent to negotiate a treaty with the [Sultan of Sulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_sultans_of_Sulu), Jamalul Kiram II. Kiram was disappointed by the American takeover, as he expected to regain sovereignty after the defeat of Spanish forces in the archipelago. Bates' main goal was to guarantee Moro neutrality in the [Philippine–American War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War), and to establish order in the southern Philippines. After some negotiation, the Bates Treaty was signed, which was based on an earlier Spanish treaty.[^cite_note-64] The Bates Treaty did ensure the neutrality of the Muslims in the south, but it was actually set up to buy time for the Americans until the war in the north ended.[^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67] On March 20, 1900, Bates was replaced by Brigadier General [William August Kobbé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_August_Kobb%C3%A9) and the District of Mindanao-Jolo was upgraded to a full department. American forces in Mindanao were reinforced and hostilities with the Moro people lessened, although there are accounts of Americans and other civilians being attacked and slain by Moros.

The American invasion began in 1904 and ended at the term of Major General [John J. Pershing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_J._Pershing), the third and final military governor of Moro Province, although major resistance continued in [Mount Bagsak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Bud_Bagsak) and [Bud Dajo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Battle_of_Bud_Dajo) in [Jolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jolo); in the latter, the United States military killed hundreds of Moro in the [Moro Crater massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_Crater_massacre).[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71] After the war, in 1915, the Americans imposed the Carpenter Treaty on Sulu.[^cite_note-(Teuku.)1987-72]

Repeated rebellions by the Moros against American rule continued to break out even after the main Moro Rebellion ended, right up to the [Japanese occupation of the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_occupation_of_the_Philippines) during [World War II](32927.md). During the Japanese invasion, the Moros [waged an insurgency against the Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine_resistance_against_Japan#Moro_resistance_in_Mindanao) on Mindanao and Sulu until Japan surrendered in 1945. Moro [Juramentados](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juramentado) attacked the Spanish, Americans, [Philippine Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine_Constabulary), and the Japanese.

## Philippine administrations after World War II (1946–1968)

The [American](195149.md) colonial government and subsequently the [Philippine government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_the_Philippines) pursued a policy of intra-ethnic migration by resettling significant numbers of [Christian Filipino](8276776.md) settlers from the [Visayas](72418.md) and [Luzon](69375.md) onto tracts of land in [Mindanao](20546.md), beginning in the 1920s. This policy allowed Christian Filipinos to outnumber both the Moro and [Lumad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad) populations by the 1970s, which was a contributing factor in aggravating grievances between the Moro and Filipino Christian settlers as disputes over land increased. Another grievance by the Moro people is the extraction of Mindanao's natural resources by the central government whilst many Moros continued to live in poverty.

Moro Muslims and Lumads were largely supplanted during the Spanish and American colonization programs, with Christian Filipino settlers eventually taking control of key areas along newly built roads and disrupting traditional Moro administrative structures and control over resources. The Americans preferred Christians to become administrators of newly defined townships instead of Lumad and Moro, with environmental degradation resulting from unsustainable population growth (due to the influx of settler migrants) and timber logging.[^cite_note-UmeharaBautista2004p22-73]

## Ferdinand Marcos administration (1965–1986)

See also: [Jabidah massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabidah_massacre)

### Jabidah massacre

The active phase of the Moro conflict is attributed to news about the [Jabidah massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabidah_massacre) in March 1968 – towards the end of the first term of [President](271126.md) [Ferdinand Marcos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Marcos).[^cite_note-JabidahBangsamoroHistory-74][^cite_note-George1980-75][^cite_note-Smith2015-76] A senate exposé based on the testimony of an alleged survivor claimed that at least 11 Filipino Muslim military trainees had been killed in [Corregidor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corregidor) by soldiers of the [Armed Forces of the Philippines](23448.md).[^cite_note-77][^cite_note-Timeline-49] The trainees had been brought to the island of [Corregidor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corregidor) to form a secret [commando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commando) unit called "Jabidah," which would infiltrate, destabilize, and take over [Sabah](28678.md) for the Sulu Sultan who previously owned it.[^cite_note-TessVitug20000402-78] The trainees eventually rejected their mission, for reasons that are still debated by historians today. A survivor, Jibin Arula, said that whatever the reasons behind their objections, all of the recruits aside from him were killed, and he escaped only by pretending to be dead.[^cite_note-TessVitug20130318-79]

The news created unrest among Filipino Muslims, especially among students.[^cite_note-MuslimPresident1994-80] Both Muslim intellectuals and common people suddenly became politicized, discrediting the idea of finding integration and accommodation with the rest of the country, and creating a sense of marginalization.[^cite_note-George1980-75][^cite_note-Smith2015-76]

### Early separatist movements

Various organizations pushing for Moro self rule, either through autonomy or independence, were soon formed. [Lanao del Sur](297370.md) congressman [Haroun al-Rashid Lucman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haroun_al-Rashid_Lucman) called for [Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_the_Philippines) to begin proceedings to [impeach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impeach) President Marcos, and ended up establishing the [Bangsamoro Liberation Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangsamoro_Liberation_Organization) (BMLO) in frustration after the impeachment effort couldn't gather enough congressional support.[^cite_note-81] [Cotabato](297394.md) Governor Datu Udtog Matalam[^cite_note-RevoltinMindanao:-55] established the [Muslim Independence Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_Independence_Movement) (MIM), which openly called for the [secession](144732.md) of the region to create a Muslim state,[^cite_note-82] although it only lasted until Matalam negotiated with Marcos and accepted a post in the administration cabinet.[^cite_note-BetweenIntegrationandSecession-56]

### Religious violence in Mindanao (1970-1972)

Between 1970 and 1972, Muslim and Christians attacked and massacred each other.[^cite_note-83] These violent sectarian attacks were mostly carried out by the [Ilaga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilaga), members of the [Cotabato Blackshirts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotabato_Blackshirts?action=edit\&redlink=1) and [Moro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_people) ["Barracudas"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%22Barracudas%22?action=edit\&redlink=1).[^cite_note-84][^cite_note-85]\


The Ilaga launched a series of 21 massacres, killing 518 and injuring 184. Most of whom were targeted against [Moro muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_people).[^cite_note-86] Ilaga atrocities also included the [Manili massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manili_massacre) in 1971. Which resulted in the deaths of 70 [Moro muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_people) inside a mosque and nearby a school in North Cotabato.[^cite_note-87]\


[Moro muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_people) retaliated against the massacres, killing and injuring 1,238 members of the [Ilaga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilaga) by the end of 1972.[^cite_note-88]\


As a result of the violence. An estimated 1,400-4,000 were killed and thousands were wounded,[^cite_note-89][^cite_note-90] including the deaths of more than 500 Muslims.\


Datu [Udtog Matalam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Udtog_Matalam), the founder of the [Muslim Independence Movement (MIM)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_Independence_Movement_\(MIM\)), refused to describe massacres and atrocities responsible by [Moro muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_people).[^cite_note-91]

### The Moro National Liberation Front (1972)

On October 21, 1972,[^cite_note-BetweenIntegrationandSecession-56] [University of the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_the_Philippines) professor [Nur Misuari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nur_Misuari) formed the [Moro National Liberation Front](2821886.md) (MNLF) which sought the establishment of a [Moro republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangsamoro_Republik) through the force of arms,[^cite_note-Timeline-49] attracting many members who broke away from the MIM.[^cite_note-BetweenIntegrationandSecession-56]

According to the official line of the MNLF, it was founded on an ideology of [egalitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egalitarianism), and is thus intended as a secular movement, unlike the [Moro Islamic Liberation Front](51673.md) which would later splinter from its ranks in 1978.[^cite_note-AsiaTimes_2013-92]

From its beginnings in October 1972, the MNLF has had alternating periods of conflict and relative peace with Philippine government forces, until a [1996 Final Peace Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Final_Peace_Agreement) during the administration of President [Fidel V. Ramos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fidel_V._Ramos) - although at least one major encounter - the [Zamboanga City crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zamboanga_City_crisis) of September 9 to 28, 2013 - took place after this agreement.[^cite_note-93]

#### The Siege of Jolo

Main article: [Siege of Jolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jolo)

During one of the fiercest battles of the insurgency in 1974, [Jolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jolo,_Sulu) was [extensively damaged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Jolo_\(1974\)) and news of the tragedy galvanized other [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim) around the world to pay greater attention to the conflict. Many civilians were supposedly killed when the [Armed Forces](23448.md) razed much of [Jolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jolo,_Sulu) municipality to the ground in a scorched-earth tactic.[^cite_note-94]

#### The Malisbong massacre

Main article: [Malisbong massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malisbong_massacre)

On September 24, 1974, the [Philippine Army](6032559.md) killed at least 1,000 Moro civilians who were praying in a mosque in what is known as the [Malisbong massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malisbong_massacre).[^cite_note-95]

#### The Tripoli Agreement

Main article: [1976 Tripoli Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976_Tripoli_Agreement)

Two years later, the Philippine government and the MNLF signed the Tripoli Agreement, declaring a ceasefire on both sides. The agreement provided that Mindanao would remain a part of the Philippines, but that 13 of its provinces would be governed by an [autonomous government for the Bangsamoro people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_Region_in_Muslim_Mindanao).[^cite_note-Timeline-49] President Marcos later reneged on the agreement, and violence ensued.

The Communist Party of the Philippines maintained a liaison officer in Tripoli with the MNLF.[^cite_note-96]

### The Moro Islamic Liberation Front (1977)

In 1977, Sheikh Salamat Hashim established the [Moro Islamic Liberation Front](51673.md) (MILF), a splinter group of the MNLF seeking to establish an Islamic state.[^cite_note-Estrada_MILF-97] Conflicts between these rebel groups and the [Armed Forces of the Philippines](23448.md) continued until the end of President Marcos' regime. From 1972 to 1980, at least 50,000 people were killed in the conflict, one million people were internally displaced, and more than 100,000 Philippine Muslims fled by boat to Malaysia.[^cite_note-98]

### Silsilah Dialogue Movement (1984)

In 1984, Italian Catholic priest Fr Sebastian D’Ambra began a movement called the Silsilah Dialogue Movement focused on formation of people from different religions to encourage understanding and inter-religious dialogue in Mindanao. Over time, Fr D’Ambra realised that besides strengthening interreligious ties, the Catholic community also needed a better grounding in the faith, founding the Emmaus Dialogue Movement to address this challenge.[^cite_note-99]

## Corazon Aquino and Ramos administrations (1986–1998)



Corazon Aquino was inaugurated as the [11th](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_presidents_of_the_Philippines) [president of the Philippines](271126.md) on February 25, 1986, at Sampaguita Hall (Now Kalayaan Hall).

### Negotiations after the People Power Revolution

Soon after becoming [President of the Philippines](271126.md) as a result of the civilian-led [People Power Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People_Power_Revolution), [Corazon Aquino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corazon_Aquino) arranged a meeting with the MNLF chairman Nur Misuari and several MNLF rebel groups in [Sulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulu), which paved the way for a series of negotiations.[^cite_note-100]

### Creation of the ARMM

In 1989, the [Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_Region_in_Muslim_Mindanao) (ARMM) was created under Republic Act No. 6734 or the ARMM Organic Act, pursuant to the [1987 Constitution](19554336.md).[^cite_note-ARMM_History_and_Organization-101]

Under the [Presidency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_Fidel_V._Ramos) of [Fidel V. Ramos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fidel_V._Ramos), several negotiations and peace talks[^cite_note-scribd.com-52] were held and the ARMM solidified and was to have its own geopolitical system.[^cite_note-Timeline-49]

On 9–11 December 1997 the MNLF brought the pagan indigenous Lumad leader Mai Tuan to Tehran to attend the eight OIC Islamic Summit Conference.[^cite_note-102]

### Formation of the Abu Sayyaf Group

In 1991, Abdurajak Janjalani, a former teacher who had studied [Islam](6037917.md) in the [Middle East](19323.md), formed the [Abu Sayyaf Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Sayyaf) after reportedly meeting [Osama bin Laden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osama_bin_Laden) in [Afghanistan](737.md) in the 1980s. Janjalani recruited former members of the MNLF for the more radical and theocratic Abu Sayyaf.[^cite_note-Timeline-49]

### Estrada's "All Out War" policy

During his term, President [Joseph Ejercito Estrada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Estrada) declared an "all-out war" against the [MILF](51673.md) on March 21, 2000, although a series of negotiations for cessation of hostilities were held.[^cite_note-Estrada_MILF-97] Apparently, several conflicts in and around Mindanao erupted and clashes between the [Philippine military](23448.md) and the rebel groups resulted in substantial loss of life.

During Estrada's term, the rebel groups kidnapped three [Italian](14532.md) priests, two of whom were later released and one shot dead;[^cite_note-IP1-103][^cite_note-IP2-104] seized the municipal halls of [Talayan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talayan,_Maguindanao), [Maguindanao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maguindanao), and [Kauswagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kauswagan,_Lanao_del_Norte), [Lanao del Norte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanao_del_Norte); bombed the RORO ferry M/V *Our Lady of Mediatrix* at [Ozamiz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozamiz); and took over Narciso Ramos Highway. All these incidents resulted in massive loss of investments abroad, especially in the area of Mindanao.

As a result, the [Armed Forces of the Philippines](23448.md) launched a successful campaign against the rebel groups; 43 minor camps, 13 major camps including the MILF headquarters, and Camp Abubakar[^cite_note-Camp_Abubakar-105] fell. The MILF suffered heavy losses and the head of the MILF, Sheikh Salamat Hashim, fled the country and sought refuge in [Malaysia](3607937.md). On October 5, 2000, 609 rebels surrendered in [Cagayan de Oro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cagayan_de_Oro), along with renegade town mayor Mulapandi Cosain Sarip.[^cite_note-106] This was followed by another surrender of 855 rebels on December 29, 2000. President Estrada then ordered that the [Philippine flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_the_Philippines) be raised in Mindanao, which symbolized victory. It was raised on July 9, 2000, near a [Madh'hab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madh'hab) and again the next day for President Estrada, who held a feast inside a classroom just meters away from a mosque.[^cite_note-Camp_Abubakar-105]

### Retaliatory attacks

As a result, several Islamic rebel groups retaliated, [bombing several key locations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rizal_Day_Bombings) within the [National Capital Region](72340.md) on December 30, 2000, resulting in 22 deaths and hundreds of people injured. Saifullah Yunos, one of the perpetrators, was arrested in [Cagayan de Oro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cagayan_de_Oro) as he was about to board a plane bound for Manila in May 2003.[^cite_note-MST2003May26-107] In 2004, two members of the [Jemaah Islamiyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jemaah_Islamiyah) were arrested, namely Mamasao Naga and Abdul Pata, as they were identified by [Fathur Rahman al-Ghozi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fathur_Rahman_al-Ghozi) as responsible for the train bombing.[^cite_note-SunStar2-108] Al-Ghozi was also arrested, but was later killed in a firefight when he tried to escape from prison on October 13, 2003.

## Arroyo administration (2001–2010)

On May 27, 2001, the Abu Sayyaf [seized twenty hostages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dos_Palmas_kidnappings) from an upscale resort in [Palawan](240395.md). Four of the hostages managed to escape.[^cite_note-bbc20013-109] The kidnapping group composed of 40 gunmen then seized the Dr. Jose Torres Memorial Hospital and St. Peter's Church compound in the town of [Lamitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamitan) in [Basilan](297003.md)[^cite_note-110] and claimed to have taken captive 200 people, although 20 people were confirmed to be taken captive inside the hospital, including the staff and the patients.[^cite_note-bbc20012-111][^cite_note-cdnn2001-112]

There was a crossfire between the Philippine Army and [Abu Sayyaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Sayyaf) rebels in [Lamitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamitan) following the hospital takeover which resulted in the deaths of 12 soldiers, including the army captain.[^cite_note-cdnn2001-112] Up to 22 soldiers were reportedly killed in an effort to rescue the hostages.

Five more captives escaped during the battle at Lamitan. Two of the captives were killed prior to the siege in Lamitan, including one beheading.[^cite_note-bbc20013-109] The Abu Sayyaf then conducted a [series of raids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dos_Palmas_kidnappings), including one at a coconut plantation[^cite_note-bbccoconut-113] where the rebel groups hacked the heads of two men using [bolo knives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolo_knife). The owners and a security guard were also held captive and the rebel groups burned down two buildings, including a [chapel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapel), a week after the battle in [Lamitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamitan).[^cite_note-bbccoconut-113] Another raid was conducted on August 2, 2001, on Barangay Balobo in [Lamitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamitan), [Basilan](297003.md). After three days, the Philippine Army rescued numerous hostages[^cite_note-bbclamitan2-114] after they overtook the hideout of the militants, where 11 bodies were found beheaded.[^cite_note-115] Other hostages were either released or had escaped.[^cite_note-bbclamitan2-114]

On June 13, 2001, the number of hostages was calculated at around 28, as three more people were found beheaded in [Basilan](297003.md),[^cite_note-bbc28-116] including Guillermo Sobero.[^cite_note-bbc20014-117] They were beheaded since the Philippine Army would not halt the rescue operation.[^cite_note-bbc20014-117]

The Burnhams were still in the group of 14 still held captive, according to three hostages who escaped in October 2001.[^cite_note-bbc20014-117] On June 7, 2002, after a year of the hostages being held captive, a rescue mission was conducted resulting in the deaths of Martin Burnham and a nurse named Ediborah Yap[^cite_note-bbc20015-118] after they were caught in the crossfire. Martin was killed by three gunshots to the chest while Gracia Burnham was wounded in her right leg. By this time Nur Misuari ordered his supporters to attack government targets to prevent the [holding of elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_Philippine_general_election) on ARMM in November 2001, ushering his exit as the governor of the region.[^cite_note-Timeline-49] Misuari would be later arrested in 2007 in [Malaysia](3607937.md) and was deported back to the Philippines for trial.[^cite_note-Timeline-49]

In July 2004, Gracia Burnham testified at a trial of eight Abu Sayyaf members, identifying six of the suspects as being her former captors, including Alhamzer Limbong, Abdul Azan Diamla, Abu Khari Moctar, Bas Ishmael, Alzen Jandul and Dazid Baize. Fourteen Abu Sayyaf members were sentenced to [life imprisonment](148681.md) while four were [acquitted](300834.md). Alhamzer Limbong was later killed in a [prison uprising](2999465.md).[^cite_note-119]

These rebel groups, especially the [Abu Sayyaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Sayyaf), conducted several terror attacks, namely the [bombings at Zamboanga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_Zamboanga_City_bombings) in October 2002; the [bombing of SuperFerry 14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_SuperFerry_14_bombing) in February 2004; the [simultaneous bombings in Central Mindanao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Central_Mindanao_bombings) in October 2006; the [beheadings of several Philippine Marines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_Basilan_beheading_incident) in July 2007; the [Batasang Pambansa bombing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batasang_Pambansa_bombing) in November 2007; and the 2009 [bombings in Mindanao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/July_2009_Mindanao_bombings).

One thousand [MILF](51673.md) rebels under the command of [Umbra Kato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umbra_Kato) have seized control of thirty-five villages in the [Cotabato](297394.md) province. Two thousand Philippine troops with helicopters and artillery were sent into the seized area on August 9 to liberate it from the rebels. The MILF had wanted Cotabato to be included in the [Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_Region_in_Muslim_Mindanao). The government and MILF had been negotiating for the inclusion of the province in the Muslim Autonomous Region but the [Supreme Court](1335534.md) had struck down the proposal after hearing concerns from local Christian leaders in the region.

The rebel troops were ordered to leave the area by their commanders, but the contingents under Kato refused to leave the villages they had occupied and instead dug in. The Philippine Army responded on August 9 by bombarding them. The next day, the government forces moved to retake the villages, recapturing two of them from the rebels.[^cite_note-120][^cite_note-121]

Numerous clashes erupted between the Philippine Army and rebel groups, such as the clash on June 14, 2009, that killed 10 rebels.[^cite_note-122]

Between 2002 and 2015, the Philippines and the [United States](3434750.md) were part of a joint military campaign against Islamist terrorism known as [Operation Enduring Freedom – Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Enduring_Freedom_%E2%80%93_Philippines).[^cite_note-123] This was part of the [war on terror](13425800.md).

## Benigno Aquino administration (2010–2016)

### Peace talks and Aquino-Murad meeting

During the term of [President](271126.md) [Benigno Aquino III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benigno_Aquino_III), a series of peace talks for the cessation of hostilities was held, including the meeting of [MILF](51673.md) Chair Al Haj Murad Ibrahim in [Tokyo](30057.md), Japan which was lauded on both sides.[^cite_note-Timeline-49] [Norway](21241.md) also joined the [International Monitoring Team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Monitoring_Team) (IMT) in January 2011, overseeing the ceasefire agreement between the government and MILF on [Mindanao](20546.md). Despite the peace talks, a series of conflicts erupted. On September 10, 2011, Jal Idris, a hardcore member of Abu Sayyaf, was arrested by government forces after a crossfire between the [Philippine Army](6032559.md) and the rebel group[^cite_note-124] The [Armed Forces of the Philippines](23448.md) also killed three Abu Sayyaf militants in a stand-off[^cite_note-125] the day after the arrest of Jal Idris.

Terrorism continued throughout President Aquino's term. Notable cases include when four merchants and a guide were killed by Abu Sayyaf bandits in January 2011.[^cite_note-126] Later a soldier was killed in a clash against the rebels.[^cite_note-127] In August 2011, rebel factions attacked a village in [Sulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulu), killing seven Marines and taking seven civilians captive. They later freed two of the hostages after a ransom was paid.[^cite_note-128] Also, several areas of Mindanao were bombed in August by the government, and a Filipino businesswoman was abducted in September 2011,[^cite_note-129] who was later freed after the three gunmen were gunned down by the [Armed Forces of the Philippines](23448.md).[^cite_note-130]

On October 20, 2011, the MILF was blamed for an attack on 40 government soldiers in the province of [Basilan](297003.md), which led to the deaths of 19 soldiers and six MILF fighters.[^cite_note-131] This violated the ceasefire agreement between the government and MILF, which caused outrage in the government and led to the continuation of the war against [terrorism in the country](9101671.md).

In February 2013, two main camps of the Abu Sayyaf group were overrun by forces of the [Moro National Liberation Front](2821886.md) (MNLF) in its latest offensive in [Patikul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patikul).[^cite_note-132] According to MNLF leader Nur Misuari, the MNLF offensive against the Abu Sayyaf is because of the MNLF opposition to the Abu Sayyaf's human rights abuses, which go against [Islam](6037917.md).

### Zamboanga City crisis

The [Zamboanga City crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zamboanga_City_crisis) erupted on September 9, 2013, when a MNLF faction known by other groups as the Rogue MNLF Elements (RME), under the Sulu State Revolutionary Command (SSRC), led by Ustadz Habier Malik and Khaid Ajibon attempted to raise the flag of the self-proclaimed [Bangsamoro Republik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangsamoro_Republik) at [Zamboanga City](25167165.md) Hall (which had earlier declared its independence on August 12, 2013, in Talipao, Sulu), and took civilians hostage. This armed incursion was met by the Armed Forces of the Philippines (AFP) and the Philippine National Police (PNP), which sought to free the hostages and expel the MNLF from the city. The standoff degenerated into urban warfare, and had brought parts of the city under a standstill for days. On September 28, the government declared the end of military operations in Zamboanga City after successfully defeating the MNLF and rescuing all the hostages.

### Comprehensive Agreement on the Bangsamoro

Main article: [Comprehensive Agreement on the Bangsamoro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensive_Agreement_on_the_Bangsamoro)

On January 24, 2014, the Philippines government chief negotiator [Miriam Coronel-Ferrer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miriam_Coronel-Ferrer) and MILF chief negotiator [Murad Ebrahim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murad_Ebrahim) signed a peace agreement in [Kuala Lumpur](16854.md). The agreement would pave the way for the creation of the new Muslim autonomous entity called "Bangsamoro" under a law to be approved by the Philippine Congress.[^cite_note-133] The government aims to set up the region by 2016. The agreement calls for Muslim self-rule in parts of the southern Philippines in exchange for a deactivation of rebel forces by the MILF. MILF forces would turn over their firearms to a third party to be selected by the MILF and the Philippine government. A regional police force would be established, and the Philippine military would reduce the presence of troops and help disband private armies in the area.[^cite_note-PhilippinePeaceBreakthrough2014-50] On March 27, 2014, the peace process concluded with the signing of the [Comprehensive Agreement on the Bangsamoro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensive_Agreement_on_the_Bangsamoro) between the [Government of the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_the_Philippines) and the [Moro Islamic Liberation Front](51673.md).

*[The New York Times](30680.md)* claimed that the [peace deal between the Philippines and MILF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensive_Agreement_on_the_Bangsamoro) "seeks to bring prosperity to the restive south and weaken the appeal of the extremist groups", and linked the winding down of [an American military counterterrorism operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Enduring_Freedom_%E2%80%93_Philippines) to increased American military cooperation with the Philippines against China.[^cite_note-134] *The New York Times* hailed Mr Aquino's peace agreement as an "accomplishment" as it reported on Aquino raising the alarm on China in the South China Sea.[^cite_note-135] *The New York Times* editorial board published an article siding with the Philippines against China in the South China Sea dispute and supporting the Philippines' actions against China.[^cite_note-136][^cite_note-137] *The New York Times* editorial board endorsed aggressive American military action against China in the South China Sea.[^cite_note-138][^cite_note-139]

### Abu Sayyaf association with ISIL

See also: [Islamic state in the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_state_in_the_Philippines)

On July 23, 2014, [Abu Sayyaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Sayyaf) leader Isnilon Hapilon swore loyalty to [Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Bakr_al-Baghdadi) in a video, along with the rest of the organization, giving the [Islamic State](9087364.md) (known as ISIL and ISIS) a presence in the Philippines.[^cite_note-Abu_Sayyaf_leader_swears_oath_of_loyalty_to_ISIL-140][^cite_note-ISIL_gains_supporters-141] In September 2014, the group began kidnapping people to ransom, in the name of ISIL.[^cite_note-142]

### Mamasapano clash

On January 25, 2015, the Philippine National Police's [Special Action Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Action_Force) (SAF) conducted [an operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Mamasapano_clash) to capture [Abdul Basit Usman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdul_Basit_Usman) and the Malaysian terrorist leader [Marwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zulkifli_Abdhir) in [Mamasapano, Maguindanao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamasapano,_Maguindanao). They were trapped between the [MILF](51673.md)'s 105th Base Command, the BIFF, and several armed groups. Forty four SAF members were killed on what is known as the [Mamasapano clash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamasapano_clash), but they were able to eliminate Marwan. Alleged United States involvement in the botched operation would likely be a setback for a so-called Asian "pivot" by the [United States Armed Forces](32212.md).[^cite_note-143]

In February 2015, the BIFF unsuccessfully fought for territory in the boundary of [Maguindanao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maguindanao) and [Cotabato](297394.md) provinces. Subsequently, the Philippine Army, along with the Philippine Marines, declared a state of all-out-war against the BIFF. MILF forces were pulled out to prevent them from falling victim to the fighting.

## Duterte administration (2016–2022)

The MILF and MNLF have expressed their commitment to peace and in finally ending the 47-year-old insurgency while the offensive against Abu Sayyaf and other splinter groups have continued, with skirmishes in [Jolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jolo), [Basilan](297003.md) and other parts of Mindanao. A [bombing in Davao City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Davao_City_bombing) in September 2016 killed 15 people.

### Maute group attack and siege of Marawi

See also: [Siege of Marawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Marawi)

On May 23, 2017, the [Maute group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maute_group) [attacked Marawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marawi_crisis), they were led by the Malaysian terrorists. President [Rodrigo Duterte](7407293.md) declared [Proclamation No. 216](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclamation_No._216), which placed the whole of Mindanao under a state of [martial law](50477.md) and suspended [the writ of habeas corpus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habeas_corpus). Clashes continued until October 2017 as the battle for Marawi City pitted Islamic militants against the Philippine government forces. Violence was severe. The government used heavy artillery and air strikes to shell Abu Sayyaf and Maute positions while the militant groups resorted to executing captured Christians.[^cite_note-144] In 2018, two bombing incidents involving Abu Sayyaf and the [BIFF](39963003.md) occurred, [one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Lamitan_bombing) in [Lamitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamitan), [Basilan](297003.md) and [two separate incidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Isulan_bombings) in [Isulan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isulan), [Sultan Kudarat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultan_Kudarat).

### Passage of the Bangsamoro Organic Law

Main article: [Bangsamoro Organic Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangsamoro_Organic_Law)

On July 26, 2018, Duterte signed the [Bangsamoro Organic Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangsamoro_Organic_Law),[^cite_note-PhilStar-DuterteSignsBOL-145][^cite_note-MB-DuterteSignsBOL-146] which abolished the [Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_Region_in_Muslim_Mindanao) and provided for the basic structure of government for the [Bangsamoro Autonomous Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangsamoro_Autonomous_Region), following the agreements set forth in the [Comprehensive Agreement on the Bangsamoro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensive_Agreement_on_the_Bangsamoro) peace agreement signed between the [Government of the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_the_Philippines) under President Benigno Aquino III and the MILF in 2014.[^cite_note-gmafaqs-147] From June 2019[^cite_note-148] to May 2022, as part of the "normalization process" of the Bangsamoro's war-to-peace transition, a total of about 19,200 former MILF combatants and 2,100 weapons were [decommissioned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demobilization).[^cite_note-149][^cite_note-CrisisOrg-AsiaReport-Feb2022-150]

### Moro inter-clan war

In May 2020, Philippine media reported on clan warfare that displaced approximately 4,500 civilians from the affected areas. The previous year, there was a bloody clash between two large families, forcing hundreds of people to flee. Inter-clan violence known as *rido* can be traced back to pre-colonial power struggles between local tribal leaders. The strongmen of the time had significant economic and political power, and enforced that power through their own armed forces (militias).[^cite_note-151]

### 2022

On August 10, two armed Moro groups clashed in [Pikit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pikit), [Cotabato](297394.md) forcing hundreds of locals out of the area. According to local leaders, the attacks are related to prior fatal ambush of a barangay chairman, where the municipal police tagged two clans responsible for the murder of the official.[^cite_note-152]

### 2023

On September 19, a civilian was hit by a stray bullet and killed in firefights between two rival clans affiliated with the MILF in Kaya-Kaya and Tukanolocong villages in [Datu Abdullah Sangki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Datu_Abdullah_Sangki), [Maguindanao del Sur](67871248.md).[^cite_note-153]

On November 23, a member of the Moro Islamic Liberation Front was killed while five others were wounded in explosions in Cotabato and Maguindanao del Sur.[^cite_note-154]

Later during the year, the government declared that [Sulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulu) was free of Abu Sayyaf militants. In [Basilan](297003.md), the government announced that it would build 39 houses for former Abu Sayyaf members.[^cite_note-155]

#### Mindanao State University bombing

Mindanao State University bombing aftermath

On December 3, ISIS claimed responsibility for the deadly explosion during a Catholic mass in [Mindanao State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mindanao_State_University) on the same day that claimed four lives.[^cite_note-156]

### 2025

On January 22, the Philippine Army were on their way to escort a [United Nations Development Programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Development_Programme) (UNDP) team when they were attacked by armed men allegedly linked to the MILF in [Sumisip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumisip), Basilan.[^cite_note-157] The attack occurred in the Bangsamoro region which is supervised by a transition government led by the MILF.[^cite_note-158][^cite_note-159] Two soldiers died and twelve were injured. The MILF side sustained two casualties.[^cite_note-160][^cite_note-161] It is considered the most serious breach of the [Comprehensive Agreement on the Bangsamoro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensive_Agreement_on_the_Bangsamoro) of 2014.[^cite_note-launchesprobe-162]

### 2026

On January 23, four soldiers were ambushed and killed in Lininding, [Munai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munai), [Lanao del Norte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanao_del_Norte) by the Dawlah Islamiya-Maute Group (DI-MG).[^cite_note-163] The local troops were on a humanitarian mission when the attack occurred.[^cite_note-164]

On April 17, suspected members of the DI-MG opened fire to the Philippine Army and police officers who were attempting to serve arrest warrants to their commander in [Marantao](2070517.md), [Lanao del Sur](297370.md). The clash killed 10 suspected members of the group.[^cite_note-165]
