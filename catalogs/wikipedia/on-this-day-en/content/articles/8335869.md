The **Embassy of the United States in Seoul** is the [diplomatic mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diplomatic_missions_of_the_United_States) of the [United States](3434750.md) in [South Korea](27019.md). The [embassy](8970.md) is charged with [diplomacy](28165911.md) and [South Korea–United States relations](5748222.md). The [United States Ambassador to Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambassador_of_the_United_States_to_South_Korea) is the head of the diplomatic mission of the United States to South Korea.

## History

The United States has had diplomatic relations with [Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea), with interruption, since the late 1870s.

Korean government relations with nations not aligned with [Qing China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qing_dynasty) were more or less unknown and not welcome before that time. As China's power began to seriously wane in the 1800s, and as Japan's power, and increasing industrialization was on the rise, Korea began to make changes and make overtures to other nations.



Old American Legation.

In 1883 the first Minister to [Joseon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseon) (Korea) arrived in Seoul and a legation of sorts began operation. Soon a legation was run out of the Minister's residence, a [villa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa) given to the U.S. in 1888 by a Korean royal, located just behind the [Deoksugung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deoksugung). This site is still owned by the [U.S. government](195149.md), and is the current site of the Habib House, the [official](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_residence) [ambassadorial residence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambassadorial_residence) of the U.S. Ambassador. The Habib House was built as a [hanok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanok) (traditional Korean home) in 1974 and is named after Ambassador [Philip Charles Habib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Charles_Habib).[^cite_note-1]

The rise of the [Empire of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Japan) as a [great power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_power) began with the Japanese victory over China in the [First Sino-Japanese War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Sino-Japanese_War) (1895) and victory of Japan over [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire) in the [Russo-Japanese War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Japanese_War) (1905). In 1910, Korea entered a period of [Japanese rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea_under_Japanese_rule) that ended its [independence](62450.md). After this was formalized in the [Japan-Korea Annexation Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan-Korea_Annexation_Treaty), the U.S. mission in Seoul was shut down.

A U.S. embassy was not again established in Korea until the end of [World War II](32927.md), after the [Allied forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II) [defeated the Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_War). American and [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) forces took control of Korea from the Japanese. The Americans set up an [American military government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USAMGIK) in Seoul in the South, and while the Soviets set up a government in [Pyongyang](47948.md) in the north. The U.S. recognized the government in Seoul in 1948, the same year North Korea was recognized by the Soviets.

From 1948 the American embassy operated out of a leased [hotel](14276.md) building.

### Korean War



U.S. Marine raises the flag at the old American Legation building in Seoul.

When North Korean troops invaded South Korea on June 25, 1950, the [Korean War](16772.md) began, and the U.S. Embassy in Seoul was evacuated. Seoul was captured by the North Korean forces by the end of June. The [chancery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancery_\(diplomacy\)) (on the 5th floor of [Hotel Bando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotel_Bando?action=edit\&redlink=1)) was retaken September 26 that year, by Easy Company of [2nd Battalion, 1st Marine Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2nd_Battalion_1st_Marines), acting as a [United Nations](31769.md) force.[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3] It was lost again to [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Volunteer_Army) and [North Korean forces](21263.md) in early 1951.

In April 1951, United Nations forces retook Seoul for good. The Korean War ended in July 1953 in [stalemate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalemate), and the U.S. Embassy was eventually re-opened.

### After the Korean War

The embassy has operated in Seoul continuously since the end of the Korean War.

The current [chancery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancery_\(building\)) building was built in 1962 and was initially an office for the [United States Agency for International Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Agency_for_International_Development) (USAID). Several years later, the building was given to the U.S. Embassy. The Chancery has a virtually identical sister building, built at the same time, directly to its north. This building was once also part of the USAID office in Seoul, but was turned over to the [South Korean government](4389689.md), which uses it as [National Museum of Korean Contemporary History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Korean_Contemporary_History).

The Chancery is located in the heart of [downtown Seoul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downtown_Seoul), in the [Jongno District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jongno_District). It is on the main street leading to the [Gyeongbokgung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyeongbokgung) and directly next to several major Korean government agency buildings. At the time of its construction the building was one of the tallest in Seoul and comparatively modern. Today the Chancery building is much too small and antiquated for the embassy's needs.

Attempts at building a new Chancery have been in the plans since about 1980, but funding issues by the [United States Congress](31756.md), botched negotiations with the South Korean government, and political wrangling between the U.S. and South Korean governments over an appropriate site, have led to the lengthy delay in a new Chancery. In 1986 the U.S. purchased land, the [Kyunggi Girls' High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyunggi_Girls'_High_School?action=edit\&redlink=1) site, near the U.S. Ambassador's residence. This was encouraged by the South Korean government at the time, with the idea that this site would be the home to a new Chancery building. Full efforts to build there by the Americans were rebuffed by recently empowered local community groups who believed that construction at that site would disturb buried [archaeological evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological_site), would overshadow the nearby [Deoksugung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deoksugung) and ruin the aesthetics of the neighborhood. Current plans call for a new Chancery to be built on land to be relinquished by the [U.S. Army](32087.md) leaving the [Yongsan Garrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yongsan_Garrison) south of [downtown Seoul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downtown_Seoul), but still north of the [Han River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_River_\(Korea\)).

## Role

The U.S. Embassy in Seoul has traditionally played a key and prominent role in South Korea politics. Issues such as the ever-present danger from North Korea, the [presence of U.S. troops in South Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Forces_Korea), and the deference of South Korea to the U.S. have been factors that lead to such importance.

Increasingly the U.S. Embassy has been the focus of demonstrations in Seoul. Whenever a controversial issue crops up involving the U.S. presence in Korea, a [demonstration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demonstration_\(people\)) will almost invariably occur at the U.S. Embassy. Several [platoons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platoon) of Korean [riot police](1148542.md) are always present in front of the U.S. Embassy. In the 1990s, other U.S. diplomatic posts in Korea have been shut down, the last being the Consulate in Pusan in early 1999.

## Busan Consulate

A [consulate](6770059.md) in [Busan](68115.md) (Pusan) was closed in 1999, but in February 2007 a [Virtual Presence Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_eDiplomacy) was opened in Busan (after 1999 the city changed its [romanized spelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revised_Romanization_of_Korean)). In October 2007, VPP Busan was changed to the status of an [American Presence Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Presence_Post)—equivalent to a consulate—with a resident consul. In 2016, the office was promoted to a full Consulate. The Busan Consulate is located in a commercial [office building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_building), and is staffed by a single foreign service officer with several foreign service nationals playing key roles. It is increasingly conducting community outreach. It does not have a visa issuance section and it reports to and makes all visa issuance referrals to the Embassy in Seoul.
