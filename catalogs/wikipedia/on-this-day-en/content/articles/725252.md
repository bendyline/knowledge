# Broadway theatre

This article is about the type of theatre. For other uses, see [Broadway Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadway_Theatre_\(disambiguation\)).



From right to left: the [John Golden Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Golden_Theatre), [Bernard B. Jacobs Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_B._Jacobs_Theatre), [Gerald Schoenfeld Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_Schoenfeld_Theatre), and [Booth Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booth_Theatre) on [West 45th Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/45th_Street_\(Manhattan\)) in [Manhattan](45470.md)'s [Theater District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theater_District,_Manhattan)

**Broadway theatre**[^cite_note-1] is a [theatre](20913771.md) genre that consists of the theatrical performances presented in [41 professional theaters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Broadway_theaters), each with 500 or more seats, in the [Theater District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theater_District,_Manhattan) and [Lincoln Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Center) along [Broadway](4626.md), in [Midtown Manhattan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midtown_Manhattan), New York City.[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3] Broadway and [London](17867.md)'s [West End](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_End_theatre) together represent the highest commercial level of live theater in the [English-speaking world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English-speaking_world).[^cite_note-4]

While the [Broadway thoroughfare](4626.md) is eponymous with the district, it is closely identified with [Times Square](31389.md). Only three [theaters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theater_\(structure\)) are located on Broadway itself: the [Broadway Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadway_Theatre_\(53rd_Street\)), [Palace Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_Theatre_\(New_York_City\)), and [Winter Garden Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_Garden_Theatre). The rest are located on the numbered cross streets, extending from the [Nederlander Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nederlander_Theatre) one block south of Times Square on West 41st Street, north along either side of Broadway to [53rd Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/53rd_Street_\(Manhattan\)), and [Vivian Beaumont Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivian_Beaumont_Theater), at [Lincoln Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Center) on West 65th Street. While exceptions exist, the term "Broadway theatre" is used predominantly to describe venues with seating capacities of at least 500 people. Smaller theaters in New York City are referred to as [off-Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Off-Broadway), regardless of location, while very small venues with fewer than 100 seats are called [off-off-Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Off-off-Broadway), a term that can also apply to non-commercial, [avant-garde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde), or productions held outside of traditional theater venues.[^cite_note-5]

The Theater District is an internationally prominent [tourist attraction in New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_New_York_City). According to [The Broadway League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Broadway_League), shows on Broadway sold tickets worth about US\$1.54 billion in the 2022–2023 season, repeating the feat the 2023–2024 season. About 12.3 million people attended per season.[^cite_note-BroadwayLeagueStatistics-6]

Most Broadway shows are [musicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_theatre). Historian [Martin Shefter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Shefter) argues that "Broadway musicals, culminating in the productions of [Rodgers and Hammerstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodgers_and_Hammerstein), became enormously influential forms of [American popular culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_popular_culture)" and contributed to making New York City the [cultural capital of the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_New_York_City).[^cite_note-7]

The [Tony Awards](54741.md) presented annually by the [American Theatre Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Theatre_Wing) and [The Broadway League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Broadway_League)[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9] recognize excellence in live Broadway theatre and are the [American theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_theatre) industry's highest honor.&#x20;

## History

### Early theater in New York



The interior of [Park Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park_Theatre_\(Manhattan\)), built in 1798

New York City's first significant theatre was established in the mid-18th century, around 1750, when actor-managers Walter Murray and Thomas Kean established a resident theatre company at the [Theatre on Nassau Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_on_Nassau_Street) in [Lower Manhattan](455646.md), which held about 280 people. They presented [William Shakespeare](32897.md)'s plays and [ballad operas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballad_operas) such as *[The Beggar's Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beggar's_Opera)*.[^cite_note-Kenrick-10] In 1752, [William Hallam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Hallam_\(theatre_manager\)) sent a company of twelve actors from Britain to the colonies with his brother [Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Hallam) as their manager. They established a theatre in [Williamsburg, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Williamsburg,_Virginia), and opened with *[The Merchant of Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Merchant_of_Venice)* and *The Anatomist*. The company moved to New York in 1753, performing ballad operas and ballad-farces like *Damon and Phillida*.

During the [Revolutionary War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War), theatre was suspended in New York City. But after the war's end, theatre resumed in 1798, when the 2,000-seat [Park Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park_Theatre_\(Manhattan\)) was built on Chatham Street on present-day [Park Row](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park_Row_\(Manhattan\)).[^cite_note-Kenrick-10] A second major theatre, [Bowery Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowery_Theatre), opened in 1826,[^cite_note-11] followed by others.

By the 1840s, [P.T. Barnum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P.T._Barnum) was operating an entertainment complex in Lower Manhattan. In 1829, at Broadway and Prince Street, [Niblo's Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niblo's_Garden) opened and soon became one of New York's premier nightspots. The 3,000-seat theatre presented all sorts of [musical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_theatre) and non-musical entertainments. In 1844, [Palmo's Opera House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palmo's_Opera_House) opened and presented opera for only four seasons before bankruptcy led to its rebranding as a venue for plays under the name Burton's Theatre. The [Astor Opera House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astor_Opera_House) opened in 1847. A riot broke out in 1849 when the lower-class patrons of the Bowery Theatre objected to what they perceived as snobbery by the upper-class audiences at Astor Place: "After the [Astor Place Riot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astor_Place_Riot) of 1849, [entertainment in New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entertainment_industry) was divided along class lines: opera was chiefly for the upper-middle and upper classes, minstrel shows and melodramas for the middle-class, variety shows in concert saloons for men of the working class and the slumming middle-class."[^cite_note-12]

The plays of [William Shakespeare](32897.md) were frequently performed on the Broadway stage during the period, most notably by American actor [Edwin Booth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Booth) who was internationally known for his performance as [Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Hamlet). Booth played the role for a famous 100 consecutive performances at the [Winter Garden Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_Garden_Theatre_\(1850\)) in 1865 (with the run ending just a few months before Booth's brother [John Wilkes Booth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wilkes_Booth) assassinated [Abraham Lincoln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln)), and would later revive the role at his own [Booth's Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booth's_Theatre) (which was managed for a time by his brother [Junius Brutus Booth Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junius_Brutus_Booth_Jr.)). Other renowned Shakespeareans who appeared in New York in this era were [Henry Irving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Irving), [Tommaso Salvini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tommaso_Salvini), [Fanny Davenport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fanny_Davenport), and [Charles Fechter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Fechter).

### Birth of the musical and post-Civil War

Theatre in New York moved from [Downtown](455646.md) gradually to [Midtown Manhattan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midtown_Manhattan), beginning around 1850, seeking less expensive real estate. At the beginning of the nineteenth century, the area that now comprises the [Theater District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theater_District,_Manhattan) was owned by a handful of families and comprised a few farms. In 1836, Mayor [Cornelius Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelius_Lawrence) opened [42nd Street](61068.md) and invited Manhattanites to "enjoy the pure clean air."[^cite_note-Urban_Development-13] Close to 60 years later, theatrical entrepreneur [Oscar Hammerstein I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Hammerstein_I) built the iconic [Victoria Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_Theatre_\(Hammerstein's\)) on West 42nd Street.[^cite_note-Urban_Development-13]

Broadway's first "long-run" musical was a 50-performance hit called *The Elves* in 1857. In 1870, the heart of Broadway was in [Union Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Square_\(New_York_City\)), and by the end of the century, many theatres were near [Madison Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madison_Square). Theatres arrived in the [Times Square](31389.md) area in the early 1900s, and the Broadway theatres consolidated there after a large number were built around the square in the 1920s and 1930s. New York runs continued to lag far behind those in London,[^cite_note-longrun-14] but [Laura Keene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laura_Keene)'s "musical burletta" *[The Seven Sisters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Seven_Sisters_\(play\))* (1860) shattered previous New York records with a run of 253 performances.



*[The Black Crook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Black_Crook)* (1866), considered by some historians to be the first musical.

[^cite_note-Morley-15]

&#x20;[Poster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poster) for the 1873 revival by [The Kiralfy Brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Kiralfy_Brothers).

The first theatre piece that conforms to the modern conception of a musical, adding dance and original music that helped to tell the story, is considered to be *[The Black Crook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Black_Crook)*, which premiered in New York on September 12, 1866. The production was five-and-a-half hours long, but despite its length, it ran for a record-breaking 474 performances. The same year, *The Black Domino/Between You, Me and the Post* was the first show to call itself a "musical comedy".[^cite_note-Morley-15]

[Tony Pastor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Pastor) opened the first [vaudeville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaudeville) theatre one block east of Union Square in 1881, where [Lillian Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lillian_Russell) performed. Comedians [Edward Harrigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Harrigan) and [Tony Hart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Hart_\(theater\)) produced and starred in musicals on Broadway between 1878 (*The Mulligan Guard Picnic*) and 1890, with book and lyrics by Harrigan and music by his father-in-law [David Braham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Braham). These musical comedies featured characters and situations taken from the everyday life of New York's lower classes and represented a significant step forward from vaudeville and burlesque, towards a more literate form. They starred high-quality professional singers (Lillian Russell, [Vivienne Segal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivienne_Segal), and [Fay Templeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fay_Templeton)), instead of the amateurs, often sex workers, who had starred in earlier musical forms.

As transportation improved, poverty in New York diminished, and street lighting made for safer travel at night, the number of potential patrons for the growing number of theatres increased enormously. Plays could run longer and still draw in the audiences, leading to better profits and improved production values. As in England, during the latter half of the century, the theatre began to be cleaned up, with less [prostitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prostitution) hindering the attendance of the theatre by women. [Gilbert and Sullivan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_and_Sullivan)'s family-friendly [comic opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comic_opera) hits, beginning with *[H.M.S. Pinafore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H.M.S._Pinafore)* in 1878, were imported to New York (by the authors and also in numerous unlicensed productions). They were imitated in New York by American productions such as [Reginald Dekoven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reginald_De_Koven)'s *Robin Hood* (1891) and [John Philip Sousa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Philip_Sousa)'s *[El Capitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Capitan_\(operetta\))* (1896), along with operas, ballets, and other British and European hits.



Sheet music to "Give My Regards to Broadway"

[Charles H. Hoyt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_H._Hoyt)'s show *[A Trip to Chinatown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Trip_to_Chinatown)* (1891) became Broadway's long-run champion when it surpassed *[Adonis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adonis_\(musical\))* and its 603 total performances in 1893, holding the stage for 657 performances. *Chinatown* itself was surpassed by the musical *[Irene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irene_\(musical\))* (1919) in 1921 as the longest-running Broadway musical, and even earlier, in March 1920, by *[Lightnin'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lightnin'_\(play\))* (1918) as the longest-running Broadway show. In 1896, theatre owners [Marc Klaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc_Klaw) and [A. L. Erlanger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._L._Erlanger) formed the [Theatrical Syndicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatrical_Syndicate), which controlled almost every legitimate theatre in the U.S. for the next sixteen years.[^cite_note-16] However, smaller vaudeville and variety houses proliferated, and [Off-Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Off-Broadway) was well established by the end of the nineteenth century.

*[A Trip to Coontown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Trip_to_Coontown)* (1898) was the first musical comedy entirely produced and performed by [African Americans](2154.md) in a Broadway theatre (inspired largely by the routines of the [minstrel shows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minstrel_show)), followed by the [ragtime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ragtime)-tinged *[Clorindy: The Origin of the Cakewalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clorindy:_The_Origin_of_the_Cakewalk)* (1898), and the highly successful *[In Dahomey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_Dahomey)* (1902). Hundreds of musical comedies were staged on Broadway in the 1890s and early 1900s made up of songs written in New York's [Tin Pan Alley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin_Pan_Alley) involving composers such as [Gus Edwards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gus_Edwards_\(vaudeville\)), [John Walter Bratton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Walter_Bratton), and [George M. Cohan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_M._Cohan) (*[Little Johnny Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Johnny_Jones)* (1904), *45 Minutes From Broadway* (1906), and *George Washington Jr.* (1906)). Still, New York runs continued to be relatively short, with a few exceptions, compared with London runs, until [World War I](4764461.md).[^cite_note-longrun-14] A few very successful British musicals continued to achieve great success in New York, including *[Florodora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florodora)* in 1900–01.

### Early 20th century



Victor Herbert

In the early years of the twentieth century, translations of popular late-nineteenth century continental operettas were joined by the "Princess Theatre" shows of the 1910s, by writers such as [P. G. Wodehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._G._Wodehouse), [Guy Bolton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy_Bolton), and [Harry B. Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_B._Smith). [Victor Herbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Herbert), whose work included some intimate musical plays with modern settings as well as his string of famous operettas (*[The Fortune Teller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Fortune_Teller_\(operetta\))* (1898), *[Babes in Toyland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babes_in_Toyland_\(operetta\))* (1903), *[Mlle. Modiste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mlle._Modiste)* (1905), *[The Red Mill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Red_Mill)* (1906), and *[Naughty Marietta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naughty_Marietta_\(operetta\))* (1910)).[^cite_note-17]

Beginning with *The Red Mill*, Broadway shows installed electric signs outside the theatres. Since colored bulbs burned out too quickly, white lights were used, and Broadway was nicknamed "The Great White Way". In August 1919, the [Actors' Equity Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actors'_Equity_Association) demanded a standard contract for all professional productions. After a strike shut down all the theatres, the producers were forced to agree. By the 1920s, the [Shubert Brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shubert_Brothers) had risen to take over the majority of the theatres from the Erlanger syndicate.[^cite_note-18]

During this time, the play *[Lightnin'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lightnin'_\(play\))* by [Winchell Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winchell_Smith) and [Frank Bacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Bacon_\(actor\)) became the first Broadway show to reach 700 performances. From then, it would go on to become the first show to reach 1,000 performances. *Lightnin'* was the longest-running Broadway show until being overtaken in performance totals by *[Abie's Irish Rose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abie's_Irish_Rose)* in 1925.

### Competing with motion pictures



Broadway north from 38th St., New York City, showing the [Casino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casino_Theatre_\(New_York,_New_York\)) and [Knickerbocker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knickerbocker_Theatre_\(Broadway\)) Theatres ("Listen, Lester", visible at lower right, played the Knickerbocker from December 23, 1918, to August 16, 1919), a sign pointing to [Maxine Elliott's Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxine_Elliott_Theatre), which is out of view on 39th Street, and a sign advertising the [Winter Garden Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_Garden_Theatre), which is out of view at 50th Street. All but the Winter Garden are demolished. The old [Metropolitan Opera House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Opera_House_\(39th_St\)) and the old [Times Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Times_Square) are visible on the left.

The motion picture mounted a challenge to the stage. At first, films were [silent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_film) and presented only limited competition. By the end of the 1920s, films like *[The Jazz Singer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jazz_Singer_\(1927_film\))* were presented with synchronized sound, and critics wondered if cinema would replace live theatre altogether. While live vaudeville could not compete with these inexpensive films that featured vaudeville stars and major comedians of the day, other theatres survived. The musicals of the [Roaring Twenties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roaring_Twenties), borrowing from vaudeville, [music hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_hall), and other light entertainment, tended to ignore plot in favor of emphasizing star actors and actresses, big dance routines, and popular songs.

[Florenz Ziegfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florenz_Ziegfeld) produced annual spectacular song-and-dance revues on Broadway featuring extravagant sets and elaborate costumes, but there was little to tie the various numbers together. Typical of the 1920s were lighthearted productions such as *[Sally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sally_\(musical\))*; *[Lady Be Good](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Be_Good_\(musical\))*; *[Sunny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunny_\(musical\))*; *[No, No, Nanette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No,_No,_Nanette)*; *Harlem*; *[Oh, Kay!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oh,_Kay!)*; and *[Funny Face](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funny_Face_\(musical\))*. Their books may have been forgettable, but they produced enduring standards from [George Gershwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Gershwin), [Cole Porter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cole_Porter), [Jerome Kern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome_Kern), [Vincent Youmans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_Youmans), and [Rodgers and Hart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodgers_and_Hart), among others, and [Noël Coward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No%C3%ABl_Coward), [Sigmund Romberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_Romberg), and [Rudolf Friml](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Friml) continued in the vein of Victor Herbert. Live theatre has survived since the invention of cinema.

### Between the wars

&#x20;Leaving these comparatively frivolous entertainments behind and taking the drama a step forward, *[Show Boat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Show_Boat)* premiered on December 27, 1927, at the [Ziegfeld Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ziegfeld_Theatre_\(1927\)). It represented a complete integration of book and score, with dramatic themes, as told through the music, dialogue, setting, and movement, woven together more seamlessly than in previous musicals. It ran for 572 performances.[^cite_note-19]



This February 21, 1925 *[Judge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judge_\(magazine\))* magazine cover by [Ralph Barton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Barton) features caricatures of various movie and theater personalities from the 1920s; click on a caricature to be taken to the corresponding person's Wikipedia article.

The 1920s also spawned a new age of American playwright with the emergence of [Eugene O'Neill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_O'Neill), whose plays *[Beyond the Horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beyond_the_Horizon_\(play\))*, *[Anna Christie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Christie)*, *[The Hairy Ape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hairy_Ape)*, *[Strange Interlude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strange_Interlude)*, and *[Mourning Becomes Electra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mourning_Becomes_Electra)* proved that there was an audience for serious drama on Broadway, and O'Neill's success paved the way for major dramatists like [Elmer Rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elmer_Rice), [Maxwell Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell_Anderson), [Robert E. Sherwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_E._Sherwood), [Clifford Odets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifford_Odets), [Tennessee Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tennessee_Williams), and [Arthur Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Miller), as well as writers of comedy like [George S. Kaufman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_S._Kaufman) and [Moss Hart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moss_Hart). Classical revivals also proved popular with Broadway theatre-goers, notably [John Barrymore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Barrymore) in *[Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet)* and *[Richard III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_III_\(play\)),&#x20;*[John Gielgud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gielgud) in *Hamlet*, *[The Importance of Being Earnest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Importance_of_Being_Earnest)* and *[Much Ado About Nothing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Much_Ado_About_Nothing)*, [Walter Hampden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Hampden) and [José Ferrer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Ferrer) in *[Cyrano de Bergerac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrano_de_Bergerac_\(play\))*, [Paul Robeson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Robeson) and Ferrer in *[Othello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Othello)*, [Maurice Evans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Evans_\(actor\)) in *[Richard II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_II_\(play\))* and the plays of [George Bernard Shaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Bernard_Shaw), and [Katharine Cornell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katharine_Cornell) in such plays as *[Romeo and Juliet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romeo_and_Juliet)*, *[Antony and Cleopatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antony_and_Cleopatra)*, and *[Candida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candida_\(play\))*.

In 1930, [Theatre Guild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Guild)'s production of *[Roar, China!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roar,_China!)* was Broadway's first play with a majority Asian cast.[^cite_note-\:Gao-20]

As [World War II](32927.md) approached, a dozen Broadway dramas addressed the rise of Nazism in Europe and the issue of American non-intervention. The most successful was [Lillian Hellman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lillian_Hellman)'s *[Watch on the Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watch_on_the_Rhine_\(play\))*, which opened in April 1941.[^cite_note-21]

### Postwar era

After the lean years of the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression), Broadway theatre had entered a golden age with the blockbuster hit *[Oklahoma!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma!)*, in 1943, which ran for 2,212 performances. According to [John Kenrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Kenrick_\(theatre_writer\))'s writings on Broadway musicals, "Every season saw new stage musicals send songs to the top of the charts. Public demand, a booming economy and abundant creative talent kept Broadway hopping. To this day, the shows of the 1950s form the core of the musical theatre repertory."[^cite_note-22]

### Decline in late 1960s

Kenrick notes that "the late 1960s marked a time of cultural upheaval. All those changes would prove painful for many, including those behind the scenes, as well as those in the audience."[^cite_note-23] Of the 1970s, Kenrick writes: "Just when it seemed that traditional book musicals were back in style, the decade ended with critics and audiences giving mixed signals."[^cite_note-24]

[Ken Bloom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Bloom_\(writer\)) observed that "The 1960s and 1970s saw a worsening of the area \[Times Square] and a drop in the number of legitimate shows produced on Broadway."[^cite_note-25] By way of comparison, in the 1950 to 1951 season (May to May), 94 productions opened on Broadway; in the 1969 to 1970 season (June to May), there were 59 productions (fifteen were revivals).[^cite_note-26][^cite_note-27] In the 1920s, there were 70–80 theaters; however, by 1969, there were 36 left.[^cite_note-28]

During this time, many Broadway productions struggled due to low attendance rates, which resulted in perceived mediocrity among such plays. For this reason, the [Theatre Development Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Development_Fund) was created with the purpose of assisting productions with high cultural value that likely would struggle without subsidization, by [offering tickets to those plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TKTS) to consumers at reduced prices.[^cite_note-29]

### Resurgence in early 1980s

In early 1982, [Joe Papp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Papp), the theatrical producer and director who established [The Public Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Public_Theater), led the "Save the Theatres" campaign.[^cite_note-30] It was a not-for-profit group supported by the [Actors Equity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actors_Equity) union to save the theater buildings in the neighborhood from demolition by monied Manhattan development interests.[^cite_note-31][^cite_note-google1-32][^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34] Papp provided resources, recruited a publicist and celebrated actors, and provided audio, lighting, and technical crews for the effort.[^cite_note-google1-32]

At Papp's behest, in July 1982, a bill was introduced in the [97th Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/97th_Congress), entitled "H.R.6885, A bill to designate the Broadway/Times Square Theatre District in the City of New York as a national historic site".[^cite_note-HR6885-35] The legislation would have provided certain U.S. government resources and assistance to help the city preserve the district.[^cite_note-HR6885-35] Faced with strong opposition and lobbying by [Mayor Ed Koch's Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Koch) and corporate Manhattan development interests, the bill was not passed. The Save the Theatres campaign then turned their efforts to supporting the establishment of the Theater District as a registered [historic district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historic_district).[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-google2-37] In December 1983, Save the Theatres prepared "The Broadway Theater District, a Preservation Development and Management Plan", and demanded that each theater in the district receive landmark designation.[^cite_note-google2-37] Mayor Ed Koch ultimately reacted by creating a Theater Advisory Council, which included Papp.[^cite_note-google1-32]

### 2020s: COVID-19 and aftermath

Due to the [COVID-19 pandemic in New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_New_York_City), Broadway theaters closed on March 12, 2020, shuttering 16 shows that were playing or were in the process of opening. The Broadway League shutdown was extended multiple times as 2020 passed into 2021.[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39] Governor [Andrew Cuomo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Cuomo) announced that most sectors of [New York](8210131.md) would have their restrictions lifted on May 19, 2021, but he stated that Broadway theatres would not be able to immediately resume performances on this date due to logistical reasons. In May 2021, Cuomo set a target that Broadway theaters should be allowed to reopen on September 14. The League confirmed that performances would resume in the fall season.[^cite_note-40]

*[Springsteen on Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springsteen_on_Broadway)* became the first full-length show to resume performances, opening on June 26, 2021, to 1,721 vaccinated patrons at the [St. James Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._James_Theatre).[^cite_note-41] *[Pass Over](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pass_Over_\(play\))* then had its first preview on August 4, and opened on August 22, 2021, becoming the first new play to open.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43] *[Hadestown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadestown)* and *[Waitress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waitress_\(musical\))* were the first musicals to resume performances on September 2, 2021.[^cite_note-44] The [74th Tony Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/74th_Tony_Awards) were also postponed; the Tony nominations were announced on October 15, 2020,[^cite_note-45] and took place on September 26, 2021.[^cite_note-46] On July 30, 2021, it was announced that all Broadway theaters required attendees aged 12+ to provide proof of full [COVID-19 vaccination](63319438.md). Those under age 12 were required to provide a negative COVID-19 test (PCR within 72 hours or antigen within six hours of the performance start time). Beginning November 8, those ages 5–11 also had the option to provide proof of at least one vaccination shot. Effective December 14, in accordance with NYC's vaccination mandate, guests ages 5–11 were required to have at least one vaccination shot until January 29, 2022, where they had to be fully vaccinated.[^cite_note-47] The vaccine mandate lasted until April 30,[^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49] and attendees were also required to wear [face masks](63631542.md) until July 1.[^cite_note-50]

During the COVID-19 shutdown, the Shubert Organization, the Nederlander Organization, and Jujamcyn had pledged to increase racial and cultural diversity in their theaters, including naming at least one theater for a Black theatrical personality.[^cite_note-51] The [August Wilson Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Wilson_Theatre), owned by Jujamcyn, had been renamed after Black playwright [August Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Wilson) in 2005.[^cite_note-Paulson2022-52] The Shuberts announced in March 2022 that the [Cort Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Earl_Jones_Theatre), which was under renovation at the time, would be renamed after actor [James Earl Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Earl_Jones).[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-NBC_News_2022-54] In June 2022, the Nederlanders announced that the [Brooks Atkinson Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooks_Atkinson_Theatre) would be renamed after [Lena Horne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lena_Horne),[^cite_note-55][^cite_note-CBS2022-56][^cite_note-Paulson2022-52] The James Earl Jones Theatre was rededicated in September 2022,[^cite_note-57] while the Lena Horne Theatre was rededicated that November.[^cite_note-58]

While existing musicals and revivals continued with acceptable returns post-2021, most newly-created musicals have struggled in the post-coronavirus era. As of January 2026, just four post-2021 musicals reported profits: *[MJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MJ_the_Musical)*, *[& Juliet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/&_Juliet)*, *[Six](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six_\(musical\))*, and *[The Outsiders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Outsiders_\(musical\))*. Various explanations have been offered. Putting on a musical is substantially more expensive than earlier, with more money tied up in salaries, equipment, rent, and fees; but ticket prices have not risen proportionately. The pandemic meant a glut of shows were written and waiting to be performed, then all competed with each other for attention in a short period.[^cite_note-59]

## Description

### Schedule

Although there are some exceptions, shows with open-ended runs generally have evening performances Tuesday through Saturday, with a 7\:00 p.m. or 8\:00 p.m. "curtain". The afternoon "[matinée](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/matin%C3%A9e)" performances are at 2\:00 p.m. on Wednesdays and Saturdays and at 3\:00 p.m. on Sundays. This makes for an eight-performance week. On this schedule, most shows do not play on Monday and the shows and theatres are said to be "dark" on that day.[^cite_note-schedule-60][^cite_note-sunday-61] The actors and the crew in these shows tend to regard Sunday evening through Monday evening as their weekend. The Tony award presentation ceremony is usually held on a Sunday evening in June to fit this schedule.

In recent years, some shows have moved their Tuesday show time an hour earlier to 7\:00 pm.[^cite_note-schedule-60] The rationale for this move was that since fewer tourists take in shows midweek, Tuesday attendance depends more on local patrons. The earlier curtain makes it possible for suburban patrons to get home by a reasonable hour after the show. Some shows, especially those [Disney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney_Theatrical_Productions) produces, change their performance schedules fairly frequently depending on the season. This is done in order to maximize access to their target audience.

### Producers and theatre owners

Most Broadway producers and theatre owners are members of [The Broadway League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Broadway_League) (formerly "The League of American Theatres and Producers"), a trade organization that promotes Broadway theatre as a whole, negotiates contracts with the various theatrical unions and agreements with the guilds, and co-administers the [Tony Awards](54741.md) with the [American Theatre Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Theatre_Wing), a service organization. While the League and the theatrical unions are sometimes at loggerheads during those periods when new contracts are being negotiated, they also cooperate on many projects and events designed to promote professional theatre in New York.

All four of the four non-profit theatre companies with Broadway theatres ([Lincoln Center Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivian_Beaumont_Theater), [Manhattan Theatre Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Theatre_Club), [Roundabout Theatre Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundabout_Theatre_Company), and [Second Stage Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Stage_Theatre)) belong to the [League of Resident Theatres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Resident_Theatres) and have contracts with the theatrical unions which are negotiated separately from the other Broadway theatre and producers. ([Disney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney_Theatrical_Productions) also negotiates apart from the League, as did [Livent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livent) before it closed down its operations.)

The majority of Broadway theatres are owned or managed by three organizations: the [Shubert Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shubert_Organization), a for-profit arm of the non-profit Shubert Foundation, which owns seventeen theatres; the [Nederlander Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nederlander_Organization), which controls nine theatres; and [ATG Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATG_Entertainment), which owns seven Broadway houses.

### Personnel

Both musicals and straight plays on Broadway often rely on casting well-known performers in leading roles to draw larger audiences or bring in new audience members to the theatre. Actors from film and television are frequently cast for the revivals of Broadway shows or are used to replace actors leaving a cast. There are still, however, performers who are primarily stage actors, spending most of their time "on the boards", and appearing in screen roles only secondarily. As Patrick Healy of *[The New York Times](30680.md)* noted:

> Broadway once had many homegrown stars who committed to working on a show for a year, as [Nathan Lane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Lane) has for *[The Addams Family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Addams_Family_\(musical\))*. In 2010, some theater heavyweights like Mr. Lane were not even nominated; instead, several Tony Awards were given for productions that were always intended to be short-timers on Broadway, given that many of their film-star performers had to move on to other commitments.[^cite_note-62]

According to Mark Shenton, "One of the biggest changes to the commercial theatrical landscape—on both sides of the Atlantic—over the past decade or so is that sightings of big star names turning out to do plays has \[sic] gone up; but the runs they are prepared to commit to has gone down. Time was that a producer would require a minimum commitment from his star of six months, and perhaps a year; now, the 13-week run is the norm."[^cite_note-63]

The minimum size of the Broadway orchestra is governed by an agreement with the musicians' union (Local 802, American Federation of Musicians) and The Broadway League. For example, the agreement specifies the minimum size of the orchestra at the Minskoff Theatre to be eighteen, while at the Music Box Theatre it is nine.[^cite_note-64]

### Runs

See also: [List of the longest-running Broadway shows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_longest-running_Broadway_shows)

Most Broadway shows are commercial productions intended to make a profit for the producers and investors ("backers" or "angels"), and therefore have open-ended runs (duration that the production plays), meaning that the length of their presentation is not set beforehand, but depends on critical response, word of mouth, and the effectiveness of the show's advertising, all of which determine ticket sales. Investing in a commercial production carries a varied degree of financial risk. Shows need not make a profit immediately; should they make their "nut" (weekly operating expenses), or lose money at a rate acceptable to the producers, they may continue to run in the expectation that, eventually, they will pay back their initial costs and become profitable. In some borderline situations, producers may ask that royalties be temporarily reduced or waived, or even that performers—with the permission of their unions—take reduced salaries, to prevent a show from closing. Theatre owners, who are not generally profit participants in most productions, may waive or reduce rents, or even lend money to a show to keep it running.

Some Broadway shows are produced by non-commercial organizations as part of a regular subscription season—[Lincoln Center Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Center), [Roundabout Theatre Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundabout_Theatre_Company), [Manhattan Theatre Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Theatre_Club), and [Second Stage Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Stage_Theater) are the four non-profit theatre companies that currently have permanent Broadway venues. Some other productions are produced on Broadway with "limited engagement runs" for several reasons, including financial issues, prior engagements of the performers, or temporary availability of a theatre between the end of one production and the beginning of another. However, some shows with planned limited engagement runs may, after critical acclaim or box office success, extend their engagements or convert to open-ended runs. This was the case with 2007's *[August: Osage County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August:_Osage_County)*, 2009's *[God of Carnage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_of_Carnage)*, 2012's *[Newsies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newsies_\(musical\))*, and 2022's *[Take Me Out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Take_Me_Out_\(play\))*.[^cite_note-65]

Historically, musicals on Broadway tend to have longer runs than "straight" (i.e., non-musical) plays. On January 9, 2006, *[The Phantom of the Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Phantom_of_the_Opera_\(1986_musical\))* at the [Majestic Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majestic_Theatre_\(Broadway\)) became the longest-running Broadway musical, with 7,486 performances, overtaking *[Cats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cats_\(musical\))*.[^cite_note-66] *The Phantom of the Opera* closed on Broadway on April 16, 2023, soon after celebrating its 35th anniversary, after a total of 13,981 performances.[^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68]

### Audience

Attending a Broadway show is a common [tourist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism) activity in New York. The [TKTS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TKTS) booths sell same-day tickets (and in certain cases, next-day matinee tickets) for many Broadway and [Off-Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Off-Broadway) shows at a discount of 20 to 50%.[^cite_note-69] The TKTS booths are located in [Times Square](31389.md), in [Lower Manhattan](455646.md), and at [Lincoln Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Center). This service is run by [Theatre Development Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Development_Fund). Many Broadway theatres also offer special student rates, same-day "rush" or "lottery" tickets, or standing-room tickets to help ensure that their theatres are as full—and their grosses as high—as possible.[^cite_note-70]

According to [The Broadway League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Broadway_League), total Broadway attendance was 14.77 million in 2018–2019, compared to 13.79 million in 2017–2018.[^cite_note-newleague-71] The average age of the Broadway audience in the 2017–18 theater season was 40, the lowest it had been in nearly two decades.[^cite_note-72] By 2018, about 20% of Broadway tickets were sold to international visitors, although many visitors reported not being able to use their tickets.[^cite_note-Fierberg_2018_z474-73] In 2022–2023, the first full season since the COVID-19 pandemic, Broadway theaters sold 12.3 million tickets, of which 35% were to local residents and 17% to international visitors. At the time, the average age of theatergoers was 40.4; nearly two-thirds of the audience were women; and 29% identified as a racial minority.[^cite_note-Tsioulcas_2023_l625-74]

### Off-Broadway and off-off-Broadway

Main articles: [Off-Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Off-Broadway) and [Off-off-Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Off-off-Broadway)

The classification of theatres is governed by language in [Actors' Equity Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actors'_Equity_Association) contracts. To be eligible for a Tony, a production must be in a house with 500 seats or more and in the Theater District, which are the criteria that define Broadway theatre. [Off-Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Off-Broadway) and [off-off-Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Off-off-Broadway) shows often provide a more experimental, challenging, and intimate performance than is possible in the larger Broadway theatres. Some Broadway shows, however, such as the musicals *[Hair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hair_\(musical\))*, *[Little Shop of Horrors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Shop_of_Horrors_\(musical\))*, *[Spring Awakening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_Awakening_\(musical\))*, *[Next to Normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Next_to_Normal)*, *[Rent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rent_\(musical\))*, *[Avenue Q](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avenue_Q)*, *[In the Heights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_the_Heights)*, *[Fun Home](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fun_Home_\(musical\))*, *[A Chorus Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Chorus_Line)*, *[Dear Evan Hansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dear_Evan_Hansen)*, and *[Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton_\(musical\))*, began their runs Off-Broadway and later transferred to Broadway, seeking to replicate their intimate experience in a larger theatre. Other productions are first developed through [workshops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workshop_production) and then out-of-town [tryouts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tryout_\(theatre\)) before transferring to Broadway. *[Merrily We Roll Along](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merrily_We_Roll_Along_\(musical\))* famously skipped an out-of-town tryout and attempted to do an in-town tryout—actually [preview performances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preview_\(theatre\))—on Broadway before its official opening, with disastrous results.[^cite_note-Sondheim_Page_382-75][^cite_note-Harrison-76]

### Broadway national tours

After, or even during, successful runs in Broadway theatres, producers often remount their productions with new casts and crew for the Broadway national tour, which travels to theatres in major cities across the country. Sometimes when a show closes on Broadway, the entire production, with most if not all of the original cast intact, is relaunched as a touring company, hence the name "Broadway national tour". Some shows may even have several touring companies out at a time, whether the show is still running in New York or not, with many companies "sitting down" in other major cities for their own extended runs. For Broadway national tours of top-tier cities, the entire Broadway production is transplanted almost entirely intact and may run for many months (or years) at each stop. For example, the first U.S. tour of *The Phantom of the Opera* required 26 53-foot-long (16.1 m) [semi-trailers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-trailer) to transport all its sets, equipment, and costumes, and it took almost 10 days to properly unload all those trucks and install everything into a theater.[^cite_note-Slaton-77]

Second-tier and smaller cities can also attract national tours, but these are more likely to be "bus and truck" tours.[^cite_note-Slaton-77] These are scaled-down versions of the larger, national touring productions, historically acquiring their name because the casts generally traveled by bus instead of by air, while the sets and equipment traveled by truck. Tours of this type often run for weeks rather than months, and frequently feature a reduced physical production to accommodate smaller venues and tighter schedules, and to fit into fewer trucks.[^cite_note-Slaton-77] A typical second-tier city can usually sell only up to about eight shows (one week) of tickets.[^cite_note-Slaton-77] For cities smaller than that, a touring production might move twice a week ("split weeks") or every day ("one-nighters").[^cite_note-Slaton-77] For "bus and truck" tours, the production values are usually less lavish than the typical Broadway national tour or national touring production, and the actors, while still members of the actors' union, are compensated under a different, less lucrative union contract. The [Touring Broadway Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Touring_Broadway_Awards), presented by [The Broadway League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Broadway_League), honored excellence in touring Broadway.

### Awards

Broadway productions and artists are honored by the annual Antoinette Perry Awards (commonly called the "[Tony Awards](54741.md)", or "Tonys"), given by the [American Theatre Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Theatre_Wing) and [The Broadway League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Broadway_League), and that were first presented in 1947.[^cite_note-78] The Tony is Broadway's most prestigious award, comparable to the [Academy Awards](324.md) for [Hollywood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hollywood_\(film_industry\)) film productions. Their importance has increased since 1967 when the awards presentation show began to be broadcast on national television. In a strategy to improve the television ratings, celebrities are often chosen to host the show, some with scant connection to the theatre.[^cite_note-79] The most recent Tony Awards ceremony was held on [June 7, 2026](82230076.md). Other awards given to Broadway productions include the [Drama Desk Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama_Desk_Award), presented since 1955, the [New York Drama Critics' Circle Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Drama_Critics'_Circle), first given in 1936, and the [Outer Critics Circle Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_Critics_Circle_Award), initially presented in 1950.
