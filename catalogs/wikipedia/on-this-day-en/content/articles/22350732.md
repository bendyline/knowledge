**Shia Islam in Iraq** ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): الشيعة في العراق) has a history going back to the times of [Ali ibn Abi Talib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali) who moved the capital of the [Rashidun Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_Caliphate) from [Medina](36636.md) to [Kufa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kufa), two decades after the death of [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad). The **Iraqi Shias** are an [ethnoreligious group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnoreligious_group) of Iraq that constitute the majority of the population. Their historical stronghold has been [Lower Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Mesopotamia), historically known as [Babylonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia).[^cite_note-1]

Iraqi Shias vary between religious, moderately religious and [secular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secular).[^cite_note-2] Since Iraq is a predominantly [tribal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribal) society, one's sectarian affiliation is often dependent on one's tribe regardless of personal religious convictions or lack thereof. The term 'Iraqi Shia' a socio-political and religious identifier, which also works as an ethnic identity.[^cite_note-3] Since 2005, due to *Muhasasah*, an informally adopted political system of sect-based power sharing, sectarian stratification has occurred for political reasons.[^cite_note-4] The vast majority of Iraqi Shias are ethnically [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab).[^cite_note-5]

Shia Muslims are generally considered to constitute the majority of the [Iraqi population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_Iraq) with varying estimates over their percentages, such as a lower estimate reporting it to be between 55% and 60%,[^cite_note-6] and a higher estimate ranging between 64% and 69% of the population of Iraq.[^cite_note-8] Iraq is the location of the holy cities of [Najaf](200512.md) and [Karbala](210957.md), in addition to [Kadhimiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kadhimiya) and [Samarra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samarra), pilgrimage sites for millions of Shia Muslims. Modern Iraqi Shias follow the [Twelver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelver_Shi'ism) sect. Historically, there were followers of [Isma'ilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isma'ilism) among [Musha'sha'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musha'sha') [Arabs](2185.md), [Zaydism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaydism) among [Kurds](17068.md), and [Ibrahimiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibrahimiyya) among [Turkmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_Turkmen), which all declined. Since 2010, the number of people identifying as [atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheists) has increased, especially among the youth.[^cite_note-9]

Najaf is the site of Ali's tomb, and Karbala is the site of the tomb of Muhammad's grandson, third Shia Imam [Husayn ibn Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husayn_ibn_Ali). Najaf is also a center of Shia learning and seminaries. Two other holy sites for [Twelver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelver) Shia in Iraq are the [Al-Kadhimiya Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kadhimiya_Mosque) in [Baghdad](4492.md), which contains the tombs of the seventh and ninth Shia Imams ([Mūsā al-Kādhim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C5%ABs%C4%81_al-K%C4%81dhim) and [Muhammad al-Jawad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_al-Jawad)) and the [Al-Askari Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Askari_Mosque) in Samarra, which contains the tombs of the tenth and eleventh Shia Imams ([Ali al-Hadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_al-Hadi) and [Hasan al-‘Askarī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasan_al-%E2%80%98Askar%C4%AB)).

Iraq is known as the center of Shia Islam, with Najaf being the hub of Shia scholarship. After the [U.S.-led invasion](201936.md) in 2003, widespread sectarian violence erupted between [Shias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shias) and [Sunnis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) in Iraq, which led to war in [2006–2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_civil_war_\(2006%E2%80%932008\)) and [2013–2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_Iraq_\(2013%E2%80%932017\)), with the latter one involving the [Islamic State](9087364.md).

## History

### 7th to 10th centuries



The [Great Mosque of Kufa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Kufa), formerly the home of Imam Ali, also contains the shrine of [Muslim ibn Aqil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_ibn_Aqil)

After being named [caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliph) in 657, Imam [Ali ibn Abi Talib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali) established his capital at [Kufa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kufa) in present-day Iraq.[^cite_note-Britannica738-10] The [Battle of Karbala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Karbala) took place in 680, where [Husayn ibn Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husayn_ibn_Ali) was martyred by [Umayyad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_dynasty) forces of [Ubayd Allah ibn Ziyad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ubayd_Allah_ibn_Ziyad) and [Umar ibn Sa'd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umar_ibn_Sa'd) at the orders of [Yazid ibn Mu'awiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yazid_I).[^cite_note-11] Many called for vengeance. [Sulayman ibn Surad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulayman_ibn_Surad) led the [Tawwabin uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tawwabin_uprising) in January 685, but was defeated and killed in [Battle of Ayn al-Warda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ayn_al-Warda). After the failed uprising, [Mukhtar al-Thaqafi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mukhtar_al-Thaqafi) once again called for the establishment of an Alid caliphate and for retaliation for Husayn's killing, and took over Kufa in October 685. Aided by [Ibrahim ibn al-Ashtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibrahim_ibn_al-Ashtar), they successfully drove the Umayyads out of Kufa and defeated them in several battles, including the [Battle of Khazir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Khazir) in 686, but were defeated shortly afterwards in 687, when Kufa was besieged by the governor of Basra [Mus'ab ibn al-Zubayr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mus'ab_ibn_al-Zubayr).[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15]

In the early Islamic period, Kufa effectively became the "second capital" of Shiism after [Medina](36636.md), the residence of the [Twelve Imams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_Imams), and acted as a source of many Shiite scholars and disciples of the Twelve Imams, including: [Hisham ibn al-Hakam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hisham_ibn_al-Hakam), [Zurarah ibn A'yun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zurarah_ibn_A'yun), [Burayd ibn Mu'awiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burayd_ibn_Mu'awiya_al-'Ijli), [Mu'min al-Taq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu'min_al-Taq), [Aban ibn Taghlib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aban_ibn_Taghlib), [Abu Basir al-Asadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Basir_al-Asadi) and [Muhammad bin Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_bin_Muslim), all disciples of Imam [Ja'far al-Sadiq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ja'far_al-Sadiq) (702–765 CE). It was in Kufa where [Zayd ibn Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zayd_ibn_Ali), the principal figure of [Zaydism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaydism), led an [uprising against the Umayyad rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolt_of_Zayd_ibn_Ali) of [Hisham ibn Abd al-Malik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hisham_ibn_Abd_al-Malik) which ended with Zayd's execution and burning,[^cite_note-16] while [Basra](57580.md) witnessed the [Alid revolt of 762–763](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alid_revolt_of_762%E2%80%93763) by [Muhammad al-Nafs al-Zakiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_al-Nafs_al-Zakiyya)'s brother Ibrahim, who was said to have amassed a force as large as 100,000.[^cite_note-17]

The 7th Twelver Imam [Musa al-Kazim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musa_al-Kazim) was repeatedly imprisoned in [Baghdad](4492.md) and Basra at the orders of [Abbasid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate) caliphs [al-Mansur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mansur), [al-Hadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hadi), [al-Mahdi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mahdi) and [Harun al-Rashid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harun_al-Rashid). During [Al-Ma'mun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ma'mun)'s reign, in a sudden departure of anti-Shia policy, Imam [Ali al-Rida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_al-Rida) was designated heir apparent of al-Ma'mun, but was later poisoned by al-Ma'mun himself.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19] Some Shia officials managed to gain influence in the Abbasid court, such as [Ali ibn Yaqteen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_ibn_Yaqteen?action=edit\&redlink=1), a Kufan who became a minister of the Abbasid caliph with the approval of Imam Musa al-Kadhim to assist the Shia.[^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21]

The Twelver sect historically had been the most common among Shias in Iraq. However, [Zaydism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaydism) had a presence among Shia Kurds, and [Isma'ilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isma'ilism) had a presence among Shia Arabs in [Musha'sha'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musha'sha'). Zaydism and Isma'ilism later declined.[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23] [Qizilbashism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qizilbash) also had a presence among the Iraqi Turkmen, who continued their practices until the 1920s, when orthodox Twelver missionaries from Southern Iraq began to convert them. A known sect among them was [Ibrahimiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibrahimiyya). The Turkmen with Qizilbash practices were very secretive about their religious practices to outsiders.[^cite_note-24]

Aside from mainstream Shia Islam, Iraq was also the home of many Shia sects which no longer exist. Kufan followers of Mukhtar al-Thaqafi later formed the [Kaysanite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaysanites) sect, who traced the line of Imamate to [Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_al-Hanafiyya). The Kaysanites had a significant role in the [Abbasid Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Revolution) after they managed to rally Shia support in Iraq for the uprising against the Umayyads. However, after the revolution, most Kaysanites soon joined Ja'far al-Sadiq or Muhammad al-Nafs al-Zakiyya, and eventually Ja'far al-Sadiq after the demise of Muhammad.[^cite_note-25]

Another extinct sect are the [Qarmatians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qarmatians), a sect of [Isma'ili Shias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isma'ilism) founded by the Iraqi-born [Hamdan Qarmat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamdan_Qarmat). Hamdan assumed the leadership of Isma'ili missionary activity in the rural environs (*[sawād](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sawad)*) of Kufa and southern Iraq, and Qarmatian creed soon flourished in southern Iraq. Among the Iraqi *dā'ī*s trained and sent to missions by Hamdan and Abu Muhammad were [Ibn Hawshab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Hawshab) (to the [Yemen](350939.md)), and [Abu Abdallah al-Shi'i](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Abdallah_al-Shi'i), a Kufan-born dā'ī who later helped convert the [Kutama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kutama) in [Ifriqiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ifriqiya) and opened the way to the establishment of the [Fatimid Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimid_Caliphate).[^cite_note-26][^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28]

### 10th to 15th centuries



Hamdanid territory in 955 during the rule of [Sayf al-Dawla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayf_al-Dawla)

The [Hamdanid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamdanid_dynasty) of Banu [Taghlib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taghlib) was among the first Twelver Shia dynasties formed in northern Iraq. The Hamdanids first emerged as governors of [Mardin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mardin) in 890 and [Mosul](200475.md) in 905, and by 950 had expanded into most of [Syria](7515849.md) and western Iraq, informally forming a parallel authority to the one in [Baghdad](4492.md).[^cite_note-29][^cite_note-30] During the 930s and 940s, the Hamdanids and the [Buyids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buyid_dynasty) were in contest with another Shia, [Abu Abdallah al-Baridi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Abdallah_al-Baridi), an Iraqi tax-official who used the enormous wealth gained from [tax farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_farming) to vie for control of the rump [Abbasid Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate), temporarily holding Baghdad with brother twice.[^cite_note-31][^cite_note-32]

The Hamdanids were succeeded in Mosul by another Shia dynasty, the [Uqaylids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uqaylid_dynasty) who ruled roughly the same territory as the Hamdanids from 990 to 1096. In northern Syria, they incorporated the Shia [Mirdasids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirdasids) into their service, who later rebelled against the [Fatimids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimids) under [Salih ibn Mirdas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salih_ibn_Mirdas) and established themselves as the *[emirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emir)* of most of present-day Syria, western Iraq and [Lebanon](17771.md), ruling from [Aleppo](159244.md).



The Emirate of Banu Mazyad c. 1086

In central Iraq, the [Mazyadids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Mazyad) ruled an autonomous emirate in the area around [Kūfa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%ABfa) and [Hīt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C4%ABt) between 961 and 1160 from their capital city of [Hillah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillah).[^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34] They were originally in the service of the [Buyid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buyid_dynasty), another Twelver Shia dynasty which expanded into most of western Iran and Iraq, seizing Baghdad and making it as their capital. Later on, [Hillah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillah) later became one of the central cities of Shia learning, where prominent Shia scholars and poets such as [al-Allama al-Hilli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Allama_al-Hilli), [Muhaqqiq al-Hilli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhaqqiq_al-Hilli), [Shahid Awwal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Jamaluddin_al-Makki_al-Amili) and [Safi al-Din al-Hilli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safi_al-Din_al-Hilli) lived and taught during the 12th–15th centuries.

### 15th to 19th centuries

When the [Safavid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_dynasty) declared Shia Islam the official religion of [Iran](14653.md) in 1501, Shia scholars from southern Iraq contributed to the conversion movement.[^cite_note-savoryeiref-35] The Safavids also invited many Shi'i Arab tribes to [Khuzestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khuzestan) to act as a bulwark against the Ottoman Empire, earning Khuzestan the name of Arabestan.[^cite_note-36]

Between the 15th and 19th centuries, many of the tribes living on the banks of the [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates) and [Tigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris), which were originally Sunni, converted to Shia Islam. During the 19th century, the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md) instituted a policy of settling the [semi-nomadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-nomadic) Sunni Arab tribes to create greater centralization in Iraq.[^cite_note-37] The tribes adopted a sedentary agricultural life in the hinterlands of [Najaf](200512.md) and [Karbala](210957.md), and frequently traded and interacted with the residents of the two cities.[^cite_note-Stephen-38][^cite_note-39] Some Sunni Arab tribes converted to protest their treatment by the Sunni Ottomans.[^cite_note-40]

Shia missionaries from Najaf and Karbala operated with relative freedom from the Ottoman Empire, and could proselytize with little official hindrance.[^cite_note-41] The Bani Sallama, [Tayy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tayy) and al-Soudan in the [Mesopotamian Marshes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamian_Marshes) were converted by the [Musha'sha'iyyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musha'sha'iyyah) dynasty, a heretical Isma'ili Shia tribal confederation founded by [Muhammad ibn Falah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Falah) which ruled the town of [Hoveyzeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoveyzeh) in [Khuzestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khuzestan) from 1435 to 1924.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44] Another tribe, Banu Khaz'al,[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-Haydari_pg.110-15-46] as well as the [Banu Kaab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Ka'b) converted during the mid-18th century.[^cite_note-Haydari_pg.110-15-46] After the fall of the [Emirate of Muhammara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate_of_Muhammara), an autonomous [emirate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate) of the Shia Banu Kaab between 1812 and 1925 in modern-day [Khuzestan province](448079.md), many [Iranian Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Arabs) fled to southern Iraq, further inflating the Shia population in the south.[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48]

The conversions continued into the 20th century, as the British noted in 1917.[^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50] Many Iraqi Shia are relatively-recent converts.[^cite_note-51] The following tribes were converted during this period:[^cite_note-Haydari_pg.110-15-46] some of the Zubaid,[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-Abdallah_Mahmud_Shukri_1928-53] [Banu Lam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Lam), Albu-Muhammad, many of the [Rabiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Rabi'ah) (including al-Dafaf'a, Bani Amir and al-Jaghayfa), [Banu Tamim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Tamim)[^cite_note-Abdallah_Mahmud_Shukri_1928-53] (including the Bani Sa’d, their largest group in Iraq), the [Shammar Toga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shammar_Toga),[^cite_note-Abdallah_Mahmud_Shukri_1928-53] some of the [Dulaim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulaim), the Zafir, the Dawwar, the Sawakin, the al-Muntafiq confederation,[^cite_note-54] the Bani Hasan (of the [Bani Malik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bani_Malik_\(tribe\))),[^cite_note-Stephen-38] the Bani Hukayyim, the Shibil of the Khazal, the al Fatla,[^cite_note-55] the tribes along the [Al-Hindiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hindiya) canal, and the five tribes of [Al Diwaniyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Diwaniyah) (Aqra’, Budayyir, Afak, [Jubur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jubur) and Jilaiha) which relied on the Daghara canal for water.

### British mandate and Kingdom of Iraq

During the start of the 20th century, the Shia opposed [Mandatory Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandatory_Iraq) and the Sunni monarchy. As a result of their neglect by Ottomans, and their poverty, the Shiites were increasingly dependent on their *[ulama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulama)*, the religious clerics. In 1920, Iraqis, whether Sunni or Shia, had grown more discontent with [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain) rule. Many Iraqis began to fear that Iraq would be incorporated into the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire). One of the eminent Shia mujtahideen, Ayatollah [Mirza Muhammad Taqi al-Shirazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirza_Taqi_al-Shirazi), then issued a [fatwa](11259.md) "declaring that service in the British administration was unlawful".[^cite_note-tripp41-56]

The revolt materialized in June 1920 and rapidly spread from [Baghdad](4492.md) to the South, notably the town of [Al-Rumaitha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Rumaitha), where the Zawalim sheikh Shaalan Abu al-Jun was arrested and subsequently freed by his tribesmen.[^cite_note-57] More Shia ulama, including [Mirza Mahdi al-Shirazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirza_Mahdi_al-Shirazi), [Mehdi Al-Khalissi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehdi_Al-Khalissi) and [Muhammad Hasan Abi al-Mahasin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Hasan_Abi_al-Mahasin) displayed their support for the revolt, and encouraged the local population to take arms. At the peak of the revolt, around 131,000 Iraqis were active against the British.[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59]

Under the [Kingdom of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hashemite_Iraq), the Shia tribes of the mid-[Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates) region saw themselves increasingly under-represented in the Sunni-dominated Iraqi government, which further deteriorated with the exclusion of key Shia sheikhs from the Iraqi parliament in 1934 elections. In addition, King [Ghazi of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghazi_of_Iraq), a Hashemite ruler of Iraq from 1933 to 1939, was driven, amongst other things, by anti-Shia ambitions.[^cite_note-tripp83-60] This ultimately led to the [1935–1936 Iraqi Shia revolts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1935%E2%80%931936_Iraqi_Shia_revolts), mostly in the towns of Al-Rumaitha and [al-Diwaniyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Diwaniyah), led by Ayatollah [Muhammad Husayn Kashif al-Ghita'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Husayn_Kashif_al-Ghita') and various Shia tribal sheikhs. For many, the 1935–1936 revolt uncovered a lack of community interest within the Iraqi Shia society and absence of strong Shia political leadership, to present their interests in Baghdad,[^cite_note-nakash-61] a predicament shared by their [correligonists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Shia_Muslims) in [Lebanon](17771.md).

Scholars such as Fanar Haddad have argued that the governments of the new Iraqi state tended to adopt the symbols of Sunni identity while suppressing Shia identity.[^cite_note-haddad-62] For example, figures such as [Saladin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saladin), [Harun al-Rashid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harun_al-Rashid) or [Omar ibn al-Khattab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omar_ibn_al-Khattab) who were venerated by Arab nationalists are viewed with suspicion in Shia folklore.[^cite_note-63] This contributes to disaffection among Iraqi Shia, while at the same time Sunni Iraqi politicians have tended to cast Shia political mobilization as alien, in particular Iranian.[^cite_note-64]

### The creation of a Shia political movement

Main article: [Islamic Dawa Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Dawa_Party)

For many years, Arab nationalism and party politics superseded Shia unity in Iraqi politics, and Shia [ayatollahs](63899.md) were politically quiescent.[^cite_note-Momen.p.262-65] The Shia were generally less well-off economically and socially, and as a result, they supported [leftist parties](18499.md), such as [Iraqi Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_Communist_Party) which was founded by [Husain al-Rahhal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husain_al-Rahhal) in 1934, and the [Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Socialist_Ba'ath_Party_%E2%80%93_Iraq_Region), which was also founded by a Shia, [Fuad al-Rikabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuad_al-Rikabi).

To counter the intellectual hold of the left, a group of clerics in Najaf created a movement that eventually evolved into the [Dawa party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Dawa_Party). Its manifesto, written by [Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Baqir_al-Sadr), probably in 1960, defined its ultimate goal as an Islamic polity.[^cite_note-66]

### Under the Baathist regime

Islamic cleric with Saddam Hussein

Ayatollah [Abu al-Qasim al-Khoei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_al-Qasim_al-Khoei) is brought in front of [Saddam Hussein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddam_Hussein) after Shia uprisings in 1991.

In 1963, a coalition of military officers and others led by the Arab nationalist and socialist [Ba'ath Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba'ath_Party) [seized power in a coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramadan_Revolution). At that point, 53 percent of its membership was Shia. In the years following the Shia were shunted aside, and by 1968, only six percent of the Ba'ath party were Shia.[^cite_note-Momen.p.262-65] [Abdul Salam Arif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdul_Salam_Arif), president from the 1963 coup until his death in 1966, used derogatory terms in leadership meetings to describe Iraqi Shia and opposed his predecessor [Abd al-Karim Qasim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Karim_Qasim)'s policy of bringing all citizens into the regime regardless of ethnicity or religion.[^cite_note-67]

Due to discrimination by the Sunni government, the Shia became increasingly disaffected during the last 1960s and 1970s. By 1968, Dawa could claim a mass following, and the Baath began to consider it a threat.[^cite_note-68] In 1974, amid rising discontent due to casualties in the [Kurdish insurgency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Iraqi%E2%80%93Kurdish_War), the regime executed five leading Dawa members.[^cite_note-69] Subsequently, the regime banned annual [Marad al-ras processions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arba'een_Pilgrimage) during the [Mourning of Muharram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mourning_of_Muharram) in the shrine cities, where mass discontent had been evident in 1974 and 1975. In 1977, tens of thousands of Dawa activists held the processions in defiance of the ban, leading to large-scale clashes known as the [Safar Intifada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1977_Shia_uprising_in_Iraq) that the regime quelled with the use of helicopter gunships. At least 16 were killed, eight executed and two died under torture.[^cite_note-70]

The success of the [Iranian Revolution](347268.md) intensified unrest and repression. In June 1979, Ayatollah Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr was arrested and placed under house arrest. Less than a year later, due to encouraging the [1979–1980 Shia uprising in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979%E2%80%931980_Shia_uprising_in_Iraq), Sadr and his sister [Bint al-Huda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amina_al-Sadr) were both executed in April 1980.[^cite_note-Momen.p.263-71] In 1982, the [Supreme Council for the Islamic Revolution in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Council_for_the_Islamic_Revolution_in_Iraq) was formed in Iran by Iraqi cleric [Mohammad Baqir al-Hakim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammad_Baqir_al-Hakim) as an umbrella group to overthrow Iraq's Sunni-dominated regime. In Iran, Hakim attempted to unite and co-ordinate the activities of al-Dawa party and other major Shia groups: [Peykar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organization_of_Struggle_for_the_Emancipation_of_the_Working_Class) (a guerilla organization similar to the Iranian [Mujahideen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mujahideen)) and *Jama'at al-'Ulama* (groups of pro-[Khomeini](26232.md) ulama).[^cite_note-Momen.p.263-71]

The Ba'ath Party's leadership made a determined effort to gain the support of Iraqi Shia during the 1980–1988 [Iran–Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War), as 80% of the Iraqi army personnel had been Iraqi Shia, diverting resources to the Shia south and emphasizing Iraqi Arabness (in contrast to Iranian Persianness) and the historic struggle between the Muslim [Arabs](2185.md) and the [Zoroastrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism) Persians in propaganda. Iraqi propaganda used symbolic keywords such as [Qādisiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_al-Q%C4%81disiyyah) (the battle in which Muslim Arab armies defeated the [Persian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Empire)), and Iranian propaganda used Shia keywords such as [Karbala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Karbala). The Baath government executed about 95 Shia ulama, many of them members of the al-Hakim family, in June 1984,[^cite_note-Momen.p.264-72] and had [executed 142–146 Shia rebels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dujail_Massacre) in the town of [Dujail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dujail) earlier in 1982.

Unrest renewed with the [1991 Iraqi uprisings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1991_Iraqi_uprisings) throughout Iraq, which took place in the Shiite and Kurdish areas of the country. In the south, the rebels seized the shrine as Ba'ath Party officials fled the city or were killed. The uprising spread within days to all of the largest Shia cities in southern Iraq: [Amarah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarah), [Diwaniya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diwaniya), [Hilla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilla), [Karbala](210957.md), [Kut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kut), [Nasiriyah](33572510.md) and [Samawah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samawah). Smaller cities were swept up in the revolution as well. Many exiled Iraqi dissidents, including thousands of Iran-based [Badr Brigades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badr_Organization) militants of SCIRI, crossed the borders and joined the rebellion.[^cite_note-horrors-73] Demonstration against the regime took place in Shia neighborhoods of Baghdad.

By April 1991, most of the rebellion was crushed by the then-incumbent Baathist government. Many of the people killed were buried in [mass graves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_graves_in_Iraq). Of the 200 mass graves the Iraqi [Human Rights Ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Human_Rights_\(Iraq\)) had registered between 2003 and 2006, the majority were in the South, including one believed to hold as many as 10,000 victims.[^cite_note-horrors-73] During the [1991 Iraqi uprisings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1991_Iraqi_uprisings), over 200,000 Shia Arabs and 100,000 Marsh Arabs died in southern Iraq.[^cite_note-74] A short period of rest once again occurred during the [1999 Shia uprising in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_Shia_uprising_in_Iraq) after the killing of [Muhammad-Sadiq al-Sadr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad-Sadiq_al-Sadr) in the Shia neighborhoods of [Baghdad](4492.md), as well as southern majority [Shiite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiite) cities of [Karbala](210957.md), [Nasiriyah](33572510.md), [Kufa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kufa), [Najaf](200512.md), and [Basra](57580.md). Though the allegations on the government behind the assassination has never been proved.

### During the Iraqi conflict (2003–present)

After the US-led [2003 invasion of Iraq](201936.md), sectarian violence between Shia and the Sunnis steadily escalated.[^cite_note-75][^cite_note-76] By 2007, the United States' [National Intelligence Estimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Intelligence_Estimate) described the violence as a "civil war". During the [2006–2008 sectarian violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sectarian_violence_in_Iraq_\(2006%E2%80%932008\)), tens to hundreds of thousands of people were killed (mainly Shia civilians) and 1.7 million were internally displaced by February 2007, according to António Guterres, UN [High Commissioner for Refugees](31964.md).[^cite_note-77] In ISIL-occupied Iraq (2014–2017) Shias faced some of the worst treatment, and thousands were killed for their faith.

## Demographics

Shia Muslims in Iraq are ethnically made up of Arabs in central and southern Iraq, Turkmens in the north, Kurds in the northeast, and [Persians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranians_in_Iraq) throughout the holy cities.[^cite_note-78] No exact and recent data is available for the ethno-religious composition of Iraq, and due to this, all numbers in this regard are approximations. In 2003 the Shia Muslims population was estimated to make up around 55% of Iraq's population.[^cite_note-79] The data on the religious affiliation of Iraq's population are uncertain. 95–99% of the population are Muslims.[^cite_note-CIA-80][^cite_note-PewResearch-81] The [CIA World Factbook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIA_World_Factbook) reports a 2015 estimate according to which 29–34% are Sunni Muslims and 61–64% Shia Muslims.[^cite_note-CIA-80] According to a 2011 survey by [Pew Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pew_Research), 51% of the Muslims identify as Shia and 42% as Sunni.[^cite_note-PewResearch-81]
