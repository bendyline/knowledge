**Law enforcement in the United Kingdom** is organised separately in each of the legal systems of the United Kingdom: [England and Wales](275187.md), [Scotland](26994.md), and [Northern Ireland](21265.md). Most [law enforcement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_enforcement) duties are carried out by police [constables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constable#United_Kingdom) of a [territorial police force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_police_force).[^cite_note-pu-1]

There are 48 police forces in the UK. These consist of 39 territorial police forces in England, four in Wales, one [in Scotland](34423036.md), and one [in Northern Ireland](294068.md). Each is responsible for most law enforcement and crime reduction in its [police area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_area). The territorial police forces of England and Wales are overseen by the [Home Office](225695.md) and by a [police and crime commissioner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_and_crime_commissioner) or other [police authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_authority), although they are operationally independent from government. The other three police forces are the [British Transport Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Transport_Police) (BTP), the [Civil Nuclear Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Nuclear_Constabulary) (CNC), and the [Ministry of Defence Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Defence_Police) (MDP), which provide [specialist policing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_police#United_Kingdom) services.[^cite_note-pu-1]

In addition, the [National Crime Agency](28153284.md) (NCA) is primarily tasked with tackling [organised crime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organised_crime) and has been compared to the [Federal Bureau of Investigation](11127.md) (FBI) in the [United States](3434750.md).

The British model of policing is based on three interrelated concepts: the Office of Constable, operational independence, and policing by consent. Police constables have certain powers that enable them to execute their duties. Their primary duties are to protect the public by detecting and preventing crime.[^cite_note-pu-1] Police officers exercise their police powers independently of government and with the implicit consent of the public. "[Policing by consent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policing_by_consent)" is the phrase used to describe this. It expresses that the legitimacy of policing in the eyes of the public is based upon a consensus of support that follows from transparency about their powers, their integrity in exercising those powers and their [accountability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_accountability) for doing so.[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-UKP-3]

Most police constables in England, Scotland, and Wales [do not carry firearms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_use_of_firearms_in_the_United_Kingdom). As of 2022, there were 142,526 police officers in England and Wales, 6,192 of whom are Authorised Firearms Officers (roughly one in 25).

## History

Main article: [History of law enforcement in the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_law_enforcement_in_the_United_Kingdom)

In the 18th century, law enforcement and policing were organised by local communities based on watchmen and constables; the government was not directly involved in policing.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5] The [City of Glasgow Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Glasgow_Police), the first professional police, was established following an Act of Parliament in 1800.[^cite_note-6] The first centrally organised police force in the world was created in Ireland, then a part of the United Kingdom, following the Peace Preservation Act in 1814 for which [Sir Robert Peel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Robert_Peel) was largely responsible.[^cite_note-7]

London had a population of nearly one and a half million people in the early 19th century, but was policed by only 450 constables and 4,500 night watchmen.[^cite_note-8] The concept of professional policing was taken up by [Sir Robert Peel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Robert_Peel) when he became Home Secretary in 1822. Peel's [Metropolitan Police Act 1829](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Police_Act_1829) established a full-time, professional and centrally-organised police force for the greater London area known as the [Metropolitan Police](192450.md).[^cite_note-9] In March 1839, Sir Edwin Chadwick presented *The Royal Commission on Constabulary Forces* to Parliament. This report was to evaluate how the burgeoning police force would work with "poor law" as well as to make the case to establish a national force based on the Metropolitan Police. Much of his argument was based on the necessity for the protection of the developing capitalism that was growing in England at the time. Chadwick also addressed the concern that building out a powerful police state could lead to a reduction in civil and personal liberties, but argued that the fear of crime made English citizens slaves, and so they were less free without aggressive policing.[^cite_note-10] Legislation in the 1830s introduced policing in boroughs and many counties, and, in the 1850s, policing was established nationally.

The so-called "[Peelian principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peelian_principles)" describe the philosophy that Sir Robert Peel is supposed to have used to define an ethical police force. The principles traditionally – but incorrectly – ascribed to Peel, state that:[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-th-12]

- Whether the police are effective is not measured by the number of arrests, but by the lack of crime.

- Above all else, an effective authority figure knows trust and accountability are paramount. Hence, Peel's most often quoted principle is that "The police are the public and the public are the police."

Some of these can be found in the "General Instructions" issued to every new police officer in the Metropolitan Police from 1829, such as policing existing to prevent crime, and to minimise the [use of force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Use_of_force). The Home Office has suggested the list was more likely to have been authored by Charles Rowan and Richard Mayne, the first and joint Commissioners of the Metropolitan Police.[^cite_note-UKP-3][^cite_note-CIVITAS-13] None of these are correct, however, and were likely codified by [Charles Reith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Reith), some from ideas that existed since the eighteenth century.[^cite_note-th-12][^cite_note-14]

The police historian [Charles Reith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Reith) explained in his *New Study of Police History* (1956) that these principles constituted a philosophy of policing "unique in history and throughout the world because it derived not from fear but almost exclusively from public co-operation with the police, induced by them designedly by behaviour which secures and maintains for them the approval, respect and affection of the public".[^cite_note-UKP-3][^cite_note-15] This approach to policing became known as "[policing by consent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policing_by_consent)".[^cite_note-CIVITAS-13]

Other historians, such as Robert Storch, David Philips and Roger Swift, argue that Peel's Metropolitan Police were built on his experience of the Royal Irish Constabulary.[^cite_note-16] Storch's view is that the English police force is not different from those of other nations and, in fact, follows a rather typical development as a colonial peacekeeping force. There is extensive documentation of [police brutality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_brutality) in the 19th century, including excessive force, racial profiling, and several charges of murder. The controversies that plagued the early years of the police force were much the same as the current complaints against modern policing.[^cite_note-17]

The first women police officers were employed during the First World War. Hull and Southampton were two of the first towns to employ women police, although Grantham was the first to have a warranted policewoman.[^cite_note-18]

Since the 1940s, police forces in the United Kingdom have been merged and modernised. Corruption at the Metropolitan Police's [Flying Squad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_Squad) led to a conviction and resignations in 1977 after the [Operation Countryman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Countryman) investigations. A [Police Complaints Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Complaints_Board) was set up to handle allegations of malpractice in response.

Changes took place to tighten police procedures in the 1980s, in response to the [Scarman Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scarman_Report), to ensure that evidence and interviews were robust, in the introduction of the [Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_and_Criminal_Evidence_Act_1984). In 1989, the [West Midlands Serious Crime Squad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Midlands_Serious_Crime_Squad) was disbanded as a series of around 100 criminal cases failed or were subsequently overturned in the West Midlands, after new forensic techniques showed police officers had been tampering with statement evidence to secure convictions, including those of the [Birmingham Six](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Six).

The Police Complaints Board was replaced by the [Police Complaints Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Complaints_Authority_\(United_Kingdom\)) in 1985, which itself was superseded by the [Independent Police Complaints Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Police_Complaints_Commission) (IPCC) in 2004. On 8 January 2018, the IPCC was replaced by the [Independent Office for Police Conduct](56063076.md) (IOPC).[^cite_note-19]

## National Crime Agency

Main article: [National Crime Agency](28153284.md)

The [National Crime Agency](28153284.md) (NCA) is the UK's lead agency against [organised crime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organised_crime), [human](19333662.md) and [drug trafficking](218449.md), [weapons](42210.md) and [cybercrime](341598.md), including economic crime that goes across regional and international borders, but it can be tasked to investigate any crime.

The NCA has a strategic role as part of which it looks at serious crime in aggregate across the UK, especially analysing how organised criminals are operating and how they can be disrupted. To do this, it works closely with regional organised crime units (ROCUs), local police forces, and other government departments and agencies. It is not a police force as such, but many of its employees do have the power of a constable.

It is the UK's point of contact for foreign agencies such as [Interpol](14338129.md), [Europol](525329.md) and other international law enforcement agencies. On a day-to-day basis, the NCA assists police forces and other law enforcement agencies (and vice versa) under voluntary assistance arrangements. In extremis, the [NCA director general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Director_General_of_the_National_Crime_Agency) has the power to direct a chief officer of a police force to give directed assistance with NCA tasks where necessary (but only with consent of the relevant Secretary of State).[^cite_note-National_Crime_Agency_telegraph10361009-20] The NCA itself can also be directed by the Secretary of State to give directed assistance to a police force or other law enforcement agency.[^cite_note-21]

It was established in 2013 as a [non-ministerial government department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-ministerial_government_department),[^cite_note-22] replacing the [Serious Organised Crime Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serious_Organised_Crime_Agency) and absorbed the previously separate [Child Exploitation and Online Protection Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_Exploitation_and_Online_Protection_Centre) (CEOP) as one of its commands.[^cite_note-23] It also assumed a number of responsibilities from other law enforcement agencies.

The NCA has also assumed a range of functions from the [National Policing Improvement Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Policing_Improvement_Agency), which has been scrapped as part of the government's changes to policing.[^cite_note-24] These include a specialist database relating to injuries and unusual weapons, expert research on potential [serial killers](42626.md), and the National Missing Persons Bureau. The agencies going into the NCA had a combined budget of [£](270673.md)812 million (equivalent to £1.1 billion or [US\$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USD)1.41 billion in 2024)[^cite_note-inflation-UKGDP-25] yet the new agency only had £464 million in its first year—a decrease of 43%.[^cite_note-National_Crime_Agency_Itv.com-26] Some of the responsibilities of the former [UK Border Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_Border_Agency) (now [Immigration Enforcement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_Enforcement) and [Border Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_Force)) relating to border policing also became part of the NCA. Like its predecessor SOCA, the NCA has been dubbed the "British [FBI](11127.md)" by the media.[^cite_note-National_Crime_Agency_telegraph10361009-20][^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29]

As of October 2021, the director general is [Graeme Biggar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graeme_Biggar).[^cite_note-30]&#x20;

## Powers of officers

### Territorial police constables

Main articles: [Powers of the police in England and Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powers_of_the_police_in_England_and_Wales) and [Powers of the police in Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powers_of_the_police_in_Scotland)

Most police officers are members of [territorial police forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_police_forces). A person must make a declaration before taking up office as a constable and having any powers; although this is sometimes still known as the [police oath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_oath), and the process sometimes referred to as "swearing in", it now takes the form of an "attestation" (in [England and Wales](275187.md) and [Northern Ireland](21265.md)) or a "declaration" (in [Scotland](26994.md)). Their primary duties are the protection of life and property, preservation of the peace, and prevention and detection of criminal offences.[^cite_note-31] The process is carried out in the presence of a magistrate, and is usually followed by the issue of a [warrant card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warrant_card). This grants the officer all the powers and privileges, duties, and responsibilities of a constable in one of the three distinct legal systems – England and Wales, Scotland, or Northern Ireland, and the territorial waters of that country. The limited circumstances where their powers extend across the border are described in the section above.

Police powers can be grouped into three categories:[^cite_note-pu-1]

- Powers to investigate crime. This includes a range of powers to collect evidence needed to identify suspects and support their fair and effective trial.

- Powers to prevent crime. This includes a range of powers to maintain public order, prevent anti-social behaviour, and manage known offenders/ suspects.

- Powers to "dispose" of criminal cases. These powers allow police officers to dispose of criminal cases outside of court or charge suspects so they can be prosecuted.

### Other constables



Ministry of Defence Police (MDP) boat in [Portsmouth Harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portsmouth_Harbour)

Many constables are not members of territorial police forces. The most notable are members of the three forces referred to as [special police forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_police_forces): the [British Transport Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Transport_Police), [Ministry of Defence Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Defence_Police), and [Civil Nuclear Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Nuclear_Constabulary). Such officers have the "powers and privileges of a constable" in matters relating to their work.[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34] BTP and MDP officers have additional jurisdiction where requested by a constable of another force, in which case they take on that constable's jurisdiction.[^cite_note-csa-35][^cite_note-36] Upon request from the [chief police officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_police_officer) of a police force, members of one of the above three forces can be given the full powers of constables in the police area of the requesting force.[^cite_note-csa-35][^cite_note-37] This was used to supplement police numbers in the areas surrounding the [2005 G8 summit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_G8_summit) at Gleneagles.

Many acts allow companies or councils to employ constables for a specific purpose. There are ten companies whose employees are sworn in as constables under section 79 of the [Harbours, Docks, and Piers Clauses Act 1847](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harbours,_Docks,_and_Piers_Clauses_Act_1847).[^cite_note-38] As a result, they have the full powers of a constable on land owned by the harbour, dock, or port and at any place within one mile of any owned land. There are also forces created by specific legislation, such as the [Port of Tilbury Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Tilbury_Police) ([Port of London Act 1968](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_London_Act_1968)), [Mersey Tunnels Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersey_Tunnels_Police) ([County of Merseyside Act 1989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_Merseyside_Act_1989?action=edit\&redlink=1)), and the [Epping Forest Keepers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epping_Forest_Keepers) ([Epping Forest Act 1878](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epping_Forest_Act_1878)).

#### Parks Constables



[Royal Borough of Kensington and Chelsea Parks Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Borough_of_Kensington_and_Chelsea_Parks_Police) vehicle

Under Article 18 of the Ministry of Housing and Local Government Provisional Order Confirmation (Greater London Parks and Open Spaces) Act 1967, [London Borough Councils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Borough_Council) are allowed to swear in council officers as constables for "securing the observance of the provisions of all enactments relating to open spaces under their control or management and of bye-laws and regulations made thereunder". Local Authority Parks Constables have all the powers of a constable in relation byelaws regulations and all enactments relating to open spaces, Article 19 of the Act was repealed by section 26(1) of the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 (Local Acts) and the power of arrest for Parks Constables is now contained in Section 24 of P.A.C.E 1984, further amendments to Article 19 covering coming to the aid and assistance of such a constable or officer were repealed by SOCPA 2005, as this provision is already covered in P.A.C.E 1984 (Legal Counsel 2007,2012). No enforcement agency with the power of arrest or detention can operate outside of the provisions of P.A.C.E 1984; therefore, all local powers of arrest and detention were brought into line under section 26(1) P.A.C.E 1984.

### Police staff

Police forces employ staff who perform many functions to assist officers and support the smooth running of their police force. They do not hold the office of constable. In England and Wales, the [chief police officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_police_officer) of a territorial police force may designate any person who is employed by the police authority maintaining that force, and is under the direction and control of that chief police officer, as one of the following:



Police Community Support Officer and [UK Border Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_Border_Force) Agent

- [Police community support officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_community_support_officer) (PCSO)

- Policing support officer.

  [^cite_note-39]

PCSOs were created by the [Police Reform Act 2002](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Reform_Act_2002),[^cite_note-40] with a range of standard powers, as well as additional powers that can be conferred at the discretion of their chief police officer. Unlike a police constable, a PCSO only has powers when on duty and in uniform, and within the area policed by their respective force.

The policing support officer role was originally as three separate roles in [Police Reform Act 2002](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Reform_Act_2002), each with a specific list of discretionary powers that may be awarded by a chief constable:

- Investigating officer,

- [Detention officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Custody_assistant), or

- Escort officer.

  [^cite_note-41]

The [Policing and Crime Act 2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policing_and_Crime_Act_2017) reformed this and streamlined it to the two roles above, and gave full discretionary powers to Chief Constables, such that they may assign any powers, other than [powers reserved for constables only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_community_support_officer#Expansion_of_powers), to any police staff or volunteer.

Until 1991, parking enforcement was primarily conducted by police-employed [traffic wardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_warden). Since the passage of the Road Traffic Act 1991, [decriminalised parking enforcement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decriminalised_parking_enforcement) has enabled local authorities to take on this role, and now very few forces still employ police traffic wardens. These include the Metropolitan Police Service; however, it has combined the role with PCSOs as *traffic community support officers*.

In Scotland, [police custody and security officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_custody_and_security_officer) have powers similar to those of detention officers and escort officers in England and Wales.[^cite_note-42] Similar powers are available in Northern Ireland.[^cite_note-43]

### Accredited persons

[Chief police officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_police_officer) of territorial police forces[^cite_note-44] (and the British Transport Police[^cite_note-45]) can also give limited powers[^cite_note-46] to people not employed by the police authority, under [Community Safety Accreditation Schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_Safety_Accreditation_Scheme). A notable example is officers of the [Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Driver_and_Vehicle_Standards_Agency), who have been given powers to stop vehicles.[^cite_note-47] This practice has been criticised by the [Police Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Federation_of_England_and_Wales) who described it as "half-baked".[^cite_note-48]

### Members of the armed forces

In [Northern Ireland](21265.md) only, members of [British Armed Forces](31730.md) have powers to stop people[^cite_note-49] or vehicles,[^cite_note-50] arrest and detain people for three hours[^cite_note-51] and enter buildings to keep the peace[^cite_note-52] or search for people who have been kidnapped.[^cite_note-53] Additionally, [commissioned officers](36301328.md#United_Kingdom) may close roads.[^cite_note-54] If necessary, they may use force when exercising these powers as long as it is reasonable.[^cite_note-55]

Under the Customs Management Act 1979, members of the armed forces may detain people if they believe they have committed an offence under the Customs & Excise Acts, and may seize goods if they believe they are liable to forfeiture under the same acts.[^cite_note-56]

[Service police/military police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_police_of_the_United_Kingdom) personnel are not constables under UK law and they do not have any police powers over the general public; however, they have the full range of policing powers that constables possess when dealing with service personnel or [civilians subject to service discipline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilians_subject_to_service_discipline), drawing their powers from the Armed Forces Act 2006. The service police assist territorial police forces in towns in the UK with nearby military barracks, where there are likely to be significant numbers of service personnel off duty. In British Overseas Territories, they are sometimes sworn in as constables to assist and/or act as the police force (e.g. the [British Indian Ocean Territory Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Indian_Ocean_Territory_Police) which is made up of tri-service police personnel and are known as "Royal Overseas Police Officers"[^cite_note-57]) and anywhere that British Forces are stationed or deployed. Generally, when carrying out this assistance, service policemen/women are unarmed, but have a range of [PPE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_uniforms_and_equipment_in_the_United_Kingdom#Equipment), including batons, handcuffs, and stab vests.

## Accountability

From 22 November 2012, police authorities outside London were replaced by directly elected [Police and Crime Commissioners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_and_Crime_Commissioner) or Police, Fire and Crime Commissioners. In London, the [City of London Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London_Police) continued to be overseen by [City of London Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London_Corporation), whilst the [Mayor of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_London) has responsibility for the governance of the [Metropolitan Police](192450.md).[^cite_note-68] The mayors of [Greater Manchester](44287322.md) and [West Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_West_Yorkshire) also have responsibility for governance.

These forces operate across the United Kingdom, and their responsibility is to the specific activities they were established to police.

### Ranks

Main article: [Police ranks of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_ranks_of_the_United_Kingdom)

Throughout the United Kingdom, the rank structure of police forces is identical up to the rank of [chief superintendent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_superintendent). At higher ranks, structures are distinct within [London](17867.md) where the [Metropolitan Police Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Police_Service) and the [City of London Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London_Police) have a series of [commander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_Commander) and [commissioner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_commissioner) ranks as their top ranks whereas other UK police forces have [assistants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assistant_Chief_Constable), [deputies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deputy_Chief_Constable) and a [chief constable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_constable) as their top ranks. All [commissioners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_commissioner) and [chief constables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_constable) are equal in rank.

[Police community support officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_community_support_officer) (PCSOs) were introduced following the passing of the [Police Reform Act 2002](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Reform_Act_2002), although some have criticised these as being a cheap alternative to fully trained police officers.[^cite_note-70]

#### Firearms

See also: [Police use of firearms in the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_use_of_firearms_in_the_United_Kingdom)

As of 2022, there were 142,526 police officers in England and Wales, 6,192 of whom were firearms authorised.[^cite_note-73] Every territorial force has a specialist [Firearms Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firearms_Unit),[^cite_note-waldren-74] which maintains [armed response vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_response_vehicle) to respond to firearms-related emergency calls. Metropolitan and City of London Police operate with three officers per armed response vehicle, composed of a driver, a navigator, and an observer who gathers information about the incident and liaises with other units. Other police forces carry two [authorised firearms officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authorised_firearms_officer) instead of three.



Armed Police in London

Armed police carry various weapons, ranging from semi-automatic carbines to sniper rifles, baton guns (which fire [baton rounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baton_rounds)) and [shotguns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shotguns). All officers also carry a sidearm. Since 2009, [Tasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasers) have been issued to armed officers as an alternative to deadly force.

The majority of officers on mobile patrol will do so in a marked police vehicle, namely an Incident Response Vehicle (IRV). Officers typically hold a "response" permit, allowing them to utilise blue lights and sirens to make an emergency response. Some officers may not have undergone the additional training, and as such, are only permitted to use emergency equipment when positioned at a scene or to pull over a vehicle. Officers who have undergone additional training to reach the "initial pursuit phase" standard are allowed to pursue vehicles, should they fail to stop. Vans are used as IRVs and, more specifically, to transport arrested suspects in a cage, who are unsuitable to be taken to custody in a car.

Some forces utilise Area Cars in addition to IRVs. Like IRVs, they respond to 999 calls and are manned by officers from response teams. However, officers are trained as "advanced" drivers – allowing them to drive high-performance vehicles and pursue fleeing vehicles in the tactical phase of a pursuit. Some drivers may also be trained in skills like [Tactical Pursuit and Containment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PIT_maneuver) (TPAC).

In addition, forces' specialist units utilise a wide variety of vehicles to help perform their role effectively. Roads Policing Units (RPU) utilise performance vehicles to primarily enforce traffic laws and pursue fleeing suspects. Armed Response Vehicles (ARV) are used to transport armed officers and carry weaponry. Tactical/operational support units use larger vans, equipped with windscreen cages and/or reinforced glass, to transport officers into public order situations.

&#x20;Forces also utilise unmarked vehicles for a wide variety of roles. Covert surveillance vehicles are typically not fitted with any emergency equipment, as it is not necessary. Some forces utilise unmarked response vehicles to aid in proactive work. Similarly, some roads policing vehicles and ARVs are unmarked to help officers identify offences and use pre-emptive tactics to stop a suspect fleeing. Additionally, some forces have dedicated road crime units who use high-performance vehicles to primarily focus on organised criminals using the road committing offences.

### Initial training

#### England and Wales

The [College of Policing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_of_Policing) defines six curricula for new police constables, [special constables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_constable) and police community support officers:[^cite_note-pl-75]

- Police Constable Degree Apprenticeship (PCDA), Degree Holder Entry Programme (DHEP) or a pre-join Policing degree course for police constables

  [^cite_note-76]

- Initial Learning for the Special Constabulary (IL4SC) for special constables

- PCSO National Learning Programme for police community support officers

Several alternative programmes exist to join police forces, including [Police Now](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Now) and Fast Track programmes to the rank of Inspector and Superintendent for those with substantial management experience in other sectors.

##### Initial Learning for the Special Constabulary

Derived from the IPLDP, and although not linked to a formal qualification as such, IL4SC requires that the learning outcomes and National Occupational Standards (NOSs) be met to become compliant. This curriculum will bring an officer to the 'point of safe and lawful accompanied patrol'.[^cite_note-77] This course equates to roughly 3.5 weeks of direct learning.

##### PCSO National Learning Programme

Successful completion of the PCSO NLP over a period of six months to a year will result in a non-mandatory Certificate in Policing, and this equates to 10 weeks of direct learning and consists of six mandatory units. Four of these units also feature within the IPLDP, and being a QCF qualification, this can allow for officers wishing to become police officers for 'Recognition of Prior Learning' (RPL) and the transfer of such units to the IPLDP scheme.[^cite_note-pl-75]

#### Scotland

All initial probationer training in Scotland is undertaken at the [Scottish Police College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Police_College) (or SPC) at [Tulliallan Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulliallan_Castle). Recruits initially spend 12 weeks at the SPC before being posted to their divisions, and over the next two years return to the SPC several times to complete examinations and fitness tests.[^cite_note-78] Training is composed of four distinct modules undertaken at various locations, with some parts being delivered locally and some centrally at the SPC.[^cite_note-79]

Training for Special Constables is delivered locally at seven locations throughout Scotland over a series of evenings and/or weekends. The training is split into two parts, with the first phase being delivered in a classroom environment before being sworn in as a Special Constable, and the second phase is delivered after being sworn in. Upon successful completion of both parts of the training programme, Special Constables are awarded a certificate of achievement and are eligible to complete an abbreviated course at the Scottish Police College should they later wish to join the [Police Service of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Service_of_Scotland) as a regular officer.[^cite_note-80]

### Organisation of police forces

As all police forces are autonomous organisations, there is much variation in organisation and nomenclature; however, outlined below are the main strands of policing that make up police forces:

- All police forces have teams of officers who are responsible for general beat duties and response to emergency and non-emergency calls from the public. These officers are generally the most visible and will invariably be the first interface a member of the public has with police. In general terms, these officers will normally patrol by vehicle (though also on foot or bicycle in urban areas). They will generally patrol a sub-division or whole division of a police force area, or in the case of the Metropolitan Police Service, a borough. Nearly all police officers begin their careers in this area of policing, with some moving on to more specialist roles. The Metropolitan Police Service calls this area of policing 'Response Teams', whilst other forces use terms such as 'patrol', 'section', and other variations.

- Most local areas or wards in the country have at least one police officer who is involved in trying to build links with the local community and resolve long-term problems. In London, the Metropolitan Police Service addresses this area of policing with Safer Neighbourhood Teams. This entails each political ward in London having a Police Sergeant, two police constables, and a few PCSOs who are ring-fenced to address problems and build community links in their respective wards. Other police forces have similar systems but can be named 'Area officers', 'Neighbourhood officers', 'Beat Constables', and several other variations.

- Criminal Investigation Departments (CID) can be found in all police forces. Generally, these officers deal with investigations of a more complex, serious nature; however, this again can differ from force to force. Most officers within this area are detectives. Depending on the force in question, this area of policing can be further divided into a myriad of other specialist areas, such as fraud. Smaller forces tend to have detectives who deal with a wide range of varied investigations, whereas detectives in larger forces can have a very specialist remit.

- All police forces have specialist departments that deal with certain aspects of policing. Larger forces such as Greater Manchester Police, Strathclyde Police, and West Midlands Police have many and varied departments and units, such as traffic, firearms, marine, horse, and tactical support, all named differently depending on the force. Smaller forces such as Dyfed Powys Police and Warwickshire Police will have fewer specialists and will rely on cross-training, such as firearms officers also being traffic-trained officers. The Metropolitan Police, the largest force in the country, has a large number of specialist departments, some of which are unique to the Metropolitan Police due to policing the capital and its national responsibilities. For example, the [Diplomatic Protection Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diplomatic_Protection_Group) and [Counter Terrorism Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter_Terrorism_Command).

#### Border Force

Main article: [UK Border Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_Border_Force)



A Border Force patrol vessel

As part of the wide-ranging review of the Home Office, the then Home Secretary, [John Reid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Reid,_Baron_Reid_of_Cardowan), announced in July 2006 that all British immigration officers would be uniformed. On 1 April 2007, the [Border and Immigration Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_and_Immigration_Agency) (BIA) was created and commenced operation. However, there were no police officers in the Agency, a matter that attracted considerable criticism when the Agency was established: agency officers have limited powers of arrest. Further powers for designated officers within the Agency, including powers of detention pending the arrival of a police officer, were introduced by the UK Borders Act 2007.[^cite_note-82]

The Government effectively admitted the shortcomings of the Agency by making several fundamental changes within a year of its commencement. On 1 April 2008, the BIA became the [UK Border Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_Border_Agency) following a merger with [UKvisas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UKvisas), the [port of entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_entry) functions of [HM Revenue and Customs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HM_Revenue_and_Customs). The [Home Secretary](149104.md), Jacqui Smith, announced that the UK Border Agency (UKBA) *"...will bring together the work of the Border and Immigration Agency, UK Visas and parts of [HM Revenue and Customs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HM_Revenue_and_Customs) at the border,&#x20;*&#x20;\[and] *will work closely with the police and other law enforcement agencies to improve border controls and security."*[^cite_note-83]

Within months of this, the [Home Secretary](149104.md) revealed (in a 16-page response to a report by [Lord Carlile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Carlile), the independent reviewer of [UK terrorism legislation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrorism_Act_2006)) that the Home Office would issue a [Green Paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Paper) proposing to take forward proposals by the Association of Chief Police Officers (England and Wales) for the establishment of a new 3,000-strong national border police force to work alongside the Agency.[^cite_note-84][^cite_note-85]

Following a major enquiry into the [UK Border Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_Border_Agency) that exposed significant flaws in the operation of border controls, the Home Secretary, [Theresa May](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theresa_May), announced in 2012 that the [Border Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_Force), which is responsible for manning all points of entry into the United Kingdom, would be split from the control of the UKBA and become a separate organisation with direct accountability to ministers and a "law-enforcement ethos".[^cite_note-86] [Brian Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Moore_\(police_officer\)), the former [Chief Constable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Constable) of [Wiltshire Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiltshire_Police), was appointed as the first head of the new UKBF.[^cite_note-87]

#### Overseas British Territories and Crown Dependencies Police Forces

The [Crown Dependencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_Dependencies) and [British Overseas Territories](207114.md) have their own police forces, the majority of which use the British model. Because they are not part of the United Kingdom, they are not answerable to the British Government; instead they are organised by and are responsible to their own governments (an exception to this is the [Sovereign Base Areas Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign_Base_Areas_Police); as the SBAs' existence is solely for the benefit of the British armed forces and do not have full overseas territory status, the SBA Police are responsible to the Ministry of Defence). Because they are based on the British model of policing, these police forces conform to the standards set out by the British government, which includes voluntarily submitting themselves to inspection by the HMIC.[^cite_note-88] Their vehicles share similarities with the vehicles owned by forces based in the UK, such as the use of [Battenburg markings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battenburg_markings).

These are typically training and mentoring posts, but sometimes involve carrying out executive policing duties.[^cite_note-89]

##### Greater London



British police officers

One of the most common merger proposals is to merge the [City of London Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London_Police) and London operations of the [British Transport Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Transport_Police) into the [Metropolitan Police](192450.md).

The 2005–06 merger proposals had not included Greater London. This was due to two separate reviews of policing in the capital. The first was a review by the Department of Transport into the future role and function of the British Transport Police. The second was a review by the Attorney-General into national measures for combating fraud (the City of London Police is one of the major organisations for combating economic crime).[^cite_note-94] Both the [Metropolitan Police Commissioner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Police_Commissioner), [Sir Ian Blair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Ian_Blair), and the [Mayor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_London), [Ken Livingstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Livingstone), stated that they would like to see a single police force in London, with the [Metropolitan Police](192450.md) also absorbing the functions of the [British Transport Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Transport_Police) in London.[^cite_note-95] However, the proposal to merge both the BTP and City forces with the Met drew significant criticism from several areas: the House of Commons Transport Select Committee severely criticised the idea of the Metropolitan Police taking over policing of the rail network in a report published on 16 May 2006,[^cite_note-96] while the [City of London Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London_Corporation) and several major financial institutions in the [City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London) made public their opposition to the City Police merging with the Met.[^cite_note-97] In a statement on 20 July 2006, the Transport Secretary announced that there would be no structural or operational changes to the British Transport Police, effectively ruling out any merger[^cite_note-98] The interim report by the Attorney General's fraud review recognised the role taken by the City Police as the lead force in London and the South-East for tackling fraud, and made a recommendation that, should a national lead force be required, the City Police, with its expertise, would be an ideal candidate to take this role.[^cite_note-99] This view was confirmed on the publication of the final report, which recommended that the City of London Police's Fraud Squad should be the national lead force in combatting fraud, to "act as a centre of excellence, disseminate best practice, give advice on complex inquiries in other regions, and assist with or direct the most complex of such investigations".[^cite_note-100]

Separate from the proposals raised by the Mayor of London and Metropolitan Police Commissioner was a plan by the government to reform policing in the [Royal Parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Parks_of_London). Since 1872, this had been the responsibility of the [Royal Parks Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Parks_Constabulary). A report by former Metropolitan Police Assistant Commissioner Anthony Speed provided three options to reform the RPC, and it was decided that it should be merged with the Metropolitan Police.[^cite_note-101] The Met took over responsibility for policing the Royal Parks on 1 April 2004 with the formation of the [Royal Parks Operational Command Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Parks_Operational_Command_Unit). The full merger and abolition of the Royal Parks Constabulary took place in May 2006.[^cite_note-102]

In May 2016, following [his election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_London_mayoral_election), the Mayor of London [Sadiq Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadiq_Khan) ordered a review, led by [Lord Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toby_Harris,_Baron_Harris_of_Haringey), of London's preparedness in the face of potential terror attacks. Amongst the recommendations, which were published in October 2016, was a revisiting of the idea of merging the Metropolitan Police, City of London Police, and British Transport Police. In commenting, both the City Police and BTP cautioned against the proposal.[^cite_note-103]

### Funding



[New Scotland Yard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Scotland_Yard_\(building\)) is the headquarters of the [Metropolitan Police Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Police_Service)

The police are funded both by the central government and by the local government.[^cite_note-parliament16-104] Central government funding is calculated based on a formula, based on several population and socio-economic factors which are used to determine the expected cost of policing the area.[^cite_note-parliament16-104]

For the 2017/18 fiscal year, the budget for local Police and Crime Commissioners to spend on police is [£](270673.md)11 billion (equivalent to £14.11 billion or [US\$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USD)18.03 billion in 2024)[^cite_note-inflation-UKGDP-25] with an extra £1.5 billion allocated to counter-terrorism and other special programmes.[^cite_note-105] The combined funding will reduce from £12.3 billion in 2017/18 to [£](270673.md)11.6 billion (equivalent to £13.61 billion or [US\$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USD)17.39 billion in 2024)[^cite_note-inflation-UKGDP-25] in 2020/21. [His Majesty's Inspectorate of Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/His_Majesty's_Inspectorate_of_Constabulary) (HMIC) estimates that officer numbers will fall by around 2%.

According to the [National Audit Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Audit_Office_\(United_Kingdom\)), funding was decreased between 2011 and 2016 by 22% in real terms[^cite_note-parliament16-104] and police officer numbers fell by 20,000 from 2010 to 2017.[^cite_note-guardianrising-106] Funding levels stayed the same in real terms between 2015 and 2018, with a decrease in central government funding made up for by an increase in local government funding.[^cite_note-107] Increased spending in some areas such as counter-terrorism has been offset by decreased spending in other departments.[^cite_note-bbc2/3/2017-108] In 2018 further funding cuts will force further cuts in the numbers of police officers.[^cite_note-109] 80% of UK people believe Britain is less safe due to cuts to police funding.[^cite_note-110]

In 2017, a report from the Inspectorate found that most police forces were providing a good service, though it noted that some aspects such as investigations and neighbourhood policing were being compromised by "rationing" and cutbacks.[^cite_note-bbc2/3/2017-108] A report from the Inspectorate in March 2018 had similar findings; it reported improvement in neighbourhood policing and highlighted issues with response policing.[^cite_note-111][^cite_note-112] Several current and former chief constables were raising concerns about whether the police can meet foreseeable challenges with current levels of funding.[^cite_note-bbc2/3/2017-108][^cite_note-113][^cite_note-114]

Category A: This category also encompasses deaths of those under arrest who are held in temporary police accommodation or have been taken to hospital following arrest. It also includes those who die, following arrest, whilst in a police vehicle.

- they have been taken to a police station after being arrested for an offence, or

Category B: Where the deceased was otherwise in the hands of the police or death resulted from the actions of a police officer in the purported execution of his duty.

- when suspects are being interviewed by the police but have not been detained;

- when persons are actively attempting to evade arrest;

- when persons are stopped and searched or questioned by the police; and

### Racism

See also: [Institutional racism in the Metropolitan Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutional_racism_in_the_Metropolitan_Police)

The Macpherson Report coined the phrase "[institutionalised racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutionalised_racism)" to describe policies and procedures that adversely affect persons from ethnic minority groups after the [death of Stephen Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_Stephen_Lawrence).

In 2003, ten police officers from [Greater Manchester Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Manchester_Police), [North Wales Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Wales_Police) and [Cheshire Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheshire_Constabulary) were forced to resign after a [BBC](19344654.md) documentary, *The Secret Policeman*, shown on 21 October, alleged racism among recruits at [Bruche Police National Training Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruche_Police_National_Training_Centre) at [Warrington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warrington).[^cite_note-125][^cite_note-pf-126] In March 2005 it was reported that minor disciplinary action would be taken against twelve other officers in connection with the programme, but that they would not lose their jobs.[^cite_note-pf-126] In November 2003, allegations were made that some police officers were members of the [British National Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_National_Party).

In June 2015, the [Metropolitan Police Commissioner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Police_Commissioner), [Sir Bernard Hogan-Howe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Bernard_Hogan-Howe), said there was "some justification" in claims that the Metropolitan Police Service is "institutionally racist":

> I have always said if other people think we are institutionally racist, then we are. It is no good for me to say we are not and then say you must believe me; it's nonsense, if they believe that. I think it is a label, but in some sense, there is a truth there for some people .... You're very much more likely to be stopped and searched if you're a young black man. I can't explain that fully. I can give you reasons, but I can't fully explain it. So there is some justification ... I think in some ways society is institutionally racist. We see a lack of representation in many fields, of which the police are one.[^cite_note-127][^cite_note-128]

The question of institutional racism arose again as a topic of controversy during the Covid-19 pandemic. The stop and search rate for all demographics rose, but increased more for certain ethnic (and age) groups. In the first lockdown, the rate of stop and search for Black and Asian people (and individuals aged 18-24) rose by 109% or more, for white people it rose by 89%.[^cite_note-129] Before the pandemic, stop and search was already controversial due to disproportionate applications of it.[^cite_note-130] Further, the style of stop and search used was reported to be cynical and unfair, with numerous incidences of officers using erroneous reasons to conduct a stop.[^cite_note-131] Disproportionality was also noted in the deployment of Fixed Penalty Notices.[^cite_note-132] Additionally, during the pandemic, the Metropolitan Police experienced controversy due to the kidnapping, rape, and murder of Sarah Everard by then serving Metropolitan Police Officer Wayne Couzens. This led to a report on practices and culture within the Metropolitan Police by Baroness Louise Casey, who concluded that the Metropolitan Police were institutionally racist.[^cite_note-133] The Angiolini Inquiry into Wayne Couzens also discovered racist messages he had sent to other police officers via WhatsApp.[^cite_note-134]

### Privacy

At the beginning of 2005 it was announced that the [Police Information Technology Organisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Information_Technology_Organisation) (PITO) had signed an eight-year [£](270673.md)122 million (equivalent to £197.47 million or [US\$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USD)252.39 million in 2024)[^cite_note-inflation-UKGDP-25] contract to introduce [biometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biometric) identification technology.[^cite_note-135] PITO are using [CCTV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CCTV) [facial recognition systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facial_recognition_system) to identify known suspects; a future link to the proposed [National Identity Register](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Identity_Register) has been suggested by some.[^cite_note-136]

### Freedom of speech

The police have sometimes been accused of infringing on [free speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_speech). ### Photography of police

Main article: [Counter-Terrorism Act 2008 § Photographs of police officers in public places](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter-Terrorism_Act_2008#Photographs_of_police_officers_in_public_places)

Section 76 of the Counter-Terrorism Act 2008 came into force on 15 February 2009[^cite_note-140] making it an offence to elicit, attempt to elicit, or publish information "...of a kind likely to be useful to a person committing or preparing an act of terrorism" about:[^cite_note-S76-141] a member of [His Majesty's Armed Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/His_Majesty's_Armed_Forces); a [constable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constable), the [Security Service](100076.md), the [Secret Intelligence Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_Intelligence_Service), or [Government Communications Headquarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Communications_Headquarters). Any person found guilty faces 10 years' imprisonment and an unlimited fine.[^cite_note-S76-141] It is a defence for a person charged with this offence to prove that they had a reasonable excuse for their action.[^cite_note-S76-141] It is not otherwise illegal to photograph or film a police officer in a public place per se.[^cite_note-142][^cite_note-143] Any film or photography recorded whilst a constable is dealing with an incident may be seized as it becomes evidence under section 19 of [PACE 1984](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PACE_1984).

### Policing of public protests

Public order policing presents challenges to the approach of [policing by consent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policing_by_consent).[^cite_note-144][^cite_note-145] In April 2009, a total of 145 complaints were made following clashes between police and protesters at the [G20 summit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_G-20_London_summit_protests).[^cite_note-146] Incidents including the death of 47-year-old [Ian Tomlinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Tomlinson),[^cite_note-147] minutes after an alleged assault by a police officer,[^cite_note-148] and a separate alleged assault on a woman by a police officer,[^cite_note-149] has led to criticism of police tactics during protests.[^cite_note-150] In response, Metropolitan Police Commissioner Sir Paul Stephenson asked Her Majesty's Chief Inspector of Constabulary (HMIC) to review policing tactics,[^cite_note-151] including the practice of [kettling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kettling).[^cite_note-152] These events sparked a debate in the UK about the relationship between the police, media and public, and the independence of the [Independent Police Complaints Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Police_Complaints_Commission).[^cite_note-Lyall-153] In response to the concerns, the Chief Inspector of Constabulary, [Denis O'Connor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_O'Connor_\(police_officer\)), published [a 150-page report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_Ian_Tomlinson#Metropolitan_police_response) in November 2009 that aimed to restore Britain's consent-based model of policing.[^cite_note-LewisLavilleNov25-154][^cite_note-155][^cite_note-156]

### Police undercover conduct

During 2010 and 2011, it emerged that, while engaged in [covert infiltration of protest groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covert_policing_in_the_United_Kingdom), [undercover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undercover) police officers had [entered into intimate relationships with several people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_undercover_policing_relationships_scandal) on false pretences and under assumed aliases, in some cases sharing a home, making plans for weddings, or fathering children, only to vanish after some years when their role was complete. In 2015, a public inquiry under a senior judge was announced. In November 2015, the Metropolitan Police published an unreserved apology in which it exonerated and apologised to those women who had been deceived and stated the methodology had constituted abuse and a "gross violation" with severely harmful effects, as part of a settlement of their cases. In 2016, new cases continued to come to light.

### Capacity to solve crimes

See also: [Crime statistics in the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_statistics_in_the_United_Kingdom)

The way in which police forces record [crime in the UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_statistics_in_the_United_Kingdom) has been subject to a [series of improvements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_statistics_in_the_United_Kingdom#Timeline_of_events) since the late 1990's.[^cite_note-157] This created a dramatic, but misleading increase in police reported crime compared to a steady decrease in crime reported by victims over that time,[^cite_note-158] a trend observed both in the UK and [internationally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_drop).[^cite_note-159]

Nationally, detection rates have been falling as police-reported crime has been rising.[^cite_note-Crime_statistics_in_the_United_Kingdom_\:02-160] In 2024, there was even a zero detection rate for crimes within 166 neighborhood areas under the responsibility of the [Metropolitan Police Service](192450.md) (MPS).[^cite_note-Crime_statistics_in_the_United_Kingdom_a479-161][^cite_note-Crime_statistics_in_the_United_Kingdom_p290-162] More generally, positive outcome rates for the MPS were just 7.3% on average, with some crimes (criminal damage and offences against vehicles) as low as 0.9% in 2022/2023, while drug offences had a 38% detection rate.[^cite_note-Crime_statistics_in_the_United_Kingdom_w3842-163] Corresponding figures for 2010/2011 were far higher, with an average total of 24%.[^cite_note-164]

However, an increase in crimes reported means the same volume of detections is therefore divided by a much larger number of total recorded offences. While this does not fully explain the drop in detection rates, it is thought to have contributed to it. Other reasons include lack of police resources[^cite_note-165] and that the crimes the police record disproportionately affect those offences which (in law, if not by the public) are treated as the least serious.[^cite_note-Crime_statistics_in_the_United_Kingdom_\:02-160]

### Police sex abuse allegations

In 1988, 19-year-old [PC Eileen Waters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waters_v_Comr_of_Police_for_the_Metropolis) alleged that another officer raped her in the police hostel section at her work, having only been in the force for around one year.[^cite_note-rc-166] She reported it the next day to colleagues. The *Evening Standard* reported that she was then targeted by a "horrific campaign of intimidation which led to a bloodstained truncheon being left in her locker."[^cite_note-rc-166] After 24 years of court cases, hearings, and her case appearing before law lords in the House of Lords, in 2012, she was compensated by the Metropolitan Police, receiving [£](270673.md)270,000 (equivalent to £374,364.09 or [US\$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USD)478,472.79 in 2024)[^cite_note-inflation-UKGDP-25][^cite_note-rc-166]

In 2016, allegations of serious [sexual abuse](7397019.md) were made against hundreds of police officers in England and Wales. Several forces in England and Wales received 436 allegations of abuse of power for sexual gain against 436 police officers, including 20 [police community support officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_community_support_officer) and eight staff in the two years to March 2016.[^cite_note-hundreds-167][^cite_note-168] [Mike Cunningham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Cunningham_\(police_officer\)), inspector of constabulary and former chief constable of [Staffordshire police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staffordshire_Police) said: "It's the most serious form of corruption. It is an exploitation of power where the guardian becomes an abuser. What can be worse than a guardian abusing the trust and confidence of an abused person? There can be no greater violation of public trust".[^cite_note-hundreds-167]

### Brexit

Before [Brexit](41688778.md), there were concerns that the UK might lose access to important cross-border databases of criminals, which would make it harder for the police to keep the public safe. Police leaders warned of "a significant loss of operational capacity" if the UK were to leave the EU without an agreement on policing.[^cite_note-guardian-2018-169] The Association of Police and Crime Commissioners sent a letter to Home Secretary Sajid Javid stressing the need for co-operation with European policing and justice organisations after March 2019. The letter stated that 32 measures were used daily, including the European Arrest Warrant, the [Schengen Information System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schengen_Information_System) (SIS) – a database giving alerts about individuals – and the [European Criminal Records Information System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Criminal_Records_Information_System). The letter stressed the importance and mutual benefit of continued cooperation between the UK and Europe to face mutual threats.[^cite_note-guardian-2018-169][^cite_note-170]

The loss of access to SIS has led to police officers being unable to see alerts on criminals from EU countries, and vice versa. UK police agencies have replaced these with Interpol, but this comes with additional administrative overhead and reduced powers, and they are unable to know if they are missing any entries that are in SIS but not in Interpol systems.[^cite_note-171][^cite_note-shellaker-172] The loss of access to [European Investigation Orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Investigation_Order) has been especially damaging.[^cite_note-shellaker-172] However, the UK has retained access to DNA, fingerprint, vehicle, and flight data.[^cite_note-173]

### Allegations of "two-tier policing"

Main article: [Two-tier policing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-tier_policing)

Critics have accused the police of "two-tier policing", claiming that the [enforcement is biased](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selective_enforcement) and treats people from certain political or racial groups more harshly than others.[^cite_note-tb-174] These allegations have been popularised with the outbreak of [Gaza war protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaza_war_protests_in_the_United_Kingdom) since 2023 and after the [2024 riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_riots), and have been promoted by the [right](56522.md)[^cite_note-badenoch-2t-175] and [far-right](221220.md), and to a lesser extent the [far-left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far-left).[^cite_note-tb-174][^cite_note-iv-176][^cite_note-177] Critics on the left, such as [Jamie Driscoll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamie_Driscoll), argue that the police have a right-wing bias and are harsher against left-wingers, ethnic minorities, and pro-Palestinian groups.[^cite_note-hh-178][^cite_note-179] Critics on the right, such as [Nigel Farage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Farage) and [Robert Jenrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Jenrick), argue that the police have a left-wing bias and are more harsh against right-wingers and white people, and the white [working class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_class) in particular.[^cite_note-bp-180][^cite_note-fc-181][^cite_note-iv-176]

Examples of prominent policing incidents in which the accusation have been made include a peaceful protest by [Just Stop Oil](70538003.md) climate protesters in July 2024, in which some protesters were given five-year sentences while a far-right rioter who assaulted a police officer was given a three-year sentence.[^cite_note-\:0-182] In 2025, the government proscribed [Palestine Action](67777543.md) as a terrorist organisation, leading to the arrested of hundreds of peaceful protestors who expressed their support for the organisation. Protesters believed that the police had targeted them disproportionately, with far-right groups like the [English Defence League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Defence_League) still not proscribed and their supporters not persecuted.[^cite_note-hh-178] In June 2026 allegations arose again following the [murder of Henry Nowak](83248158.md) by [British Sikh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Sikh) Vickrum Digwa; after being stabbed and later falsely accused of a racist attack by Digwa, Nowak was handcuffed by police and died shortly afterwards.[^cite_note-BBC2June-183]

## Jurisdictions and territories

As of 2021, there were 39 territorial police forces in England, 4 in Wales, [one in Scotland](34423036.md), and [one in Northern Ireland](294068.md).[^cite_note-pu-1] Every geographic place in the United Kingdom is defined in law as part of certain [police area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_area).[^cite_note-184] In England and Wales, this is currently defined in Section 1 of the Police Act 1996. A police area defines the geographic area for which a territorial police force is responsible for policing. This is different from legal jurisdiction (see below). Special police forces (such as the BTP) do not have police areas, and ultimately the Chief Constable of a territorial police force is responsible for maintaining law and order throughout his/her police area, even if, for example, BTP have a presence at railway stations within the police area. Scotland and Northern Ireland have national police forces.

In England, Police forces are funded by a combination of sources, including [central government](25318118.md) and through the police precept tax levied as part of [Council Tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_Tax), which is charged by local governments.[^cite_note-185] The local police force precept can be increased via [referendum](51582.md). Since 2013, police forces in England (and Wales) have been overseen by a [directly elected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directly_elected) [Police and crime commissioner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_and_crime_commissioner) (PCC) who holds the force to account for the public. PCCs do not have operational control of the police force; operational management of the police force is the responsibility of the [chief constable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_constable) in most English police forces, although the equivalent position is referred to as commissioner in the London Metropolitan Police and the City of London Police. Administration of police matters is not generally affected by the [Government of Wales Act 2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Wales_Act_2006).

#### Proposed mergers for England and Wales

In 1981, [James Anderton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Anderton), Chief Constable of [Greater Manchester Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Manchester_Police), called for the number of forces to be reduced to nine in England (one for each [Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regions_of_England)) and one for Wales.[^cite_note-187] A 2004 proposal by the [Police Superintendents' Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Superintendents'_Association) for the creation of a single national police force, similar to [Garda Síochána](383207.md), was objected to by the [Association of Chief Police Officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_of_Chief_Police_Officers). The government did not accept the proposal at the time.[^cite_note-188]

Administration of police matters is not generally affected by the [Government of Wales Act 2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Wales_Act_2006). From 2005 to 2006, the government considered merging several territorial police forces in England and Wales. The review only concerned policing outside of Scotland, Northern Ireland, and Greater London. Likewise, the major non-territorial forces ([British Transport Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Transport_Police), [Civil Nuclear Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Nuclear_Constabulary), [Ministry of Defence Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Defence_Police)) are responsible to other government departments, and would not have been affected either. The primary argument for merging forces is that forces with 4,000 or more officers would perform better and could save costs.[^cite_note-189] The view was supported by [HM Inspectorate of Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HM_Inspectorate_of_Constabulary), who said in September 2005 that the existing structure was "no longer working".[^cite_note-190]

Merger proposals were announced by the Home Secretary in early 2006. They proposed reducing the number of police forces to less than 25, with [Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-Wales_police_force) and several [regions of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regions_of_England) having one force each.[^cite_note-191][^cite_note-192][^cite_note-193][^cite_note-194][^cite_note-195][^cite_note-196] The consultation period on this second batch of mergers started on 11 April 2006, and would have finished on 11 August, with a target of April 2008 for the mergers coming into effect.[^cite_note-197]

On 20 June 2006, the then Home Secretary, [John Reid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Reid,_Baron_Reid_of_Cardowan), announced that the contested mergers would be delayed for further discussion.[^cite_note-198] The only merger agreed to was with [Lancashire Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancashire_Constabulary) and [Cumbria Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumbria_Constabulary). On 12 July 2006, the Home Office confirmed that all the mergers were to be abandoned, with the entire proposal taken back for consultation.[^cite_note-199]

In 2026, Home Secretary [Shabana Mahmood](27266407.md) announced plans to reduce the number of police forces in England and Wales.[^cite_note-200]

### Scotland

Main article: [Police Scotland](34423036.md)

In 2013, the 8 territorial police forces in Scotland were merged into a single Scottish police force, named "The Police Service of Scotland", or colloquially [Police Scotland](34423036.md). Merging these forces had been first mooted in 2010, and was supported by the [Scottish National Party](28887.md), [Scottish Labour Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Labour_Party) and [Scottish Conservative Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Conservative_Party) ahead of the [2011 Scottish Parliament election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Scottish_Parliament_election).[^cite_note-201] After a consultation process,[^cite_note-202][^cite_note-203] the [Scottish Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Government) confirmed on 8 September 2011 that a single police service would be created in Scotland.[^cite_note-204] The Scottish Government stated that "reform will safeguard frontline policing in communities by creating designated local senior officers for every council area with a statutory duty to work with councils to shape local services. Establishing a single service aims to ensure more equal access to national and specialist services and expertise such as major investigation teams and firearms teams, whenever and wherever they are needed."[^cite_note-scotland1-205] The Police and Fire Reform (Scotland) Bill was published in January 2012[^cite_note-206] and was approved on 27 June 2012 after scrutiny in the [Scottish Parliament](61188.md).[^cite_note-scotland1-205] The Bill received [royal assent](153067.md) as the [Police and Fire Reform (Scotland) Act 2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_and_Fire_Reform_\(Scotland\)_Act_2012). This created a force of approximately 17,000 police officers, the second largest in the United Kingdom after the [Metropolitan Police](192450.md) in London.[^cite_note-207][^cite_note-208]

In March 2015, following the transfer of police oversight powers to the Scottish Government,[^cite_note-209] the Justice Secretary announced proposals to further unify policing in Scotland by merging the [British Transport Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Transport_Police)'s operations north of the border with Police Scotland. This proposal was eventually dropped due to costs and the complexities of jurisdictions.[^cite_note-210]

### Cross-jurisdiction powers

Territorial police constables have certain powers of arrest in another one of the [UK's three legal jurisdictions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_jurisdictions_of_the_United_Kingdom) than they were attested in. There are four main provisions for them to do so: arrest with a warrant, arrest without a warrant for an offence committed in their home jurisdiction whilst in another jurisdiction, arrest without a warrant for an offence committed in another jurisdiction whilst in that jurisdiction, and mutual aid. A fifth power of cross-jurisdictional arrest was introduced by section 116 of the [Policing and Crime Act 2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policing_and_Crime_Act_2017), which fills a loophole in arrest powers in certain situations. This power came into force in March 2018.[^cite_note-ca-211] This new power allows a constable from one jurisdiction to arrest without warrant a person suspected of an offence in another jurisdiction whilst in their home jurisdiction. This power is in relation to more serious offences as listed in the act. The Act sets out how long the person can be detained in custody by the "arresting force" in one jurisdiction until constables from the "investigating force" in another jurisdiction can travel to re-arrest the person and deal accordingly.[^cite_note-pa-212] Below is a summary of these five powers with a practical example due to the complicated nature of this area of law. Note: this section applies to territorial police constables only, and not to others, except the British Transport Police, who also have [certain cross-border powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Transport_Police#Cross-border_powers) in addition to their natural powers.

#### Arrest with warrant

Certain warrants can be executed by constables even though they are outside their jurisdiction: [arrest warrants](146746.md) and [warrants of committal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warrant_of_committal) (all); and a warrant to arrest a witness (England, Wales or Northern Ireland); a warrant for committal, a warrant to imprison (or to apprehend and imprison), and a warrant to arrest a witness (Scotland).[^cite_note-S136-213] A warrant issued in one legal jurisdiction may be executed in either of the other two jurisdictions by a constable from either the jurisdiction where it was issued, or the jurisdiction where it is executed.[^cite_note-S136-213]

When executing a warrant issued in Scotland, the constable executing it shall have the same powers and duties, and the person arrested the same rights, as they would have had if execution had been in Scotland by a constable of a police force in Scotland. When executing a warrant issued in England and Wales or Northern Ireland, a constable may use reasonable force and has specified search powers provided by section 139 of the Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994.[^cite_note-214]

#### Arrest without warrant: offences committed in home jurisdiction

In very simple terms, this power allows constables of one jurisdiction to travel to another jurisdiction and arrest a person they suspect of committing an offence in their home jurisdiction. For example, constables from [Cumbria Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumbria_Police) investigating an offence of assault that occurred in their police area could travel over the border into Scotland and arrest the suspect without a warrant found in [Gretna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gretna,_Dumfries_and_Galloway).

If a constable suspects that a person has committed or attempted to commit an offence in their legal jurisdiction, and that person is now in another jurisdiction, the constable may arrest them in that other jurisdiction.[^cite_note-S137-215]

A constable from England and Wales is subject to the same necessity tests for arrest (as under section 24 of the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984[^cite_note-216]) as they would be in England and Wales, a constable from Scotland may arrest if it would have been lawful to do so in Scotland and a constable from Northern Ireland is subject to the same necessity tests for arrest (as under Article 26 of the Police and Criminal Evidence (Northern Ireland) Order 1989.[^cite_note-217]) as they would be in Northern Ireland.[^cite_note-S137-215]

A person arrested under the above powers:[^cite_note-S137-215]

- in Scotland, shall be taken to the nearest convenient designated police station or to a designated police station in a police area in which the offence is being investigated (England and Wales or Northern Ireland),

- in England or Wales, shall be taken to the nearest convenient police station (Scotland) or to a police station within a sheriffdom in which the offence is being investigated (Scotland), to the nearest convenient designated police station (Northern Ireland) or to a designated police station in which the offence is being investigated (Northern Ireland), or

- In Northern Ireland, shall be taken either to the nearest convenient designated police station (England and Wales) or to a designated police station in a police area in which the offence is being investigated (England and Wales) or to the nearest convenient police station (Scotland) or to a police station within a sheriffdom in which the offence is being investigated (Scotland).

#### Arrest without warrant: offences committed in other jurisdictions (known as reciprocal powers of arrest)

In simple terms, this power gives a constable of one jurisdiction, whilst in another jurisdiction the same power of arrest as a constable of the jurisdiction they are visiting. As a practical example, if constables from [Police Scotland](34423036.md) are over the border in Cumbria on enquiries and come across a burglary in progress, they can arrest the suspect on suspicion of burglary using the same arrest powers as a constable of England or Wales.

A constable from one legal jurisdiction has, in the other jurisdictions, the same powers of arrest as a constable of that jurisdiction would have.[^cite_note-S140-218]

A constable from England or Wales has:[^cite_note-S140-218]

- In Scotland, the same power of arrest as a constable from Scotland

- In Northern Ireland, the same power of arrest as a constable from Northern Ireland would have under Article 26 of the Police and Criminal Evidence (Northern Ireland) Order 1989 (necessity test).

  [^cite_note-219]

A constable from Scotland has:[^cite_note-S140-218]

- In England and Wales, the same power of arrest as a constable from England or Wales would have under section 24 of the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 (necessity test).

  [^cite_note-section_24-220]

- in Northern Ireland, the same power of arrest as a constable from Northern Ireland would have under Article 26 of the Police and Criminal Evidence (Northern Ireland) Order 1989 (necessity test).

  [^cite_note-221]

A constable from Northern Ireland has:[^cite_note-S140-218]

- In Scotland, the same power of arrest as a constable from Scotland

- In England and Wales, the same power of arrest as a constable from England or Wales would have under section 24 of the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 (necessity test).

  [^cite_note-section_24-220]



A British Transport Police public order officer on "mutual aid", supporting the Metropolitan Police

When a constable arrests a person in England and Wales, the constable is subject to the requirements of section 28 (informing of arrest),[^cite_note-222] section 30 (taking to a designated police station)[^cite_note-223] and section 32 (search on arrest).[^cite_note-S140-218][^cite_note-224] When a constable arrests a person in Scotland, the arrested person shall have the same rights and the constable the same powers and duties as they would have were the constable a constable of a police force in Scotland.[^cite_note-S140-218] When a constable arrests a person in Northern Ireland, the constable is subject to the requirements of Article 30 (informing of arrest),[^cite_note-225] Article 32 (taking to a designated police station)[^cite_note-226] and Article 34 (search on arrest).[^cite_note-S140-218][^cite_note-227]

#### Arrest without warrant by a constable in his home jurisdiction for an offence committed in another jurisdiction

This power allows a constable of one jurisdiction to arrest without warrant a person suspected of an offence in another jurisdiction whilst in their home jurisdiction. The Policing and Crime Act 2017 sets out which offences this power of arrest will apply to in each jurisdiction (generally serious offences), and how long the person arrested can be kept in custody, with relevant authorities, by the "arresting force" to allow sufficient time for officers from the "investigating force" in another jurisdiction to travel and re-arrest the detained person for their investigation.[^cite_note-pa-212]

This relatively new power came into force in March 2018.[^cite_note-ca-211] Until the introduction of this power, there was an issue whereby a constable in his home jurisdiction could not arrest a person suspected of an offence in another jurisdiction without a warrant.[^cite_note-228]

#### Other situations (including Mutual Aid)

Police forces often support each other with large-scale operations, such as those that require specialist skills or expertise and those that require policing levels that the host forces cannot provide. Referred to as mutual aid, constables loaned from one force to another have the powers and privileges of a constable of the host force.[^cite_note-229] Constables from the [Metropolitan Police](192450.md) who are on protection duties in Scotland or Northern Ireland have all the powers and privileges of a constable of the host police force.[^cite_note-230] A constable who is taking a person to or from a [prison](19008450.md) retains all the powers, authority, protection and privileges of his office regardless of his location.[^cite_note-231] Regardless of where they are in the United Kingdom, a constable may arrest under section 41[^cite_note-232] and may stop and search under section 43[^cite_note-233] of the [Terrorism Act 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrorism_Act_2000) on suspicion of terrorism (defined by section 40[^cite_note-234])
