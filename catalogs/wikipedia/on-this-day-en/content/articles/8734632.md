**Islamic extremism** is characterised by extremist beliefs, behaviours, and ideologies adhered to by some [Muslims](19541.md) within [Islam](6037917.md). The term "Islamic extremism" is contentious, encompassing a spectrum of definitions, ranging from academic interpretations of [Islamic supremacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremacism#Islam) to the notion that all ideologies other than Islam have failed and are inferior.[^cite_note-auto-1] Islamic [extremism](428002.md) is different from [Islamic fundamentalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_fundamentalism) or [Islamism](15012.md). Islamic fundamentalism refers to a movement among [Muslims](19541.md) advocating a return to the fundamental principles of an [Islamic state](2646438.md) in [Muslim-majority countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim-majority_countries). Meanwhile, Islamism constitutes a form of [political Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_Islam). However, both Islamic fundamentalism and Islamism have also been classified as subsets of Islamic extremism by some. Acts of violence committed by [Islamic terrorists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_terrorists) and [jihadists](7826589.md) are associated with these extremist beliefs, while many Muslims see extremists as not following Islam. The term "Islamism" has been criticised as having been given connotations of violence, extremism, and violations of human rights, by the Western mass media, leading to Islamophobia and stereotyping.[^cite_note-Shepard-2] During the [Cold War](325329.md) following [World War II](32927.md), the United States and the United Kingdom launched covert and overt campaigns to encourage and strengthen Islamic fundamentalist groups in the Middle East, North Africa, and southern Asia. These groups were seen as a hedge against potential expansion by the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union), and as a counterweight against [nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism) and [socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism) movements that were seen as a threat to the interests of the Western nations.[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5] In a 2018 interview with *The Washington Post*, [Mohammed bin Salman](38689358.md), the *de facto* ruler of Saudi Arabia, said that Saudi Arabia's [International propagation of the Salafi movement and Wahhabism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_propagation_of_the_Salafi_movement_and_Wahhabism) campaign "was rooted in the Cold War, when allies asked Saudi Arabia to use its resources to prevent inroads in Muslim countries by the Soviet Union."[^cite_note-6] According to some estimates, between the 1960s and 2016, [Saudi Arabia](349303.md) funnelled over US\$100 billion to spread Wahhabi Islam.[^cite_note-7] According to [political scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science) Alex Alexiev, the impetus for the international propagation of Salafism and Wahhabism was "the largest worldwide [propaganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda) campaign ever mounted",[^cite_note-auto1-8] [David A. Kaplan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_A._Kaplan) described it as "dwarfing the [Soviets' propaganda efforts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_measures) at the height of the Cold War".[^cite_note-auto1-8] In 2013, the [European Parliament](9581.md) identified [Wahhabism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wahhabism) as the main source of global terrorism.[^cite_note-9]

## Definitions

### Academic definition

The academic definition of radical Islam consists of two parts:

- Islamic thought that states that all ideologies other than Islam, whether associated with the West (capitalism or democracy) or the East (communism or socialism), have failed and demonstrated their bankruptcy.

  [^cite_note-auto-1]

- Islamic thought that states that (semi-)secular regimes are wrong because of their negligence of Islam.

  [^cite_note-10]

### UK High Court definition

The UK High Court has ruled in two cases on Islamic extremism and provided definitions.

Aside from those, two major definitions have been offered for Islamic extremism, incorporating overlapping yet distinct aspects of extreme interpretations and pursuits of [Islamic](6037917.md) ideology:

- The use of violent tactics such as bombings and assassinations for achieving perceived Islamic goals (see [Jihadism](7826589.md); [Zeyno Baran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeyno_Baran), Senior Fellow and Director of the Center for Eurasian Policy at the [Hudson Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudson_Institute), prefers the term *[Islamist](15012.md) extremism*).

  [^cite_note-Baran-11]

- An extremely conservative view of Islam,

  [^cite_note-Farmer2007-12]

  &#x20;which does not necessarily entail violence

  [^cite_note-13]

  &#x20;(see also [Islamic fundamentalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_fundamentalism); Baran again prefers the term *Islamism*).

  [^cite_note-Baran-11]

In 2019, the United States Institute of Peace issued a report on extremism in [fragile states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fragile_state), advocating the establishment of a shared understanding, an operational framework for prevention, and international cooperation.[^cite_note-14]

## Key influences of radical Islam

### Early Islam

According to the academic definition of radical Islam, the second condition for something to be designated radical Islam is that it is anti-governmental. Consequently, the presence of a government is a prerequisite. #### Kharijites

Main article: [Kharijites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharijites)

Islamic extremism dates back to the [early history of Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_history_of_Islam) with the emergence of the [Kharijites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharijites) in the 7th century CE.[^cite_note-Izutsu_2006-15] The original schism between Kharijites, [Sunnīs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam), and [Shīʿas](26961.md) arose over the [political and religious succession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Succession_to_Muhammad) to the leadership of the [Muslim community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ummah) (*Ummah*) following the death of the [Islamic prophet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_prophet) [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad).[^cite_note-Izutsu_2006-15] From their essentially political stance, the Kharijites developed extreme doctrines that set them apart from both mainstream Sunnī and Shīʿa Muslims.[^cite_note-Izutsu_2006-15] Shīʿas believe [ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali) was the true successor to Muhammad, while Sunnīs consider [Abu Bakr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Bakr) to hold that position. The Kharijites broke away from both groups during the [First Fitna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Fitna) (the first Islamic Civil War);[^cite_note-Izutsu_2006-15] they were particularly noted for adopting a radical approach to *[takfīr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takfir)* (excommunication), declaring both Sunnī and Shīʿa Muslims to be either [infidels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kafir) (*kuffār*) or [false Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munafiq) (*munāfiḳūn*), and deeming them [worthy of death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_punishment_in_Islam) for perceived [apostasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostasy_in_Islam) (*ridda*).[^cite_note-Izutsu_2006-15][^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17]

Islamic tradition traces the origin of the Kharijites to the [battle between ʿAlī and Mu'awiya at Siffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Siffin) in 657 CE. When ʿAlī faced a military stalemate and agreed to submit the dispute to arbitration, some members of his party withdrew their support. "Judgement belongs to God alone" (لاَ حُكْمَ إلَا لِلّهِ) became the slogan of these secessionists.[^cite_note-Izutsu_2006-15] They also referred to themselves as *al-Shurat* ("the Vendors"), reflecting their willingness to give their lives in [martyrdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martyrdom_in_Islam).[^cite_note-\:0-18]

These early Kharijites opposed both ʿAlī and Mu'awiya and appointed their own leaders. They were decisively defeated by ʿAlī, who was later assassinated by a Kharijite. Kharijites subsequently engaged in guerrilla warfare against the [Umayyads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate), becoming a major force during the [Second Fitna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Fitna) (the second Islamic Civil War), when they temporarily controlled more territory than any of their rivals. They presented a significant challenge to Ibn al-Zubayr's bid for the caliphate, controlling Yamama and most of southern Arabia, and capturing the oasis town of al-Ta'if.[^cite_note-\:0-18]

The Azariqa, considered the most extreme Kharijite faction, controlled parts of western Iran under the Umayyads until their defeat in 699 CE. The more moderate [Ibadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibadi_Islam) Kharijites lasted longer, continuing to hold political influence in North and East Africa and eastern Arabia during the ['Abbasid period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate). Because of their readiness to declare opponents apostates, extreme Kharijite factions frequently fragmented into small groups. A key consensus among these splinter groups was their view of the caliphate, which diverged from other Islamic theories on two primary points:

- First, they were principled egalitarians, maintaining that any pious Muslim ("even an [Ethiopian slave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_slavery_in_the_Muslim_world)") could become caliph, and that family or tribal lineage was irrelevant. The sole criteria for leadership were personal piety and communal acceptance.

- Second, they maintained that believers had a duty to depose any leader who fell into error. Applying these principles to early Islamic history, Kharijites recognised only Abu Bakr and 'Umar as legitimate caliphs; of 'Uthman's rule, they accepted only the first six years, while rejecting 'Ali entirely.

By the time Ibn al-Muqaffa' composed his political treatise early in the 'Abbasid era, the Kharijites were no longer a primary political threat in the [Islamic heartlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslim_conquests). However, the memory of their challenge to unity and their ideological radicalism continued to influence Islamic political and religious thought.[^cite_note-\:0-18] The Ibadis represent the only Kharijite branch surviving into modern times.

#### Ottoman Empire

See also: [Kadizadeli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kadizadeli)

The **Kadızadelis** (also *Qādīzādali*) were a seventeenth-century puritanical reform movement in the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md) following [Kadızade Mehmed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kad%C4%B1zade_Mehmed) (1582–1635), a revivalist preacher. Kadızade and his followers were staunch opponents of [Sufism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism) and [popular religious practices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_religion#Folk_Islam). They condemned various Ottoman traditions as [bidʻah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bid%CA%BBah) ("non-Islamic innovations") and advocated "reviving the beliefs and practices of the first Muslim generation in the first/seventh century" ("[enjoining good and forbidding wrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enjoining_good_and_forbidding_wrong)").[^cite_note-Evstatiev-2016-213-19]

Driven by impassioned rhetoric, Kadızade Mehmed attracted numerous followers seeking to eliminate perceived corruption across the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md). Movement leaders held official posts as preachers in major mosques in Baghdad, combining popular support with backing from within the state apparatus.[^cite_note-Cook-91-20] Between 1630 and 1680, violent clashes occurred between the Kadızadelis and their opponents. As the movement developed, adherents entered "mosques, [tekkes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanqah) and [Ottoman coffeehouses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_coffeehouse) in order to mete out punishments to those contravening their version of orthodoxy."

The [*Salafiyya* movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_movement) is a conservative,[^cite_note-21] *[Islahi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islah)* (reform)[^cite_note-22] movement within [Sunnī Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) that emerged in the second half of the 19th century and advocates a return to the traditions of the "devout ancestors" (*[Salaf al-Salih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salaf)*). It has been described as the "fastest-growing Islamic movement", with scholars expressing diverse views across social, theological, and political spectra. Salafis follow a doctrine that can be summed up as taking "a [fundamentalist approach to Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_fundamentalism), emulating the Prophet [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad) and his earliest followers—*al-salaf al-salih*, the 'pious forefathers'....They reject religious innovation, or *[bidʻah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bid%CA%BBah)*, and support the implementation of *[Sharia](28840.md)* (Islamic law)."[^cite_note-Economist27Jun15-23] The Salafi movement is often divided into three categories: the largest group are the purists (or [quietists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_quietism_in_Islam)), who avoid politics; the second largest group are the [militant activists](15012.md), who get involved in politics; the third and smallest group are the [jihadists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism), who constitute a minority.[^cite_note-Economist27Jun15-23] Most violent Islamist groups stem from the Salafi-jihadist movement and its subgroups.[^cite_note-Homegrown_2021-24] In recent years, jihadi-Salafist doctrines have often been associated with armed insurgencies and [terrorist organisations](566821.md) targeting civilians, both Muslims and non-Muslims, such as [al-Qaeda](1921.md), [ISIL/ISIS](9087364.md), and [Boko Haram](23769768.md).[^cite_note-Sageman2011-25][^cite_note-Oliveti2002-26][^cite_note-Economist27Jun15-23][^cite_note-Homegrown_2021-24] The second-largest group, the Salafi activists, has a long tradition of political involvement, including within organisations such as the [Muslim Brotherhood](20742.md). In the aftermath of wide-scale repression following the [Arab Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Spring), accompanied by political setbacks, activist Salafi movements experienced a decline. The quietists, representing the largest segment, advocate political disengagement and maintain allegiance to established rulers to avoid *[fitna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitna_\(word\))* (chaos).[^cite_note-Economist27Jun15-23]

The [Wahhabi movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wahhabism) was founded by the [Hanbali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanbali) scholar and theologian [Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Abd_al-Wahhab),[^cite_note-Peskes2012-27][^cite_note-Bokhari-Senzai_2013-28][^cite_note-Ágoston-Masters_2009-29] a religious preacher from the [Najd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Najd) region in the [Arabian Peninsula](47858.md),[^cite_note-Wagemakers_2021-30][^cite_note-Laoust2012-31][^cite_note-Haykel2013-32][^cite_note-Esposito2004-33][^cite_note-Oxford2020-34] and was instrumental in the rise of the [House of Saud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Saud) to power in the Arabian Peninsula.[^cite_note-Peskes2012-27] Ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhab sought to [revive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_revival) and purify [Islam](6037917.md) from what he perceived as non-Islamic popular religious beliefs and practices by returning to what he believed were the [fundamental principles of the Islamic religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_fundamentalism).[^cite_note-Laoust2012-31][^cite_note-Haykel2013-32][^cite_note-Esposito2004-33][^cite_note-Oxford2020-34] His works were generally short, full of quotations from the [Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran) and [*Hadith* literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith), such as his main theological treatise, *Kitāb at-Tawḥīd* ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): كتاب التوحيد; "The Book of Oneness").[^cite_note-Laoust2012-31][^cite_note-Haykel2013-32][^cite_note-Esposito2004-33][^cite_note-Oxford2020-34] He taught that the primary doctrine of Islam was the [uniqueness and oneness of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tawhid) (*tawḥīd*), and denounced what he held to be popular religious practices akin to [heretical innovation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bid%CA%BBah) (*bidʿah*) and [polytheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shirk_\(Islam\)) (*shirk*).[^cite_note-Laoust2012-31][^cite_note-Haykel2013-32][^cite_note-Esposito2004-33][^cite_note-Oxford2020-34]

Wahhabism has been described as a conservative, strict, and [fundamentalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_fundamentalism) branch of Sunnī Islam,[^cite_note-Musa_2018-35] with [puritan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puritanical) views,[^cite_note-Musa_2018-35] believing in a literal interpretation of the Quran.[^cite_note-Peskes2012-27] The terms "[Wahhabism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wahhabism)" and "[Salafism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafism)" are sometimes invoked interchangeably, although the designation "[Wahhabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wahhabism#Definitions_and_etymology)" is specifically applied to the followers of Muhammad ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhab and his [reformist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islah) doctrines.[^cite_note-Peskes2012-27] The label "Wahhabi" was not claimed by his followers, who usually refer to themselves as *al-Muwaḥḥidūn* ("affirmers of the singularity of God"), but is rather employed by Western scholars and critics.[^cite_note-Peskes2012-27][^cite_note-Bokhari-Senzai_2013-28][^cite_note-Haykel2013-32] Starting in the mid-1970s and 1980s, the [international propagation of Salafism and Wahhabism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_propagation_of_Salafism_and_Wahhabism) within Sunnī Islam,[^cite_note-Musa_2018-35] favoured by the [Kingdom of Saudi Arabia](349303.md)[^cite_note-Wagemakers_2021-30][^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37] and other [Arab states of the Persian Gulf](3088555.md), achieved what French political scientist [Gilles Kepel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilles_Kepel) defined as a "preeminent position of strength in the global expression of Islam."[^cite_note-38]

Twenty-two months after the [September 11 attacks](5058690.md), when the [FBI](11127.md) considered [al-Qaeda](1921.md) "the number one terrorist threat to the United States", journalist [Stephen Schwartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Suleyman_Schwartz) and U.S. Senator [Jon Kyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Kyl) stated during a June 2003 hearing before the Subcommittee on Terrorism, Technology, and Homeland Security of the [U.S. Senate](24909346.md) that "Wahhabism is the source of the [overwhelming majority of terrorist atrocities in today's world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_terrorist_incidents)".[^cite_note-govinfo.gov-39] As part of the global "[War on terror](13425800.md)", Wahhabism has been described by the [European Parliament](9581.md), Western security analysts, and think tanks such as the [RAND Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAND_Corporation) as "a source of global terrorism".[^cite_note-govinfo.gov-39][^cite_note-Haider-40] Furthermore, Wahhabism has been accused of causing division within the [Muslim community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ummah) (*Ummah*) and criticised for its followers' [destruction of historic sites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destruction_of_early_Islamic_heritage_sites_in_Saudi_Arabia) associated with the [early history of Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_history_of_Islam) and the first generation of Muslims ([Muhammad's family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahl_al-Bayt) and his [companions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Companions_of_the_Prophet)) in Saudi Arabia.[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-Rabasa_2004_103,_note_60-42][^cite_note-TI-43][^cite_note-finn-destruction-44]

The contemporary period begins after 1924. With the [defeat and dissolution of the Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defeat_and_dissolution_of_the_Ottoman_Empire) (1908–1922), the [Ottoman Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Caliphate) was also [abolished](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolition_of_the_Caliphate). This event heavily influenced Islamic thought in general, as well as what would later be termed radical Islamic thought.[^cite_note-48] Key thinkers who wrote about Islam in the 20th century, especially concerning *[jihad](16203.md)*, include [Muhammad Abduh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Abduh), [Rashid Rida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashid_Rida), [Hassan al-Banna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hassan_al-Banna), [Abul A'la Maududi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abul_A'la_Maududi), and [Sayyid Qutb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyid_Qutb).

[Sayyid Qutb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyid_Qutb), an Egyptian Islamist ideologue and prominent figure in the [Muslim Brotherhood](20742.md) in Egypt, was influential in promoting [Pan-Islamist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Islamism) ideology in the 1960s.[^cite_note-Polk_2018-49] Following Qutb's execution by the [Egyptian government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_government) under the [regime of Gamal Abdel Nasser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Egypt_under_Gamal_Abdel_Nasser), [Ayman al-Zawahiri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayman_al-Zawahiri) formed the organisation [Egyptian Islamic Jihad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_Islamic_Jihad) to replace the government with an Islamic state reflecting Qutb's vision of [Islamic revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_revival).[^cite_note-50] [Qutbist ideology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qutbism) has influenced jihadist movements and [terrorist groups](566821.md) seeking to overthrow secular governments, notably [Osama bin Laden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osama_bin_Laden) and Ayman al-Zawahiri of [al-Qaeda](1921.md),[^cite_note-Gallagher_2021-45][^cite_note-Aydinli_2018-46][^cite_note-Moussalli_2012-47] as well as the [Salafi-jihadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_jihadism) group [ISIL/ISIS](9087364.md).[^cite_note-Baele_2019-51] Moreover, Qutb's works have frequently been cited by bin Laden and [Anwar al-Awlaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anwar_al-Awlaki).[^cite_note-NYT:_path_to_terror-52][^cite_note-Irwin-53][^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55][^cite_note-56][^cite_note-57]

Sayyid Qutb has been described by scholars as a foundational figure of modern radical Islam.[^cite_note-Moussalli_2012-47][^cite_note-Polk_2018-49][^cite_note-Cook_2015-58] Unlike other Islamic thinkers mentioned above, Qutb was not an [apologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apologetics).[^cite_note-Cook_2015-58] He was a leading ideologue of the [Muslim Brotherhood](20742.md) and among the first to articulate these anathemising principles in his magnum opus *Fī ẓilāl al-Qurʾān* (*In the Shade of the Qurʾān*) and his 1966 manifesto *Maʿālim fīl-ṭarīq* (*[Milestones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milestones_\(book\))*), which led to his execution by the Egyptian government.[^cite_note-Cook_2015-58][^cite_note-59] Other Salafi movements in the [Middle East and North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East_and_North_Africa) and across the [Muslim world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world) adopted many of his principles.[^cite_note-Moussalli_2012-47][^cite_note-Cook_2015-58]

According to Qutb, the [Muslim community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ummah) (*Ummah*) had been absent for centuries, reverting to *[jahiliyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahiliyah)* (pre-Islamic ignorance) because societies failed to enforce [*sharia* law](28840.md).[^cite_note-Moussalli_2012-47][^cite_note-Cook_2015-58] To [restore Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_revival) and free Muslims from what he considered the clutches of ignorance, Qutb advocated rejecting secular societal norms, establishing an ideological vanguard modelled on early Muslims, and preparing for *[jihad](16203.md)* against *jahili* governance to overthrow it.[^cite_note-Moussalli_2012-47][^cite_note-Cook_2015-58] [Qutbism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qutbism), the radical Islamist ideology derived from Qutb's writings,[^cite_note-Moussalli_2012-47] was subsequently rejected by many mainstream Muslim scholars and several leaders within the Muslim Brotherhood, such as [Yusuf al-Qaradawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yusuf_al-Qaradawi).

## Foreign political support

According to British historian [Mark Curtis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Curtis_\(British_author\)) in *[Secret Affairs: Britain's Collusion with Radical Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_Affairs:_Britain's_Collusion_with_Radical_Islam)*, Britain supported radical Islamic groups to counter secular nationalism because nationalist movements sought to control their domestic resources for internal development, which challenged British interests.[^cite_note-84] The United States similarly supported radical Islamic factions as a counterweight against secular nationalism, which was viewed as a threat to Western geopolitical influence. [Noam Chomsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noam_Chomsky) and his co-authors also argue that during the [1967 Six-Day War](29328.md), Israel targeted Egypt and Syria as bastions of secular [Arab nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_nationalism) opposing Saudi Arabia, which they considered the primary sponsor of radical Islam.[^cite_note-85]
