The **peso** (established as the *peso convertible*; several older currencies were also named *peso*) is the currency of [Argentina](18951905.md) since 1992, identified within Argentina by the symbol [\$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar_sign) preceding the amount in the same way as many countries using [peso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peso) or [dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar) currencies. It is subdivided into 100 *[centavos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centavo)*, but with 10 pesos being worth about 1 US cent in early 2025, smaller denominations are not issued or in normal use. Its [ISO 4217](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_4217) code is **ARS**.[^cite_note-3] It replaced the [austral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_austral) at a rate of 10,000 australes to one peso.

Argentine currency has experienced severe [inflation](38286.md), with periods of [hyperinflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation), since the mid-20th century, with periodic change of the currency valuation to a new version at a rate ranging from 100\:1 to 10,000\:1. A new peso introduced in 1992, officially the *peso convertible de curso legal*, was worth 10,000,000,000,000 (ten trillion) *[pesos moneda nacional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_peso_moneda_nacional)*, the currency in use until 1970. Since the early 21st century, the peso has experienced further substantial inflation, reaching 289.4% year-on-year in April 2024, the highest since the current peso was introduced in the [Convertibility plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convertibility_plan) of 1991.[^cite_note-inflat-4]

The official exchange rate for the [United States dollar](18717338.md) valued the *peso convertible de curso legal* at one US dollar at its introduction in 1992, which was maintained until early 2002. Afterwards, it went from a 3\:1 exchange rate with the US dollar in 2003 to 178\:1 in early 2023. On 14 August 2023, the official exchange rate was fixed at ARS\$350 to one US dollar; the [unregulated rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_exchange_rate) valued the peso at ARS\$665 to one US dollar.[^cite_note-5] On 15 November 2023, the [crawling peg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crawling_peg) was restored.[^cite_note-6]



[USD](18717338.md)/Argentine Peso [exchange rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_rate)

On 12 December 2023, following the election of president [Javier Milei](56599840.md), economy minister [Luis Caputo](52767901.md) changed the official exchange rate to 800 pesos to the U.S. dollar from the previous 366.5, a devaluation of 54%, to be followed by a [monthly devaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crawling_peg) target of 2%[^cite_note-The_Guardian-7] (about 27% per year). At the time, the unofficial exchange rate was around 1,000 pesos per dollar.[^cite_note-8] The monthly devaluation target was lowered to 1% in February 2025 after inflation eased.[^cite_note-\:3-9]

From 14 April 2025 on, the official peso is set to freely floating within a [currency band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_band) of 1,000 to 1,400 pesos per dollar, as part of an agreement with the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md).[^cite_note-\:0-10][^cite_note-\:1-11] Since then, as a consequence of the liberalization of the currency market, the official and unofficial rates have remained close to each other.[^cite_note-\:2-12] The upper limit of the trading band was breached in September and October 2025, triggering interventions by the Argentine central bank and the United States Treasury in the foreign exchange market, by buying pesos, to keep the exchange rate within the limits of the currency band.[^cite_note-\:5-13][^cite_note-\:4-14]

## History

Amounts in earlier pesos were sometimes preceded by a "\$" sign and sometimes, particularly in formal use, by symbols identifying that it was the specific currency in use at the time, for example "\$+m⁄n100" or "m\$n100" for *pesos moneda nacional*. The currency introduced in 1992 was called *peso convertible*, simplified to just *peso* in 2002. A value in pesos is written with a preceding "\$" sign only. Earlier pesos replaced currencies also called peso, and sometimes two varieties of peso coexisted, making it necessary to have a distinguishing term to use, at least in the transitional period; the 1992 peso replaced a currency with a different name, *austral*.

### Peso before 1826

The *peso* was a name often used for the silver Spanish eight-[real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_real) coin. Following [independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_Declaration_of_Independence), Argentina began issuing its own coins, denominated in [reales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_real), *[soles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_sol)* and *escudos*, including silver eight-real (or sol) coins still known as pesos. These coins, together with those from neighbouring countries, circulated until 1881.

### Peso fuerte, 1826–1881



200 *pesos fuertes* banknote issued in 1869

In 1826, two paper money issues began, denominated in pesos. One, the *peso fuerte* (*\$F*) was a convertible currency, with 17 pesos fuerte equal to one Spanish ounce (27.0643 g) of 0.916 fine gold. It was replaced by the peso moneda nacional at par in 1881.

### Peso moneda corriente, 1826–1881

Main article: [Argentine peso moneda corriente](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_peso_moneda_corriente)



"Cinco pesos" *moneda corriente* banknote, issued 1869

The non-convertible *[peso moneda corriente](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_peso_moneda_corriente)* (everyday currency) (*\$m/c*) was also introduced in 1826. It started at par with the peso fuerte, but depreciated with time. Although the Argentine Confederation issued 1-, 2- and 4-*centavo* coins in 1854, with 100 *centavos* equal to 1 peso = 8 [reales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_real), Argentina did not decimalize until 1881. The *peso moneda nacional* (*m\$n* or *\$m/n*) replaced the earlier currencies at the rate of 1 peso moneda nacional = 8 reales = 1 peso fuerte = 25 peso moneda corriente. Initially, one peso moneda nacional coin was made of silver and known as patacón. However, the 1890 economic crisis ensured that no further silver coins were issued.

### Gold and silver pesos, 1881–1970

The Argentine gold coin from 1875 was the gold peso fuerte, one and two-thirds of a gram of gold of fineness 900, equivalent to one and a half grams of fine gold, defined by Law no. 733 of 1875. This unit was based on that recommended by the European Congress of Economists in Paris in 1867 and adopted by Japan in 1873 (the Argentine 5 peso fuerte coin was equivalent to the Japanese 5 [yen](34392.md)). However, these provisions were not implemented.[^cite_note-minthistory-15]

The system before 1881 has been described as "monetary anarchism" (*anarquía monetaria*).[^cite_note-minthistory-15] Law no. 1130 of 1881 put an end to this; it established the monetary unit as the *peso oro sellado* ("stamped gold peso"), a coin of 1.612 [g](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram) of gold of [fineness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millesimal_fineness) 900 (90%), and the silver peso, 25 [g](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram) of silver of fineness 900.[^cite_note-minthistory-15] Gold coins of 5 and 2.5 pesos were to be used, silver coins of one peso and 50, 20, 10 and 5 centavos, and copper coins of 2 and 1 centavos.

### Peso moneda nacional, 1881–1970

Main article: [Argentine peso moneda nacional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_peso_moneda_nacional)



5 cents *moneda nacional* banknote featuring Nicolás Avellaneda, 1891

The depreciated *peso moneda corriente* was replaced in 1881 by the paper peso *moneda nacional* (national currency, (*m\$n* or *\$m/n*)) at a rate of 25 to 1. This currency was used from 1881 until 1 January 1970.[^cite_note-16] The design was changed in 1899, and again in 1942. Initially, the peso m\$n was convertible, with a value of one *peso oro sellado*. Convertibility was maintained off and on, with decreasing value in gold, until it was finally abandoned in 1929, when m\$n 2.2727 was equivalent to one peso oro.

### Peso ley, 1970–1983

Main article: [Argentine peso ley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_peso_ley)

The *peso ley 18.188* ([ISO 4217](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_4217): **ARL**) (informally called the *[peso ley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_peso_ley)*) replaced the previous currency at a rate of 1 peso ley to 100 pesos moneda nacional.

### Peso argentino, 1983–1985

Main article: [Argentine peso (1983–1985)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_peso_\(1983%E2%80%931985\))

The *[peso argentino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_peso_argentino)* (*\$a*) ([ISO 4217](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_4217): **ARP**) replaced the previous currency at a rate of 1 peso argentino to 10,000 pesos ley (1 million pesos m\$n). The currency was introduced just before the return of democracy, on 1 June 1983. However, it rapidly lost its purchasing power, was devalued several times, and was replaced by a new currency called the austral in June 1985.

### Austral, 1985–1991

Main article: [Argentine austral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_austral)

The *[austral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_austral)* ("₳") ([ISO 4217](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_4217): **ARA**) replaced the [peso argentino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peso_argentino) at a rate of 1 austral to 1,000 pesos (one billion pesos m\$n). During the period of circulation of the austral, Argentina suffered from [hyperinflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation). The last months of President [Raul Alfonsín](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raul_Alfons%C3%ADn)'s period in office in 1989 saw prices increase constantly (200% in July alone), reflected in a worsening exchange rate. Emergency notes of 10,000, 50,000 and 500,000 australes were issued, and provincial administrations issued their own currency for the first time in decades. The value of the currency stabilized two years after President [Carlos Menem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlos_Menem) was elected.

### Peso convertible, since 1992

In 1992 a new peso ([ISO 4217](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_4217): **ARS**) was introduced, referred to as *peso convertible* since the international [exchange rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_rate) was [fixed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_exchange_rate) by the [Central Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banco_Central_de_la_Rep%C3%BAblica_Argentina) at 1 peso to 1 [U.S. dollar](18717338.md), and for every *peso convertible* [circulating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetary_base), there was a US dollar in the Central Bank's [foreign currency reserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_currency). It replaced the austral at a rate of 1 peso = 10,000 australes. After the various changes of currency and dropping of zeros, one peso convertible of 1992 was equivalent to 10 trillion pesos moneda nacional of 1970.

After the [financial crisis of 2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998%E2%80%932002_Argentine_great_depression), the fixed exchange rate system was abandoned in January 2002, and the exchange rate fluctuated, up to a peak of four pesos to one dollar (a 75% [devaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devaluation)) at the time. The resulting [export](307592.md) boom produced a massive inflow of dollars into the Argentine economy, which helped lower their price. For a time the administration stated and maintained a strategy of keeping the exchange rate at between 2.90 and 3.10 pesos per US dollar, in order to maintain the competitiveness of exports and encourage [the replacement of imports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Import_substitution_industrialization) by local industries. When necessary, the Central Bank issues pesos and buys dollars in the free market (sometimes large amounts, of the [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_magnitude) of 10 to US\$100 million per day) to keep the dollar price from dropping, and had amassed over US\$27 billion in reserves before the US\$9.81 billion payment to the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md) in January 2006.

The effect of this may be compared to the neighboring [Brazilian real](34201323.md), which was roughly on par with the Argentine peso until the beginning of 2003, when both currencies were about three per U.S. dollar. The *real* started gaining in value more than the peso due to Brazil's slower build-up of dollar reserves; by 29 December 2009, a *real* was worth almost 2.2 pesos.[^cite_note-17] In December 2015, US dollar [exchange restrictions were removed in Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_currency_controls_\(2011%E2%80%932015\)) following the election of President [Mauricio Macri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauricio_Macri). As a result, the difference between the official rate and the [unofficial "blue" rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_exchange_rate) almost disappeared for a time. The official exchange rate was on 1 April 2016 of 14.4 to US\$1.[^cite_note-18] The rate gradually worsened; on 29 July 2022 one U.S. dollar was quoted at 131.22 pesos at the official rate and 298 pesos, 2.27 times higher (+127%), in unregulated markets.[^cite_note-19] By September 2023, the official exchange rate had reached 350 pesos to the dollar, and over 720 pesos on unregulated markets.[^cite_note-20]

On 12 December 2023, following the election of president [Javier Milei](56599840.md), economy minister [Luis Caputo](52767901.md) changed the official exchange rate to 800 pesos to the dollar from the previous 366.5, a devaluation of 54%. At the time, the unofficial exchange rate was around 1,000 pesos per dollar. This was followed by a monthly devaluation target of 2%, being afterwards lowered to 1% in February 2025 after inflation eased.[^cite_note-The_Guardian-7][^cite_note-\:3-9][^cite_note-21] From 14 April 2025 on, the official peso has operated within a [currency band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_band) of 1000 to 1400 pesos per dollar, as part of an agreement with the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md).[^cite_note-\:0-10][^cite_note-\:1-11] Since then, as a consequence of the liberalization of the currency market, the official and unofficial rates have remained close to each other.[^cite_note-\:2-12] The upper limit of the trading band was breached in September and October 2025, triggering interventions by the Argentine central bank and the United States Treasury in the foreign exchange market, by buying pesos, to keep the exchange rate within the limits of the currency band.[^cite_note-\:5-13][^cite_note-\:4-14]

## Coins

In 1992, 1, 5, 10, 25 and 50 centavo coins were introduced, followed by 1 peso in 1994. Two-peso coins were introduced in 2010. One-centavo coins were last [minted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mint_\(facility\)) in 2001. In 2017 a new series of coins was issued in denominations of \$1 and \$5, followed by \$2 and \$10 in 2018.[^cite_note-22] While they are still [legal tender](267793.md), they are not accepted in stores and bank branches due to their negligible value and inconvenience in storage.

2 peso coins were issued in 1999 to commemorate the centenary of the birth of writer [Jorge Luis Borges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jorge_Luis_Borges), with Borges portrayed on the obverse and a [labyrinth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labyrinth) and the [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_alphabet) letter [aleph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleph_\(letter\)) on the reverse. Some other 50-centavo and 1-peso coins commemorate different events, including the 50th anniversary of the creation of [UNICEF](19698110.md) (1996); the [attainment of voting rights by women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_suffrage#Timeline) (1997); the establishment of [Mercosur](25869317.md) (1998); and the death of [José de San Martín](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_de_San_Mart%C3%ADn) (2001). ## Banknotes of the peso convertible (since 1992)

### First series (*Convertibles*, 1992)

In 1992, the first series of peso convertible banknotes were introduced in denominations of 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, and 100 pesos. The 1-peso note was replaced by a coin in 1994. Until 2001 banknotes bore the legend "Convertibles de curso legal", meaning that their value was fixed to the same amount in US dollars. As most older bills have been replaced, it is rare to find ones marked as convertible except in the \$100 denominations. All bills are 155 × 65 mm in size.[^cite_note-46]

## Exchange rates

At the end of 2011, exchange control measures were implemented, which managed to reduce capital flight by 85%. One consequence of these measures was the appearance of multiple exchange rates and a parallel market (colloquially called the "blue dollar"), which was accessed by individuals and companies. From 14 April 2025 on, the official peso has operated within a [currency band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_band) of 1000 to 1400 pesos per dollar, as part of an agreement with the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md).[^cite_note-\:0-10][^cite_note-\:1-11] Since then, as a consequence of the liberalization of the currency market, the official and unofficial (parallel) rates have remained close to each other.[^cite_note-\:2-12] The upper limit of the trading band was breached in September and October 2025, triggering interventions by the Argentine central bank and the United States Treasury in the foreign exchange market, by buying pesos, to keep the exchange rate within the limits of the currency band.[^cite_note-\:5-13]

Special official exchange rates are sometimes created and abolished, to support sectors of the economy. There has been a "soybean dollar", a special rate for soybean exports, that was applicable between 5 and 30 September 2022,[^cite_note-70] between 20 November and 30 December 2022,[^cite_note-71] again between 8 April and 31 May 2023,[^cite_note-72] and again from 5 September to 25 October 2023.[^cite_note-73] A "corn dollar" for corn exports existed between 25 July and 31 August 2023.[^cite_note-74] On 4 November 2022. a "foreign tourist dollar" rate close to the black market rate (355 pesos to the dollar instead of the official 195 at the end of February 2023) was introduced, for purchases made with foreign [payment cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payment_card). This was to encourage people to visit the country while discouraging them from using the currency black market. Tourist dollars spent in January 2023 were nearly five times as many as in January 2022.[^cite_note-75]
