**Kharkiv**,[^cite_note-11] also known as **Kharkov**, is the second-largest [city](4077166.md) in [Ukraine](31750.md).[^cite_note-Kharkiv:_#2_+_situation_October_2014-12] Located in the northeast of the country, it is the largest city of the historic region of [Sloboda Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloboda_Ukraine). Kharkiv is the administrative center of [Kharkiv Oblast](494901.md) and [Kharkiv Raion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Raion). Prior to the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md) in early 2022, it had an estimated population of 1,421,125.[^cite_note-living_kharkiv-13]

Founded in 1654 as a [Cossack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cossacks) fortress, by the late 19th century Kharkiv had developed within the [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire) as a major commercial and industrial center. From December 1919 to January 1934, Kharkiv was the capital of the [Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic). During this period, migration from the distressed countryside and the relaxation of restrictions on Ukrainian cultural expression changed the city's ethnic complexion: [Ukrainian](145144.md) replaced [Russian](59510.md) as the largest recorded nationality, however Russian remained the dominant spoken language. The city was the focus of major battles in [World War II](32927.md), and under [German occupation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_occupation) witnessed the systematic destruction of its substantial Jewish population. In the last decades of the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union), it reemerged as a major cultural, scientific, educational, transport, and industrial center.&#x20;

Among the city's principal landmarks are the [Annunciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annunciation_Cathedral,_Kharkiv) and [Dormition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dormition_Cathedral,_Kharkiv) cathedrals, the [Derzhprom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derzhprom) building in [Freedom Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Square_\(Kharkiv\)), the [Kharkiv Railway Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_railway_station), the [National University of Kharkiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_University_of_Kharkiv), and the [Kharkiv Tractor Factory (HTZ)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Tractor_Plant). [Machinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_industry), [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics) and military hardware have been the leading industries.

In March and April 2014, the city saw both pro-Russia and pro-Ukrainian demonstrations, and an aborted attempt by [Russian-backed separatists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_people's_militias_in_Ukraine) to seize control of the city and regional administration. Launching their full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, the Russians reached the outskirts of Kharkiv but after several weeks of fighting were forced back toward the international border. By February 2026, four years of continued Russian shelling, drone and missile attacks had reportedly destroyed a quarter of the city and driven much of public life underground.

The city's name can be originated from its [namesake river, *Kharkiv*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_\(river\)).[^cite_note-14] There is a folk etymology that connects the name of both the settlement and the river to a legendary [Cossack founder named *Kharko*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zakhary_Chepiha)[^cite_note-15] (a diminutive form of the Greek name [Chariton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chariton_\(name\)), [Ukrainian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_language): Харитон, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Ukrainian): *Kharyton*,[^cite_note-KUW231114-3] or [Zechariah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zechariah_\(given_name\)), [Ukrainian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_language): Захарій, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Ukrainian): *Zakharii*).[^cite_note-wikisource-16] But the river's name is attested earlier than the foundation of the fortress.[^cite_note-17]

*Kharkov*, the transliteration of the name from Russian, was the traditional standard English spelling of the city's name favored prior to [Ukraine's independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Ukraine) in 1991 (similar to the spelling of Kiev versus [Kyiv](585629.md)).

Like all other cities across the country, *Kharkiv* became the internationally standardized Latin-alphabet transliteration of the Ukrainian name according to the [Ukrainian National romanization system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Ukrainian#Ukrainian_National_transliteration), which was adopted for official use by Ukraine's cabinet in 2010, approved by the [UN Group of Experts on Geographical Names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Group_of_Experts_on_Geographical_Names) in 2012, and adopted by the BGN/PCGN in 2019.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21] This spelling appears in *Encyclopædia Britannica*[^cite_note-22] and in dictionaries as the spelling for the Ukrainian city.[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26] The spelling *Kharkiv* has also been adopted as the Library of Congress Name Authority Heading.[^cite_note-27] As noted by the *[Christian Science Monitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Science_Monitor)*, many in the English-language media outlets historically spelled the city *Kharkov*, even after changing the spelling of Kiev to Kyiv, but since the beginning of Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine more outlets[^cite_note-CSM_spellings-28][^cite_note-USA_Today_spellings-29] and style guides[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31][^cite_note-32] have been shifting away from Russian transliterations.

### Early history

The earliest historical references to the region are to [Scythian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythians) and [Sarmatian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarmatians) settlement in the 2nd century BC. Between the 2nd to the 6th centuries AD there is evidence of [Chernyakhov culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernyakhov_culture), a multiethnic mix of the [Geto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getae)-[Dacian](100550.md), [Sarmatian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarmatians), and [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goths) populations. [^cite_note-33] In the 8th to 10th centuries the [Khazar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khazar) fortress of *Verkhneye Saltovo* stood about 25 miles (40 km) east of the modern city, near [Staryi Saltiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staryi_Saltiv).[^cite_note-34] During the 12th century, the area was part of the territory of the [Cumans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumans), and then from the mid-13th century of the [Mongol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_Empire)/[Tartar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tatars) [Golden Horde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Horde).

By the early 17th century the area was a contested frontier region with renegade populations that had begun to organize in [Cossack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cossacks) formations and communities defined by a common determination to resist both [Tatar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tatars) slavery and [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szlachta), [Lithuanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Duchy_of_Lithuania) and [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsardom_of_Russia) [serfdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serfdom). Mid-century, the [Khmelnytsky uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmelnytsky_uprising) against the [Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth) saw the brief establishment of an independent [Cossack Hetmanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cossack_Hetmanate).[^cite_note-Solchanyk2001-35]

### Kharkiv Fortress



Kharkiv's alleged founder [Kharko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharko?action=edit\&redlink=1) (1890s postcard).

During this period of turmoil in [Right-bank Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-bank_Ukraine), groups of settlers arrived at the banks of the [Lopan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lopan_River) and [Kharkiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_River) rivers and, in 1654, rebuilt and fortified an abandoned settlement.[^cite_note-living_kharkiv-13]

The settlement reluctantly accepted the protection and authority of a Russian [voivode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voivode) from [Chuhuiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuhuiv) 40 kilometres (25 mi) to the east. The first appointed voivode from [Moscow](19004.md) was Voyin Selifontov in 1656, who began to build a local [ostrog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostrog_\(fortress\)) (fort). In 1658, a new voivode, Ivan Ofrosimov, commanded the locals to kiss the cross in a demonstration of loyalty to [Tsar Alexis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexis_of_Russia). Led by their [otaman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otaman) Ivan Kryvoshlyk, they refused. However, with the election of a new otaman, Tymish Lavrynov, relations appear to have been repaired, the Tsar in Moscow granting the community's request (signed by the [deans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_\(Christianity\)) of the new [Dormition Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dormition_Cathedral,_Kharkiv) and parish churches of Annunciation and Trinity) to establish a local market.[^cite_note-living_kharkiv-13]

At that time the population of Kharkiv was just over 1000, half of whom were local Cossacks. Selifontov had brought with him a Moscow garrison of only 70 soldiers.[^cite_note-living_kharkiv-13] Defense rested with a local Sloboda Cossack regiment under the jurisdiction of the [Razryad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Razriad) [Prikaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prikaz), a military agency commanded from [Belgorod](478861.md).[^cite_note-living_kharkiv-13]

The original walls of Kharkiv enclosed today's streets: Kvitky-Osnovianenko Street, Constitution Square, Pavlivskyi Square, Serhiivskyi Square, and Sobornyi Descent.[^cite_note-living_kharkiv-13] There were 10 towers of which the tallest, Vestovska, was some 16 metres (52 ft) high. In 1689 the fortress was expanded to include the [Intercession Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercession_Cathedral,_Kharkiv) and Monastery, which became a seat of a local church hierarch, the [Protopope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protopope).[^cite_note-living_kharkiv-13]

### Russian Empire

Administrative reforms led to Kharkiv being governed from 1708 from [Kyiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyiv_Governorate),[^cite_note-1708List-36] and from 1727 from [Belgorod](478861.md). In 1765 Kharkiv was established as the seat of a separate [Sloboda Ukraine Governorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloboda_Ukraine_Governorate).[^cite_note-voronezh-37]



A 19th-century view of Kharkiv, with the belltower of the [Dormition Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dormition_Cathedral,_Kharkiv) dominating the skyline



The first railway station in Kharkiv, built in 1869

[Kharkiv University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_University) was established in 1805 in the Palace of [Governorate-General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governorate-General).[^cite_note-living_kharkiv-13] Alexander Mickiewicz, brother of the Polish national poet [Adam Mickiewicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Mickiewicz), was a professor of law in the university, while another celebrity, [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethe), searched for instructors for the school.[^cite_note-living_kharkiv-13] One of its later graduates was the Ukrainian poet and writer [Ivan Franko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Franko), to whom it awarded a doctorate in Russian linguistics in 1906.[^cite_note-living_kharkiv-13][^cite_note-38]

The streets were first cobbled in the city center in 1830.[^cite_note-39] In 1844 the 90 metres (300 ft) tall Alexander Bell Tower, commemorating the victory over [Napoleon I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon) in 1812, was built next to the first [Dormition Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dormition_Cathedral,_Kharkiv) (later to be transformed by the Soviet authorities into a [radio tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_tower)). A system of running water was established in 1870.[^cite_note-living_kharkiv-13]

In the course of the 19th century, although predominantly Russian speaking, Kharkiv became a center of Ukrainian culture.[^cite_note-hromada-40] The first Ukrainian newspaper was published in the city in 1812. Soon after the [Crimean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_War), in 1860–61, a [hromada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hromada_\(secret_society\)) was established in the city, one of a network of secret societies that laid the groundwork for the appearance of a Ukrainian national movement. Its most prominent member was the philosopher, linguist and pan-slavist activist [Oleksandr Potebnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Potebnja). Members of a student hromada in the city included the future national leaders [Borys Martos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borys_Martos) and [Dmytro Antonovych](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmytro_Antonovych),[^cite_note-hromada-40] and reputedly were the first to employ the slogan "Glory to Ukraine!" and its response "Glory on all of earth!".[^cite_note-41]

In 1900, the student hromada founded the [Revolutionary Ukrainian Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutionary_Ukrainian_Party) (RUP), which sought to unite all Ukrainian national elements, including the growing number of socialists.[^cite_note-42] Following the [revolutionary events 1905](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1905_Russian_Revolution) in which Kharkiv distinguished itself by avoiding a [reactionary pogrom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pogroms_in_the_Russian_Empire) against its Jewish population,[^cite_note-43] the RUP in Kharkiv, [Poltava](38581.md), [Kyiv](585629.md), [Nizhyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nizhyn), [Lubny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lubny), and [Yekaterinodar](474128.md) repudiated the more extreme elements of [Ukrainian nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_nationalism). Adopting the [Erfurt Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erfurt_Program) of [German Social Democracy](60585.md), they restyled themselves as the [Ukrainian Social Democratic Labour Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Social_Democratic_Labour_Party) (USDLP). This was to remain independent of, and opposed by, the [Bolshevik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolsheviks) faction of the [Russian SDLP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Social_Democratic_Labour_Party).[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-\:6-45]

### Revolution and war of independence



The Intercession Cathedral with bell tower and Ozerianska church (right) built in 1689

After the [February Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_Revolution) of 1917, the USDLP was the main party in the first Ukrainian government, the [General Secretariat of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Secretariat_of_Ukraine). The [Tsentralna Rada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsentralna_Rada) (central council) of Ukrainian parties in Kyiv authorized the Secretariat to negotiate national autonomy with the [Russian Provisional Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Provisional_Government). In the succeeding months, as wartime conditions deteriorated, the USDLP lost support in Kharkiv and elsewhere to the [Ukrainian Socialist Revolutionary Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Socialist_Revolutionary_Party) (SR) which organized both in peasant communities and in disaffected military units.[^cite_note-\:6-45]

In the [Russian Constituent Assembly election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1917_Russian_Constituent_Assembly_election) held in November 1917, the [Bolsheviks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolsheviks) who had seized power in [Petrograd](24320051.md) and Moscow received just 10.5 percent of the vote in the [Governorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kherson_Governorate), compared to 73 percent for a bloc of Ukrainian and [Russian Socialist Revolutionaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Revolutionary_Party). Commanding worker, rather than peasant, votes, within the city itself the Bolsheviks won a plurality.[^cite_note-Radkey1989-115-46]

When in January 1918, Lenin's [Council of People's Commissars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_People's_Commissars) disbanded the [Constituent Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Constituent_Assembly) after its first sitting in [Petrograd](24320051.md), the [Tsentralna Rada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsentralna_Rada) in [Kyiv](585629.md) proclaimed the independence of the [Ukrainian People's Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_People's_Republic) (UPR).[^cite_note-KUW231114-3] [Bolsheviks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolsheviks) withdrew from Tsentralna Rada and formed their own Rada (national council) in Kharkiv.[^cite_note-hdU_Katchanovski-47][^cite_note-48] By February 1918 their forces had [captured much of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Civil_War).[^cite_note-49]

The Bolsheviks made Kharkiv the capital of the [Donetsk-Krivoy Rog Soviet Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donetsk-Krivoy_Rog_Soviet_Republic).[^cite_note-50] Six weeks later, under the treaty terms agreed with the [Central Powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Powers) at [Brest-Litovsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Brest-Litovsk), they abandoned the city and ceded the territory to the German-occupied [Ukrainian State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_State).[^cite_note-51] On 6 March 1918, the city briefly became the seat a newly established [zemlia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Administrative_division_of_Ukraine_\(1918\)) of the Ukrainian People's Republic. On 29 April 1918 it was disbanded by [Pavlo Skoropadsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavlo_Skoropadsky), [Hetman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hetman_of_all_Ukraine) of the German-backed [Ukrainian State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_State).

### Soviet era

#### Capital of Soviet Ukraine



The [Derzhprom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derzhprom) (Palace of Industry) completed in 1928

After the German withdrawal in November 1918, the [Red Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Army) returned but, in June 1919, withdrew again before the advancing forces of [Anton Denikin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anton_Denikin)'s [White movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_movement) [Volunteer Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volunteer_Army).[^cite_note-52] By December 1919, Soviet authority was restored.[^cite_note-53] The Bolsheviks established Kharkiv as [the capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_\(political\)) of the [Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_SSR) and, in 1922, this was formally incorporated as a constituent republic of the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union).[^cite_note-54]

Plan of Kharkov, 1930

Plan of Kharkiv, 1930

In the 1920s and 30s, a number of prestige construction projects in new officially-approved [Constructivist style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructivist_architecture) were completed.[^cite_note-55] Among these were the [Derzhprom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derzhprom) (Palace of Industry) then the tallest building in the Soviet Union (and the second tallest in Europe),[^cite_note-56] the Red Army Building, the Ukrainian Polytechnic Institute of Distance Learning (UZPI), the [Kharkiv City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_City_Council) building, with its massive asymmetric tower, and the central department store that was opened on the 15th anniversary of the [October Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/October_Revolution).[^cite_note-living_kharkiv-13] As new buildings were going up, many of city's historic architectural monuments were being torn down. These included most of the baroque churches: Saint Nicholas's Cathedral of the Ukrainian Autocephalous Orthodox church, the Church of the Myrrhophores, Saint Demetrius's Church, and the Cossack fortified Church of the Nativity.[^cite_note-\:5-57]

Under [Stalin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin)'s [First Five Year Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Five-Year_Plans_\(Pakistan\)), the city underwent intensified industrialization, led by a number of national projects. Chief among these were the [Kharkiv Tractor Factory (HTZ)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Tractor_Plant), described by Stalin as "a steel bastion of the [collectivisation of agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectivization_of_agriculture) in the Ukraine",[^cite_note-58] and the [Malyshev Factory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malyshev_Factory), an enlargement of the old Kharkiv Locomotive Factory, which at its height employed 60,000 workers in the production of heavy equipment.[^cite_note-AJKtfF15-59] By 1937, the output of Kharkiv's industries was reported as being 35 times greater than in 1913.[^cite_note-\:5-57]

&#x20;Since the turn of the century, the influx of new workers from the countryside changed the ethnic composition of Kharkiv. According to census returns, by 1939, the Russian share of the population had fallen from almost two-thirds to one third, while the Ukrainian share rose from a quarter to almost half. The Jewish population rose from under six percent of the total, to over 15 percent[^cite_note-\:1-60][^cite_note-\:3-61] (sustaining a [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language) secondary school, a popular Jewish university and extensive publication in [Yiddish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yiddish) and Hebrew).[^cite_note-\:9-62]



Starved peasants on the street during the [Holodomor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holodomor) (1933)

&#x20;In the 1920s, the [Ukrainian SSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_SSR) promoted the use of the [Ukrainian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_language), mandating it for all schools. In practice the share of secondary schools teaching in the [Ukrainian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_language) remained lower than the ethnic [Ukrainian](145144.md) share of the [Kharkiv Oblast](494901.md)'s population.[^cite_note-63] The [Ukrainization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainization) policy was reversed, with the prosecution in Kharkiv in 1930 of the [Union for the Freedom of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_for_the_Freedom_of_Ukraine_process). Hundreds of Ukrainian intellectuals were arrested and deported.[^cite_note-64]



A monument to the [persecuted kobzars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecuted_kobzars_and_bandurists)&#x20;

In 1932 and 1933, the combination of grain seizures and the forced collectivization of peasant holdings created famine conditions (the [Holodomor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holodomor)), driving people off the land and into Kharkiv and other cities in search of food.[^cite_note-Leonavičius_|first1=Vylius_Ozolinčiūtė_2019-65][^cite_note-Ellman2007-66] Eye-witness accounts by westerners — among them those of [American Communist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_USA) [Fred Beal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Beal) employed in the [Kharkiv Tractor Factory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Tractor_Plant)[^cite_note-\:2-67] — were cited in the international press but, until the era of *[Glasnost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasnost)* were consistently [denounced in the Soviet Union as fabrications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holodomor_denial).[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-reflections-69][^cite_note-70]

In 1934, hundreds of Ukrainian writers, intellectuals and cultural workers were arrested and executed in an attempt to eradicate all vestiges of [Ukrainian nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_nationalism). The purges continued into 1938. Blind [Ukrainian street musicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kobzar) were rounded up in Kharkiv and murdered by the [NKVD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NKVD).[^cite_note-71] Confident in his control over Ukraine, in January 1934 Stalin had the capital of the Ukrainian SSR moved from Kharkiv to Kyiv.[^cite_note-George_O._Liber_1992-72]

During April and May 1940, about 3,900 Polish prisoners in[Starobilsk](534897.md) camp were executed in the Kharkiv NKVD building, later secretly buried on the grounds of an NKVD *pansionat* in [Piatykhatky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piatykhatky,_Kharkiv_Oblast) forest (part of the [Katyn massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katyn_massacre)) on the outskirts of Kharkiv.[^cite_note-Fischer-73][^cite_note-74] The site also contains numerous bodies of Ukrainian cultural workers arrested and shot during the [1937–38 Stalinist purges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Purge).

#### German occupation



German troops entering Kharkiv in 1941

During [World War II](32927.md), Kharkiv was the focus of major battles. The city [was captured](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Battle_of_Kharkov) by [Nazi Germany](21212.md) on 24 October 1941.[^cite_note-kharkiv_at_war-75][^cite_note-76] A disastrous [Red Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Army) [offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Battle_of_Kharkov) failed to recover the city in May 1942.[^cite_note-77][^cite_note-78] It was retaken ([Operation Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Star)) on 16 February 1943, but [again lost to the Germans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Battle_of_Kharkov) on 15 March 1943. 23 August 1943 saw a final [liberation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_battle_of_kharkov).[^cite_note-\:7-79]

On the eve of the occupation, Kharkiv's pre-war population of 700,000 had been doubled by the influx of refugees.[^cite_note-Evening_Kharkiv-80] What remained of the pre-war Jewish population of 130,000 were slated by the Germans for "special treatment". Between December 1941 and January 1942, they massacred and buried an estimated 15,000 Jews in a ravine outside of town named [Drobytsky Yar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drobytsky_Yar).[^cite_note-81] Over their 22 months' occupation the Germans executed a further 30,000 residents, among them suspected [Soviet partisans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_partisans) and, after a brief period of toleration, Ukrainian nationalists. 80,000 people died of hunger, cold and disease. 60,000 were forcibly transported to Germany as slave workers (*[Ostarbeiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostarbeiter)*).[^cite_note-KvUah5e-82][^cite_note-\:5-57]

By the time of Kharkiv's liberation in August 1943, the surviving population had been reduced to under 200,000.[^cite_note-Evening_Kharkiv-80] Seventy percent of the city had been destroyed.[^cite_note-\:7-79] According to a *New York Times* piece, "The city was more battered than perhaps any other in the Soviet Union save Stalingrad."[^cite_note-83]

#### Post-World War II

Before the occupation, Kharkiv's [tank industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malyshev_Factory) had been evacuated to the [Urals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urals) with all their equipment, and became the heart of [Red Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Army)'s tank programs, particularly the Kharkiv-designed [T-34](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-34). These enterprises returned to Kharkiv after the war, and became central elements of the post-war Soviet [military industrial complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military%E2%80%93industrial_complex).[^cite_note-KvUah5e-82] Houses and factories were rebuilt, and much of the city's center was reconstructed in the style of [Stalinist Classicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalinist_architecture).[^cite_note-living_kharkiv-13] [Kharkiv's Jewish community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Kharkiv) revived after World War II: by 1959 there were 84,000 Jews living in the city. However, [Soviet anti-Zionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_anti-Zionism) restricted expressions of Jewish religion and culture, and was sustained until the final Gorbachev years. The confiscated [Kharkiv Choral Synagogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Choral_Synagogue) reopened as a synagogue in 1990.[^cite_note-\:9-62]



[Mirror Stream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zerkal%CA%B9naya_struya) fountain



Kharkiv in 1981

In the [Brezhnev era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brezhnev_era), Kharkiv was promoted as a "model Soviet city". Propaganda made much of its "youthfulness", a designation broadly used to suggest the relative absence in the city of "material and spiritual relics" from the pre-revolutionary era, and its commitment to the new frontiers of Soviet industry and science. The city's machine-and-weapons building prowess was attributed to a forward-looking collaboration between its large-scale industrial enterprises and new research institutes and laboratories.[^cite_note-84]

The last [Communist Party chief of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Secretary_of_the_Communist_Party_of_Ukraine), [Vladimir Ivashko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Ivashko), appointed in 1989, trained as a mining engineer and served as a party functionary in Kharkiv.[^cite_note-85] He led the Communists to victory in Kharkiv and across the country in the [parliamentary election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1990_Ukrainian_parliamentary_election) held in the Ukrainian SSR in March 1990.[^cite_note-kalinichenko-86] The election was relatively free, but occurred well before organized political parties had time to form, and did not arrest the decline in the [CPSU's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union) legitimacy.[^cite_note-87] This was accelerated by the intra-party coup attempt against President [Mikhail Gorbachev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev) and his reforms on 18 August 1991, during which Ivashko temporarily replaced Gorbachev as [CPSU General Secretary.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Secretary_of_the_Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union)[^cite_note-88]

[The National University of Kharkiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_University_of_Kharkiv) was at the forefront of democratic agitation. In October 1991, a call from Kyiv for an all-Ukrainian university strike to protest Gorbachev's [new](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_of_Sovereign_States#Development) [Union Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Union_Treaty) and to call for new multi-party elections was met with a rally at the entrance to the university attended not only by students and university teachers, but also by a range of public and cultural figures.[^cite_note-89] The protests — the so-called [Revolution on Granite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution_on_Granite)[^cite_note-lesson-revolution-granite-90] — ended on 17 October with a resolution of the [Verkhovna Rada](844993.md) of the Ukrainian SSR promising further democratic reform. In the event, the only demand fulfilled was the removal of the Communist Prime Minister.[^cite_note-91]

### Independent Ukraine

In the 1 December 1991 [Referendum on the Act of Declaration of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1991_Ukrainian_independence_referendum), on a turnout of 76 percent, 86 percent of the [Kharkiv Oblast](494901.md) approved separate Ukrainian statehood.[^cite_note-92]

During the [1990s post-Soviet aliyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1990s_post-Soviet_aliyah), many Jews emigrated to [Israel](9282173.md) or to Western countries.[^cite_note-93] The city's Jewish population, 62,800 in 1970,[^cite_note-\:9-62] dropped to 50,000 by the end of the century.[^cite_note-94]



New Year's decoration of [Freedom Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Square_\(Kharkiv\)) in Kharkiv in 2018

The [collapse of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union) disrupted, but did not sever, the ties that bound Kharkiv's heavy industries to the integrated Soviet market and supply chains, and did not diminish dependency on Russian oil, minerals, and gas.[^cite_note-95] In Kharkiv and elsewhere in eastern Ukraine, the limited prospects for securing new economic partners in the West, and concern for the rights of Russian-speakers in the new national state, combined to promote the interests of political parties and candidates emphasizing understanding and cooperation with the [Russian Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Federation). In the new century, these were represented by the [Party of Regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party_of_Regions) and by the presidential ambitions of [Victor Yanukovych](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Yanukovych),[^cite_note-96] which in Kharkiv triumphed in the [city council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_City_Council) elections of 2006, in the parliamentary elections of 2007 and in the presidential elections of 2010.[^cite_note-97]

Although never attaining the level of protest witnessed in Kyiv and in communities further west, following the disputed [2012 Parliamentary elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Ukrainian_parliamentary_election) public opposition to [President Yanukovych](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Yanukovych) and his party surfaced in Kharkiv amid accusations of systematic corruption and of sabotaging prospects for new ties to the European Union.[^cite_note-98]

#### Pro-Russian unrest and violence

Main article: [2014 pro-Russian unrest in Ukraine § Kharkiv Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_pro-Russian_unrest_in_Ukraine#Kharkiv_Oblast)

The [Euromaidan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euromaidan) protests in the winter of 2013–2014 against then president [Viktor Yanukovych](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Yanukovych) consisted of daily gatherings of about 200 protestors near the statue of [Taras Shevchenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taras_Shevchenko) and were predominantly peaceful.[^cite_note-separatistarrests25324984-99] Disappointed at the turnout, an activist at Kharkiv University suggested that his fellow students "proved to be as much of an inert, grey and cowed mass as Kharkiv's ‘*biudzhetniki*’ " (those whose income derives from the state budget, mostly public servants).[^cite_note-100] But Pro-Yanukovych demonstrations, held near the [statue of Lenin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue_of_Lenin_in_Kharkiv) in [Freedom (previously Dzerzhinsky) Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Square_\(Kharkiv\)), were similarly small.[^cite_note-separatistarrests25324984-99]

In the wake of Yanukovych's ouster in February, there were attempts in Kharkiv to follow the example of separatists in neighboring [Donbas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donbas).[^cite_note-Ukraine_crisis_timeline_BBC-101] On 2 March 2014, a Russian "tourist" from Moscow replaced the [Ukrainian flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_flag) with a [Russian flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_flag) on the Kharkiv Regional State Administration Building.[^cite_note-102] On 6 April 2014 pro-Russian protesters occupied the building and unilaterally declared independence from Ukraine as the "[Kharkiv People's Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_People's_Republic)".[^cite_note-separatistarrests25324984-99][^cite_note-Focus_Information_Agency-103] Doubts arose about their local origin as they had initially targeted the city's [Opera and Ballet Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_National_Academic_Opera_and_Ballet_Theatre) before recognizing their mistake.[^cite_note-104]

Kharkiv's mayor, [Hennadiy "Gepa" Kernes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hennadiy_Kernes), elected in 2010 as the nominee of the [Party of Regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party_of_Regions), was placed under house arrest. Claiming to have been "prisoner of Yanukovych's system",[^cite_note-prisoner_of_Yanukovych-105] he now declared his loyalty to acting President [Oleksandr Turchynov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oleksandr_Turchynov).[^cite_note-separatistarrests25324984-99] In a televised address on 7 April, Turchynov had announced that "a second wave of the Russian Federation's special operation against Ukraine \[has] started" with the "goal of destabilising the situation in the country, toppling Ukrainian authorities, disrupting the elections, and tearing our country apart".[^cite_note-106] Kernes persuaded the police to storm the regional administration building and push out the separatists. He was allowed to return to his mayoral duties.[^cite_note-\:8-107]

Police action against the separatists was reinforced by a special forces unit from [Vinnytsia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinnytsia) directed by Ukrainian Interior Minister [Arsen Avakov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsen_Avakov) and [Stepan Poltorak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stepan_Poltorak) the acting commander of the [Ukrainian Internal Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_Troops_of_Ukraine).[^cite_note-separatistarrests25324984-99][^cite_note-KRR2-108] On 13 April, some pro-Russian protesters again made it inside the Kharkiv regional state administration building, but were quickly evicted.[^cite_note-KRR2-108][^cite_note-kharkivinfiltrate-109][^cite_note-Unian.net-110] Violent clashes resulted in the severe beating of at least 50 pro-Ukrainian protesters in attacks by pro-Russian protesters.[^cite_note-kharkivinfiltrate-109][^cite_note-Unian.net-110]

On 28 April, [Kernes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hennadiy_Kernes) was shot by a sniper,[^cite_note-111] a victim, commentators suggested, of his former pro-Russian allies.[^cite_note-\:8-107] Following recovery from his wounds, he was twice been re-elected, but in December 2020 died of COVID-19 related complications.[^cite_note-3156284kharkivITwaKmWa-112][^cite_note-246371KeysKernesKmWa-113] Kernes was succeeded as mayor by [Ihor Terekhov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ihor_Terekhov) of the "[Kernes Bloc — Successful Kharkiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernes_Bloc_%E2%80%94_Successful_Kharkiv)".[^cite_note-7313698KharkivSnap-5][^cite_note-Kharkiv7308397Terekhov-6]

Relatively peaceful demonstrations continued to be held, with "pro-Russian" rallies gradually diminishing and "pro-Ukrainian unity" demonstrations growing in numbers.[^cite_note-OSCE624-114][^cite_note-OSCE257-115][^cite_note-Status_quo_17814-116] On 28 September 2014, activists dismantled Ukraine's largest monument to Lenin at a pro-Ukrainian rally in the central square.[^cite_note-117] Polls conducted from September to December 2014 found little support in Kharkiv for joining Russia.[^cite_note-Navalny140923-118][^cite_note-DT150103-119]

From early November until mid-December, Kharkiv was struck by seven non-lethal bomb blasts. Targets of these attacks included a rock pub known for raising money for Ukrainian forces, a hospital for Ukrainian forces, a military recruiting center, and a [National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Guard_of_Ukraine) base.[^cite_note-120] According to [SBU](709222.md) investigator Vasyliy Vovk, [Russian covert forces](614404.md) were behind the attacks, and had intended to destabilize the otherwise calm city of Kharkiv.[^cite_note-121] Attacks continued into 2015, including a bombing that killed four people during a march commemorating the [Euromaidan victims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_people_killed_during_the_2014_Ukrainian_revolution).[^cite_note-separatistarrests25324984-99]

After the Euromaidan events and Russian [actions in the Crimea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annexation_of_Crimea_by_the_Russian_Federation) and [Donbas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_Donbas_\(2014%E2%80%932022\)) ruptured relations with Moscow, the Kharkiv region experienced a sharp fall in output and employment. Once a hub of cross border trade, Kharkiv was turned into a border fortress. A reorientation to new international markets, increased defense contracts (after Kyiv, the region contains the second-largest number of military-related enterprises) and export growth in the economy's services sector helped fuel a recovery, but people's incomes did not return to pre-2014 levels.[^cite_note-\:02-122]

By 2018 Kharkiv officially has the lowest unemployment rate in Ukraine, 6 percent. But in part this reflected labor shortages caused by the steady outflow of young and skilled workers to Poland and other European countries.[^cite_note-\:02-122]

Until 18 July 2020, Kharkiv was incorporated as a [city of oblast significance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_regional_significance_\(Ukraine\)) and served as the administrative center of Kharkiv Raion though it did not belong to the raion. In July 2020, as part of the administrative reform of Ukraine, which reduced the number of raions of Kharkiv Oblast to seven, the city of Kharkiv was merged into Kharkiv Raion.[^cite_note-123][^cite_note-124]

#### 2022 Russian invasion

Main articles: [Battle of Kharkiv (2022)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kharkiv_\(2022\)) and [Kharkiv strikes (2022–present)](74401306.md)

During the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md), Kharkiv [was the site of heavy fighting between the Ukrainian and Russian forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kharkiv_\(2022\)).[^cite_note-125] On 27 February, the governor of Kharkiv Oblast [Oleh Syniehubov](68861798.md) claimed that Russian troops were repelled from Kharkiv.[^cite_note-126]

According to a 28 February 2022, report from Agroportal 24h, the [Kharkiv Tractor Plant (KhTZ)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Tractor_Plant), in the south east of the city, was destroyed and "engulfed in fire" by "massive shelling" from Russian forces.[^cite_note-\:4-127] Video purported to record explosions and fire at the plant on 25 and 27 February 2022.[^cite_note-128][^cite_note-129] UNESCO has confirmed that in the first three weeks of bombardment the city experienced the loss or damage of at least 27 major historical buildings.[^cite_note-130]

On 4 March 2022, Human Rights Watch reported that on the fourth day of the [invasion of Ukraine by the Russian Federation](79400268.md), 28 February 2022, Federation forces used cluster munitions in the [KhTZ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrialnyi_District,_Kharkiv), the [Saltivskyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saltivskyi_District) and [Shevchenkivskyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shevchenkivskyi_District,_Kharkiv) districts of the city. The rights group — which noted the "inherently indiscriminate nature of cluster munitions and their foreseeable effects on civilians" — based its assessment on interviews and an analysis of 40 videos and photographs.[^cite_note-131] In March 2022, during the [Battle of Kharkiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kharkiv_\(2022\)), the city was designated as a [Hero City of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hero_City_of_Ukraine).[^cite_note-132]

In May 2022, Ukrainian forces began a counter-offensive to drive Russian forces away from the city and towards the international border. By 12 May, the United Kingdom Ministry of Defence reported that Russia had withdrawn units from the Kharkiv area.[^cite_note-fox-133] Russian artillery and rockets remain within range of the city, and it [continues to suffer shelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombing_of_Kharkiv_\(2022%E2%80%93present\))[^cite_note-134] and missile strikes.[^cite_note-135][^cite_note-136]

Main article: [Northern Kharkiv Oblast front of the Russo-Ukrainian war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Kharkiv_Oblast_front_of_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war)

In May 2024, after two weeks intensive fighting, and the loss of a number of border villages, Ukrainian forces halted a renewed Russian advance toward Kharkiv. The Ukrainian defense was assisted by American-supplied [HIMARS missiles](147011.md), and by US permission to fire these across the border at military targets within Russian territory.[^cite_note-137] The Russians retaliated with [missile strikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/25_May_2024_Kharkiv_missile_strikes), including a [glide-bomb](233100.md) attack that hit and destroyed an [Epicentr K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicentr_K) hypermarket killing 18 civilians.[^cite_note-138]

In February 2026, the city mayor, [Ihor Terekov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ihor_Terekhov), reported a quarter of the city had been destroyed, including 4,700 apartment buildings and 3,500 houses; that energy infrastructure was in a constant state of repair; and that "much of public life has gone underground".[^cite_note-139]

## Geography



The Lopan-Kharkiv river spur

Kharkiv is located at the banks of the [Kharkiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_River), [Lopan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lopan_River), and [Udy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Udy_River) rivers, where they flow into the [Siverskyi Donets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donets) watershed in the north-eastern region of Ukraine.

Historically, Kharkiv lies in the [Sloboda Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloboda_Ukraine) region (*Slobozhanshchyna* also known as *Slobidshchyna*) in Ukraine, in which it is considered to be the main city.

The approximate dimensions of city of Kharkiv are: from the North to the South — 24.3 km; from the West to the East — 25.2 km.

Based on Kharkiv's topography, the city can be conditionally divided into four lower districts and four higher districts.

The highest point above sea level, in Piatykhatky, is 202m, and the lowest is Novoselivka in Kharkiv is 94m.

Kharkiv lies in the large valley of rivers of [Kharkiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_River), [Lopan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lopan), [Udy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Udy_\(river\)), and Nemyshlia. This valley lies from the North West to the South East between the Mid Russian highland and Donets lowland. All the rivers interconnect in Kharkiv and flow into the river of [Northern Donets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seversky_Donets). A special system of concrete and metal dams was designed and built by engineers to regulate the water level in the rivers in Kharkiv.

Kharkiv has a large number of green city parks with a long history of more than 100 years with very old oak trees and many flowers. [Central Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Park_\(Kharkiv\)) is Kharkiv's largest public garden. The park has nine areas: children, extreme sports, family entertainment, a medieval area, entertainment center, French park, cable car, sports grounds, retro park. This park was previously named after [Maxim Gorky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxim_Gorky) until June 2023 when it was renamed Central Park for Culture and Recreation.[^cite_note-140]

### Climate

Kharkiv's climate is [humid continental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humid_continental_climate) ([Köppen climate classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) *Dfa*/*Dfb*) with long, cold, snowy winters and warm to hot summers.

The average rainfall totals 519 mm (20 in) per year, with the most in June and July.

## Governance

### Legal status and local government

See also: [List of mayors of Kharkiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mayors_of_Kharkiv)

The [Mayor of Kharkiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_Kharkiv) and the [Kharkiv City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_City_Council) govern all the business and administrative affairs in the City of Kharkiv.

The mayor of Kharkiv has the executive powers; the city council has the administrative powers as far as the government issues are concerned.

The mayor of Kharkiv is elected by direct public election in Kharkiv every four years.

The city council is composed of elected representatives, who approve or reject the initiatives on the budget allocation, tasks priorities and other issues in Kharkiv. The representatives to the city council are elected every four years.

The mayor and city council hold their regular meetings in the City Hall in Kharkiv.

## Religion



The [Annunciation Orthodox Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annunciation_Cathedral,_Kharkiv) is one of the [tallest Orthodox churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_Orthodox_churches) in the world. It was completed on 2 October 1888.

Kharkiv is an important religious center in Eastern Ukraine.

There are many old and new religious buildings, associated with various denominations in Kharkiv. [Dormition Orthodox Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dormition_Cathedral,_Kharkiv) was built in Kharkiv in the 1680s and rebuilt in the 1820s and 1830s.[^cite_note-kharkiv-167] Holy Trinity Orthodox Church was built in Kharkiv in 1758–1764 and rebuilt in 1857–1861.[^cite_note-kharkiv4-168] [Annunciation Orthodox Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annunciation_Cathedral,_Kharkiv), one of the [tallest Orthodox churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_Orthodox_churches) in the world, was completed in Kharkiv on 2 October 1888.[^cite_note-kharkiv3-169]

Recently built churches include St. Valentine's Orthodox Church and St. Tamara's Orthodox Church.[^cite_note-kharkiv5-170][^cite_note-kharkiv6-171]

Kharkiv's Jewish population is estimated to be around 8,000 people.[^cite_note-jewishkharkov-172] It is served by the old [Kharkiv Choral Synagogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Choral_Synagogue), which was fully renovated in Kharkiv in 1991–2016.

There are two mosques including the [Kharkiv Cathedral Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Cathedral_Mosque) and one Islamic center in Kharkiv.

## Economy



[Sumska Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumska_Street) is the main thoroughfare of Kharkiv.

The 2016–2020 economic development strategy: "Kharkiv Success Strategy", is created in Kharkiv.[^cite_note-kharkiv8-173][^cite_note-kharkiv9-174][^cite_note-kharkiv10-175] Kharkiv has a diversified service economy, with employment spread across a wide range of professional services, including financial services, manufacturing, tourism, and high technology.

### Industrial corporations



[Kvant-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kvant-2) module – its control system was designed at [Khartron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khartron) in Kharkiv.

During the Soviet era, Kharkiv was the capital of industrial production in Ukraine and a large center of industry and commerce in the [USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union). After the [collapse of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Soviet_Union_\(1985%E2%80%931991\)#Dissolution_of_the_USSR) the largely defense-systems-oriented industrial production of the city decreased significantly. In the early 2000s, the industry started to recover and adapt to market economy needs. The enterprises comprise machine-building, electro-technology, instrument-making, and energy conglomerates.

State-owned industrial giants, such as [Turboatom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turboatom) and [Elektrovazhmash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elektrovazhmash?action=edit\&redlink=1)[^cite_note-176] occupy 17% of the heavy power equipment construction (e.g., turbines) market worldwide. Multipurpose aircraft are produced by the [Antonov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonov) aircraft manufacturing plant. The [Malyshev factory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malyshev_factory) produces not only [armored fighting vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armoured_fighting_vehicle), but also harvesters. [Khartron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khartron)[^cite_note-177] is the leading designer of space and commercial control systems in Ukraine and the former [CIS](36870.md).

### IT industry

As of April 2018, there were 25,000 specialists in IT industry of the Kharkiv region, 76% of them were related to computer programming. Thus, Kharkiv accounted for 14% of all IT specialists in Ukraine, which made it the second largest IT location in the country, right after the capital Kyiv.[^cite_note-178]

At the time, the number of active IT companies in the region was 445, five of them employing more than 601 people. Besides, there were 22 companies employing between 201 and 600 workers. More than half of IT companies located in the Kharkiv region fell into the "extra small" category with less than 20 persons engaged. The list was compiled with 43 medium (81–200 employees) and 105 small companies (21–80).

Due to the comparably narrow market for IT services in Ukraine, the majority of Kharkiv's companies are export oriented with more than 95% of total sales generated overseas in 2017. Overall, the estimated revenue of Kharkiv IT companies was expected to grow from \$800 million in 2018 to \$1.85 billion by 2025. The major markets are North America (65%) and Europe (25%).[^cite_note-179]

### Higher education

The [Vasyl N. Karazin Kharkiv National University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_National_University) is the most prestigious reputable classic university, which was founded due to the efforts by [Vasily Karazin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasily_Karazin) in Kharkiv in 1804–1805.[^cite_note-kharkiv12-187][^cite_note-kyiv-188] On 29 January \[[O.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Style) 17 January] 1805, the Decree on the Opening of the Imperial University in Kharkiv came into force.

The Roentgen Institute opened in 1931. It was a specialist cancer treatment facility with 87 research workers, 20 professors, and specialist medical staff. The facilities included chemical, physiology, and bacteriology experimental treatment laboratories. It produced x-ray apparatus for the whole country.[^cite_note-Socialist_Health_Association-189]

The city has 13 national universities and numerous professional, technical and private higher education institutions, offering its students a wide range of disciplines. These universities include [Kharkiv National University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_National_University) (12,000 students), [National Technical University "KhPI"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Polytechnical_Institute) (20,000 students), [Kharkiv National University of Radioelectronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_National_University_of_Radioelectronics) (12,000 students), [Yaroslav Mudryi National Law University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaroslav_Mudryi_National_Law_University), [Kharkiv National Aerospace University "KhAI"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_National_Aerospace_University_%22KhAI%22), [Kharkiv National University of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_National_University_of_Economics), [Kharkiv National University of Pharmacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Academy_of_Pharmacy?action=edit\&redlink=1), and [Kharkiv National Medical University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_National_Medical_University).

More than 17,000 faculty and research staff are employed in the institutions of higher education in Kharkiv.

### Scientific research

The city has a high concentration of research institutions, which are independent or loosely connected with the universities. Among them are three national science centers: [Kharkiv Institute of Physics and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Institute_of_Physics_and_Technology), Institute of Meteorology, Institute for Experimental and Clinical Veterinary Medicine and 20 national research institutions of the [National Academy of Science of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Science_of_Ukraine), such as the [B Verkin Institute for Low Temperature Physics and Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B_Verkin_Institute_for_Low_Temperature_Physics_and_Engineering), [Institute for Problems of Cryobiology and Cryomedicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Problems_of_Cryobiology_and_Cryomedicine), State Scientific Institution "Institute for Single Crystals", Usikov Institute of Radiophysics and Electronics (IRE), Institute of Radio Astronomy (IRA), and others. A total number of 26,000 scientists are working in research and development.

A number of world-renowned scientific schools appeared in Kharkiv, such as the [theoretical physics school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Theoretical_Physics_School) and the [mathematical school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Mathematical_School).

There is the Kharkiv Scientists House in the city, which was built by A. N. Beketov, architect in Kharkiv in 1900. All the scientists like to meet and discuss various scientific topics at the Kharkiv Scientists House in Kharkiv.[^cite_note-khdu-190]

Since 1993, Kharkiv has been home to the [National Academy of Legal Sciences of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Legal_Sciences_of_Ukraine?action=edit\&redlink=1) (NALS), the country's only state branch academy of sciences located outside the capital city of [Kyiv](585629.md). As the highest state scientific institution in the field of state and law, the academy coordinates fundamental and applied legal research, provides scientific support for legal reforms, and drafts legislation, reinforcing Kharkiv's status as the nation's primary legal and [jurisprudential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisprudence) hub.[^cite_note-191]

## Culture

Kharkiv is one of the main cultural centers in Ukraine. It is home to 20 museums, over 10 theatres  and a number of art galleries. Until 2022, large music and cinema festivals were hosted in Kharkiv almost every year.

### Literature



The Kharkiv Academic Drama Theatre

In the 1930s Kharkiv was referred to as a Literary [Klondike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klondike_Gold_Rush). It was the center for the work of literary figures such as: [Les Kurbas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Kurbas), [Mykola Kulish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mykola_Kulish), [Mykola Khvylovy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mykola_Khvylovy), [Mykola Zerov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mykola_Zerov), [Valerian Pidmohylny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valerian_Pidmohylny), Pavlo Filipovych, Marko Voronny, Oleksa Slisarenko. Over 100 of these writers were repressed during the Stalinist purges of the 1930s. This tragic event in Ukrainian history is called the "Executed Renaissance" (Rozstrilene vidrodzhennia). Today, a literary museum located on Frunze Street marks their work and achievements.

Today, Kharkiv is often referred to as the "capital city" of Ukrainian science fiction and fantasy.[^cite_note-204][^cite_note-205] It is home to a number of popular writers, such as [H. L. Oldie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._L._Oldie), [Alexander Zorich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Zorich), [Andrey Dashkov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Dashkov), [Yuri Nikitin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuri_Nikitin_\(author\)) and [Andrey Valentinov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Valentinov); most of them [write in Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language_in_Ukraine) and are popular in both Russia and Ukraine. The annual [science fiction convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction_convention) "Star Bridge" (Звёздный мост) has been held in Kharkiv since 1999.[^cite_note-206]

Several notable writers are active in Kharkiv, including [Serhiy Zhadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serhiy_Zhadan), [Iryna Yevsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iryna_Yevsa), and the science fiction and fantasy authors [Andriy Valentynov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andriy_Valentynov) and [Henry Lion Oldie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._L._Oldie) (the joint pseudonym of [Dmytro Hromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._L._Oldie#Dmitry_Gromov) and [Oleh Ladyzhensky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oleg_Ladyzhensky)). The city also serves as the setting for several novels.[^cite_note-207]

[The Kharkiv orthography of the Ukrainian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_orthography_of_1928) was adopted in 1927 during the All-Ukrainian Orthographic Conference held in the city. It was subsequently approved by the [Ukrainian Academy of Sciences](2281470.md) on 31 March 1929, and by the [Shevchenko Scientific Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shevchenko_Scientific_Society) in [Lviv](49968.md) on 29 May 1929.[^cite_note-208]

Today, this orthography is still widely used by the Ukrainian diaspora in many of its publications, most notably in *[Svoboda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Svoboda_\(newspaper\))*, the oldest continuously published Ukrainian-language newspaper. It is also utilized by certain contemporary Ukrainian writers, literary editors, and linguists.[^cite_note-209]

### Films

Kharkiv played a significant role in the early development of cinematography. [Joseph Timchenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Timchenko), a native of the [Kharkiv Governorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkov_Governorate) and a mechanic at [Kharkiv University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Kharkov_University), designed an early moving-picture camera before the [Lumière brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumi%C3%A8re_brothers). His apparatus was demonstrated on 9 January 1894 at the congress of Russian naturalists in [Moscow](19004.md).[^cite_note-215]

The first motion pictures produced within the [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire) by one of its citizens were filmed in Kharkiv by the prominent local photographer [Alfred Fedetsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Fedetsky). His debut film, *The Solemn Transfer of the Miraculous Ozeryanka Icon from the Kuryazh Monastery to Kharkiv*, was shot on Ekaterinoslavskaya Street (now Poltavskyi Shlyakh) on 30 September 1896. Fedetsky also directed the empire's second and third films later that year: *Trick Riding by Cossacks of the First Orenburg Regiment* at the Kharkiv racetrack on 15 October, and *Inspection of the Kharkiv Railway Station at the Moment of Departure of a Train with Officials on the Platform* around 4 November.[^cite_note-216]

Between 1896 and 2002, more than one thousand documentary chronicles and short newsreels were filmed in Kharkiv.

#### Kharkiv Zoo

Main article: [Kharkiv Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Zoo)

[Kharkiv Zoological Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Zoo) (commonly known as Kharkiv Zoo) is a state-owned zoo in Kharkiv. It is the oldest zoo in Ukraine, established in 1896 and opened to visitors in 1903. It is located in the city's Nahorny district, within the Taras Shevchenko Garden.[^cite_note-245]

In 1983, the zoo was designated a [nature conservation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_conservation) site of republican significance within the [Ukrainian SSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic). In 1992, in accordance with the Law of Ukraine "On the Nature Reserve Fund of Ukraine," the institution received the status of a zoological park of national importance.[^cite_note-246]

The zoo houses representatives of rare and endangered species listed in the [Red Data Book of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Data_Book_of_Ukraine) and the [IUCN Red List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IUCN_Red_List) (including the [American alligator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_alligator), [Cuban crocodile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_crocodile), [Asian elephant](379035.md), [Przewalski's horse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Przewalski's_horse), [chimpanzee](7844.md), [jaguar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaguar), [Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal_tiger) and [Sumatran tigers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumatran_tiger), and [Burmese python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burmese_python), among others). Appropriate living conditions that approximate their natural habitats have been created for these animals, along with specialized dietary plans; several rare species successfully breed in captivity.[^cite_note-247]

## Language

## Transport



Historical building of [Kharkiv Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_International_Airport)

The city of Kharkiv has been one of the largest transportation centers in Ukraine, connected to numerous other cities of the world by air, rail and road traffic. Kharkiv is one of four Ukrainian cities with a subway system.[^cite_note-251]

[Kharkiv's Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Metro) is the city's rapid transit system operating since 1975. It includes three different lines with 30 stations in total.[^cite_note-252][^cite_note-PPoPmK-253] Trolleybuses, [trams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Kharkiv) (which celebrated its 100-year anniversary of service in 2006), and *[marshrutkas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshrutka)* (private minibuses) are also important means of transportation in the city.



[Kharkiv railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_railway_station)

The first railway connection of Kharkiv was opened in 1869. The first train to arrive in Kharkiv came from the north on 22 May 1869, and on 6 June 1869, traffic was opened on the [Kursk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kursk)–Kharkiv–[Azov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azov) line. Kharkiv's passenger railway station was reconstructed and expanded in 1901, to be later destroyed in the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War). A new [Kharkiv railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_railway_station) was built in 1952.[^cite_note-uz-254]

Kharkiv is connected with all main cities in Ukraine and abroad by regular railway services. Regional trains known as [elektrychkas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elektrichka) connect Kharkiv with nearby towns and villages.

&#x20;Until the closure of Ukrainian airspace to civilian flights due to the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md),[^cite_note-255] Kharkiv was served by [Kharkiv International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_International_Airport). Charter flights are also available. The [Kharkiv North Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_North_Airport) was an industrial airfield serving the [Antonov aircraft company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonov).

## Awards and honors

During the Soviet period, Kharkiv was awarded the [Order of Lenin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Lenin) by a decree of the [Presidium of the Supreme Soviet of the USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidium_of_the_Supreme_Soviet_of_the_Soviet_Union) on 4 December 1970, and the [Order of the October Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_October_Revolution) on 22 August 1983.[^cite_note-271]

Kharkiv has received the full set of awards from the [Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe (PACE)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_Assembly_of_the_Council_of_Europe), including the European Diploma, the Flag of Honour, the Plaque of Honour, and the Europe Prize.[^cite_note-272]

On 6 March 2022, by a decree of the [President of Ukraine](20688479.md), Kharkiv was awarded the honorary title "[Hero City of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hero_City_of_Ukraine)" to recognize the heroism, mass bravery, and resilience shown by its citizens in defending the city during the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](70149799.md).[^cite_note-273]
