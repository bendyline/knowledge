The **Old City of Jerusalem** ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): المدينة القديمة, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Arabic): *al-Madīna al-Qadīma*; [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language): הָעִיר הָעַתִּיקָה, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Hebrew): *Ha'ír Ha'atiká*) is a 0.9-square-kilometre (0.35 sq mi) [walled area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walls_of_Jerusalem)[^cite_note-3] in [East Jerusalem](343508.md).

In a tradition that may have begun with an [1840s British map of the city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1840%E2%80%9341_Royal_Engineers_maps_of_Palestine,_Lebanon_and_Syria), the Old City is divided into four uneven quarters: the [Muslim Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_Quarter_\(Jerusalem\)), the [Christian Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Quarter), the [Armenian Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Quarter), and the [Jewish Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Quarter_\(Jerusalem\)).[^cite_note-Wager-4] A fifth area is the [Al-Aqsa Mosque Compound or Al-Masjid Al-Aqsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Aqsa) ([Temple Mount hill](65719.md)) and home to the monumental [Dome of the Rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dome_of_the_Rock), the [Qibli (Al-Aqsa) Mosque](65759.md), and was once the site of [the Jewish Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_in_Jerusalem).[^cite_note-Teller-5]

The Old City's [modern walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walls_of_Jerusalem) and city gates were built by the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md) from 1535 to 1542 under [Suleiman the Magnificent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suleiman_the_Magnificent). The Old City is home to several sites of key importance and holiness to the three major [Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religions): the Temple Mount area and the [Western Wall](75225.md) for [Judaism](15624.md), the [Church of the Holy Sepulchre](7810.md) for [Christianity](5211.md), and the [Al-Aqsa Mosque Compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Aqsa) including its Dome of the Rock and Qibli Mosque for [Islam](6037917.md). The Old City, along with its walls, was added to the [World Heritage Site](44940.md) list of [UNESCO](21786641.md) in 1981.

In spite of its name, the Old City of Jerusalem's current layout is different from that of ancient times. Most archeologists believe that the [City of David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_David_\(archaeological_site\)), an [archaeological site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological_site) on a rocky spur south of the Temple Mount, was the original settlement core of Jerusalem during the [Bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age) and [Iron Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age).[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9][^cite_note-\:1-10] At times, the ancient city spread to the east and north, covering [Mount Zion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Zion) and the Temple Mount. The Old City as defined by the walls of Suleiman is thus shifted a bit northwards compared to earlier periods of the city's history, and smaller than it had been in its peak, during the late [Second Temple period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem_during_the_Second_Temple_Period). The Old City's current layout has been documented in significant detail, notably in [old maps of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartography_of_Jerusalem) over the last 1,500 years.

Until the mid-19th century, the entire city of Jerusalem, with the exception of [David's Tomb complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David's_Tomb), was enclosed within the Old City walls. The [departure from the walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expansion_of_Jerusalem_in_the_19th_century) began in the 19th century, when the city's municipal borders were expanded to include [Arab](2185.md) villages such as [Silwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silwan) and new [Jewish](25955086.md) neighborhoods such as [Mishkenot Sha'ananim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mishkenot_Sha'ananim). The Old City came under [Jordanian control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordanian_annexation_of_the_West_Bank) following the [1948 Arab–Israeli War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_Arab%E2%80%93Israeli_War). During the [1967 Six-Day War](29328.md), Israel occupied East Jerusalem; since then, the entire city has been under Israeli control. Israel unilaterally asserted in its 1980 [Jerusalem Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem_Law) that the whole of Jerusalem was Israel's capital.[^cite_note-basiclaw-11] In international law, [East Jerusalem](4024423.md) is defined as [territory occupied by Israel](2781576.md).

The population of the Old City resides mostly in the Muslim quarter. In 2007, the total population was 36,965; there were 27,500 Muslims (growing to over 30,000 by 2013); 5,681 non-Armenian Christians, 790 Armenians (who decreased in number to about 500 by 2013); and 3,089 Jews (with almost 3,000 plus some 1,500 [yeshiva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeshiva) students by 2013).[^cite_note-urbanfabric-12][^cite_note-Bracha_Slae-13][^cite_note-Beltran-14] By 2020, Old City's population dropped to 31,120.[^cite_note-\:0-15]

## Political status

See also: [Status of Jerusalem](4024423.md)

During the [1948 Arab–Israeli War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_Arab%E2%80%93Israeli_War), the Old City was [captured by Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordanian_annexation_of_the_West_Bank) and all its Jewish residents were evicted. During the [Six-Day War](29328.md) in 1967, which saw hand-to-hand fighting on the [Temple Mount](65719.md), Israeli forces captured the Old City along with the rest of [East Jerusalem](343508.md), subsequently annexing them as Israeli territory and reuniting them with the western part of the city. Today, the Israeli government controls the entire area, which it considers part of its national capital. However, the [Jerusalem Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem_Law) of 1980, which effectively annexed East Jerusalem to Israel, was declared null and void by [United Nations Security Council Resolution 478](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council_Resolution_478). East Jerusalem is now regarded by the international community as part of [occupied](816214.md) Palestinian territory.[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17]

## History

See also: [Timeline of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_Jerusalem)

### Israelite period

Further information: [City of David (archaeological site)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_David_\(archaeological_site\))



[The Broad Wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broad_Wall_\(Jerusalem\)), part of the wall surrounding the city's western hill, built by [Hezekiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hezekiah), king of [Judah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Judah), during the late-8th century BCE

According to the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible), before [King David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_David)'s [conquest of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jebus) in the 11th century BCE the city was home to the [Jebusites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jebusites). The [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible) describes the city as heavily fortified with a strong [city wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_wall), a fact confirmed by archaeology. The Bible names the city ruled by King David as the [City of David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_David_\(historic\)), in [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language) Ir David, which was identified southeast of the Old City walls, outside the [Dung Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dung_Gate). In the Bible, David's son, [King Solomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Solomon), extended the city walls to include the Temple and Temple Mount. After the partition of the [United Kingdom of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Israel_\(united_monarchy\)), the southern tribes remained in Jerusalem, with the city becoming the capital of the [Kingdom of Judah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Judah).[^cite_note-18]

Jerusalem was largely extended westwards after the [Neo-Assyrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Assyrian) destruction of the northern [Kingdom of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Israel_\(Samaria\)) and the resulting influx of refugees. King [Hezekiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hezekiah) had been preparing for an Assyrian invasion by fortifying the walls of the capital, building towers, and constructing a tunnel to bring fresh water to the city from a spring outside its walls.[^cite_note-Spar-19] He made at least two major preparations that would help Jerusalem to resist conquest: the construction of the [Siloam Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siloam_Tunnel), and construction of the [Broad Wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broad_Wall_\(Jerusalem\)).

The First Temple period ended around 586 BCE, as [Nebuchadnezzar's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebuchadnezzar_II) [Neo-Babylonian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Babylonian_Empire) [conquered Judah and Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_\(587_BC\)), and laid waste to [Solomon's Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon's_Temple) and the city.[^cite_note-BU22-20]

### Second Temple period

Main article: [Jerusalem during the Second Temple Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem_during_the_Second_Temple_Period)

In 538 BCE, the [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire) [King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_kings_of_Persia) [Cyrus the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_the_Great) invited the [Jews of Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_captivity) to [return to Judah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_to_Zion) to rebuild the Temple.[^cite_note-21] Construction of the [Second Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Temple_of_Jerusalem) was completed in 516 BCE, during the reign of [Darius the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_I_of_Persia), 70 years after the destruction of the First Temple.[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23]

In about 440 BCE, the city was rebuilt on a smaller scale during the Persian period, when, according to the Bible, [Nehemiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nehemiah) led the Jews who returned from the Babylonian Exile. An additional, so-called Second Wall, was built by King [Herod the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_the_Great), who also [expanded the Temple Mount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod's_Temple) and rebuilt the Temple. In 41–44 CE, [Agrippa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agrippa_I), king of [Judea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judea), started building the so-called "Third Wall" around the northern suburbs. The entire city was totally [destroyed by the Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destruction_of_jerusalem) in 70 CE.[^cite_note-24]



Stones from the [Western Wall](75225.md) of the [Temple Mount](65719.md) thrown during the Roman [Siege of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_\(70_CE\)) in 70 CE

### Late Roman, Byzantine, and Early Muslim periods

Further information: [Aelia Capitolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aelia_Capitolina), [History of Jerusalem during the Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Jerusalem_during_the_Middle_Ages), and [History of Jerusalem during the Early Muslim period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Jerusalem_during_the_Early_Muslim_period)

The northern part of the city was rebuilt by the Emperor [Hadrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadrian) around 130, under the name Aelia Capitolina. In the [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine) period Jerusalem was extended southwards and again enclosed by city walls.

[Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim) occupied Byzantine Jerusalem in the 7th century (637 CE) under the second caliph, \`[Umar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umar) Ibn al-Khattab who annexed it to the Islamic [Arab Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_Caliphate). He granted its inhabitants an assurance treaty. After the siege of Jerusalem, [Sophronius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophronius_of_Jerusalem) welcomed \`Umar, allegedly because, according to biblical prophecies known to the [Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Church) in Jerusalem, "a poor, but just and powerful man" would rise to be a protector and ally to the [Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_\(word\)) of Jerusalem. Sophronius believed that \`Umar, a great warrior who led an austere life, was a fulfillment of this prophecy. In the account by the [Patriarch of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_of_Alexandria), [Eutychius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_Eutychius_of_Alexandria), it is said that \`Umar paid a visit to the [Church of the Holy Sepulchre](7810.md) and sat in its courtyard. When the time for prayer arrived, however, he left the church and prayed outside the compound, in order to avoid having future generations of Muslims use his prayer there as a pretext for converting the church into a mosque. Eutychius adds that \`Umar also wrote a decree which he handed to the Patriarch, in which he prohibited Muslims gathering in prayer at the site.[^cite_note-25] Caliph Umar and the subsequent Umayyad caliphs maintained generally cordial relations with the city's Christian communities and permitted Jews to resume annual pilgrimages to Jerusalem.[^cite_note-26]



The [Dome of the Rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dome_of_the_Rock) (c. 685–692) built during the reign of Caliph [Abd al-Malik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Malik_ibn_Marwan)&#x20;

&#x20;Because of the religious significance of [Al-Masjid Al-Aqsa (Al-Aqsa Mosque Compound)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Aqsa) in Islam, Caliph Umar ordered the construction of the first [congregational mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congregational_mosque) at the southern end of the mosque compound following the Muslim conquest of Jerusalem. This congregational mosque later became known as the [Qibli Mosque](65759.md), also referred to as Al-Aqsa Mosque. During the Umayyad period, Jerusalem acquired greater political importance, prompting the construction of numerous religious, administrative, and residential structures. The first Umayyad caliph, [Muawiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu'awiya_I) (r. 661–680), chose Jerusalem as the site of his proclamation as caliph, reportedly at Al-Aqsa, and is traditionally credited with repairing or rebuilding the congregational Qibli Mosque. During the reign of Caliph [Abd al-Malik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Malik_ibn_Marwan) (r. 685–705), the [Dome of the Rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dome_of_the_Rock) was constructed in the form that largely survives today. Abd al-Malik also initiated the second major reconstruction of the congregational Qibli (Al-Aqsa) Mosque, a project that was completed during the reign of his son and successor, Caliph [al-Walid I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Walid_I) (r. 705–715).[^cite_note-27]



Remains of Late Roman-period gate under [Damascus Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus_Gate)

### Crusader & Ayyubid periods

Further information: [History of Jerusalem during the Kingdom of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Jerusalem_during_the_Kingdom_of_Jerusalem)

In 1099, Jerusalem was captured by the [Western Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Christian) army of the [First Crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Crusade) and it remained in their hands until recaptured by the [Arab Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Muslims), led by [Saladin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saladin), on October 2, 1187. He summoned the Jews and permitted them to resettle in the city. In 1219, the walls of the city were razed by [Sultan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultan_of_Damascus) [Al-Mu'azzam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mu'azzam_Isa) of [Damascus](8914.md); in 1229, by treaty with [Egypt](8087628.md), Jerusalem came into the hands of [Frederick II of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_II,_Holy_Roman_Emperor). In 1239 he began to rebuild the walls, but they were demolished again by [Da'ud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Da'ud), the emir of [Kerak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerak). In 1243, Jerusalem came again under the control of the Christians, and the walls were repaired. The [Khwarazmian Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khwarazmian_army_between_1231_and_1246) took the city in 1244 and Sultan Malik al-Muazzam razed the walls, rendering it again defenseless and dealing a heavy blow to the city's status.

### Mamluk & Ottoman periods

&#x20;During the Mamluk period (1250–1517), numerous structures were erected in the city, especially within and around the Al-Aqsa Mosque compound, including madrasas, sabils, and other religious and charitable buildings, particularly along its western and northern walls.[^cite_note-28] The Al-Aqsa Mosque Compound underwent major restoration campaigns under Sultan al-Nasir Muhammad and later under Sultan [Qaitbay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaitbay), both of whom sponsored extensive renovations. Most of the mosque's existing minarets also date from this period.&#x20;

The current walls of the Old City were built in 1535–42 by the Ottoman Turkish sultan Suleiman the Magnificent. The walls stretch for approximately 4.5 km (2.8 miles), and rise to a height of between 5 and 15 metres (16.4–49 ft), with a thickness of 3 metres (10 feet) at the base of the wall.[^cite_note-Wager-4] Altogether, the Old City walls contain 35 towers, of which 15 are concentrated in the more exposed northern wall.[^cite_note-Wager-4] Suleiman's wall had six gates, to which a seventh, the New Gate, was added in 1887; several other, older gates, have been walled up over the centuries. The Golden Gate was at first rebuilt and left open by Suleiman's architects, only to be walled up a short while later. The New Gate was opened in the wall surrounding the Christian Quarter during the 19th century. Two secondary gates were reopened in recent times on the southeastern side of the city walls as a result of archaeological work.

## UNESCO status

In 1980, [Jordan](7515964.md) proposed that the Old City be listed as a [UNESCO](21786641.md) [World Heritage Site](44940.md).[^cite_note-30] It was added to the List in 1981.[^cite_note-31] In 1982, Jordan requested that it be added to the [List of World Heritage in Danger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_in_Danger). The [United States](3434750.md) government opposed the request, noting that the Jordanian government had no standing to make such a nomination and that the consent of the Israeli government would be required since it effectively controlled Jerusalem.[^cite_note-32] In 2011, UNESCO issued a statement reiterating its view that [East Jerusalem](343508.md) is "part of the occupied [Palestinian territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestinian_territories), and that the status of [Jerusalem](16043.md) must be resolved in permanent status negotiations."[^cite_note-unesco-20110715-33]

## Archaeology

### Israelite period



The Israelite Tower dating back to the Iron Age

Among the Israelite period finds in the Old City are two portions of the 8th and 7th century BCE city walls, in the area of the [Israelite Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israelite_Tower), probably including parts of a gate where numerous projectiles were found, attesting to the Babylonian sack of Jerusalem in 586 BCE.[^cite_note-VaughnKill-34][^cite_note-Shelby2-35][^cite_note-36] Another part of the late 8th-century BCE fortification discovered was dubbed the ["broad wall"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broad_Wall_\(Jerusalem\)), after the way it was described in the *[Book of Nehemiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Nehemiah)*, built to defend Jerusalem against the [Assyrian siege of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_siege_of_Jerusalem) of 701 BCE.[^cite_note-Shelby2-35][^cite_note-VaughnKill-34][^cite_note-37]

### Hellenistic period



Possible remains of the [Acra fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acra_\(fortress\))

In 2015, archaeologists uncovered the remnants of an impressive fort, built by Greeks in the center of old Jerusalem. It is believed that it is the remnants of the [Acra fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acra_\(fortress\)). The team also found coins that date from the time of [Antiochus IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiochus_IV) to the time of [Antiochus VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiochus_VII). In addition, they found Greek arrowheads, slingshots, ballistic stones and [amphorae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphorae).[^cite_note-38]

In 2018, archaeologists discovered a 4-centimeter-long [filigree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filigree) gold earring with a ram's head around 200 meters south of the [Temple Mount](65719.md). The [Israel Antiquities Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_Antiquities_Authority) said it was consistent with jewelry from the early [Hellenistic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period) (3rd or early 2nd century BCE). Adding that it was the first time somebody finds a golden earring from the Hellenistic times in Jerusalem.[^cite_note-39]

### Herodian period

Many structures dated to the Herodian period were discovered in the Jewish Quarter during archaeological excavations carried out between 1967 and 1983. Among them was unearthed a palatial mansion from the Herodian period,[^cite_note-40] believed to be the residence of [Annas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annas) the [High Priest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Priest_of_Israel).[^cite_note-Carta-41] In its vicinity, a depiction of the [Temple menorah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menorah_\(Temple\)) was discovered, carved while its model still stood in the Temple, engraved in a plastered wall.[^cite_note-Carta-41] The palace has been destroyed during the final days of the [Roman siege of 70 CE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_\(70_CE\)), suffering the same fate as the so-called [Burnt House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnt_House), a building belonging to the Kathros priestly family, which was found nearby.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-Shelby2-35]



Fresco showing signs of burning, dating to the time of the destruction of the Second Temple, Wohl Archaeological Museum

In 1968, the [Trumpeting Place inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trumpeting_Place_inscription) was found at the southwest corner of Temple Mount, and is believed to mark the site where the [priests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohen) used to declare the advent of [Shabbat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shabbat) and other Jewish holidays.[^cite_note-43]

### Byzantine period

In the 1970s, while excavating the remains of the [Nea Church (the New Church of the Theotokos)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Church_of_the_Theotokos), a [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) inscription was found. It reads: "This work too was donated by our most pious Emperor [Flavius Justinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian_I), through the provision and care of Constantine, most saintly priest and abbot, in the 13th year of the [indiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiction)."[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-timesofisrael.com-45] A second dedicatory inscription bearing the names of Emperor Justinian and of the same abbot of the Nea Church was discovered in 2017 among the ruins of a pilgrim hostel about a kilometre north of Damascus Gate, which proves the importance of the Nea complex at the time.[^cite_note-timesofisrael.com-45][^cite_note-46]

### Umayyad period



Entrance leading to the Umayyad Palace Garden, with the dome of the [Qibli (Al-Aqsa) Mosque](65759.md) visible in the background, 2018&#x20;

During a ten-year archaeological excavation of the southern area adjacent to the Al-Aqsa Mosque Compound, archaeologists uncovered a complex of Umayyad palaces. The buildings share a characteristic quadrangular plan centered on an open courtyard surrounded by four [riwaqs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riwaq_\(arcade\)) (arcades). One of the principal structures has been identified as the *Dar al-Imara* (Governor's Palace), the administrative residence of the provincial governor. The palace complex foundations were found beneath the 16th-century city walls reconstructed by Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent, indicating that Jerusalem's early Islamic city walls extended beyond the line of the present-day Ottoman-era city walls.[^cite_note-47]

## Quarters



Map of the Old City's modern quarters

The Old City is currently divided into four uneven quarters: the Muslim Quarter, the Christian Quarter, the Armenian Quarter and the Jewish Quarter. Matthew Teller writes that this four-quarter convention may have originated in the [1841 British Royal Engineers map of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1840%E2%80%9341_Royal_Engineers_maps_of_Palestine,_Lebanon_and_Syria),[^cite_note-Teller-5] or at least Reverend [George Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Williams_\(priest\))' subsequent labelling of it.[^cite_note-Teller2-48] A fifth quarter, the [Mughrabi Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughrabi_Quarter), was demolished following the [Six-Day War](29328.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGorenberg200742-49]

This 19th-century cartographic partition into four quarters represented the historical development of the city that had previously been divided into many more *harat* ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): حارَة, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Arabic): *Hārat*: "quarters", "neighborhoods", "districts" or "areas", see [wikt\:حارة](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%D8%AD%D8%A7%D8%B1%D8%A9));[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnon19925–7-50] the Christian and Jewish areas of the city had grown considerably over the preceding centuries.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnon199212-51][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnon199216-52][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnon199217-53]

Despite the names, there was no governing principle of ethnic segregation: 30 percent of the houses in the Muslim quarter were rented out to Jews, and 70 percent of the Armenian quarter.[^cite_note-54] The [1905 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographic_history_of_Palestine_\(region\)) by the Ottoman authorities found mixed populations in the quarters of Jerusalem, for instance, it counted 388 Jewish family heads and 383 Muslim family heads living on [al-Wad Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Wad_Street), according to historian [Vincent Lemire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_Lemire), this reflects a broader pattern of lack of ethnoreligious homogeniety in the quarters of the old city at the time.[^cite_note-lemire2022-55]<sup>: 204–205 </sup>[^cite_note-lemire2017-56]<sup>: 37 </sup>

### Muslim Quarter

Main article: [Muslim Quarter (Jerusalem)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_Quarter_\(Jerusalem\))



[Cotton Merchants' Market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_Merchants'_Market)

The [Muslim Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_Quarter_\(Jerusalem\)) ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): حارَة المُسلِمين, Hārat al-Muslimīn) is the largest and most populous of the four quarters and is situated in the northeastern corner of the Old City, extending from the Lions' Gate in the east, along the northern wall of the [Temple Mount](65719.md) in the south, to the [Western Wall](75225.md) – [Damascus Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus_Gate) route in the west. During the British Mandate, Sir [Ronald Storrs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Storrs) embarked on a project to rehabilitate the [Cotton Merchants' Market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_Merchants'_Market), which was badly neglected under the Turks. He describes it as a public latrine with piles of debris up to five feet high. With the help of the [Pro-Jerusalem Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro-Jerusalem_Society), vaults, roofing and walls were restored, and looms were brought in to provide employment.[^cite_note-58]

Like the other three quarters of the Old City, until the [riots of 1929](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1929_Palestine_riots) the Muslim quarter had a mixed population of Muslims, Christians, and also Jews.[^cite_note-59] Today, there are "many Israeli settler homes" and "several [yeshivas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeshiva)", including [Yeshivat Ateret Yerushalayim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ateret_Cohanim#Yeshivat_Ateret_Yerushalayim), in the Muslim Quarter.[^cite_note-urbanfabric-12] Its population was 21,850 in 2020.[^cite_note-\:0-15]

### Christian Quarter

Main article: [Christian Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Quarter)



[Church of the Holy Sepulchre](7810.md)

The [Christian Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Quarter) ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): حارة النصارى, Ḩārat an-Naşāra) is situated in the northwestern corner of the Old City, extending from the New Gate in the north, along the western wall of the Old City as far as the [Jaffa Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaffa_Gate), along the Jaffa Gate – [Western Wall](75225.md) route in the south, bordering the Jewish and Armenian Quarters, as far as the [Damascus Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gates_in_Jerusalem's_Old_City_Walls) in the east, where it borders the [Muslim Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_Quarter_\(Jerusalem\)). The quarter contains the [Church of the Holy Sepulchre](7810.md), viewed by many as Christianity's holiest place. Its population was 3,870 in 2020.[^cite_note-\:0-15]

### Armenian Quarter

Main article: [Armenian Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Quarter)



Armenian flag in the Armenian Quarter

The [Armenian Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Quarter) ([Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_language): Հայկական Թաղամաս, Haygagan T'aġamas, [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): حارة الأرمن, Ḩārat al-Arman) is the smallest of the four quarters of the Old City. Although the [Armenians](387816.md) are Christian, the Armenian Quarter is distinct from the [Christian Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Quarter). Despite the small size and population of this quarter, the Armenians and their [Patriarchate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarchate) remain staunchly independent and form a vigorous presence in the Old City. After the 1948 Arab–Israeli War, the four quarters of the city came under [Jordanian](7515964.md) control. According to a 2007 study, the quarter housed 2,424 people (6.55% of Old City's total).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKhamaisiBrooksMargalitNasrallah200922,_71-60] Its population was 2,160 in 2020.[^cite_note-\:0-15]

### Jewish Quarter

Main article: [Jewish Quarter (Jerusalem)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Quarter_\(Jerusalem\))



The [Western Wall](75225.md) and [Western Wall Plaza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Wall_Plaza)

The [Jewish Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Quarter_\(Jerusalem\)) ([Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language): הרובע היהודי, *HaRova HaYehudi*, known colloquially to residents as *HaRova*, [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): حارة اليهود, *Ḩārat al-Yahūd*) lies in the southeastern sector of the walled city, and stretches from the Zion Gate in the south, bordering the [Armenian Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Quarter) on the west, along the [Cardo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cardo_\(Jerusalem\)) to Chain Street in the north and extends east to the [Western Wall](75225.md) and the [Temple Mount](65719.md). The quarter has a rich history, with several long periods of [Jewish](15624.md) presence covering much of the time since the eighth century BCE.[^cite_note-61][^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65] In 1948, its population of about 2,000 Jews was besieged, and forced to leave en masse. The quarter was completely bombed during the [Battle for Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_for_Jerusalem).[^cite_note-Bracha_Slae-13]



Hurva Synagogue

The Jewish quarter remained under [Jordanian](7515964.md) control until its recapture by [Israeli paratroopers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/35th_Paratroopers_Brigade_\(Israel\)) in the [Six-Day War](29328.md) of 1967. A few days later, Israeli authorities ordered the demolition of the adjacent [Moroccan Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moroccan_Quarter), forcibly relocating all of its inhabitants, in order to facilitate public access to the Western Wall. 195 properties – synagogues, yeshivas, and apartments – were registered as Jewish and fell under the control of Jordan's [Custodian of Enemy Property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Custodian_of_Enemy_Property).[^cite_note-MD-66] Most were occupied by Palestinian refugees expelled by Israeli forces from West Jerusalem and its contiguous villages until [UNRWA](32270.md) and Jordan constructed the [Shuafat Refugee Camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shu'fat_Camp), where many were shifted, leaving most of the properties empty of inhabitants.[^cite_note-MD-66]

In 1968, after the Six Day War, Israel confiscated 12%, including the Jewish quarter and contiguous areas, of the Old City for public use. Some 80% of this confiscated infrastructure consisted of properties not owned by Jews.[^cite_note-MD-66] After reconstruction the parts of the quarter destroyed prior to 1967, these properties were then offered for sale exclusively to the Israeli and Jewish public.[^cite_note-MD-66] The prior owners mostly refused compensation, often because their properties were part of Islamic or family [waqfs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waqf), which cannot be put up for sale.[^cite_note-MD-66] As of 2005, the population stood at 2,348.[^cite_note-67] Many large educational institutions have taken up residence. Before being rebuilt, the quarter was carefully excavated under the supervision of [Hebrew University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_University_of_Jerusalem) archaeologist [Nahman Avigad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nahman_Avigad). The archaeological remains are on display in a series of museums and outdoor parks, which tourists can visit by descending two or three stories beneath the level of the current city.

Its population was 3,240 in 2020.[^cite_note-\:0-15]

### Mughrabi Quarter

Main article: [Mughrabi Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughrabi_Quarter)



The now demolished [Moroccan quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moroccan_Quarter) in 1917

There was previously a small [Mughrabi Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughrabi_Quarter) in the Old City. Within a week of the [Six-Day War](29328.md)'s end, the quarter was largely destroyed in order to give visitors better access to the Western Wall by creating the [Western Wall Plaza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Wall_Plaza). The parts of the quarter that were not destroyed are now part of the Jewish Quarter. Simultaneously with the demolition, a new regulation was set into place by which the only access point for non-Muslims to the Temple Mount is through the [Gate of the Moors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gates_of_the_Temple_Mount#Moors_Gate), which is reached via the so-called [Mughrabi Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughrabi_Bridge).[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69]

## Gates

Main article: [Gates of the Old City of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gates_of_the_Old_City_of_Jerusalem)

During different periods, the city walls followed different outlines and had a varying number of gates. During the era of the [crusader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades) [Kingdom of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Jerusalem) for instance, Jerusalem had four gates, one on each side. The current walls were built by [Suleiman the Magnificent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suleiman_the_Magnificent), who provided them with six gates; several older gates, which had been walled up before the arrival of the Ottomans, were left as they were. As to the previously sealed Golden Gate, Suleiman at first opened and rebuilt it, but then walled it up again as well. The number of operational gates was brought back to seven after the addition of the New Gate in 1887; a smaller one, popularly known as the [Tanners' Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gates_of_the_Old_City_of_Jerusalem#Previous_gates), has been opened for visitors after being discovered and unsealed during excavations in the 1990s. The sealed historic gates comprise four that are at least partially preserved (the double Golden Gate in the eastern wall, and the Single, Triple, and Double Gates in the southern wall), with several other gates discovered by archaeologists of which only traces remain (the Gate of the Essenes on [Mount Zion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Zion), the gate of Herod's royal palace south of the [citadel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_of_David), and the vague remains of what 19th-century explorers identified as the Gate of the Funerals (Bab al-Jana'iz) or of al-Buraq (Bab al-Buraq) south of the Golden Gate[^cite_note-Necipoğlu-70]).

Until 1887, each gate was closed before sunset and opened at sunrise. These gates have been known by [a variety of names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gates_of_the_Old_City_of_Jerusalem#List) used in different historical periods and by different communities.

The ethno-religious partition of the Old City on the nineteenth-century maps reflected a situation rooted in history. Crusader Jerusalem of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, the capital of the Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem, was partitioned among the residential territories of people from different European countries, Oriental Christian communities and knights orders. In 1244 Jerusalem returned to Moslem hands when it became part of the Ayyubid Sultanate of Egypt. The change of government coincided with a devastation of the city by the Central Asian tribe of Khawarizm which all but annihilated the city's population. In 1250 the Mamluks rose to power in Egypt. Under their rule Jerusalem became a magnet to pilgrims from all parts of the Islamic world. People from various regions, towns and tribes settled in it. The parts of the city preferred by the Moslems were those adjoining the north and west sides of the Temple Mount (the other two sides lay outside the city) on which stood their two revered mosques, the Dome of the Rock and al-Agsa Mosque. Christians from different denominations resettled in the north-west of the city, at the vicinity of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. Armenians settled in its south-west, near their Cathedral of St James which had been destroyd by the Khawarizms. Jews settled in Jerusalem, beginning with the second half of the thirteenth century, near the south wall of the city, because the territory there was not settled by any other community and separated from their venerated place, the West (Wailing) Wall, only by a small quarter of North-African Moslems. When the city changed hands again at the beginning of the sixteenth century, falling to the Ottoman Turks, no change in the city's population, and hence in its quarters occurred."
