# Disarmament of Iraqi militias

The **disarmament of Iraqi militias** predominantly involves Shia militias in Iraq, many of which have received long-standing support from Iran through the [Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps](1285472.md). Following the collapse of Iraqi security forces during the [Islamic State offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_State_invasion_of_Iraq) in 2014 and a fatwa issued by [Ali al-Sistani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_al-Sistani), the [Popular Mobilisation Forces](45671374.md) was formed as an umbrella group of Iraqi militias which played a key role in defeating [ISIS](9087364.md), but it became controversial due to allegations of [war crimes](48626.md), political autonomy, and a lack of accountability. Although incorporated into Iraq’s security framework under PMF Commission Law No. 40 in 2016, several militias retained independent command structures and close ideological ties to Iran. A 2025 attempt to further institutionalise the PMF as a permanent, semi-autonomous force failed, amid concerns over sovereignty, leading to renewed pressure for militia disarmament and state control. In 2025, Iraq’s Shia [Coordination Framework](82383522.md) proposed a plan to disarm six Iranian-backed militias in partial response to US demands, with all concerned groups reportedly agreeing except for Kataeb Hezbollah.

## Background

Since the [Islamic Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_Iranian_revolution), Iran has sought to back Shia [Islamist](15012.md) paramilitary organisations across the [Middle East](19323.md).[^cite_note-\:0-1] Through Iran’s Revolutionary Guards and the elite [Quds Force](4028355.md) these militias received arms, training and financial support.[^cite_note-\:0-1] In Iraq the Iranian-backed militias include [Kataib Hezbollah](23626646.md), [Asaib Ahl al Haq](26106154.md), [Harakat Hezbollah al Nujaba](48027962.md), [Badr Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badr_Organization), and [Kataib Sayyad al Shuhada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kata'ib_Sayyid_ul-Shuhada).[^cite_note-\:0-1]

In 2014, Iraqi armed forces collapsed in the face of the battle with ISIS, which conquered one third of Iraq's territory, including its second largest city, [Mosul](200475.md).[^cite_note-\:1-2][^cite_note-\:2-3] Prime Minister [Nouri al-Maliki](4723094.md) gave the order to assemble these militias and others under a single paramilitary umbrella, and Shiite cleric Ali al-Sistani subsequently issued a *[fatwa](11259.md)* calling for volunteers to exercise their duty to fight and defend Iraq against ISIS, after which these groups joined forces under the government paid organisation, the [Popular Mobilisation Forces](45671374.md) (PMF),[^cite_note-\:1-2][^cite_note-\:2-3] which was dominated by the militias loyal to Iran.[^cite_note-4] The PMF eventually succeeded in taking back most of the conquered territories in Iraq,[^cite_note-\:2-3] though according to [Amnesty International](18947898.md) and [Human Rights Watch](46530.md) it was guilty of committing war crimes, and since then has been viewed in Iraq as being unaccountable to the state or the [rule of law](25166191.md).[^cite_note-\:2-3]

In several regions of Iraq the militias assert their own control and are perceived by some as more effective than local authorities.[^cite_note-\:6-5] Some units of the PMF retain an independent political structure, thereby preventing a unified chain of command,[^cite_note-\:6-5] and militias have resisted full integration, maintaining autonomous operations tied to Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) and carrying out attacks against coalition forces.[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-Smith-7] As such, many of the militias were designated as terrorist groups by various governments worldwide.[^cite_note-\:1-2]

## PMF Commission Law No. 40

At the end of 2016, Iraqi parliament passed PMF Commission Law No. 40, according to which, the "Popular Mobilisation Committee" (PMC) was formed as a governing body for the PMF.[^cite_note-\:3-8] The PMF was then incorporated into the Iraqi military, with a formal hierarchy of command, at the top of which stood the [Iraqi Prime Minister](208879.md) as the [Commander in Chief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander-in-chief) of the Armed Forces under country’s Constitution.[^cite_note-\:3-8] Over time the law was expanded, formalising ranks, salaries and legality.[^cite_note-\:3-8] A revised version of Law No. 40, including multiple provisions, was proposed by the [Iraqi cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Ministers_\(Iraq\)) to the parliament in March 2025.[^cite_note-\:4-9] According to the law's expanded version, the PMF would be legalised as a parallel Armed Force without being absorbed into the military, and its autonomy, both on the domestic and regional stages, would be formalised while being anchored within the state.[^cite_note-\:4-9][^cite_note-\:6-5] This fixed ambiguous status would allow the militias to retain close ideological ties with Iran.[^cite_note-\:6-5] Due to internal divisions and external pressure, the legislation ultimately did not pass.[^cite_note-10]

The law was opposed by the [US](3434750.md) as it was said to cement Iran's influence in the region, turning the organisation into a permanent and financially independent ministry-like institution,[^cite_note-\:4-9] allowed to intervene in Iraqi politics, [economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Iraq) and [culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Iraq), thereby replicating the model of Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, known for spreading its control over all walks of Iranian life.[^cite_note-\:5-11] US [secretary of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_state_\(U.S._state_government\)) [Marco Rubio](5502549.md) is quoted as stating that if the legislation passed it "would institutionalize Iranian influence and armed terrorist groups undermining Iraq’s sovereignty".[^cite_note-\:5-11] The opposition was amplified by the Human Right violations the PMF committed against civilians during the protests of 2019,[^cite_note-\:4-9] And the attacks PMF affiliated groups conducted against the US military.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-Smith-7]

## The 2026 Nation Defense Authorization Act

In December 2025, the [US senate](24909346.md) passed a compromise version of the fiscal [2026 National Defense Authorization Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Defense_Authorization_Act_for_Fiscal_Year_2026) (NDAA), according to which Iraq must satisfy three conditions if it is to receive 75% of funds under the Coalition Train and Equip Fund (CTEF):[^cite_note-\:7-13]

- The militias must be disarmed, demobililsed, and reintegrated into the Iraqi state.

  [^cite_note-\:7-13]

- The militias must answer to the Iraqi prime Minister.

  [^cite_note-\:7-13]

- If the militias break the law, they must be held accountable by the Iraqi government.

  [^cite_note-\:7-13]

## Response and disarmament

In 2025, it was reported that the Shia [Coordination Framework](82383522.md) proposed a plan for the Iraqi government to disarm six Iranian-backed Iraqi militias, including [Kataib Imam Ali](46264336.md) and [Kataib Hezbollah](23626646.md), thus partially complying with US demands.[^cite_note-\:8-14] Reportedly, all groups but Kataeb Hezbollah, agreed to the plan.[^cite_note-\:8-14]

On 18 December 2025, Shibl al Zaidi, leader of Iran-backed Iraqi militia Kataib Imam Ali called for all Iranian-backed militias in Iraq to disarm and integrate into the Iraqi state.[^cite_note-\:8-14] This statement was viewed as an initial step towards the satisfying the first condition of the NDAA.[^cite_note-\:8-14]

In December 2025, the head the Supreme Judicial Council in Iraq, [Faiq Zidan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faiq_Zaidan), said that leaders of several armed factions had agreed to coordinate the restriction of weapons to state control and to supporting the rule of law.[^cite_note-\:9-15][^cite_note-\:10-16] The pro-Iran militias Asaib Ahl al-Haq, Harakat Ansar Allah al-Awfiya, and Kataeb Imam Ali are reported as the groups who agreed to disarm, however they did not satisfy US demands by providing a binding commitment.[^cite_note-\:9-15][^cite_note-\:10-16] In addition, Kataeb Hezbollah, one of the most prominent pro-Iran factions, refused to relinquish its weapons, and agreed to consider it only when foreign forces leave Iraq.[^cite_note-\:9-15][^cite_note-\:10-16]
