**Ireland**[^cite_note-11] is an island in the North [Atlantic Ocean](698.md), in [Northwestern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwestern_Europe). Geopolitically, the island is divided between the [Republic of Ireland](14560.md) (officially [named Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_the_Irish_state) – a sovereign state covering five-sixths of the island) and [Northern Ireland](21265.md) (part of the [United Kingdom](31717.md) – covering the remaining sixth). It is separated from [Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain) to its east by the [North Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Channel_\(Great_Britain_and_Ireland\)), the [Irish Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Sea), and [St George's Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_George%27s_Channel). Ireland is the [second-largest island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_islands_of_the_British_Isles) of the [British Isles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Isles), the [third-largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_European_islands_by_area) in Europe, and the [twentieth-largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_islands_by_area) in the world.[^cite_note-unep-12] As of 2022, the [population of the entire island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_population_analysis) is just over 7 million, with 5.1 million in the Republic of Ireland and 1.9 million in Northern Ireland, ranking it the [second-most populous island in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_European_islands_by_population) after Great Britain.[^cite_note-2022population-13]&#x20;

The [geography of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Ireland) comprises relatively low-lying mountains surrounding a central plain, with [several navigable rivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivers_of_Ireland) extending inland. Much of Ireland was woodland until the end of the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages). Today, woodland makes up about 10% of the island, compared with a European average of over 33%,[^cite_note-forest-14] with most of it being non-native conifer plantations.[^cite_note-forest2-15][^cite_note-forest3-16] Its lush vegetation is a product of [its mild climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_Ireland), which is influenced by the Atlantic Ocean and thus very moderate,[^cite_note-17] and winters are milder than expected for such a northerly area, although summers are cooler than those in continental Europe. Rainfall and cloud cover are abundant.&#x20;

[Gaelic Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_Ireland) had emerged by the 1st century AD. The island was [Christianised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianised) from the 5th century onwards. During this period Ireland was divided amongst petty kings, who in turn served under the kings of the [traditional provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provinces_of_Ireland) (*Cúige*; lit. 'fifth') vying for dominance and the title of [High King of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_King_of_Ireland). Between the late 8th and early 11th centuries, [Viking raids and settlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking_Age) took place culminating in the [Battle of Clontarf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Clontarf) on 23 April 1014 which resulted in the end of Viking power in Ireland. Following the 12th-century [Anglo-Norman invasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Norman_invasion_of_Ireland), [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_England) claimed sovereignty. However, English rule did not extend over the whole island until the 16th–17th century [Tudor conquest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_conquest_of_Ireland), which led to [colonisation by settlers from Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantations_of_Ireland). In the 1690s, a system of [Protestant English rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Ascendancy) was designed to materially disadvantage the [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic) majority and [Protestant dissenters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Dissenters), and was extended during the 18th century. With the [Acts of Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_Union_1800) in 1801, Ireland became [a part of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countries_of_the_United_Kingdom) the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland). The [Great Famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_\(Ireland\)) of the 1840s saw the population fall by over 20%, through death and emigration. A [war of independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_War_of_Independence) in the early 20th century was followed by the [partition of the island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_Ireland), leading to the creation of the [Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Free_State), which became increasingly sovereign over the following decades until it declared a republic in 1948 (Republic of Ireland Act, 1948) and Northern Ireland, which remained a part of the United Kingdom. Northern Ireland saw much [civil unrest from the late 1960s until the 1990s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Troubles). This subsided following the [Good Friday Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Friday_Agreement) in 1998. In 1973, both the Republic of Ireland and the United Kingdom, with Northern Ireland as part of it, joined the [European Economic Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Economic_Community). Following a referendum vote in 2016, the United Kingdom, Northern Ireland included, left the [European Union](9317.md) (EU) in 2020. Northern Ireland was granted a limited special status and allowed to operate within the EU single market for goods without being in the European Union.[^cite_note-18]&#x20;

[Irish culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_culture) has had an influence on other cultures, particularly in [literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_literature). Alongside mainstream [Western culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture), an indigenous culture exists, as expressed through [Gaelic games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_games), [Irish folk music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_traditional_music), [Irish language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language), and [Irish dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_dance). The island's culture shares many features with that of Great Britain, including the English language, and sports such as association football, rugby, horse racing, golf, and boxing.&#x20;

## Name

The names *Ireland* and *Éire* both derive from [Old Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Irish) *[Ériu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89riu)*, which in turn comes from [Proto-Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Celtic) \**[ɸīweriyū](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Reconstruction\:Proto-Celtic/%C9%B8%C4%ABweriy%C5%AB)* meaning 'fertile soil'. The [Classical Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Latin) name for the island of Ireland, *[Ivernia](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Ivernia#Latin)*, also comes from this same root,[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20] and it remains a popular, poetic synonym for the island (usually respelt *[Hibernia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibernia)*, [under the influence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphological_reanalysis) of *[hibernum](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/hibernum#Latin)*, the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language) word for 'winter').&#x20;

## History

Main article: [History of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Ireland)

### Prehistoric Ireland

Main article: [Prehistoric Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistoric_Ireland)

During the [last glacial period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_glacial_period), much of Ireland was periodically covered by ice sheets.[^cite_note-National_Museum-21] The [relative sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_sea_level) was less than 50 m lower, resulting in an [ice bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_bridge) rather than a [land bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_bridge) forming between Ireland and Great Britain.[^cite_note-Marine_Geology-22] By 14,000 BC this ice bridge existed only between Northern Ireland and Scotland and by 12,000 BC Ireland was completely separated from Great Britain.[^cite_note-drowning-23] Later, around 6,100 BC, Great Britain became separated from continental Europe.[^cite_note-24] Until recently, the earliest evidence of human activity in Ireland was dated at 12,500 years ago, demonstrated by a butchered bear bone found in a cave in [County Clare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Clare).[^cite_note-BBC2016-03-21-25] In 2021, a reindeer bone with chop marks, which had been found in Castlepook Cave near [Doneraile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doneraile), County Cork in the early twentieth century during [Richard J. Ussher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_J._Ussher)'s excavations, was dated to 33,000 years ago by Dr Ruth Carden, a [paleozoologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleozoologist) from the School of Archaeology in [University College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_Dublin), pushing back the earliest signs of human activity by 20,000 years.[^cite_note-26]&#x20;

By about 8,000 BC, more sustained occupation of the island has been shown, with evidence for [Mesolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesolithic) communities around the island.[^cite_note-27]&#x20;

Some time before 4,000 BC, [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_Europe) [settlers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_European_Farmers) introduced cereal [cultivars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultivar), domesticated animals such as cattle and sheep, built large timber buildings, and stone monuments.[^cite_note-28][^cite_note-National_Museum-21] The earliest evidence for farming in Ireland or Great Britain is from [Ferriter's Cove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferriter%27s_Cove), [County Kerry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Kerry), where a flint knife, cattle bones and a sheep's tooth were [carbon-dated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiocarbon_dating) to c. 4,350 BC.[^cite_note-Ireland's_DNA-29] Field systems were developed in different parts of Ireland, including at the *[Céide Fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C3%A9ide_Fields)*, that has been preserved beneath a blanket of peat in present-day [Tyrawley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrawley) in northwest Mayo. An extensive [field system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_system), arguably the oldest in the world,[^cite_note-30] consisted of small divisions separated by [dry-stone walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry-stone_wall). The fields were farmed for several centuries between 3,500 BC and 3,000 BC. Wheat and barley were the principal crops.[^cite_note-National_Museum-21]&#x20;



[Poulnabrone dolmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poulnabrone_dolmen), a Neolithic portal tomb dating to about 3800–3200 BC, [the Burren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Burren), [County Clare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Clare), Ireland

Ancient DNA evidence indicates that the arrival of [Neolithic farmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_European_farmers) was accompanied by a major genetic shift, with incoming farming populations largely replacing the earlier Mesolithic [hunter-gatherer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherer) ancestry. The subsequent arrival of [Bell Beaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Beaker_culture)-associated populations during the third millennium BC brought another significant genetic shift, including the introduction of [Steppe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Steppe_Herders)-related ancestry, although the Early Bronze Age population also retained ancestry from earlier Neolithic populations.[^cite_note-31]&#x20;

The [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age) began around 2,500 BC, with technology changing people's everyday lives during this period through innovations such as the wheel, harnessing [oxen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxen), [weaving textiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weaving), brewing [alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_\(drug\)) and [metalworking](266443.md),[^cite_note-National_Museum-21] which produced new weapons and tools, along with fine gold decoration and jewellery, such as [brooches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooch) and [torcs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torc).&#x20;

### Celts

How and when the island became Celtic has been debated for close to a century, with the migrations of the [Celts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celts) being one of the more enduring themes of archaeological and linguistic studies. The most recent genetic research strongly associates the spread of [Indo-European languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages) (including [Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_languages)) through Western Europe with a people bringing a composite [Bell Beaker culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Beaker_culture), with its arrival in Britain and Ireland dated to around the middle of the third millennium BC.[^cite_note-Reich_2018_115-32] According to [John T. Koch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_T._Koch) and others, Ireland in the Late Bronze Age was part of a maritime trading culture called the [Atlantic Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Bronze_Age) that also included Britain, western France and Iberia, and that this is where Celtic languages developed.[^cite_note-Koch-33][^cite_note-Koch2009-34][^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36] This contrasts with the traditional view that their origin lies in mainland Europe with the [Hallstatt culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallstatt_culture).[^cite_note-37]&#x20;



[The Uragh Stone Circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uragh_Stone_Circle), a [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic) [stone circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone_circle) in [Tuosist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuosist), close to Gleninchaquin Park, [County Kerry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Kerry)

The long-standing traditional view is that the Celtic language, [Ogham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogham) script and culture were brought to Ireland by waves of invading or migrating Celts from mainland Europe. This theory draws on the *[Lebor Gabála Érenn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebor_Gab%C3%A1la_%C3%89renn)*, a medieval Christian pseudo-history of Ireland, along with the presence of Celtic culture, language and artefacts found in Ireland such as Celtic bronze spears, shields, torcs and other finely crafted Celtic associated possessions. The theory holds that there were four separate Celtic invasions of Ireland. The [Priteni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priteni) were said to be the first, followed by the [Belgae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgae) from northern [Gaul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaul) and Britain. Later, [Laighin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laigin) tribes from [Armorica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armorica) were said to have invaded Ireland and Britain more or less simultaneously. Lastly, the [Milesians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milesians_\(Irish\)) ([Gaels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaels)) were said to have reached Ireland from either northern Iberia or southern Gaul.[^cite_note-38] It was claimed that a second wave named the Euerni, belonging to the Belgae people of northern Gaul, began arriving about the 6th century BC. They were said to have given their name to the island.[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40]&#x20;

The theory was advanced in part because of the lack of archaeological evidence for large-scale Celtic immigration, though it is accepted that such movements are notoriously difficult to identify. Historical linguists are skeptical that this method alone could account for the absorption of Celtic language, with some saying that an assumed processual view of Celtic linguistic formation is 'an especially hazardous exercise'.[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42] Genetic lineage investigation into the area of Celtic migration to Ireland has led to findings that showed no significant differences in [mitochondrial DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondrial_DNA) between Ireland and large areas of continental Europe, in contrast to parts of the Y-chromosome pattern. When taking both into account, a study concluded that modern Celtic speakers in Ireland could be thought of as European "Atlantic Celts" showing a shared ancestry throughout the Atlantic zone from northern Iberia to western Scandinavia rather than substantially central European.[^cite_note-43] In 2012, research showed that the occurrence of genetic markers for the earliest farmers was almost eliminated by Beaker-culture immigrants: they carried what was then a new Y-chromosome R1b marker, believed to have originated in Iberia about 2,500 BC. The prevalence amongst modern Irish men of this mutation is a remarkable 84%, the highest in the world, and closely matched in other populations along the Atlantic fringes down to Spain. A similar genetic replacement happened with lineages in mitochondrial DNA.[^cite_note-Ireland's_DNA-29][^cite_note-44] This conclusion is supported by recent research carried out by the geneticist [David Reich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Reich_\(geneticist\)), who says: "British and Irish skeletons from the Bronze Age that followed the Beaker period had at most 10 per cent ancestry from the first farmers of these islands, with other 90 per cent from people like those associated with the Bell Beaker culture in the Netherlands." He suggests that it was Beaker users who introduced an Indo-European language, represented here by Celtic (i.e. a new language and culture introduced directly by migration and genetic replacement).[^cite_note-Reich_2018_115-32]&#x20;

### Late antiquity and early medieval times

Main article: [History of Ireland (800–1169)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Ireland_\(800%E2%80%931169\))



The [Scoti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scoti) were [Gaelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaels)-speaking people from Ireland who settled in western Scotland in the 6th century or before.

The earliest written records of Ireland come from classical [Greco-Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Roman_world) geographers. [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy) in his *[Almagest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almagest)* refers to Ireland as *Mikra Brettania* ("Little Britain"), in contrast to the larger island which he called *Megale Brettania* ("Great Britain").[^cite_note-45] In [his map of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy%27s_map_of_Ireland) in his later work, *[Geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_\(Ptolemy\))*, Ptolemy refers to Ireland as *[Iouernia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibernia)* and to Great Britain as *Albion*. These names were likely to have been the local names for the islands at the time. The earlier names, [in contrast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exonym_and_endonym), were likely to have been coined before direct contact with local peoples was made.[^cite_note-46]&#x20;

The [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Romans) referred to Ireland by its [Latinised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latinisation_of_names) name, *Hibernia*, and later also used the term [Scotia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotia).[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48] Ptolemy records 16 nations inhabiting every part of Ireland in 100 AD.[^cite_note-49] The relationship between the Roman Empire and the kingdoms of ancient Ireland is unclear. However, finds of Roman coins have been made, for example at the Iron Age settlement of Freestone Hill near [Gowran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gowran) and [Newgrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newgrange).[^cite_note-50]&#x20;

Ireland continued as a patchwork of rival kingdoms; however, beginning in the 7th century, a concept of national kingship gradually became articulated through the concept of a [High King of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_King_of_Ireland). [Medieval Irish literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Irish_literature) portrays an almost unbroken sequence of high kings stretching back thousands of years, but some modern historians believe the scheme was constructed in the 8th century to justify the status of powerful political groupings by projecting the origins of their rule into the remote past.[^cite_note-DOC-51]&#x20;

All of the Irish kingdoms had their own kings but were nominally subject to the high king. The high king was drawn from the ranks of the provincial kings and ruled also the royal [kingdom of Meath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Meath), with a ceremonial capital at the [Hill of Tara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill_of_Tara). The concept did not become a political reality until the [Viking Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking_Age#Ireland) and even then was not a consistent one.[^cite_note-52] Ireland did have a culturally unifying rule of law: the early written judicial system, the [Brehon Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brehon_Laws), administered by a professional class of jurists known as the *brehons*.[^cite_note-53]&#x20;

*[The Chronicle of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chronicle_of_Ireland)* records that in 431, Bishop [Palladius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palladius_\(bishop_of_Ireland\)) arrived in Ireland on a mission from [Pope Celestine I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Celestine_I) to minister to the Irish "already believing in Christ".[^cite_note-54] The same chronicle records that [Saint Patrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Patrick), Ireland's best known [patron saint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patron_saints_of_places), arrived the following year. There is continued debate over the missions of Palladius and Patrick, but the consensus is that they both took place[^cite_note-55] and that the older [druid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druid) tradition collapsed in the face of Christianity.[^cite_note-cah-56] Irish Christian scholars excelled in the study of Latin and Greek learning and Christian theology. In the monastic culture that followed the Christianisation of Ireland, Latin and Greek learning was preserved in Ireland during the [Early Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) in contrast to elsewhere in Western Europe, where the [Dark Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages) followed the [Fall of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire).[^cite_note-cah-56][^cite_note-Eer-57]&#x20;



A folio of the [Book of Kells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Kells) showing Christ enthroned

The arts of [manuscript illumination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illuminated_manuscript), metalworking and sculpture flourished and produced treasures such as the *[Book of Kells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Kells)*, ornate jewellery and the many carved stone crosses[^cite_note-58] that still dot the island today. A mission founded in 563 on [Iona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iona) by the Irish monk Saint [Columba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columba) began [a tradition of Irish missionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiberno-Scottish_mission) work that spread [Celtic Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Christianity) and learning to Scotland, England and the [Frankish Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankish_Empire) on continental Europe after the fall of Rome.[^cite_note-59] These missions continued until the [late Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Middle_Ages), establishing monasteries and centres of learning, producing scholars such as [Sedulius Scottus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedulius_Scottus) and [Johannes Eriugena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Eriugena) and exerting much influence in Europe.[^cite_note-60]&#x20;

From the 9th century, waves of [Viking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking) raiders plundered Irish monasteries and towns.[^cite_note-61] These raids added to a pattern of raiding and [endemic warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endemic_warfare) that was already deep-seated in Ireland. The Vikings were involved in establishing most of the major coastal settlements in Ireland: [Dublin](8504.md), [Limerick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limerick), [Cork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cork_\(city\)), [Wexford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wexford), [Waterford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterford), as well as other smaller settlements.[^cite_note-62]&#x20;

### Norman and English invasions

Main articles: [Anglo-Norman invasion of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Norman_invasion_of_Ireland), [History of Ireland (1169–1536)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Ireland_\(1169%E2%80%931536\)), and [Tudor conquest of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_conquest_of_Ireland)

See also: [Bruce campaign in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_campaign_in_Ireland)



Remains of the 12th-century [Trim Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trim_Castle) in [County Meath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Meath), the largest [Norman castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_architecture) in Ireland

On 1 May 1169, an expedition of [Cambro-Norman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambro-Norman) knights, with an army of about 600 men, landed at [Bannow Strand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bannow) in present-day [County Wexford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Wexford). It was led by [Richard de Clare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_de_Clare,_2nd_Earl_of_Pembroke), known as 'Strongbow' owing to his prowess as an archer.[^cite_note-63] The invasion, which coincided with a period of renewed Norman expansion, was at the invitation of [Dermot Mac Murrough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diarmait_Mac_Murchada), [King of Leinster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kings_of_Leinster).[^cite_note-64]&#x20;

In 1166, Mac Murrough had fled to [Anjou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_Anjou), France, following a war involving [Tighearnán Ua Ruairc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tighearn%C3%A1n_Ua_Ruairc), of [Breifne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breifne), and sought the assistance of the [Angevin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angevin_Empire) King [Henry II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Henry_II_of_England), in recapturing his kingdom. In 1171, Henry arrived in Ireland in order to review the general progress of the expedition. He wanted to re-exert royal authority over the invasion which was expanding beyond his control. Henry successfully re-imposed his authority over Strongbow and the Cambro-Norman warlords and persuaded many of the Irish kings to accept him as their overlord, an arrangement confirmed in the 1175 [Treaty of Windsor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Windsor_1175).&#x20;

The invasion was legitimised by reference to provisions of the alleged [Papal Bull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papal_Bull) *[Laudabiliter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laudabiliter)*, issued by an Englishman, [Adrian IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Adrian_IV), in 1155. The document encouraged Henry to take control in Ireland in order to oversee the financial and administrative reorganisation of the [Irish Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Church) and its integration into the Roman Church system.[^cite_note-Curtis_2002_49-65] Some restructuring had already begun at the ecclesiastical level following the [Synod of Kells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synod_of_Kells) in 1152.[^cite_note-66] There has been significant controversy regarding the authenticity of *Laudabiliter*,[^cite_note-67] and there is no general agreement as to whether the bull was genuine or a forgery.[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69] Further, it had no standing in the Irish legal system.&#x20;



Political boundaries in Ireland in 1450, before the [plantations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantations_of_Ireland)

In 1172, Pope [Alexander III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Alexander_III) further encouraged Henry to advance the integration of the Irish Church with Rome. Henry was authorised to impose a tithe of one penny per hearth as an annual contribution. This church levy, called [Peter's Pence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter%27s_Pence), is extant in Ireland as a voluntary donation. In turn, Henry assumed the title of [Lord of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_of_Ireland) which Henry conferred on his younger son, [John Lackland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lackland), in 1185. This defined the Anglo-Norman administration in Ireland as the [Lordship of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lordship_of_Ireland). When Henry's successor, [Richard the Lionheart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_I_of_England) died unexpectedly in 1199, [John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_of_England) inherited the crown of England and retained the Lordship of Ireland. Over the century that followed, Norman feudal law gradually replaced the Gaelic Brehon Law across large areas, so that by the late 13th century the [Norman-Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiberno-Norman) had established a feudal system throughout much of Ireland. Norman settlements were characterised by the establishment of baronies, manors, towns and the seeds of the modern county system. A version of [Magna Carta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_Carta) (the [Great Charter of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Charter_of_Ireland)), substituting *Dublin* for *London* and the *Irish Church* for, the English church at the time, the *Catholic Church*, was published in 1216 and the [Parliament of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_Ireland) was founded in 1297.&#x20;

### Gaelicisation

From the mid-14th century, after the [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death), Norman settlements in Ireland went into a period of decline. The Norman rulers and the Gaelic Irish elites intermarried and the areas under Norman rule became [Gaelicised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelicisation). In some parts, a hybrid Hiberno-Norman culture emerged. In response, the [Irish parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_Ireland#Middle_Ages) passed the [Statutes of Kilkenny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statutes_of_Kilkenny) in 1367. These were a set of laws designed to prevent the assimilation of the Normans into Irish society by requiring English subjects in Ireland to speak English, follow English customs and abide by English law.[^cite_note-70]&#x20;

By the end of the 15th century, central English authority in Ireland had all but disappeared, and a renewed Irish culture and language, albeit with Norman influences, was again dominant. English Crown control remained relatively unshaken in an amorphous foothold around Dublin known as [The Pale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pale_\(Ireland\)), and under the provisions of [Poynings' Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poynings%27_Law_\(on_certification_of_acts\)) of 1494, Irish Parliamentary legislation was subject to the approval of the [English Privy Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Privy_Council).[^cite_note-71]&#x20;

### Kingdom of Ireland

Main article: [Kingdom of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Ireland)



A 16th-century perception of Irish women and girls, illustrated in the manuscript "Théâtre de tous les peuples et nations de la terre avec leurs habits et ornemens divers, tant anciens que modernes, diligemment depeints au naturel". Painted by [Lucas d'Heere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucas_de_Heere) in the 2nd half of the 16th century. Preserved in the [Ghent University Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghent_University_Library).

[^cite_note-72]

The title of [King of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_of_Ireland) was re-created in 1542 by [Henry VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VIII), [King of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_of_England), of the [Tudor dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_dynasty). English rule was reinforced and expanded in Ireland during the latter part of the 16th century, leading to the [Tudor conquest of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_conquest_of_Ireland). A near-complete conquest was achieved by the turn of the 17th century, following the [Nine Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nine_Years%27_War_\(Ireland\)) and the [Flight of the Earls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_of_the_Earls).&#x20;

This control was consolidated during the wars and conflicts of the 17th century, including the English and Scottish colonisation in the [Plantations of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantations_of_Ireland), the [Wars of the Three Kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wars_of_the_Three_Kingdoms) and the [Williamite War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Williamite_War_in_Ireland). During the [Irish Confederate Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Confederate_Wars) (1641–1653), Ireland experienced a catastrophic demographic collapse. Modern estimates suggest that approximately 20,000–30,000 soldiers died in combat, while an estimated 200,000 to over 300,000 civilians perished from war-related famine, disease, and displacement, with some contemporary estimates, such as those by [Sir William Petty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Petty), suggesting even higher mortality figures. A further 50,000[^cite_note-75] were sent into [indentured servitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indentured_servitude) in the [West Indies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Indies). Petty estimated that 504,000 Catholic Irish and 112,000 Protestant settlers died, and 100,000 people were transported, as a result of the war.[^cite_note-76] If a prewar population of 1.5 million is assumed, this would mean that the population was reduced by almost half.&#x20;

The religious struggles of the 17th century left a deep sectarian division in Ireland. Religious allegiance now determined the perception in law of loyalty to the Irish King and Parliament. After the passing of [the Test Act 1672](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_Act#Test_Act_of_1673), and the victory of the forces of the dual monarchy of [William](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_III_of_England) and [Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_II_of_England) over the [Jacobites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobitism), Roman Catholics and nonconforming Protestant Dissenters were barred from sitting as members in the Irish Parliament. Under the [Penal Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penal_Laws_\(Ireland\)), Irish Roman Catholics and Dissenters were increasingly deprived of civil rights and the ownership of hereditary property. Additional regressive punitive legislation followed in 1703, 1709 and 1728. This completed a comprehensive systemic effort to materially disadvantage Roman Catholics and Protestant Dissenters while enriching a new ruling class of Anglican conformists.[^cite_note-77] The new Anglo-Irish ruling class became known as the [Protestant Ascendancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Ascendancy).&#x20;



[Half-hanging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-hanging) of suspected [United Irishmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Irishmen)

The "[Great Frost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Famine_\(1740%E2%80%931741\)#Cause)" struck Ireland and the rest of Europe between December 1739 and September 1741, after a decade of relatively mild winters. The winters destroyed stored crops of potatoes and other staples, and the poor summers severely damaged harvests.[^cite_note-78] This resulted in the [famine of 1740](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Famine_\(1740%E2%80%931741\)). An estimated 250,000 people (about one in eight of the population) died from the ensuing pestilence and disease.[^cite_note-cormac_famine-79] The Irish government halted export of corn and kept the army in quarters but did little more.[^cite_note-cormac_famine-79][^cite_note-crawford_feast-80] Local gentry and charitable organisations provided relief but could do little to prevent the ensuing mortality.[^cite_note-cormac_famine-79][^cite_note-crawford_feast-80]&#x20;

In the aftermath of the famine, an increase in industrial production and a surge in trade brought a succession of construction booms. The population soared in the latter part of this century and the [architectural legacy of Georgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_architecture) Ireland was built. In 1782, [Poynings' Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poynings%27_Law_\(on_certification_of_acts\)) was repealed, giving Ireland legislative independence from Great Britain for the first time since 1495. The British government, however, still retained the right to nominate the government of Ireland without the consent of the Irish parliament.&#x20;

### 1798 Rebellion

Main article: [Irish Rebellion of 1798](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Rebellion_of_1798)



"The Camp on Vinegar Hill" - an illustration by [George Cruikshank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Cruikshank) to accompany [William Hamilton Maxwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Hamilton_Maxwell)'s 1845 work *History of the Irish rebellion in 1798*

In 1798, members of the Protestant Dissenter tradition (mainly [Presbyterian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterianism)) made common cause with Roman Catholics in a republican rebellion inspired and led by the [Society of United Irishmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_United_Irishmen), with the aim of creating an independent Ireland. Despite assistance from France the [rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Rebellion_of_1798) was put down by British and Irish government and yeomanry forces. The rebellion lasted from 24 May to 12 October that year and saw the establishment of the short lived [Irish Republic (1798)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republic_\(1798\)) in the province of [Connacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connacht). It saw numerous battles across the island with an estimated 30,000 people dead.&#x20;

### Union with Great Britain

Main article: [United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland)

As a direct result of the 1798 rebellion in its aftermath in 1800, the British and Irish parliaments both passed [Acts of Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_Union_1800) that, with effect from 1 January 1801, merged the [Kingdom of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Ireland) and the [Kingdom of Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Britain) to create a [United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland).[^cite_note-Ward_1994_28-81]&#x20;

The passage of the Act in the Irish Parliament was ultimately achieved with substantial majorities, having failed on the first attempt in 1799. According to contemporary documents and historical analysis, this was achieved through a considerable degree of bribery, with funding provided by the British Secret Service Office, and the awarding of peerages, places and honours to secure votes.[^cite_note-Ward_1994_28-81] Thus, the parliament in Ireland was abolished and replaced by a [united parliament at Westminster](13964.md) in London, though resistance remained, as evidenced by [Robert Emmet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Emmet)'s failed [Irish Rebellion of 1803](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Rebellion_of_1803).&#x20;

Aside from the development of the [linen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linen) industry, Ireland was largely passed over by the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution), partly because it lacked coal and iron resources[^cite_note-82][^cite_note-83] and partly because of the impact of the sudden union with the structurally superior economy of England,[^cite_note-84] which saw Ireland as a source of agricultural produce and capital.[^cite_note-85][^cite_note-86]&#x20;



A depiction of the [Great Famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_\(Ireland\)) from *Our Boys in Ireland* by Henry Willard French (1891)

The [Great Famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_\(Ireland\)) of 1845–1851 devastated Ireland, as in those years Ireland's population fell by one-third. More than one million people died from starvation and disease, with an additional million people emigrating during the famine, mostly to the United States and Canada.[^cite_note-87] In the century that followed, an economic depression caused by the famine resulted in a further million people emigrating.[^cite_note-88] By the end of the decade, half of all [immigration to the United States](15051.md) was from Ireland. The period of civil unrest that followed until the end of the 19th century is referred to as the [Land War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_War). Mass emigration became deeply entrenched and the population continued to decline until the mid-20th century. Immediately prior to the famine the population was recorded as 8.2 million by the [1841 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1841_census_of_Ireland).[^cite_note-89] The population has never returned to this level since.[^cite_note-90] The population continued to fall until 1961; [County Leitrim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Leitrim) was the final Irish county to record a population increase post-famine, in 2006.&#x20;

The 19th and early 20th centuries saw the rise of modern [Irish nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_nationalism), primarily among the Roman Catholic population. The pre-eminent Irish political figure after the Union was [Daniel O'Connell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_O%27Connell). He was elected as Member of Parliament for [Ennis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ennis_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)) in a surprise result and despite being unable to take his seat [as a Roman Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Test_Act). O'Connell spearheaded a vigorous campaign that was taken up by the Prime Minister, the Irish-born soldier and statesman, the [Duke of Wellington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Wellesley,_1st_Duke_of_Wellington). Steering the [Catholic Relief Bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Relief_Act_1829) through Parliament, aided by future prime minister [Robert Peel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Peel), Wellington prevailed upon a reluctant [George IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_IV_of_the_United_Kingdom) to sign the Bill and proclaim it into law. [George's father](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_III) had opposed the plan of the earlier Prime Minister, [Pitt the Younger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Pitt_the_Younger), to introduce such a bill following the Union of 1801, fearing [Catholic emancipation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_emancipation) to be in conflict with the [Act of Settlement 1701](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Settlement_1701).&#x20;

Daniel O'Connell led a subsequent campaign, for the repeal of the Act of Union, which failed. Later in the century, [Charles Stewart Parnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Stewart_Parnell) and others campaigned for autonomy within the Union, or "[Home Rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Home_Rule_Bills)". Unionists, especially those located in Ulster, were strongly opposed to Home Rule, which they thought would be dominated by Catholic interests.[^cite_note-91] After several attempts to pass a Home Rule bill through parliament, it looked certain that one would finally pass in 1914. To prevent this from happening, the [Ulster Volunteers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Volunteers) were formed in 1913 under the leadership of [Edward Carson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Carson).[^cite_note-Kee_1972_478–530-92]&#x20;

Their formation was followed in 1914 by the establishment of the [Irish Volunteers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Volunteers), whose aim was to ensure that the [Home Rule Bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Home_Rule_Act) was passed. The Act was passed but with the "temporary" exclusion of the six counties of Ulster, which later became Northern Ireland. Before it could be implemented, however, the Act was suspended for the duration of the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War). The Irish Volunteers split into two groups. The majority, approximately 175,000 in number, under [John Redmond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Redmond), took the name [National Volunteers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Volunteers) and supported [Irish involvement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland_and_World_War_I) in the war. A minority, approximately 13,000, retained the Irish Volunteers' name and opposed Ireland's involvement in the war.[^cite_note-Kee_1972_478–530-92]&#x20;



Sackville Street (now [O'Connell Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O%27Connell_Street)), Dublin, after the 1916 [Easter Rising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Rising)

The [Easter Rising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Rising) of 1916 was carried out by the latter group together with a smaller socialist militia, the [Irish Citizen Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Citizen_Army). The British response, executing 15 leaders of the Rising over a period of ten days and imprisoning or interning more than a thousand people, turned the mood of the country in favour of the rebels. Support for [Irish republicanism increased](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_force_Irish_republicanism) further due to the ongoing war in Europe, as well as the [Conscription Crisis of 1918](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscription_Crisis_of_1918).[^cite_note-autogenerated34-93]&#x20;

The pro-independence republican party, [Sinn Féin](28175.md), received overwhelming endorsement in the [1918 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1918_United_Kingdom_general_election_in_Ireland), and in 1919 proclaimed an [Irish Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republic), setting up its own parliament (*[Dáil Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_D%C3%A1il)*) and government. Simultaneously the Volunteers, which became known as the [Irish Republican Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republican_Army) (IRA), launched a [three-year guerrilla war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_War_of_Independence), which ended in a truce in July 1921 (although violence continued until June 1922, mostly in Northern Ireland).[^cite_note-autogenerated34-93]&#x20;

### Partition

Main article: [Partition of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_Ireland)

In December 1921, the [Anglo-Irish Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Irish_Treaty) was concluded between the British government and representatives of the [Second Dáil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_D%C3%A1il). It gave Ireland complete independence in its home affairs and practical independence for foreign policy, but an opt-out clause allowed [Northern Ireland](21265.md) to remain within the United Kingdom, which it immediately exercised. Additionally, members of the [Free State Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3rd_D%C3%A1il) were required to swear [an oath of allegiance to the Constitution of the Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oath_of_Allegiance_\(Ireland\)) and declare fidelity to the King.[^cite_note-94] Disagreements over these provisions led to a split in the nationalist movement and a subsequent [Irish Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Civil_War) between the new government of the [Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Free_State) and those opposed to the treaty, led by [Éamon de Valera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89amon_de_Valera). The civil war officially ended in May 1923 when de Valera issued a cease-fire order.[^cite_note-95]&#x20;

#### Independence

Main articles: [History of the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland) and [Economy of the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland)



Annotated page from the [Anglo-Irish Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Irish_Treaty) that established the [Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Free_State) and independence for 26 out of 32 [Irish counties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counties_of_Ireland)

During its first decade, the Irish Free State was governed by the victors of the civil war. When de Valera achieved power, he took advantage of the [Statute of Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Westminster_1931) and [political circumstances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_VIII_abdication_crisis) to build upon inroads to greater sovereignty made by the previous government. The oath was abolished and in 1937 a new constitution was adopted.[^cite_note-autogenerated34-93] This completed a process of gradual separation from the British Empire that governments had pursued since independence. However, it was not until 1949 that the state was declared, officially, to be the [Republic of Ireland](14560.md).&#x20;

[The state was neutral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_neutrality) during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Emergency_\(Ireland\)), but offered [clandestine assistance to the Allies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_neutrality_during_World_War_II), particularly in the potential defence of Northern Ireland. Despite their country's neutrality, approximately 50,000[^cite_note-96] volunteers from independent Ireland joined the British forces during the war, four being awarded [Victoria Crosses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_Crosses).&#x20;

The [German intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abwehr) was also active in Ireland.[^cite_note-autogenerated695-97] Its operations ended in September 1941 when [police](383207.md) made arrests based on surveillance carried out on the key diplomatic legations in Dublin. To the authorities, counterintelligence was a fundamental line of defence. With a regular army of only slightly over seven thousand men at the start of the war, and with limited supplies of modern weapons, the state would have had great difficulty in defending itself from invasion from either side in the conflict.[^cite_note-autogenerated695-97][^cite_note-98]&#x20;

Large-scale emigration marked most of the post-WWII period (particularly during the 1950s and 1980s), but beginning in 1987 the economy improved, and the 1990s saw the beginning of substantial economic growth. This period of growth became known as the [Celtic Tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Tiger).[^cite_note-clancy3-99] The Republic's real GDP grew by an average of 9.6% per annum between 1995 and 1999,[^cite_note-100] in which year the Republic joined the [euro](9472.md). In 2000, it was the sixth-richest country in the world in terms of GDP per capita.[^cite_note-101] Historian [R. F. Foster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._F._Foster_\(historian\)) argues the cause was a combination of a new sense of initiative and the entry of American corporations. He concludes the chief factors were low taxation, pro-business regulatory policies, and a young, tech-savvy workforce. For many multinationals, the decision to do business in Ireland was made easier still by generous incentives from the [Industrial Development Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IDA_Ireland). In addition [European Union](9317.md) membership was helpful, giving the country lucrative access to markets that it had previously reached only through the United Kingdom, and pumping huge subsidies and investment capital into the Irish economy.[^cite_note-102]&#x20;

Modernisation brought secularisation in its wake. The traditionally high levels of religiosity have sharply declined. Foster points to three factors: First, Irish feminism, largely imported from America with liberal stances on contraception, abortion and divorce, undermined the authority of bishops and priests. Second, the mishandling of the paedophile scandals humiliated the Church, whose bishops seemed less concerned with the victims and more concerned with covering up for errant priests. Third, prosperity brought hedonism and materialism that undercut the ideals of saintly poverty.[^cite_note-103]&#x20;

The [post-2008 Irish economic downturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-2008_Irish_economic_downturn) dramatically ended this period of boom. GDP fell by 3% in 2008 and by 7.1% in 2009, the worst year since records began (although earnings by foreign-owned businesses continued to grow).[^cite_note-104] The state has since experienced deep recession, with unemployment, which doubled during 2009, remaining above 14% in 2012.[^cite_note-105]&#x20;

#### Northern Ireland

Main articles: [History of Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Northern_Ireland) and [Economy of Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Northern_Ireland)

Northern Ireland resulted from the division of the United Kingdom by the [Government of Ireland Act 1920](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Ireland_Act_1920), and until 1972 was a self-governing jurisdiction within the United Kingdom with its own parliament and prime minister. Northern Ireland, as part of the United Kingdom, was not neutral during the Second World War, and [Belfast suffered four bombing raids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belfast_Blitz) in 1941. [Conscription](5735.md) was not extended to Northern Ireland, and roughly an equal number volunteered from Northern Ireland as volunteered from the Republic of Ireland.&#x20;



[Edward Carson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Carson) signing the [Solemn League and Covenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Covenant) in 1912, declaring opposition to [Home Rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Home_Rule_bills) "using all means which may be found necessary"

Although Northern Ireland was largely spared the strife of the civil war, in the decades that followed partition there were sporadic episodes of inter-communal violence. Nationalists, mainly Roman Catholic, wanted to unite Ireland as an independent republic, whereas unionists, mainly Protestant, wanted Northern Ireland to remain in the United Kingdom. The Protestant and Catholic communities in Northern Ireland voted largely along [sectarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sectarian) lines, meaning that the government of Northern Ireland (elected by ["first-past-the-post"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plurality_electoral_system) from 1929) was controlled by the [Ulster Unionist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Unionist_Party). Over time, the minority Catholic community felt increasingly alienated with further disaffection fuelled by practices such as [gerrymandering](12987.md) and discrimination in housing and employment.[^cite_note-whyte-106][^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108]&#x20;

In the late 1960s, nationalist grievances were aired publicly in mass civil rights protests, which were often confronted by [loyalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_loyalism) counter-protests.[^cite_note-109] The government's reaction to confrontations was seen to be one-sided and heavy-handed in favour of unionists. Law and order broke down as unrest and inter-communal violence increased.[^cite_note-110] The Northern Ireland government requested the [British Army](4887.md) to aid the police and protect the [Irish Nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Nationalist) population. In 1969, the paramilitary [Provisional IRA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provisional_Irish_Republican_Army), which favoured the creation of a [united Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Ireland), emerged from a split in the [Irish Republican Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republican_Army_\(1922%E2%80%931969\)) and began a campaign against what it called the "British occupation of the six counties".&#x20;

Other groups, both the unionist and nationalist participated in violence, and a period known as "[the Troubles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Troubles)" began. More than 3,600 deaths resulted over the subsequent three decades of conflict.[^cite_note-111] Owing to the civil unrest during the Troubles, the British government suspended home rule in 1972 and imposed [direct rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_rule_over_Northern_Ireland). There were several unsuccessful attempts to end the Troubles politically, such as the [Sunningdale Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunningdale_Agreement) of 1973. In 1998, following a ceasefire by the Provisional IRA and multi-party talks, the [Good Friday Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Friday_Agreement) was concluded as a treaty between the British and Irish governments, incorporating the text agreed in the multi-party talks.&#x20;

The substance of the Agreement (formally referred to as the Belfast Agreement) was later endorsed by referendums in both parts of Ireland. The Agreement restored self-government to Northern Ireland on the basis of power-sharing in a regional [Executive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Executive) drawn from the major parties in a new [Northern Ireland Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Assembly), with entrenched protections for the two main communities. The Executive is jointly headed by a [First Minister and deputy First Minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Minister_and_deputy_First_Minister) drawn from the unionist and nationalist parties. Violence decreased significantly following the 1994 Provisional IRA and loyalist ceasefires. In 2005, the Provisional IRA announced the end of its armed campaign, and an [independent commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_International_Commission_on_Decommissioning) supervised the disarmament of both nationalist and unionist paramilitary organisations.[^cite_note-112]&#x20;

The Assembly and power-sharing Executive were suspended several times but were restored again in 2007. In that year the British government officially ended its military support of the police in Northern Ireland ([Operation Banner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Banner)) and began withdrawing troops. On 27 June 2012, Northern Ireland's deputy first minister and former IRA commander, [Martin McGuinness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_McGuinness), shook hands with Queen Elizabeth II in Belfast, symbolising reconciliation between the two sides.[^cite_note-113]&#x20;

## Politics



Political entities on the island of Ireland

The island is divided between the Republic of Ireland, an independent [state](23604120.md), and Northern Ireland, a constituent country of the United Kingdom. They share an [open border](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_border) and both are part of the [Common Travel Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Travel_Area) and as a consequence, there is [free movement of people, goods, services and capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_market) across the border.&#x20;

The Republic of Ireland is a member state of the European Union while the United Kingdom is a former member state, having both acceded to its precursor entity, the European Economic Community (EEC), in 1973 but the UK [left the European Union in 2020](41688778.md) after a [referendum on EU membership was held in 2016](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_Kingdom_European_Union_membership_referendum) which resulted in 51.9% of UK voters choosing to leave the bloc.&#x20;

### Republic of Ireland

Main article: [Republic of Ireland](14560.md)



*[Áras an Uachtaráin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%81ras_an_Uachtar%C3%A1in)*, the official residence of the [President of Ireland](24676.md)

The Republic of Ireland is a [parliamentary democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_democracy) based on the [Westminster system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_system), with a [written constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Ireland) and a popularly elected [president](24676.md) whose role is mostly ceremonial. The [Oireachtas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oireachtas) is a [bicameral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicameral) parliament, composed of [Dáil Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1il_%C3%89ireann) (the Dáil), a house of representatives, and [Seanad Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seanad_%C3%89ireann) (the Seanad), an [upper house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_house). The [government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Ireland) is headed by a prime minister, the [Taoiseach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoiseach), who is appointed by the president on the nomination of the Dáil. Its capital is Dublin.&#x20;

The Republic of Ireland today ranks among the wealthiest countries in the world in terms of [GDP per capita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GDP_per_capita)[^cite_note-114] and in 2015 was ranked the sixth most developed nation in the world by the United Nations' [Human Development Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Development_Index).[^cite_note-115] A period of rapid economic expansion from 1995 onwards became known as the [Celtic Tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Tiger) period, was brought to an end by the post-2008 Irish economic downturn and an economic depression in 2009. According to the 2024 [Global Peace Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Peace_Index), Ireland is the second most peaceful country in the world.[^cite_note-116]&#x20;

### Northern Ireland

Main article: [Northern Ireland](21265.md)



[Parliament Buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_Buildings_\(Northern_Ireland\)), in [Stormont Estate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stormont_Estate), seat of the [Northern Ireland Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Assembly)

Northern Ireland is a part of the United Kingdom with a local [executive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Executive) and [assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Assembly) which exercise devolved powers. The executive is jointly headed by the first and deputy first minister, with the ministries being allocated in proportion to each party's representation in the assembly. Its capital is [Belfast](5046.md).&#x20;

Ultimately political power is held by the [UK government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_government), from which Northern Ireland has gone through intermittent periods of direct rule during which devolved powers have been suspended. Northern Ireland elects 18 of the UK [House of Commons](13828.md)' 650 MPs. The [Northern Ireland Secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Secretary) is a cabinet-level post in the British government.&#x20;

Along with [England and Wales](275187.md) and with Scotland, Northern Ireland forms one of the three separate legal jurisdictions of the UK, all of which share the [Supreme Court of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_Kingdom) as their court of final appeal.&#x20;

### All-island institutions

As part of the Good Friday Agreement, the British and Irish governments agreed on the creation of all-island institutions and areas of cooperation. The [North/South Ministerial Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North/South_Ministerial_Council) is an institution through which ministers from the Government of Ireland and the Northern Ireland Executive agree all-island policies. At least six of these policy areas must have an associated all-island "implementation body", and at least six others must be implemented separately in each jurisdiction. The implementation bodies are: [Waterways Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterways_Ireland), the [Food Safety Promotion Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_Safety_Promotion_Board), [InterTradeIreland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/InterTradeIreland), the [Special European Union Programmes Body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_European_Union_Programmes_Body), [the North/South Language Body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_North/South_Language_Body) and the [Foyle, Carlingford and Irish Lights Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commissioners_of_Irish_Lights).&#x20;

The [British–Irish Intergovernmental Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British%E2%80%93Irish_Intergovernmental_Conference) provides for co-operation between the Government of Ireland and the Government of the United Kingdom on all matters of mutual interest, especially Northern Ireland. In light of the Republic's particular interest in the governance of Northern Ireland, "regular and frequent" meetings co-chaired by the Irish Minister for Foreign Affairs and the British Secretary of State for Northern Ireland, dealing with non-devolved matters to do with Northern Ireland and non-devolved [all-Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-Ireland) issues, are required to take place under the establishing treaty.&#x20;

The [North/South Inter-Parliamentary Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North/South_Inter-Parliamentary_Association) is a joint parliamentary forum for the island of Ireland. It has no formal powers but operates as a forum for discussing matters of common concern between the respective legislatures.&#x20;

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Ireland)



Physical features of Ireland

Ireland is located in the [north-west of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North-West_Europe), between latitudes [51°](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/51st_parallel_north) and [56° N](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/56th_parallel_north), and longitudes [11°](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/11th_meridian_west) and [5° W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5th_meridian_west). The island is separated from Great Britain by the [Irish Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Sea) and the [North Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Channel_\(Great_Britain_and_Ireland\)), which has a width of 23 kilometres (14 mi)[^cite_note-117] at its narrowest point. To the west is the northern Atlantic Ocean and to the south is the [Celtic Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Sea), which lies between Ireland and [Brittany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brittany), in France. Ireland has a total area of 84,421 km<sup>2</sup> (32,595 mi<sup>2</sup>),[^cite_note-irlgeog-1][^cite_note-royle-2][^cite_note-118] of which the Republic of Ireland occupies 83 percent.[^cite_note-119] Ireland and Great Britain, together with many nearby smaller islands, are known collectively as the [British Isles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Isles).[^cite_note-ONS_Geography_Guide-120] As [the term *British Isles* can be controversial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Isles_naming_dispute) in relation to Ireland, the alternate term *Britain and Ireland* is sometimes used as a neutral term for the islands.[^cite_note-GuardianMOS01-121]&#x20;

A ring of coastal mountains surrounds low plains at the centre of the island. The highest of these is [Carrauntoohil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrauntoohil) ([Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language): *Corrán Tuathail*) in [County Kerry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Kerry), which rises to 1,039 m (3,409 ft) above sea level.[^cite_note-OSI_faqs-122] The most arable land lies in the province of [Leinster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leinster).[^cite_note-123] Western areas are mainly mountainous and rocky with green panoramic vistas. [River Shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Shannon), the island's longest river at 360.5 km (224 mi) long, rises in [County Cavan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Cavan) in the north-west and flows through [Limerick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limerick) in the midwest.[^cite_note-OSI_faqs-122][^cite_note-124]&#x20;

### Geology

Main article: [Geology of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology_of_Ireland)



[Topography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topography) of Ireland

The island consists of varied [geological provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologic_province). In the west, around County Galway and [County Donegal](45576.md), is a medium- to high-grade metamorphic and igneous complex of [Caledonide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caledonides) affinity, similar to the [Scottish Highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Highlands). Across southeast Ulster and extending southwest to [Longford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longford) and south to [Navan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navan) is a province of [Ordovician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordovician) and [Silurian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silurian) rocks, with similarities to the [Southern Uplands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Uplands) province of Scotland. Further south, along the [County Wexford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Wexford) coastline, is an area of granite [intrusives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intrusion) into more Ordovician and Silurian rocks, like that found in Wales.[^cite_note-125][^cite_note-126]&#x20;

In the southwest, around [Bantry Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantry_Bay) and the mountains of [MacGillycuddy's Reeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacGillycuddy%27s_Reeks), is an area of substantially deformed, lightly [metamorphosed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamorphic_rock) [Devonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devonian)-aged rocks.[^cite_note-127] This partial ring of "hard rock" geology is covered by a blanket of [Carboniferous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboniferous) limestone over the centre of the country, giving rise to a comparatively fertile and lush landscape. The west-coast district of [the Burren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Burren) around [Lisdoonvarna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisdoonvarna) has well-developed [karst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karst) features.[^cite_note-128] Significant stratiform lead-zinc mineralisation is found in the limestones around [Silvermines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silvermines) and [Tynagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tynagh).&#x20;

[Hydrocarbon exploration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon_exploration) is ongoing following the first major find at the [Kinsale Head gas field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinsale_Head_gas_field) off [Cork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cork_\(city\)) in the mid-1970s.[^cite_note-energyfiles-129][^cite_note-sch-130] In 1999, economically significant finds of natural gas were made in the [Corrib Gas Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrib_Gas_Field) off the County Mayo coast. This has increased activity off the west coast in parallel with the "[West of Shetland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea_oil#West_of_Shetland)" step-out development from the [North Sea hydrocarbon province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea_oil). In 2000, the [Helvick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helvick) oil field was discovered, which was estimated to contain over 28 million barrels (4,500,000 m<sup>3</sup>) of oil.[^cite_note-131]&#x20;

### Climate

Main article: [Climate of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_Ireland)

The island's lush vegetation, a product of its mild climate and frequent rainfall, earns it the sobriquet *the Emerald Isle*. Overall, Ireland has a mild but changeable [oceanic climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_climate) with few extremes. The climate is typically insular and [temperate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate_climate), avoiding the extremes in temperature of many other areas in the world at similar latitudes.[^cite_note-MEclimate-132] This is a result of the moist winds which ordinarily prevail from the southwestern Atlantic.&#x20;

Precipitation falls throughout the year but is light overall, particularly in the east. The west tends to be wetter on average and prone to Atlantic storms, especially in the late autumn and winter months. These occasionally bring destructive winds and higher total rainfall to these areas, as well as sometimes snow and hail. The regions of north County Galway and east County Mayo have the highest incidents of recorded lightning annually for the island, with lightning occurring approximately five to ten days per year in these areas.[^cite_note-MErainfall-133] [Munster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munster), in the south, records the least snow whereas [Ulster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster), in the north, records the most.&#x20;

Inland areas are warmer in summer and colder in winter. Usually around 40 days of the year are below freezing 0 °C (32 °F) at inland [weather stations](206018.md), compared to 10 days at coastal stations. Ireland is sometimes affected by heat waves, most recently in 1995, [2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_European_heat_wave), [2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_European_heat_wave#Ireland), 2013 and [2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_British_Isles_heat_wave). In common with the rest of Europe, Ireland experienced unusually cold weather during [the winter of 2010–11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_of_2010%E2%80%9311_in_Great_Britain_and_Ireland). Temperatures fell as low as −17.2 °C (1 °F) in County Mayo on 20 December[^cite_note-134] and up to a metre (3 ft) of snow fell in mountainous areas.&#x20;

## Flora and fauna

Main articles: [Fauna of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fauna_of_Ireland), [Flora of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flora_of_Ireland), and [Trees of Britain and Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trees_of_Britain_and_Ireland)

The red fox is common in Ireland.

Two [red foxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_fox) (*Vulpes vulpes*) in Gubbeen, County Cork

Unlike Great Britain which had a land bridge with [mainland Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland_Europe), Ireland only had an ice bridge ending around 14,000 years ago at the end of the [last ice age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Glacial_Period) and as a result, it has fewer land animal and plant species than Great Britain or mainland Europe.[^cite_note-Marine_Geology-22][^cite_note-drowning-23] There are [55 mammal species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mammals_of_Ireland) in Ireland, and of them, only 26 land mammal species are considered native to Ireland.[^cite_note-Costello,_M.J_93-138] Some species, such as, the [red fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_fox), [hedgehog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_hedgehog) and [badger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_badger), are very common, whereas others, like the [Irish hare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_hare), [red deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_deer) and [pine marten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pine_marten) are less so. Aquatic wildlife, such as species of sea turtle, shark, seal, whale, and dolphin, are common off the coast. About 400 species of birds have been recorded in Ireland. Many of these are migratory, including the [barn swallow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barn_swallow).&#x20;



[Red deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_deer) (*Cervus elaphus*) in [Killarney National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killarney_National_Park)

Several different habitat types are found in Ireland, including farmland, open woodland, [temperate broadleaf and mixed forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate_broadleaf_and_mixed_forests), [conifer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conifer) plantations, [peat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peat) bogs and a variety of coastal habitats. However, agriculture drives current land use patterns in Ireland, limiting natural habitat preserves,[^cite_note-www2000-139] particularly for larger wild mammals with greater territorial needs. With no large [apex predators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apex_predator) in Ireland other than humans and dogs, such populations of animals as semi-wild deer that cannot be controlled by smaller predators, such as the fox, are controlled by annual [culling](2980973.md).&#x20;

There are no snakes in Ireland, and only one species of reptile (the [common lizard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viviparous_lizard)) is native to the island. Extinct species include the [Irish elk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_elk), the [great auk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_auk), [brown bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_bear) and the [wolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolves_in_Ireland). Some previously extinct birds, such as the [golden eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_eagle), have been reintroduced after decades of [extirpation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_extinction).[^cite_note-140]&#x20;

Ireland is now one of the least forested countries in Europe.[^cite_note-141][^cite_note-142] Until the end of the Middle Ages, Ireland was heavily forested. Native species include [deciduous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deciduous) trees such as [oak](39696.md), [ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraxinus_excelsior), [hazel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corylus_avellana), [birch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birch), [alder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alnus_glutinosa), [willow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willow), [aspen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Populus_tremula), [rowan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorbus_aucuparia) and [hawthorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crataegus_monogyna), as well as [evergreen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evergreen) trees such [Scots pine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scots_pine), [yew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxus_baccata), [holly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilex_aquifolium) and [strawberry trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbutus_unedo).[^cite_note-143] Only about 10% of Ireland today is woodland;[^cite_note-forest-14] most of this is non-native conifer [plantations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantation), and only 2% is native woodland.[^cite_note-forest2-15][^cite_note-forest3-16] The average woodland cover of European countries is over 33%.[^cite_note-forest-14] In the Republic, about 389,356 hectares (3,893.56 km<sup>2</sup>) is owned by the state, mainly by the forestry service [Coillte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coillte).[^cite_note-forest-14] Remnants of native forest can be found scattered around the island, particularly in the [Killarney National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killarney_National_Park).&#x20;



Furze (*[Ulex europaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulex_europaeus)*)

Much of the land is now covered with pasture and there are many species of wild-flower. Gorse (*[Ulex europaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulex_europaeus)*), a wild [furze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Furze), is commonly found growing in the uplands and ferns are plentiful in the more moist regions, especially in the western parts. It is home to hundreds of plant species, some of them unique to the island, and has been "invaded" by some grasses, such as *[Spartina anglica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spartina_anglica)*.[^cite_note-144]&#x20;

The algal and seaweed flora is that of the cold-temperate variety. The total number of species is 574[^cite_note-Guiry_and_Nic_Dhonncha_01-145] The island has been invaded by some algae, some of which are now well established.[^cite_note-Minchin_01-146]&#x20;

Because of its mild climate, many species, including [sub-tropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtropics) species such as [palm trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arecaceae), are grown in Ireland. [Phytogeographically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phytogeography), Ireland belongs to the Atlantic European province of the [Circumboreal Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumboreal_Region) within the [Boreal Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boreal_Kingdom). The island can be subdivided into two [ecoregions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecoregion): the Celtic broadleaf forests and North Atlantic moist mixed forests.&#x20;

### Impact of agriculture



[Silage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silage) harvesting in Clonard, [County Meath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Meath)

The long history of agricultural production, coupled with modern intensive agricultural methods such as pesticide and fertiliser use and runoff from contaminants into streams, rivers and lakes, has placed pressure on biodiversity in Ireland.[^cite_note-147][^cite_note-148] A land of green fields for crop cultivation and cattle rearing limits the space available for the establishment of native wild species. Hedgerows, however, traditionally used for maintaining and demarcating land boundaries, act as a refuge for native wild flora. This ecosystem stretches across the countryside and acts as a network of connections to preserve remnants of the ecosystem that once covered the island. Subsidies under the [Common Agricultural Policy](141924.md), which supported agricultural practices that preserved hedgerow environments, are undergoing reforms. The Common Agricultural Policy had in the past subsidised potentially destructive agricultural practices, for example by emphasising production without placing limits on indiscriminate use of fertilisers and pesticides; but reforms have gradually decoupled subsidies from production levels and introduced environmental and other requirements.[^cite_note-cap_reforms-149] 32% of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions are correlated to agriculture.[^cite_note-150] Forested areas typically consist of monoculture plantations of non-native species, which may result in habitats that are not suitable for supporting native species of invertebrates. Natural areas require fencing to prevent over-grazing by [deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deer_of_Ireland) and sheep that roam over uncultivated areas. Grazing in this manner is one of the main factors preventing the natural regeneration of forests across many regions of the country.[^cite_note-151]&#x20;

## Demographics

Main articles: [Irish people](775859.md), [Demographics of the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland), and [Demographics of Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_Northern_Ireland)



Proportion of respondents to the Ireland census 2011 or the Northern Ireland census 2011 who stated they were Catholic. Areas in which Catholics are in the majority are blue. Areas in which Catholics are in a minority are red.

The population of Ireland is just over 7 million, of which approximately 5.1 million reside in the Republic of Ireland and 1.9 million reside in Northern Ireland.[^cite_note-2022population-13]&#x20;

People have lived in Ireland for over 9,000 years. Early historical and genealogical records note the existence of major groups such as the *[Cruthin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruthin)*, *[Corcu Loígde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corcu_Lo%C3%ADgde)*, *[Dál Riata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1l_Riata)*, *[Dáirine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1irine)*, *[Deirgtine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deirgtine)*, *[Delbhna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delbhna)*, *[Érainn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89rainn)*, *[Laigin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laigin)*, *[Ulaid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulaid)*. Later major groups included the *[Connachta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connachta)*, *[Ciannachta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciannachta)*, *[Eóganachta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E%C3%B3ganachta)*. Smaller groups included the *aithechthúatha* (see *[Attacotti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attacotti)*), *[Cálraighe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C3%A1lraighe)*, *[Cíarraige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C3%ADarraige)*, *[Conmaicne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conmaicne)*, *[Dartraighe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartraighe)*, *[Déisi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A9isi)*, *[Éile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89ile)*, *[Fir Bolg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fir_Bolg)*, *[Fortuatha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortuatha)*, *[Gailenga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gailenga)*, *[Gamanraige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamanraige)*, *[Mairtine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mairtine)*, *[Múscraige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%BAscraige)*, *[Partraige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partraige)*, *[Soghain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soghain)*, *[Uaithni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uaithni)*, *[Uí Maine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U%C3%AD_Maine)*, *[Uí Liatháin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U%C3%AD_Liath%C3%A1in)*. Many survived into late medieval times, others vanished as they became politically unimportant. Over the past 1,200 years, [Vikings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vikings), [Normans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normans), [Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_people), [Flemings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flemings), [Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_people), English, [Africans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_diaspora) and Eastern Europeans have all added to the population and have had significant influences on Irish culture.&#x20;

The population of Ireland rose rapidly from the 16th century until the mid-19th century, interrupted briefly by the [Famine of 1740–41](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Famine_\(1740%E2%80%9341\)), which killed roughly two-fifths of the island's population. The population rebounded and multiplied over the next century, but the Great Famine of the 1840s caused one million deaths and forced over one million more to emigrate in its immediate wake. Over the following century, the population was reduced by over half, at a time when the general trend in European countries was for populations to rise by an average of three-fold.&#x20;

Ireland's largest religious group is Christianity. The largest denomination is [Roman Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholicism), representing over 73% of the island (and about 87% of the Republic of Ireland). Most of the rest of the population adhere to one of the various [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant) denominations (about 48% of Northern Ireland).[^cite_note-niprotestants-152] The largest is the [Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglicanism) [Church of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Ireland). The [Muslim community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Ireland) is growing in Ireland, mostly through increased immigration, with a 50% increase in the republic between the 2006 and 2011 census.[^cite_note-153] The island has a small [Jewish community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Ireland). About 4% of the Republic's population and about 14% of the Northern Ireland population[^cite_note-niprotestants-152] describe themselves as of no religion. In a 2010 survey conducted on behalf of the [Irish Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Times), 32% of respondents said they went to a religious service more than once per week.&#x20;

In a system that developed between the 13th and 17th centuries,[^cite_note-154] Ireland has [32 traditional counties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counties_of_Ireland). Twenty-six of these counties are in the Republic of Ireland, and [six are in Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counties_of_Northern_Ireland). The six counties that constitute Northern Ireland are all in the province of Ulster (which has nine counties in total). However, they are still treated as counties for cultural and some official purposes, for example, postal addresses and by the [Ordnance Survey Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordnance_Survey_Ireland). Counties in Northern Ireland are [no longer used](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_government_in_Northern_Ireland) for local governmental purposes,[^cite_note-155] but, as in the Republic, their traditional boundaries are still used for informal purposes such as sports leagues and in cultural or tourism contexts.[^cite_note-156]&#x20;

City status in Ireland is decided by [legislative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislation) or [royal charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_charter). Dublin, with over one million residents in the [Greater Dublin Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Dublin_Area), is the largest city on the island. Belfast, with 579,726 residents, is the largest city in Northern Ireland. City status does not directly equate with population size. For example, [Armagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armagh), with 14,590 is the seat of the Church of Ireland and the [Roman Catholic](606848.md) [Primate of All Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primate_of_All_Ireland) and was re-granted [city status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_status_in_the_United_Kingdom#Northern_Ireland) by Queen [Elizabeth II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_II) in 1994 (having lost that status in [local government reforms of 1840](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipal_Corporations_\(Ireland\)_Act_1840)). In the Republic of Ireland, [Kilkenny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilkenny), the seat of the [Butler dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butler_dynasty), while no longer a city for administrative purposes (since the 2001 [Local Government Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Government_Act_2001)), is entitled by law to continue to use the description.&#x20;

### Migration

The population of Ireland collapsed dramatically during the second half of the 19th century. A population of over eight million in 1841 was reduced to slightly over four million by 1921. In part, the fall in population was caused by death from the Great Famine of 1845 to 1852, which took roughly one million lives. The remaining decline of around three million was due to the entrenched culture of emigration caused by the dire economic state of the country, lasting until the late 20th century.&#x20;

Emigration from Ireland in the 19th century contributed to the populations of England, the United States, Canada and Australia, in all of which a large [Irish diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_diaspora) lives. As of 2006, 4.3 million Canadians, or 14% of the population, were of Irish descent,[^cite_note-160] while around one-third of the Australian population had an element of Irish descent.[^cite_note-SMH_Irish-161] As of 2013, there were 40 million Irish-Americans[^cite_note-162] and 33 million Americans who claimed Irish ancestry.[^cite_note-163]&#x20;

With growing prosperity since the last decade of the 20th century, Ireland became a destination for immigrants. Since the European Union expanded to include Poland in 2004, [Polish people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_people) have comprised the largest number of immigrants (over 150,000)[^cite_note-164] from Central Europe. There has also been significant immigration from Lithuania, Czech Republic and Latvia.[^cite_note-165]&#x20;

[The Republic of Ireland in particular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland#Immigration) has seen large-scale immigration, with 420,000 foreign nationals as of 2006, about 10% of the population.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-166] Nearly a quarter of births (24 percent) in 2009 were to mothers born outside of Ireland.[^cite_note-167] Up to 50,000 eastern and central European migrant workers left Ireland in response to the post-2008 Irish economic downturn.[^cite_note-affluenceends-168]&#x20;

### Languages

Main article: [Languages of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Ireland)



Proportion of respondents who said they could speak Irish in the Ireland census in 2011 or the Northern Ireland census in 2011

The two official languages of the Republic of Ireland are Irish and English. Each language has produced noteworthy literature. Irish, though now only the language of a minority, was the vernacular of the Irish people for thousands of years and was possibly introduced during the [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age). It began to be written down after Christianisation in the 5th century and spread to Scotland and the Isle of Man, where it evolved into the [Scottish Gaelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Gaelic) and [Manx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manx_Language) languages, respectively.&#x20;

The Irish language has a vast treasury of written texts from many centuries and is divided by linguists into [Old Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Irish) from the 6th to 10th century, [Middle Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Irish) from the 10th to 13th century, Early Modern Irish until the 17th century, and the Modern Irish spoken today. It remained the dominant language of Ireland for most of those periods, having influences from [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Language), [Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse), [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Norman_language) and English. It declined under British rule but remained the majority tongue until the early 19th century, and since then has been a minority language.&#x20;

The [Gaelic Revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_Revival) of the late 19th and early 20th centuries had a long-term influence. Irish is taught in mainstream Irish schools as a compulsory subject, but teaching methods have been criticised for their ineffectiveness, with most students showing little evidence of fluency even after 14 years of instruction.[^cite_note-169]&#x20;

There is now a growing population of urban Irish speakers in both the Republic and Northern Ireland, especially in Dublin[^cite_note-schism-170][^cite_note-171] and Belfast,[^cite_note-172] with the children of such Irish speakers sometimes attending Irish-medium schools (*[Gaelscoil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelscoil) or Gaelscoileanna*). It has been argued that they tend to be more highly educated than monolingual English speakers.[^cite_note-cso.ie-173] Recent research suggests that urban Irish is developing in a direction of its own, both in pronunciation and grammar.[^cite_note-irishtimes.com-174]&#x20;

Traditional rural Irish-speaking areas, known collectively as the *[Gaeltacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaeltacht)*, are in linguistic decline. The main *Gaeltacht* areas are in the west, south-west and north-west, in Galway, Mayo, Donegal, western Cork and Kerry with smaller *Gaeltacht* areas near [Dungarvan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dungarvan) in Waterford and in Meath.[^cite_note-175]&#x20;

[English in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiberno-English) was first introduced during the Norman invasion. It was spoken by a few peasants and merchants brought over from England and was largely replaced by Irish before the Tudor conquest of Ireland. It was introduced as the official language during the Tudor and Cromwellian conquests. The Ulster plantations gave it a permanent foothold in Ulster, and it remained the official and upper-class language elsewhere, the Irish-speaking chieftains and nobility having been deposed. Language shift during the 19th century replaced Irish with English as the first language for a vast majority of the population.[^cite_note-176]&#x20;

Fewer than 2% of the population of the Republic of Ireland today speak Irish on a daily basis, and under 10% regularly, outside of the education system[^cite_note-177] and 38% of those over 15 years are classified as "Irish speakers". In Northern Ireland, English is the de facto official language, but official recognition is afforded to Irish, including specific protective measures under Part III of the [European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Charter_for_Regional_or_Minority_Languages). A lesser status (including recognition under Part II of the Charter) is given to [Ulster Scots dialects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Scots_dialects), which are spoken by roughly 2% of Northern Ireland residents, and also spoken by some in the Republic of Ireland.[^cite_note-178] Since the 1960s with the increase in immigration, many more languages have been introduced, particularly deriving from Asia and Eastern Europe.&#x20;

Also native to Ireland are [Shelta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelta_language), the language of the nomadic [Irish Travellers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Travellers),[^cite_note-McArthur-179] [Irish Sign Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Sign_Language), and [Northern Ireland Sign Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Sign_Language).&#x20;

## Culture

Main articles: [Culture of Ireland](991296.md) and [Culture of Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Northern_Ireland)

Tall stone cross, with intricate carved patterns, protected by metal railings surrounded by short cut grass. Trees are to either side, cows in open countryside are in the middle distance.

[Ardboe High Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ardboe_High_Cross), County Tyrone

Ireland's culture comprises elements of the culture of ancient peoples, later immigrant and broadcast cultural influences (chiefly Gaelic culture, [Anglicisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglicisation), [Americanisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americanisation) and aspects of broader [European culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_culture)). In broad terms, Ireland is regarded as one of the [Celtic nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_nations) of Europe, alongside Scotland, Wales, Cornwall, [Isle of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_of_Man) and Brittany. This combination of cultural influences is visible in the intricate designs termed *Irish [interlace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlace_\(visual_arts\))* or *[Celtic knotwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_knot).* These can be seen in the ornamentation of medieval religious and secular works. The style is still popular today in jewellery and graphic art,[^cite_note-BBC_1-180] as is the distinctive style of [traditional Irish music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Irish_music) and dance, and has become indicative of modern "Celtic" culture in general.&#x20;

[Religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Ireland) has played a significant role in the cultural life of the island since ancient times (and since the 17th century plantations, has been the focus of political identity and divisions on the island). Ireland's pre-Christian heritage fused with the Celtic Church following the missions of Saint Patrick in the fifth century. The Hiberno-Scottish missions, begun by the Irish monk Saint Columba, spread the Irish vision of Christianity to [pagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pagan) England and the Frankish Empire. These missions brought written language to an illiterate population of Europe during the Dark Ages that followed the [fall of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Rome), earning Ireland the sobriquet, "the island of saints and scholars".&#x20;

Since the 20th century [Irish pubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_pub) worldwide have become outposts of Irish culture, especially those with a full range of cultural and gastronomic offerings.&#x20;

#### Literature

Main article: [Literature of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature_of_Ireland)

Ireland has made a substantial contribution to world literature in all its branches, both in Irish and English. Poetry in Irish is among the oldest [vernacular poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernacular_literature) in Europe, with the earliest examples dating from the 6th century. Irish remained the dominant literary language down to the 19th century, despite the spread of English from the 17th century on. Prominent names from the medieval period and later include [Gofraidh Fionn Ó Dálaigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gofraidh_Fionn_%C3%93_D%C3%A1laigh) (14th century), [Dáibhí Ó Bruadair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1ibh%C3%AD_%C3%93_Bruadair) (17th century) and [Aogán Ó Rathaille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aog%C3%A1n_%C3%93_Rathaille) (18th century). [Eibhlín Dubh Ní Chonaill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eibhl%C3%ADn_Dubh_N%C3%AD_Chonaill) (c. 1743 – c. 1800) was an outstanding poet in the oral tradition. The latter part of the 19th century saw a rapid replacement of Irish by English. By 1900, however, cultural nationalists had begun the [Gaelic revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_revival), which saw the beginnings of modern literature in Irish. This was to produce a number of notable writers, including [Máirtín Ó Cadhain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A1irt%C3%ADn_%C3%93_Cadhain), [Máire Mhac an tSaoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A1ire_Mhac_an_tSaoi) and others. Irish-language publishers such as [Coiscéim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coisc%C3%A9im) and [Cló Iar-Chonnacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cl%C3%B3_Iar-Chonnacht) continue to produce scores of titles every year.&#x20;

In English, [Jonathan Swift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Swift), often called the foremost satirist in the English language, gained fame for works such as *[Gulliver's Travels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulliver%27s_Travels)* and *[A Modest Proposal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Modest_Proposal)*. Other notable 18th-century writers of Irish origin included [Oliver Goldsmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Goldsmith) and [Richard Brinsley Sheridan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Brinsley_Sheridan), though they spent most of their lives in England. The Anglo-Irish novel came to the fore in the 19th century, featuring such writers as [Charles Kickham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Kickham), [William Carleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Carleton), and (in collaboration) [Edith Somerville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edith_Somerville) and [Violet Florence Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violet_Florence_Martin). The playwright and poet [Oscar Wilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Wilde), noted for his epigrams, was born in Ireland.&#x20;

In the 20th century, Ireland produced four winners of the [Nobel Prize for Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Literature): [George Bernard Shaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Bernard_Shaw), [William Butler Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Butler_Yeats), [Samuel Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett) and [Seamus Heaney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seamus_Heaney). Although not a Nobel Prize winner, [James Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce) is widely considered to be one of the most significant writers of the 20th century. Joyce's 1922 novel *[Ulysses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_\(novel\))* is considered one of the most important works of [Modernist literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernist_literature) and his life is celebrated annually on 16 June in Dublin as "[Bloomsday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomsday)".[^cite_note-181] A comparable writer in Irish is [Máirtín Ó Cadhain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A1irt%C3%ADn_%C3%93_Cadhain), whose 1949 novel *[Cré na Cille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cr%C3%A9_na_Cille)* is regarded as a modernist masterpiece and has been translated into several languages.&#x20;

Modern Irish literature is often connected with its rural heritage[^cite_note-182] through English-language writers such as [John McGahern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_McGahern) and Seamus Heaney and Irish-language writers such as [Máirtín Ó Direáin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A1irt%C3%ADn_%C3%93_Dire%C3%A1in) and others from the Gaeltacht.&#x20;

#### Music and dance

Main articles: [Music of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Ireland) and [Irish dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_dance)

Music has been in evidence in Ireland since prehistoric times.[^cite_note-183] Although in the early Middle Ages the church was "quite unlike its counterpart in continental Europe",[^cite_note-184] there was a considerable interchange between monastic settlements in Ireland and the rest of Europe that contributed to what is known as [Gregorian chant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_chant). Outside religious establishments, musical genres in early Gaelic Ireland are referred to as a triad of weeping music (*goltraige*), laughing music (*geantraige*) and sleeping music (*suantraige*).[^cite_note-185] Vocal and instrumental music (e.g. for the harp, pipes, and various [string instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_instrument)) was transmitted orally, but the [Irish harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_harp), in particular, was of such significance that it became Ireland's national symbol. Classical music following European models first developed in urban areas, in establishments of Anglo-Irish rule such as [Dublin Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Castle), [St Patrick's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Patrick%27s_Cathedral,_Dublin) and [Christ Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_Church_Cathedral,_Dublin) as well as the country houses of the Anglo-Irish ascendancy, with the first performance of [Handel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Frideric_Handel)'s *[Messiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messiah_\(Handel\))* (1742) being among the highlights of the baroque era. In the 19th century, public concerts provided access to classical music to all classes of society. Yet, for political and financial reasons Ireland has been too small to provide a living to many musicians, so the names of the better-known [Irish composers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Irish_classical_composers) of this time belong to emigrants.&#x20;

Irish [traditional music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_music) and dance have seen a surge in popularity and global coverage since the 1960s. In the middle years of the 20th century, as Irish society was modernising, traditional music had fallen out of favour, especially in urban areas.[^cite_note-186] However during the 1960s, there was a revival of interest in Irish traditional music led by groups such as [the Dubliners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dubliners), [the Chieftains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chieftains), [the Wolfe Tones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wolfe_Tones), the [Clancy Brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clancy_Brothers), [Sweeney's Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweeney%27s_Men) and individuals like [Seán Ó Riada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Se%C3%A1n_%C3%93_Riada) and [Christy Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christy_Moore). Groups and musicians including [Horslips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horslips), [Van Morrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Morrison) and [Thin Lizzy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin_Lizzy) incorporated elements of Irish traditional music into contemporary rock music and, during the 1970s and 1980s, the distinction between traditional and rock musicians became blurred, with many individuals regularly crossing over between these styles of playing. This trend can be seen more recently in the work of artists like [Enya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enya), [the Saw Doctors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Saw_Doctors), [the Corrs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Corrs), [Sinéad O'Connor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sin%C3%A9ad_O%27Connor), [Clannad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clannad_\(musical_group\)), [the Cranberries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cranberries) and [the Pogues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pogues) among others.&#x20;

#### Art

Main articles: [Art of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_of_Ireland) and [Architecture of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_Ireland)

The earliest known Irish graphic art and sculpture are Neolithic carvings found at sites such as Newgrange[^cite_note-187] and is traced through Bronze Age artefacts and the religious carvings and [illuminated manuscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illuminated_manuscripts) of the medieval period. During the course of the 19th and 20th centuries, a strong tradition of painting emerged, including such figures as [John Butler Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Butler_Yeats), [William Orpen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Orpen), [Jack Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Yeats) and [Louis le Brocquy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_le_Brocquy). Contemporary Irish visual artists of note include [Sean Scully](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sean_Scully), [Kevin Abosch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Abosch), and [Alice Maher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice_Maher).&#x20;

#### Drama and theatre

Main article: [Irish theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_theatre)

The Republic of Ireland's national theatre is the [Abbey Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey_Theatre), which was founded in 1904, and the national Irish-language theatre is *[An Taibhdhearc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Taibhdhearc)*, which was established in 1928 in [Galway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galway).[^cite_note-188][^cite_note-189] Playwrights such as [Seán O'Casey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Se%C3%A1n_O%27Casey), [Brian Friel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Friel), [Sebastian Barry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebastian_Barry), [Conor McPherson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conor_McPherson) and [Billy Roche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billy_Roche) are internationally renowned.[^cite_note-190]&#x20;

### Science



[Robert Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Boyle) formulated Boyle's Law.

The Irish philosopher and theologian [Johannes Scotus Eriugena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Scotus_Eriugena) was considered one of the leading intellectuals of the early Middle Ages. Sir [Ernest Henry Shackleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Henry_Shackleton), an Irish explorer, was one of the principal figures of Antarctic exploration. He, along with his expedition, made the first ascent of [Mount Erebus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Erebus) and the discovery of the approximate location of the [South Magnetic Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Magnetic_Pole). [Robert Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Boyle) was a 17th-century natural philosopher, chemist, physicist, inventor and early [gentleman scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentleman_scientist). He is largely regarded as one of the founders of modern chemistry and is best known for the formulation of [Boyle's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boyle%27s_law).[^cite_note-ucc_boyle_boi-191]&#x20;

19th-century physicist, [John Tyndall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tyndall), discovered the [Tyndall effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyndall_effect). [Father Nicholas Joseph Callan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Father_Nicholas_Joseph_Callan), professor of natural philosophy in [Maynooth College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maynooth_College), is best known for his invention of the [induction coil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induction_coil), [transformer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer) and he discovered an early method of [galvanisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galvanisation) in the 19th century.&#x20;

Other notable Irish [physicists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicists) include [Ernest Walton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Walton), winner of the 1951 [Nobel Prize in Physics](52497.md). With [Sir John Douglas Cockcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_John_Douglas_Cockcroft), he was the first to split the nucleus of the atom by artificial means and made contributions to the development of a new theory of [wave equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_equation).[^cite_note-192] William Thomson, or [Lord Kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Kelvin), is the person whom the absolute temperature unit, the [kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelvin), is named after. Sir [Joseph Larmor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Larmor), a physicist and mathematician, made innovations in the understanding of electricity, dynamics, thermodynamics and the electron theory of matter. His most influential work was Aether and Matter, a book on theoretical physics published in 1900.[^cite_note-physicsworld-193]&#x20;

[George Johnstone Stoney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Johnstone_Stoney) introduced the term *[electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron)* in 1891. [John Stewart Bell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Stewart_Bell) was the originator of [Bell's Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_Theorem) and a paper concerning the discovery of the [Bell-Jackiw-Adler anomaly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiral_anomaly) and was nominated for a Nobel prize.[^cite_note-194] The astronomer [Jocelyn Bell Burnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jocelyn_Bell_Burnell), from [Lurgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lurgan), County Armagh, discovered pulsars in 1967. Notable mathematicians include Sir [William Rowan Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Rowan_Hamilton), famous for work in [classical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_mechanics) and the invention of [quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternions). [Francis Ysidro Edgeworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Ysidro_Edgeworth)'s contribution, the [Edgeworth Box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgeworth_Box). remains influential in neo-classical microeconomic theory to this day; while [Richard Cantillon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Cantillon) inspired [Adam Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Smith), among others. [John B. Cosgrave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_B._Cosgrave) was a specialist in [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory) and discovered a 2000-digit [prime number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number) in 1999 and a record composite [Fermat number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_number) in 2003. [John Lighton Synge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lighton_Synge) made progress in different fields of science, including mechanics and geometrical methods in general relativity. He had mathematician [John Nash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Forbes_Nash_Jr.) as one of his students. [Kathleen Lonsdale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kathleen_Lonsdale), born in Ireland and most known for her work with [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography), became the first female president of the British Association for the Advancement of Science.[^cite_note-195]&#x20;

Ireland has nine universities, seven in the Republic of Ireland and two in Northern Ireland, including [Trinity College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin) and the [University College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_Dublin), as well as numerous third-level colleges and institutes and a branch of the Open University, the [Open University in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_University_in_Ireland). Ireland was ranked 18th in the [Global Innovation Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Innovation_Index) in 2025.[^cite_note-196][^cite_note-197]&#x20;

### Sports

Main article: [Sport in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_in_Ireland)

See also: [List of Irish sports people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Irish_people#Sport)

[Gaelic football](37688.md) is the most popular sport in Ireland in terms of match attendance and community involvement, with about 2,600 clubs on the island. In 2003 it represented 34% of total sports attendances at events in Ireland and abroad, followed by [hurling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurling) at 23%, soccer at 16% and [rugby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_football) at 8%.[^cite_note-esri-198] The [All-Ireland Football Final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-Ireland_Football_Final) is the most watched event in the sporting calendar.[^cite_note-199] Soccer is the most widely played team game on the island and the most popular in Northern Ireland.[^cite_note-esri-198][^cite_note-FootballSoccer-200]&#x20;

Other sporting activities with the highest levels of playing participation include swimming, golf, aerobics, cycling, and billiards/snooker.[^cite_note-201] Many other sports are also played and followed, including [boxing](4243.md), [cricket](25675557.md), fishing, [greyhound racing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greyhound_racing), [handball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_handball), [hockey](10886.md), horse racing, [motor sport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_sport), [show jumping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Show_jumping) and tennis.&#x20;

The island fields a single international team in most sports. One notable exception to this is association football, although both associations continued to field international teams under the name "Ireland" until the 1950s. The sport is also the most notable exception where the [Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Ireland_national_football_team) and [Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_national_football_team) field separate international teams. Northern Ireland has produced two World Snooker Champions.&#x20;

Ireland is ranked 10th in the ICC Men's Test Team Rankings 2025 with a rating of 30.[^cite_note-202]&#x20;

#### Field sports

Main articles: [Gaelic games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_games), [Rugby union in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_union_in_Ireland), [Rugby league in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_league_in_Ireland), [Association football in the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_football_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland), and [Association football in Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_football_in_Northern_Ireland)



[Tyrone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrone_GAA) v [Kerry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerry_GAA) in the [2005 All-Ireland Senior Football Championship Final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_All-Ireland_Senior_Football_Championship_Final)

Gaelic football, hurling and Gaelic handball are the best-known Irish traditional sports, collectively known as Gaelic games. Gaelic games are governed by the [Gaelic Athletic Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_Athletic_Association) (GAA), with the exception of women's Gaelic football and camogie (women's variant of hurling), which are governed by separate organisations. The headquarters of the GAA (and the main stadium) is located at[^cite_note-203] [Croke Park](322713.md) in north Dublin and has a capacity of 82,500. Many major GAA games are played there, including the semi-finals and finals of the [All-Ireland Senior Football Championship](885732.md) and [All-Ireland Senior Hurling Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-Ireland_Senior_Hurling_Championship). During the redevelopment of the [Lansdowne Road stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lansdowne_Road) in 2007–2010, international rugby and soccer were played there.[^cite_note-204] All GAA players, even at the highest level, are amateurs, receiving no wages, although they are permitted to receive a limited amount of sport-related income from commercial sponsorship.&#x20;

The [Irish Football Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Football_Association) (IFA) was originally the governing body for soccer across the island. The game has been played in an organised fashion in Ireland since the 1870s, with [Cliftonville F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliftonville_F.C.) in Belfast being Ireland's oldest club. It was most popular, especially in its first decades, around Belfast and in Ulster. However, some clubs based outside Belfast thought that the IFA largely favoured Ulster-based clubs in such matters as selection for the national team. In 1921, following an incident in which, despite an earlier promise, the IFA moved an [Irish Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Cup) semi-final replay from Dublin to Belfast,[^cite_note-205] Dublin-based clubs broke away to form the Football Association of the Irish Free State. Today the southern association is known as the [Football Association of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_Association_of_Ireland) (FAI). Despite being initially blacklisted by the [Home Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Nations)' associations, the FAI was recognised by [FIFA](11049.md) in 1923 and organised its first international fixture in 1926 (against [Italy](362466.md)). However, both the IFA and FAI continued to select their teams from the whole of Ireland, with some players earning international caps for matches with both teams. Both also referred to their respective teams as *Ireland*.&#x20;



[Paul O'Connell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_O%27Connell) reaching for the ball during a [line out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line-out_\(rugby_union\)) against [Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentina_national_rugby_union_team) in 2007

In 1950, FIFA directed the associations only to select players from within their respective territories and, in 1953, directed that the FAI's team be known only as "Republic of Ireland" and that the IFA's team be known as "Northern Ireland" (with certain exceptions). Northern Ireland qualified for the [World Cup](11370.md) finals in [1958](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1958_FIFA_World_Cup) (reaching the quarter-finals), [1982](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_FIFA_World_Cup) and [1986](157231.md) and the [European Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UEFA_European_Championship) in [2016](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UEFA_Euro_2016). The Republic qualified for the World Cup finals in [1990](157232.md) (reaching the quarter-finals), [1994](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1994_FIFA_World_Cup), [2002](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_FIFA_World_Cup) and the European Championship in [1988](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UEFA_Euro_1988), [2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UEFA_Euro_2012) and 2016. Across Ireland, there is significant interest in the [English](11250.md) and, to a lesser extent, [Scottish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Premier_League) soccer leagues.&#x20;

Ireland fields a single [national rugby team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland_national_rugby_union_team) and a single association, the [Irish Rugby Football Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Rugby_Football_Union), governs the sport across the island. The Irish rugby team have played in every [Rugby World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_World_Cup), making the quarter-finals in eight of them.[^cite_note-206] Ireland also hosted games during the [1991](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1991_Rugby_World_Cup) and the [1999 Rugby World Cups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_Rugby_World_Cup) (including a quarter-final). There are four professional Irish teams; all four play in the [Pro14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro14) and at least three compete for the [Heineken Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heineken_Cup). Irish rugby has become increasingly competitive at both the international and provincial levels since the sport went professional in 1994. During that time, [Ulster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Rugby) ([1999](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998%E2%80%9399_Heineken_Cup)),[^cite_note-heineken_champions_archive-207] [Munster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munster_Rugby) ([2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005%E2%80%9306_Heineken_Cup)[^cite_note-208] and [2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007%E2%80%9308_Heineken_Cup))[^cite_note-heineken_champions_archive-207] and [Leinster](985961.md) ([2009](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008%E2%80%9309_Heineken_Cup), [2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010%E2%80%9311_Heineken_Cup) and [2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011%E2%80%9312_Heineken_Cup))[^cite_note-heineken_champions_archive-207] have won the Heineken Cup. In addition to this, the Irish International side has had increased success in the [Six Nations Championship](26547.md) against the other European elite sides. This success, including [Triple Crowns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triple_Crown_\(rugby_union\)) in 2004, 2006 and 2007, culminated with a clean sweep of victories, known as a [Grand Slam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Slam_\(rugby_union\)), in 2009 and 2018.[^cite_note-209]&#x20;

#### Boxing

Amateur boxing on the island of Ireland is governed by the [Irish Athletic Boxing Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Athletic_Boxing_Association). Ireland has won more medals in boxing than in any other Olympic sport. [Michael Carruth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Carruth) won a gold medal and [Wayne McCullough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayne_McCullough) won a silver medal in the [Barcelona Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Summer_Olympics). #### Recreation

The west coast of Ireland, [Lahinch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lahinch) and [Donegal Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donegal_Bay) in particular, have popular surfing beaches, being fully exposed to the Atlantic Ocean. Donegal Bay is shaped like a funnel and catches west/south-west Atlantic winds, creating good surf, especially in winter. Since just before the year 2010, [Bundoran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundoran) has hosted European championship surfing. [Scuba diving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scuba_diving) is increasingly popular in Ireland with clear waters and large populations of sea life, particularly along the western seaboard. There are also many shipwrecks along the coast of Ireland, with some of the best [wreck dives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wreck_dives) being in [Malin Head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malin_Head) and off the County Cork coast.[^cite_note-221]&#x20;

With thousands of lakes, over 14,000 kilometres (8,700 mi) of fish-bearing rivers and over 7,500 kilometres (4,660 mi) of [coastline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Irish_counties_by_coastline), Ireland is a popular [angling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angling) destination. The temperate Irish climate is suited to sport angling. While [salmon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmon) and [trout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trout) fishing remain popular with anglers, salmon fishing, in particular, received a boost in 2006 with the closing of the salmon [driftnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Driftnet) fishery. [Coarse fishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coarse_fishing) continues to increase its profile. Sea angling is developed with many beaches mapped and signposted,[^cite_note-222] and the range of sea angling species is around 80.[^cite_note-223]&#x20;

### Food and drink

Main article: [Irish cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_cuisine)



[Gubbeen cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gubbeen_cheese), an example of the resurgence in Irish cheese making

Food and cuisine in Ireland take their influence from the crops grown and animals farmed in the island's temperate climate and from the social and political circumstances of Irish history. For example, whilst from the Middle Ages until the arrival of the potato in the 16th century the dominant feature of the Irish economy was the herding of cattle, the number of cattle a person owned was equated to their social standing.[^cite_note-food_companion-224] Thus herders would avoid slaughtering a milk-producing cow.[^cite_note-food_companion-224]&#x20;

For this reason, pork and [white meat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_meat) were more common than beef, and thick fatty strips of salted [bacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacon) (known as rashers) and the eating of salted butter (i.e. a dairy product rather than beef itself) have been a central feature of the diet in Ireland since the Middle Ages.[^cite_note-food_companion-224] The practice of bleeding cattle and mixing the blood with milk and butter (not unlike the practice of the [Maasai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maasai_people)) was common[^cite_note-225] and [black pudding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_pudding), made from blood, grain (usually barley) and seasoning, remains a breakfast staple in Ireland. All of these influences can be seen today in the phenomenon of the "[breakfast roll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakfast_roll)".&#x20;

The introduction of the potato in the second half of the 16th century heavily influenced cuisine thereafter. Great poverty encouraged a subsistence approach to food, and by the mid-19th century, the vast majority of the population sufficed with a diet of potatoes and milk.[^cite_note-226] A typical family, consisting of a man, a woman and four children, would eat 18 stone (110 kg) of potatoes per week.[^cite_note-food_companion-224] Consequently, dishes that are considered as national dishes represent a fundamental simplicity to cooking, such as the [Irish stew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_stew), [bacon and cabbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacon_and_cabbage), [boxty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxty), a type of potato pancake, or [colcannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colcannon), a dish of [mashed potatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mashed_potatoes) and [kale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kale) or [cabbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabbage).[^cite_note-food_companion-224]&#x20;

Since the last quarter of the 20th century, with a re-emergence of wealth in Ireland, a "New Irish Cuisine" based on traditional ingredients incorporating international influences[^cite_note-227] has emerged.[^cite_note-228] This cuisine is based on fresh vegetables, fish (especially salmon, trout, [oysters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oyster), [mussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mussel) and other shellfish), as well as traditional soda breads and the wide range of hand-made [cheeses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Irish_cheeses) that are now being produced across the country. An example of this new cuisine is "Dublin Lawyer": lobster cooked in whiskey and cream.[^cite_note-229] The potato remains however a fundamental feature of this cuisine and the Irish remain the highest per capita[^cite_note-food_companion-224] consumers of potatoes in Europe. Traditional regional foods can be found throughout the country, for example [coddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coddle) in Dublin or [drisheen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drisheen) in Cork, both a type of sausage, or [blaa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaa), a doughy white bread particular to Waterford.&#x20;



The [Old Bushmills Distillery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Bushmills_Distillery) in County Antrim

Ireland once dominated the world's market for whiskey, producing 90% of the world's whiskey at the start of the 20th century. However, as a consequence of bootleggers during the [prohibition in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prohibition_in_the_United_States) (who sold poor-quality whiskey bearing Irish-sounding names thus eroding the pre-prohibition popularity for Irish brands)[^cite_note-230] and tariffs on Irish whiskey across the British Empire during the [Anglo-Irish Trade War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Irish_Trade_War) of the 1930s,[^cite_note-231] sales of Irish whiskey worldwide fell to a mere 2% by the mid-20th century.[^cite_note-232] In 1953, an Irish government survey found that 50% of whiskey drinkers in the United States had never heard of [Irish whiskey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_whiskey).[^cite_note-233]&#x20;

Irish whiskey, as researched in 2009 by the [CNBC](223441.md) American broadcaster, remains popular domestically and has grown in international sales steadily over a few decades.[^cite_note-not_luck-234] Typically CNBC states Irish whiskey is not as smoky as a [Scotch whisky](28896.md), but not as sweet as [American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_whiskey) or [Canadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_whiskey) whiskies.[^cite_note-not_luck-234] Whiskey forms the basis of [cream liqueurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Cream), such as [Baileys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baileys_Irish_Cream), and the [Irish coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_coffee) (a [cocktail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocktail) of coffee and whiskey reputedly invented at [Foynes flying-boat station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foynes)) is probably the best-known Irish cocktail.&#x20;

[Stout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter_\(beer\)), a kind of [porter beer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter_beer), particularly [Guinness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness), is typically associated with Ireland, although historically it was more closely associated with London. Porter remains very popular, although it has lost sales since the mid-20th century to [lager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lager). [Cider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cider), particularly *[Magners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magners)* (marketed in the Republic of Ireland as *Bulmers*), is also a popular drink. [Red lemonade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_lemonade), a soft-drink, is consumed on its own and as a mixer, particularly with whiskey.[^cite_note-235]&#x20;

## Economy

Main articles: [Economy of the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland) and [Economy of Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Northern_Ireland)

See also: [International Financial Services Centre, Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Financial_Services_Centre,_Dublin)

The GDP of the Republic of Ireland as of 2021 was €423.5 billion (nominal),[^cite_note-IMFWEOIE-236] and in Northern Ireland in 2021, it was £52 billion (GVA Balanced).[^cite_note-NI_GDP-237] The GDP per capita in the Republic of Ireland was €84,049.9 (nominal) as of 2021,[^cite_note-IMFWEOIE-236] and in Northern Ireland 2021 was £27,154 (GVA Balanced).[^cite_note-NI_GDP-237] The Republic of Ireland and the United Kingdom measure these numbers differently.&#x20;

Despite the two jurisdictions using two distinct currencies (the euro and [pound sterling](270673.md)), a growing amount of commercial activity is carried out on an all-Ireland basis. This has been facilitated by the two jurisdictions' former shared membership of the European Union, and there have been calls from members of the business community and policymakers for the creation of an "all-Ireland economy" to take advantage of [economies of scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economies_of_scale) and boost competitiveness.[^cite_note-238]&#x20;

### Economic history

Main article: [Economic history of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_Ireland)

Prior to partition in 1921, Ireland had a long history as an economic colony – first, partially, of the Norse, via their cities (9th to 10th centuries CE), and later, to varying extents, of polities related to England. Though the climate and soil favoured certain forms of agriculture,[^cite_note-245] [trade barriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_barrier) frequently hobbled its development. Repeated invasions and plantations disrupted [land-ownership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_tenure), and [multiple failed uprisings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Rebellion_\(disambiguation\)) also contributed to repeated phases of [deportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penal_transportation) and of [emigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_emigrant).&#x20;

Salient events in the economic history of Ireland include:&#x20;

- 16th and 17th centuries: confiscation and redistribution of land in the [Plantations of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantations_of_Ireland)

- 1845–1849: [The Great Famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_\(Ireland\)) occasioned depopulation and mass emigration

- 1846: Westminster's repeal of the [Corn Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_Laws) disrupted Irish agriculture

### Major industries

#### Tourism

Main article: [Tourist destinations in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourist_destinations_in_Ireland)

There are two [World Heritage Sites](44940.md) on the island: the *[Brú na Bóinne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Br%C3%BA_na_B%C3%B3inne)* complex and the [Giant's Causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant%27s_Causeway).[^cite_note-247] Several other places are on the tentative list, for example the Burren, the Ceide Fields[^cite_note-248] and [Mount Stewart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Stewart).[^cite_note-249]&#x20;

Some of the most visited sites in Ireland include [Bunratty Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunratty_Castle), the [Rock of Cashel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_of_Cashel), the [Cliffs of Moher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliffs_of_Moher), [Holy Cross Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Cross_Abbey) and [Blarney Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blarney_Castle).[^cite_note-facts2006-250] Historically important monastic sites include [Glendalough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glendalough) and [Clonmacnoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clonmacnoise), which are maintained as [national monuments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Monument_\(Ireland\)) in the Republic of Ireland.[^cite_note-251]&#x20;

The Dublin region receives the most tourists[^cite_note-facts2006-250] and is home to several of the most popular attractions such as the [Guinness Storehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness_Storehouse) and Book of Kells.[^cite_note-facts2006-250] The west and south west, which includes the [Lakes of Killarney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakes_of_Killarney) and the [Dingle peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dingle_peninsula) in County Kerry and [Connemara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connemara) and the [Aran Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aran_Islands) in County Galway, are also popular tourist destinations.[^cite_note-facts2006-250]&#x20;

[Stately homes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stately_home), built during the 17th, 18th and 19th centuries in [Palladian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palladian), [Neoclassical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassical_architecture) and [neo-Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Gothic) styles, such as [Castle Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle_Ward), [Castletown House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castletown_House), [Bantry House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantry_House), [Strokestown Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strokestown_Park) and [Glenveagh Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenveagh_Castle) are also of interest to tourists. Some have been converted into hotels, such as [Ashford Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashford_Castle),[^cite_note-252] [Castle Leslie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle_Leslie)[^cite_note-253] and [Dromoland Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dromoland_Castle).[^cite_note-254]&#x20;

#### Energy

Main article: [Energy in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_in_Ireland)

Although for most of their existence electricity networks in the Republic of Ireland and Northern Ireland were entirely separate, the island has operated for some time as a single market for electricity.[^cite_note-255] Both networks were designed and constructed independently post-partition but they are now connected with three interlinks[^cite_note-256] and are also connected through Great Britain to mainland Europe. The situation in Northern Ireland is complicated by the issue of private companies not supplying [Northern Ireland Electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Electricity) with enough power. In the Republic of Ireland, the [ESB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESB_Group) has failed to modernise its power stations, and the availability of power plants has recently averaged only 66%, one of the worst such rates in Western Europe. [EirGrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EirGrid) has started building a [HVDC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-voltage_direct_current) transmission line between Ireland and Great Britain with a capacity of 500 MW,[^cite_note-257] about 10% of Ireland's peak demand.&#x20;

As with electricity, the natural gas distribution network is also now all-island, with a pipeline linking [Gormanston, County Meath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gormanston,_County_Meath), and [Ballyclare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballyclare), County Antrim.[^cite_note-258] Most of Ireland's gas comes through interconnectors between [Twynholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twynholm) in Scotland and [Ballylumford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballylumford_power_station), County Antrim and [Loughshinny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loughshinny), County Dublin. Supplies come from the Corrib Gas Field, off the coast of County Mayo, with a supply previously also coming from the Kinsale gas field off the County Cork coast.[^cite_note-259][^cite_note-260] The County Mayo field faces some localised opposition over [a controversial decision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrib_gas_controversy) to refine the gas onshore.&#x20;



Turf-cutting near [Maam Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maam_Cross) by the road to Leenane, County Galway

Ireland has an ancient industry based on [peat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peat) (known locally as "turf") as a source of energy for home fires. A form of [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass) energy, this source of heat is still widely used in rural areas. However, because of the ecological importance of peatlands in storing carbon and their rarity, the EU is attempting to protect this habitat by fining Ireland for digging up peat. In cities, heat is generally supplied by natural gas or [heating oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heating_oil), although some urban suppliers distribute sods of turf as "smokeless fuel" for domestic use.&#x20;

The Republic has a strong commitment to renewable energy and ranks as one of the top 10 markets for [clean-technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_technology) investment in the 2014 Global Green Economy Index.[^cite_note-ggei-261] Research and development in [renewable energy](25784.md) (such as [wind power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power)) has increased since 2004. Large [wind farms have been constructed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_wind_farms_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland) in Cork, Donegal, Mayo and Antrim. The construction of wind farms has in some cases been delayed by opposition from local communities, some of whom regard the [wind turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_turbine) as unsightly. The Republic is hindered by an ageing network that was not designed to handle the varying availability of power that comes from wind farms. The ESB's [Turlough Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turlough_Hill) facility is the only power-storage facility in the state.[^cite_note-262]&#x20;
