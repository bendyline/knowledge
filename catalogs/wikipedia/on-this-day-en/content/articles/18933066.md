**Florida** ([/ˈflɒrɪdə/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/3/36/En-us-Florida.ogg/En-us-Florida.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/3/36/En-us-Florida.ogg/En-us-Florida.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup> [*FLORR-id-ə*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key); Spanish: [\[floˈɾiða\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Spanish) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/5/51/Pronunciation_of_Florida_in_Spanish.ogg/Pronunciation_of_Florida_in_Spanish.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/5/51/Pronunciation_of_Florida_in_Spanish.ogg/Pronunciation_of_Florida_in_Spanish.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>) is a [state](18618239.md) in the [Southeastern](816925.md) and [South Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Atlantic_states) regions of the United States. It borders the [Gulf of Mexico](21076367.md) to the west, [Alabama](303.md) to the northwest, [Georgia](48830.md) to the north, the [Atlantic Ocean](698.md) to the east, the [Straits of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straits_of_Florida) to the south, and [The Bahamas](3451.md) to the southeast. About two-thirds of Florida occupies a peninsula between the Gulf of Mexico and the Atlantic Ocean. It has the [longest coastline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_by_coastline) in the [contiguous United States](83759.md), spanning approximately 1,350 miles (2,170 km), not including its many [barrier islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrier_island). It is the only state that borders both the Gulf of Mexico and the Atlantic Ocean. With a population of over 23 million, it is the [third-most populous state in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_population) and ranks [seventh in population density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_states_and_territories_of_the_United_States_by_population_density) as of 2020. Florida spans 65,758 square miles (170,310 km<sup>2</sup>), ranking [22nd in area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_area) among the states. The [Miami metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_metropolitan_area), anchored by the cities of [Miami](53846.md), [Fort Lauderdale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Lauderdale,_Florida), and [West Palm Beach](109634.md), is the state's largest [metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_statistical_area), with a population of 6.138 million; the most populous city is [Jacksonville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacksonville,_Florida). Florida's other major population centers include [Tampa Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tampa_Bay_area), [Orlando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlando,_Florida), [Cape Coral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Coral,_Florida), and the state capital of [Tallahassee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallahassee,_Florida).

Various [Native American groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_Florida) have inhabited Florida for at least 14,000 years. In 1513, [Spanish](963152.md) explorer and [conquistador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conquistador) [Juan Ponce de León](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Ponce_de_Le%C3%B3n) became the first European to make landfall in the region. He called the peninsula *La Pascua Florida* in recognition of the verdant landscape and because it was the Easter season, which the Spaniards called *[Pascua Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascua_Florida)* (Festival of Flowers).[^cite_note-Chang-Rodríguez2006-13][^cite_note-Vega2010-14][^cite_note-Steigman2005-15] giving the territory its present name. Spain subsequently incorporated Florida into the [Spanish Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Empire) in the early 16th century. In 1565, it founded [St. Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Augustine,_Florida), the oldest continuously inhabited European-established settlement in the United States. The region was frequently coveted and attacked by [Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain) throughout the 18th century. Britain briefly gained control of Florida in 1763, but Spain recovered it in 1783 following the British defeat in the [American Revolutionary War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War). Florida remained under Spanish rule until it was [ceded to the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adams%E2%80%93On%C3%ADs_Treaty) in 1821, in exchange for U.S. recognition of Spanish sovereignty in [Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Texas) and the resolution of the border dispute along the [Sabine River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabine_River_\(Texas%E2%80%93Louisiana\)). Florida was admitted as the [27th state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_by_date_of_admission_to_the_Union) on March 3, 1845, and was the principal location of the [Seminole Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminole_Wars) (1816–1858), the longest and most extensive of the [American Indian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Indian_Wars). The state seceded from the [Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_\(American_Civil_War\)) on January 10, 1861, becoming one of the seven original [Confederate States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederate_States_of_America), and was readmitted to the Union after the [Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War) on June 25, 1868.

Since the mid-20th century, Florida has experienced rapid demographic and economic growth. [Its economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Florida), with a [gross state product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_state_product) (GSP) of \$1.647 trillion, is the [fourth largest of any U.S. state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_GDP) and the fifteenth-largest in the world; the main sectors are [tourism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism), [hospitality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospitality_industry), [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture), real estate, and [transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_in_Florida). Florida is world-renowned for its [beach resorts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beach_resort), [amusement parks](137327.md), warm and sunny climate, and nautical recreation; attractions such as [Walt Disney World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Disney_World), the [Kennedy Space Center](16421.md), and [Miami Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Beach,_Florida) draw tens of millions of visitors annually. Florida is a popular destination for [retirees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pensioner), [seasonal vacationers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowbird_\(person\)), and both domestic and international migrants. The state's close proximity to the ocean has shaped [its culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Florida), identity, and daily life; its colonial history and successive waves of migration are reflected in [European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_American), [Latino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic_and_Latino_Americans), [African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_American), [Indigenous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas), and [Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_Americans) influences. Florida has attracted or inspired some of the most prominent American writers, including [Ernest Hemingway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Hemingway), [Marjorie Kinnan Rawlings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marjorie_Kinnan_Rawlings), and [Tennessee Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tennessee_Williams), and continues to attract celebrities and athletes, especially in [golf](19568112.md), [tennis](29773.md), [auto racing](1022.md), and [water sports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_water_sports). Initially a [battleground state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_state) in [American presidential elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_presidential_election), it has turned increasingly [Republican](32070.md) in recent years.

[Florida's climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_Florida) varies from [subtropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtropical) in the north to [tropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical) in the south. It is the only state besides [Hawaii](13270.md) to have a [tropical climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_climate), and the only continental state with a [coral reef](87410.md). Florida has several unique ecosystems, including [Everglades National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everglades_National_Park), the largest tropical wilderness in the U.S. and among the largest in the [Americas](29833.md). Unique wildlife includes the [American alligator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_alligator), [American crocodile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_crocodile), [American flamingo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_flamingo), [roseate spoonbill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roseate_spoonbill), [Florida panther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_panther), [bottlenose dolphin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_bottlenose_dolphin), and [manatee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Indian_manatee). The [Florida Reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Reef) is the only living coral barrier reef in the continental United States, and the third-largest coral barrier reef system in the world, after the [Great Barrier Reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Barrier_Reef) and the [Belize Barrier Reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belize_Barrier_Reef).

## History

Main article: [History of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Florida)

The first inhabitants, the [Paleo-Indians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleo-Indians), entered Florida at least 14,000 years ago.[^cite_note-16] By the 16th century, the earliest time for which there is a historical record, major [groups of people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_Florida#Early_modern_period) living in Florida included the [Apalachee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apalachee) of the [Florida Panhandle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Panhandle), the [Timucua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timucua) of northern and central Florida, the [Ais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ais_people) of the central Atlantic coast, the [Mayaimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayaimi) of the [Lake Okeechobee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Okeechobee) area, the [Tequesta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tequesta) of southeastern Florida, and the [Calusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calusa) of southwest Florida.[^cite_note-17]

### European arrival

Florida was the first region of what is now the [contiguous United States](83759.md) to be visited and settled by Europeans. The first recorded European explorers to reach Florida were [Spaniards](963152.md) led by [Juan Ponce de León](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Ponce_de_Le%C3%B3n), who sighted the peninsula on April 2, 1513, and came ashore the next day to seek information and take possession of this new land.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-Names_on_the_Land:_A_Historical_Account_of_Place-Naming_in_the_United_States-19] Differing theories state he named the region *La Florida* in recognition of the region's abundant [flora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flora) or *La Pascua Florida* a Spanish name for the [Easter](9325.md) season in which it was thought they first landed[^cite_note-20] The story that he was searching for the [Fountain of Youth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fountain_of_Youth) is apocryphal and appeared only long after his death.[^cite_note-21]

In May 1539, [Hernando de Soto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hernando_de_Soto) skirted the coast of Florida, searching for a deep harbor to land. He described a thick wall of red mangroves spread mile after mile, some reaching as high as 70 feet (21 m), with intertwined and elevated roots making landing difficult.[^cite_note-22] Europeans introduced [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_the_United_States), cattle, horses, sheep, the [Spanish language](26825.md), and more to Florida.[^cite_note-23] During the 1520s, an estimated 700,000 Native Americans lived in Florida, but by 1700, the number decreased to only around 2,000 people.[^cite_note-NielsenKE-24]

In the 1500s, Spain established several settlements in Florida, like in 1559 when Don [Tristán de Luna y Arellano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trist%C3%A1n_de_Luna_y_Arellano) established a settlement at present-day [Pensacola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pensacola,_Florida), making it one of the first settlements in Florida. It was mostly abandoned by 1561.[^cite_note-uwf.edu-25][^cite_note-USFhist-26] In 1564–1565, there was a French settlement at [Fort Caroline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Caroline), in present [Duval County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duval_County,_Florida), which was destroyed by the Spanish.[^cite_note-27] Today a reconstructed version of the fort stands in its location within Jacksonville, constructed in the mid-1960s.[^cite_note-28]

In 1565, the settlement of [St. Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Augustine,_Florida) (San Agustín) was established under the leadership of admiral and governor [Pedro Menéndez de Avilés](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_Men%C3%A9ndez_de_Avil%C3%A9s), creating what would become the oldest continuously occupied European settlements in the continental U.S. and establishing the first generation of Floridanos and the [Government of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_colonial_governors_of_Florida).[^cite_note-29] The marriage between Luisa de Abrego, a free black domestic servant from [Seville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seville), and Miguel Rodríguez, a white [Segovian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Segovia), occurred in 1565 in St. Augustine. It is the first recorded Christian marriage in the continental United States.[^cite_note-30]

Some Floridanos married or had unions with [Pensacola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pensacola_people), [Creek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscogee), or [African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_ethnic_groups_of_Africa) women, both slave and free, and their descendants created a mixed-race population of [mestizos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mestizo) and [mulattoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulatto). The Spanish encouraged [slaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_the_colonial_United_States) from the [Thirteen Colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies) to come to Florida as a refuge, promising freedom in exchange for conversion to [Catholicism](606848.md). [King Charles II of Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_II_of_Spain) issued a royal proclamation freeing all slaves who fled to Florida and accepted conversion and baptism. Most went to the area around St. Augustine, but [escaped slaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fugitive_slaves_in_the_United_States) also reached Pensacola. St. Augustine had mustered an all-black militia unit defending Florida as early as 1683.[^cite_note-31]

The region was frequently coveted and attacked, with English colonists and [buccaneers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buccaneer) launching several attacks on St. Augustine in the 17th and 18th centuries. Spain built the [Castillo de San Marcos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castillo_de_San_Marcos) in 1672 and [Fort Matanzas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Matanzas) in 1742 to defend Florida's capital city from attacks, and to maintain its strategic position in the defense of the [Captaincy General of Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captaincy_General_of_Cuba) and the [Spanish West Indies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_West_Indies). Established in 1738 by governor [Manuel de Montiano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_de_Montiano), [Fort Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Mose_Historic_State_Park) (near St. Augustine) became the first legally sanctioned, free Black settlement in North America, where escaped slaves gained freedom in exchange for militia service.[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33]

In 1763, as part of the [Treaty of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_\(1763\)) ending the [Seven Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Years'_War), Spain traded Florida with [Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Britain) in exchange for [Havana](49719.md), [Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captaincy_General_of_Cuba), which the British had taken previously; in compensation Spain received [Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_\(New_Spain\)) from France, and many residents, along with much of the remaining Indigenous population, departed for Cuba.[^cite_note-34] The British built the [King's Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_Road_\(Florida\)) from St. Augustine to the [Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Georgia) line, crossing the [St. Johns River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Johns_River) at *Wacca Pilatka,* later known as 'Cow Ford', which is now the core of [Downtown Jacksonville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downtown_Jacksonville).[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37] They split *Las Floridas* into [East Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_East_Florida) and [West Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_West_Florida), a division later kept by Spain, and used land grants to attract settlers, establishing English-speaking communities in what are now [Duval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duval_County,_Florida), [Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baker_County,_Florida), [St. Johns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Johns_County,_Florida), and [Nassau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nassau_County,_Florida) counties. The British promoted the cultivation of [sugarcane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugarcane), [indigo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigo), and fruit; expanded lumber exports; and introduced [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law), including [trial by jury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jury_trial), [habeas corpus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habeas_corpus), and county government.[^cite_note-The_Land_Policy_in_British_East_Florida-38][^cite_note-A_History_of_Florida-39] Neither East nor West Florida sent delegates to draft the [Declaration of Independence](31874.md), and the Floridas remained a Loyalist stronghold during the [American Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolution).[^cite_note-40][^cite_note-A_History_of_Florida-39][^cite_note-The_Land_Policy_in_British_East_Florida-38]

In [1783](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_Florida_History), Spain regained both East and West Florida after Britain's defeat in the [Revolutionary War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War) and the subsequent [Treaty of Versailles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Versailles_\(1783\)), and continued the provincial divisions until 1821.[^cite_note-41]

### Statehood and Indian removal

See also: [Republic of East Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_East_Florida), [Seminole Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminole_Wars), [Adams–Onís Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adams%E2%80%93On%C3%ADs_Treaty), [Florida Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Territory), [Admission to the Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admission_to_the_Union), and [List of U.S. states by date of admission to the Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_by_date_of_admission_to_the_Union)



A portrait of a [Cracker cowboy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_cracker) by [Frederic Remington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederic_Remington) (1861–1909)

Americans of [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Americans) and [Scots Irish descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotch-Irish_Americans) began moving into northern Florida from the backwoods of [Georgia](48830.md) and [South Carolina](27956.md). Despite Spanish territorial restrictions, authorities lacked the resources to effectively police the region, allowing settlers from the United States to continually immigrate into Florida unchecked. These migrants intermingled with remaining British loyalists, ultimately forming the population known as [Florida Crackers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Cracker).[^cite_note-42]

The American settlers established a permanent foothold in the area. The British settlers who had remained also resented Spanish governance, leading to a rebellion in 1810 and the establishment for ninety days of the so-called Free and Independent [Republic of West Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_West_Florida) on September 23.[^cite_note-43] After meetings beginning in June, rebels overcame the garrison at [Baton Rouge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baton_Rouge,_Louisiana) in present-day [Louisiana](18130.md) and unfurled the flag of the new republic: a single white star on a blue field. This flag would later become known as the "[Bonnie Blue Flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonnie_Blue_Flag)".

In 1810, parts of West Florida were annexed by the proclamation of President [James Madison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Madison), who claimed the region as part of the [Louisiana Purchase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_Purchase). These parts were incorporated into the newly formed [Territory of Orleans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territory_of_Orleans). The U.S. annexed the Mobile District of West Florida to the [Mississippi Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi_Territory) in 1812. Spain continued to dispute the area, though the United States gradually increased the area it occupied. In 1812, a group of settlers from Georgia, with de facto support from the U.S. federal government, attempted to overthrow the Floridan government in the province of East Florida. The settlers hoped to convince Floridians to join their cause and proclaim independence from Spain, but the settlers lost their tenuous support from the federal government and abandoned their cause by 1813.[^cite_note-44]

Traditionally, historians argued that [Seminoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminole) based in [East Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Florida) began raiding Georgia settlements and offering havens for runaway slaves. The [United States Army](32087.md) led increasingly frequent incursions into Spanish territory, including the 1817–1818 campaign against the Seminole Indians by [Andrew Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Jackson) that became known as the [First Seminole War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Seminole_War). The United States now effectively controlled East Florida. Control was necessary according to Secretary of State [John Quincy Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Quincy_Adams) because Florida had become "a derelict open to the occupancy of every enemy, civilized or savage, of the United States, and serving no other earthly purpose than as a post of annoyance to them."[^cite_note-45]



A [U.S. Marine](17349325.md) boat searching the [Everglades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everglades) for [Seminoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminole) (hiding in foreground) during the [Second Seminole War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Seminole_War), which lasted from 1835 to 1842

More recent historians describe that after U.S. independence, settlers in [Georgia](48830.md) increased pressure on Seminole lands, and skirmishes near the border led to the [First Seminole War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Seminole_War) (1816–1819). The United States purchased Florida from Spain by the [Adams-Onis Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adams-Onis_Treaty) (1819) and took possession in 1821. The Seminole were moved out of their rich farmland in northern Florida and confined to a large reservation in the interior of the Florida peninsula by the [Treaty of Moultrie Creek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Moultrie_Creek) (1823). Passage of the [Indian Removal Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Removal_Act) (1830) led to the [Treaty of Payne's Landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Payne's_Landing) (1832), which called for the relocation of all Seminole to [Indian Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Territory) (now [Oklahoma](22489.md)).[^cite_note-46]

Some resisted, leading to the [Second Seminole War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Seminole_War), the bloodiest [war against Native Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Indian_Wars) in United States history. By 1842, most Seminoles and Black Seminoles, facing starvation, were removed to Indian Territory west of the [Mississippi River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi_River). Perhaps fewer than 200 Seminoles remained in Florida after the [Third Seminole War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Seminole_War) (1855–1858), having taken refuge in the Everglades, from where they never surrendered to the US. They fostered a resurgence in traditional customs and a culture of staunch independence.[^cite_note-47]

Florida had become a burden to Spain, which could not afford to send settlers or troops due to the devastation caused by the [Peninsular War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peninsular_War). Madrid, therefore, decided to cede the territory to the United States through the [Adams–Onís Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adams%E2%80%93On%C3%ADs_Treaty), which took effect in 1821.[^cite_note-48] President [James Monroe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Monroe) was authorized on March 3, 1821, to take possession of [East Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Florida) and [West Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Florida) for the United States and provide for initial governance.[^cite_note-49] On behalf of the U.S. government, [Andrew Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Jackson), whom Jacksonville is named after, served as a military commissioner with the powers of governor of the newly acquired territory for a brief period.[^cite_note-50] On March 30, 1822, the U.S. Congress merged East Florida and part of West Florida into the [Florida Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Territory).[^cite_note-51]

By the early 1800s, [Indian removal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_removal) was a significant issue throughout the southeastern U.S. and also in Florida. In 1830, the U.S. Congress passed the [Indian Removal Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Removal_Act) and as settlement increased, pressure grew on the U.S. government to remove the Indians from Florida. Seminoles offered sanctuary to black people, who became known as the [Black Seminoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Seminoles); clashes between whites and Indians grew with the influx of new settlers. In 1832, the [Treaty of Payne's Landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Payne's_Landing) promised to the Seminoles lands west of the Mississippi River if they agreed to leave Florida. Many Seminoles left at this time.

Some Seminoles remained, and the U.S. Army arrived in Florida, leading to the [Second Seminole War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Seminole_War) (1835–1842). Following the war, approximately 3,000 Seminole and 800 Black Seminole were removed to [Indian Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Territory). A few hundred Seminole remained in Florida in the [Everglades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everglades).



The Historic Call-Collins House, the Grove, built by slaves in the 1840s, is an antebellum [plantation house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantation_complexes_in_the_Southern_United_States) in Tallahassee.



An 1840 advertisement in the *Pensacola Gazette* offering a \$10 (\$330 in 2022) reward for the return of a [fugitive slave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fugitive_slaves_in_the_United_States).

On March 3, 1845, only one day before the end of President [John Tyler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tyler)'s term in office, Florida became the 27th state,[^cite_note-52] admitted as a [slave state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_state) and no longer a sanctuary for runaway slaves. Initially its population grew slowly.[^cite_note-53]

As European settlers continued to encroach on Seminole lands, the United States intervened to move the remaining Seminoles to the West. The [Third Seminole War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Seminole_War) (1855–1858) resulted in the [forced removal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trail_of_Tears) of most of the remaining Seminoles, although hundreds of Seminole Indians remained in the Everglades.[^cite_note-54]

The first settlements and towns in South Florida were founded much later than those in the northern part of the state. The first permanent European settlers arrived in the early 19th century. People came from the [Bahamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahamas) to South Florida and the [Keys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Keys) to hunt for treasure from the ships that ran aground on the treacherous Great [Florida Reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Reef). Some accepted Spanish land offers along the Miami River. At about the same time, the [Seminole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminole) Indians arrived, along with a group of runaway slaves. The area was affected by the [Second Seminole War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Seminole_War), during which Major [William S. Harney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_S._Harney) led several raids against the Indians. Most non-Indian residents were soldiers stationed at [Fort Dallas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Dallas). It was the most devastating Indian war in American history, causing almost a total loss of population in Miami.

After the Second Seminole War ended in 1842, William English re-established a plantation started by his uncle on the [Miami River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_River_\(Florida\)). He charted the "Village of Miami" on the south bank of the Miami River and sold several plots of land. In 1844, Miami became the county seat, and six years later a census reported there were ninety-six residents in the area.[^cite_note-55] The [Third Seminole War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminole_Wars#Third_Seminole_War) was not as destructive as the second, but it slowed the settlement of southeast Florida. At the end of the war, a few of the soldiers stayed.

### Civil War and Reconstruction

Main article: [Florida in the American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_in_the_American_Civil_War)

See also: [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War) and [Reconstruction era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_era)



The [Battle of Olustee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Olustee) during the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War) in 1864

American settlers began to establish cotton [plantations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantations_in_the_American_South) in north Florida, which required numerous laborers, which they supplied by buying slaves in the domestic market. By 1860, Florida had only 140,424 people, of whom 44% were enslaved. There were fewer than 1,000 free [African Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_black) before the American Civil War.[^cite_note-56]

On January 10, 1861, nearly all delegates in the Florida Legislature approved an ordinance of secession,[^cite_note-57][^cite_note-58] declaring Florida to be "a sovereign and independent nation"—an apparent reassertion to the preamble in Florida's Constitution of 1838, in which Florida agreed with Congress to be a "Free and Independent State". The ordinance declared Florida's secession from the [Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_\(American_Civil_War\)), allowing it to become one of the founding members of the [Confederate States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederate_States_of_America).

The Confederacy received little military help from Florida; the 15,000 troops it offered were generally sent elsewhere. Instead of troops and manufactured goods, Florida did provide salt and, more importantly, beef to feed the Confederate armies. This was particularly important after 1864, when the Confederacy lost control of the Mississippi River, thereby losing access to Texas beef.[^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60] The largest engagements in the state were the [Battle of Olustee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Olustee), on February 20, 1864, and the [Battle of Natural Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Natural_Bridge), on March 6, 1865. Both were Confederate victories.[^cite_note-61] The war ended in 1865.

Following the American Civil War, Florida's [congressional](31756.md) representation was restored on June 25, 1868, albeit forcefully after [Reconstruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_era) and the installation of unelected government officials under the final authority of federal military commanders. After the Reconstruction period ended in 1876, white Democrats regained power in the state legislature. In 1885, they created a new constitution, followed by statutes through 1889 that [disfranchised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disfranchisement_after_Reconstruction_era) most blacks and many poor whites.[^cite_note-62]

In the pre-automobile era, railroads played a key role in the state's development, particularly in coastal areas. In 1883, the [Pensacola and Atlantic Railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pensacola_and_Atlantic_Railroad) connected Pensacola and the rest of the [Panhandle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Panhandle) to the rest of the state. In 1884, the [South Florida Railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Florida_Railroad) (later absorbed by [Atlantic Coast Line Railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Coast_Line_Railroad)) opened full service to [Tampa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tampa). In 1894, the [Florida East Coast Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_East_Coast_Railway) reached [West Palm Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Palm_Beach); in 1896 it reached [Biscayne Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biscayne_Bay) near [Miami](53846.md). Numerous other railroads were built all over the interior of the state.

### 20th century



Vacationers at the newly opened [Don Cesar Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Don_CeSar) in [St. Pete Beach, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Pete_Beach,_Florida), in 1928

Florida's economy has been based primarily upon agricultural products such as citrus fruits, strawberries, nuts, sugarcane and cattle.[^cite_note-63] The [boll weevil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boll_weevil) devastated cotton crops during the early 20th century.[^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65]

Until the mid-20th century, Florida was the least-populous state in the [Southern United States](179553.md). In 1900, its population was only 528,542, of whom nearly 44% were African American, the same proportion as before the Civil War.[^cite_note-66] Forty thousand blacks, roughly one-fifth of their 1900 population levels in Florida, left the state in the [Great Migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Migration_\(African_American\)). They left due to [lynchings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynching) and racial violence and for better opportunities in the North and the West.[^cite_note-67] [Disfranchisement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disfranchisement) for most African Americans in the state persisted until the [Civil Rights Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Movement) of the 1960s gained federal legislation in 1965 to enforce protection of their constitutional suffrage.

Black and white photograph of segregationists fighting on a beach

White segregationists (foreground) trying to prevent black people from swimming at a "white only" beach in St. Augustine during the [1964 Monson Motor Lodge protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_Monson_Motor_Lodge_protests)

In response to [racial segregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_segregation_in_the_United_States) in Florida, a number of protests occurred in Florida during the 1950s and 1960s as part of the Civil Rights Movement. In 1956–1957, students at [Florida A\&M University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_A\&M_University) organized a bus boycott in Tallahassee to mimic the [Montgomery bus boycott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montgomery_bus_boycott) and succeeded in integrating the city's buses.[^cite_note-68] Students also held sit-ins in 1960 in protest of segregated seating at local lunch counters, and, in 1964, an incident at a [St. Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Augustine,_Florida) motel pool, in which the owner poured acid into the water during a demonstration, influenced the passage of the [1964 Civil Rights Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_Civil_Rights_Act).[^cite_note-69]

Economic prosperity in the 1920s stimulated tourism to Florida and related development of hotels and resort communities. Combined with its sudden elevation in profile was the [Florida land boom of the 1920s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_land_boom_of_the_1920s), which brought a brief period of intense land development. In 1925, the [Seaboard Air Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seaboard_Air_Line) broke the FEC's southeast Florida monopoly and extended its freight and passenger service to West Palm Beach; two years later, it extended passenger service to Miami. Devastating hurricanes in [1926](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1926_Miami_hurricane) and [1928](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1928_Okeechobee_hurricane), followed by the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression), brought that period to a halt. Florida's economy did not fully recover until the military buildup for [World War II](32927.md).



[Miami's Freedom Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Tower_\(Miami\)), built in 1925, was added to the [National Register of Historic Places](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Register_of_Historic_Places) in 1979.

In 1939, Florida was described as "still very largely an empty State."[^cite_note-70] Subsequently, the growing availability of [air conditioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_conditioning), the climate, and a low cost of living made the state a haven. Migration from the [Rust Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rust_Belt) and the Northeast sharply increased Florida's population after 1945.

In the 1960s, many refugees from [Cuba](5042481.md), fleeing [Fidel Castro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fidel_Castro)'s communist regime, arrived in Miami at the [Freedom Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Tower_\(Miami\)), where the federal government used the facility to process, document and provide medical and dental services for the newcomers. As a result, the Freedom Tower was also called the "Ellis Island of the South".[^cite_note-71] In recent decades, more migrants have come for the jobs in a developing economy.

### 21st century



[Walt Disney World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Disney_World) opened on October 1, 1971, near the cities of [Orlando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlando,_Florida) and [Kissimmee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kissimmee,_Florida).

With a population of 21.5 million, according to the 2020 census, Florida is the most populous state in the southeastern United States and the third-most populous in the United States.[^cite_note-72] The population of Florida has boomed in recent years with the state being the recipient of the largest number of out-of-state movers in the country as of 2019.[^cite_note-73] Florida's growth has been widespread, as cities throughout the state have continued to see population growth.[^cite_note-74]

In 2012, the [killing of Trayvon Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killing_of_Trayvon_Martin), a young black man, by [George Zimmerman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Zimmerman) in [Sanford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanford,_Florida) drew national attention to Florida's [stand-your-ground laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand-your-ground_law), and sparked African American activism, including the [Black Lives Matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Lives_Matter) movement.[^cite_note-75]

After [Hurricane Maria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Maria) devastated [Puerto Rico](23041.md) in September 2017, a large population of Puerto Ricans began moving to Florida to escape the widespread destruction. Hundreds of thousands of Puerto Ricans arrived in Florida after Maria dissipated, with nearly half of them arriving in Orlando and large populations also moving to Tampa, Fort Lauderdale, and West Palm Beach.[^cite_note-76]



Memorials to the victims of the [Orlando nightclub shooting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlando_nightclub_shooting) left on the fence of the Pulse nightclub in [Orlando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlando,_Florida) in 2016

A handful of high-profile mass shootings have occurred in Florida in the 21st century. In June 2016, a gunman [killed 49 people at a gay nightclub in Orlando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlando_nightclub_shooting). It is the deadliest incident in the [history of violence against LGBT people in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_violence_against_LGBT_people_in_the_United_States), as well as the deadliest terrorist attack in the U.S. since the [September 11 attacks](5058690.md) in 2001, and it was the deadliest [mass shooting](50822291.md) by a single gunman in U.S. history until the [2017 Las Vegas shooting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Las_Vegas_shooting). In February 2018, 17 people were killed in a [school shooting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoneman_Douglas_High_School_shooting) at [Stoneman Douglas High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoneman_Douglas_High_School) in [Parkland, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkland,_Florida), leading to new gun control regulations at both the state and federal level.[^cite_note-77]

On June 24, 2021, a condominium in [Surfside, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surfside,_Florida), near [Miami](53846.md) [collapsed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surfside_condominium_building_collapse), killing at least 97 people.[^cite_note-78] The Surfside collapse is tied with the [Knickerbocker Theatre collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knickerbocker_Theatre_\(Washington,_D.C.\)) as the [third-deadliest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_accidents_and_disasters_by_death_toll#Structural_collapses) [structural engineering failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_structural_failures_and_collapses) in United States history, behind the [Hyatt Regency walkway collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyatt_Regency_walkway_collapse) and the [collapse of the Pemberton Mill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pemberton_Mill).[^cite_note-79][^cite_note-80]

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Florida)

See also: [List of counties in Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_counties_in_Florida), [List of places in Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_places_in_Florida), [List of municipalities in Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_municipalities_in_Florida), [List of islands of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_islands_of_Florida), and [List of Florida state parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Florida_state_parks)



[Florida Municipalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_municipalities_in_Florida) and [Counties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_counties_in_Florida)

Much of Florida is on a peninsula between the Gulf of Mexico, the Atlantic Ocean and the [Straits of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straits_of_Florida). Spanning two [time zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_zone), it extends to the northwest into a [panhandle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salient_\(geography\)), extending along the northern Gulf of Mexico. It is bordered on the north by [Georgia](48830.md) and [Alabama](303.md), and on the west, at the end of the panhandle, by Alabama. It is the only state that borders both the Atlantic Ocean and the Gulf of Mexico.

Florida is the southernmost of the 48 contiguous states, [Hawaii](13270.md) being the only one of the fifty states reaching farther south. Florida is west of the [Bahamas](3451.md) and 90 miles (140 km) north of [Cuba](5042481.md). Florida is one of the largest states east of the [Mississippi River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi_River), and only [Alaska](624.md) and [Michigan](18859.md) are larger in water area. The water boundary is 3 nautical miles (3.5 mi; 5.6 km) offshore in the Atlantic Ocean and 9 nautical miles (10 mi; 17 km) offshore in the Gulf of Mexico.[^cite_note-NOAA-81]

At 345 feet (105 m) [above mean sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Above_mean_sea_level), [Britton Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britton_Hill) is the [highest point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_elevation) in Florida and the lowest highpoint of any U.S. state.[^cite_note-82] Much of the state south of [Orlando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlando,_Florida) lies at a lower elevation than northern Florida, and is fairly level. Much of the state is at or near sea level. Some places, such as [Clearwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clearwater,_Florida) have [promontories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promontory) that rise 50 to 100 ft (15 to 30 m) above the water. Much of Central and North Florida, typically 25 mi (40 km) or more away from the coastline, have rolling hills with elevations ranging from 100 to 250 ft (30 to 76 m). The highest point in peninsular Florida (east and south of the [Suwannee River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suwannee_River)), [Sugarloaf Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugarloaf_Mountain_\(Florida\)), is a 312-foot (95 m) peak in [Lake County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_County,_Florida).[^cite_note-83] On average, Florida is the flattest state in the United States.[^cite_note-84]



Florida is mostly low-lying and flat as this topographic map shows.

[Lake Okeechobee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Okeechobee), the largest lake in Florida, is the [tenth-largest natural freshwater lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_lakes_of_the_United_States_by_area) among the 50 states of the United States and the second-largest natural freshwater lake contained entirely within the [contiguous 48 states](83759.md), after [Lake Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Michigan).[^cite_note-85] The longest river within Florida is the St. Johns River, at 310 miles (500 km) long. The drop in elevation from its headwaters South Florida to its mouth in Jacksonville is less than 30 feet (9.1 m).

### Climate

Main article: [Climate of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_Florida)

See also: [List of Florida hurricanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Florida_hurricanes) and [U.S. state temperature extremes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._state_temperature_extremes)



The state tree, [Sabal palmetto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabal_palmetto), flourishes in Florida's overall warm climate.



A [Köppen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) map of climate zones in Florida.

The climate of Florida is tempered somewhat by the fact that no part of the state is distant from the ocean. North of [Lake Okeechobee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Okeechobee), the prevalent climate is [humid subtropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humid_subtropical_climate) ([Köppen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification): *Cfa*), while areas south of the lake (including the [Florida Keys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Keys)) have a true [tropical climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_climate) (Köppen: *Aw*, *Am*, and *Af*).[^cite_note-86] Mean high temperatures for late July are primarily in the low 90s Fahrenheit (32–34 °C). Mean low temperatures for early to mid-January range from the low 40s Fahrenheit (4–7 °C) in north Florida to above 60 °F (16 °C) from Miami on southward. With an average daily temperature of 70.7 °F (21.5 °C), it is the warmest state in the U.S.[^cite_note-87][^cite_note-88]

In the summer, high temperatures in the state rarely exceed 100 °F (37.8 °C). Several record cold maxima have been in the 30s °F (−1 to 4 °C) and record lows have been in the 10s (−12 to −7 °C). These temperatures normally extend at most a few days at a time in the northern and central parts of Florida. South Florida rarely dips below freezing.[^cite_note-89] The hottest temperature ever recorded in Florida was 109 °F (43 °C), which was set on June 29, 1931, in [Monticello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monticello,_Florida). The coldest temperature was −2 °F (−19 °C), on February 13, 1899, just 25 miles (40 km) away, in Tallahassee.[^cite_note-90][^cite_note-91]

Due to its subtropical and tropical climate, Florida rarely receives measurable [snowfall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snow_in_Florida).[^cite_note-92] On rare occasions, a combination of cold moisture and freezing temperatures can result in snowfall in the farthest northern regions like [Jacksonville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacksonville), [Gainesville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gainesville,_Florida) or [Pensacola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pensacola). [Frost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frost), which is more common than [snow](28191.md), sometimes occurs in the panhandle.[^cite_note-93] The USDA Plant [hardiness zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardiness_zone) for the state range from zone 8a (no colder than 10 °F or −12 °C) in the inland western [panhandle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_panhandle) to zone 11b (no colder than 45 °F or 7 °C) in the lower [Florida Keys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Keys).[^cite_note-94] [Fog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fog) also occurs all over the state or climate of Florida.[^cite_note-95]

Florida's nickname is the "Sunshine State", but severe weather is a common occurrence in the state. Central Florida is known as the lightning capital of the United States, as it experiences more lightning strikes than anywhere else in the country.[^cite_note-102] Florida has one of the highest average precipitation levels of any state,[^cite_note-103] in large part because afternoon thunderstorms are common in much of the state from late spring until early autumn.[^cite_note-104] A narrow eastern part of the state including Orlando and Jacksonville receives between 2,400 and 2,800 hours of sunshine annually. The rest of the state, including Miami, receives between 2,800 and 3,200 hours annually.[^cite_note-105]

Florida leads the United States in tornadoes per area (when including [waterspouts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterspout)),[^cite_note-106] but they do not typically reach the intensity of those in the [Midwest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midwest) and [Great Plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Plains). [Hail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hail) often accompanies the most severe thunderstorms.[^cite_note-107]

[Hurricanes](8282374.md) pose a severe threat each year from June 1 to November 30, particularly from August to October. Florida is the most hurricane-prone state, with subtropical or tropical water on a lengthy coastline. Of the [category 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_4_typhoon) or higher storms that have struck the United States, 83% have either hit Florida or Texas.[^cite_note-fltoday_fact-108]

From 1851 to 2006, Florida was struck by 114 hurricanes, 37 of them major—[category 3](255313.md) and above.[^cite_note-fltoday_fact-108] It is rare for a hurricane season to pass without any impact in the state by at least a tropical storm.[^cite_note-109]

In 1992, Florida was the site of what was then the costliest weather disaster in U.S. history, [Hurricane Andrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Andrew), which caused more than \$25 billion in damages when it struck during August; it held that distinction until 2005, when [Hurricane Katrina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Katrina) surpassed it, and it has since been surpassed by six other hurricanes. Andrew is the second-costliest hurricane in Florida's history.[^cite_note-110]

### Flora

Further information: [Florida mangroves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_mangroves) and [List of invasive plant species in Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_invasive_plant_species_in_Florida)



Red mangroves in [Everglades National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everglades_National_Park)

The state has more than 26,000 square miles (67,000 km<sup>2</sup>) of forests, covering about half of the state's land area.[^cite_note-119]

There are about 3,000 types of [wildflowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildflower) in Florida.[^cite_note-120] This is the third-most diverse state in the union, behind [California](5407.md) and [Texas](29810.md), both larger states.[^cite_note-121] In Florida, wild populations of coconut palms extend up the East Coast from Key West to [Jupiter Inlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter_Inlet), and up the West Coast from [Marco Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marco_Island) to [Sarasota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarasota). Many of the smallest coral islands in the [Florida Keys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Keys) are known to have abundant coconut palms sprouting from coconuts deposited by ocean currents. Coconut palms are cultivated north of south Florida to roughly Cocoa Beach on the East Coast and the Tampa Bay area on the West Coast.[^cite_note-122]

On the east coast of the state, [mangroves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mangroves) have normally dominated the coast from [Cocoa Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocoa_Beach,_Florida) southward; [salt marshes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_marsh) from [St. Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Augustine,_Florida) northward. From St. Augustine south to Cocoa Beach, the coast fluctuates between the two, depending on the annual weather conditions.[^cite_note-ft140107-116] All three mangrove species flower in the spring and early summer. [Propagules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propagule) are produced from late summer through early autumn.[^cite_note-123] Florida mangrove plant communities covered an estimated 430,000 to 540,000 acres (1,700 to 2,200 km<sup>2</sup>) in Florida in 1981. Ninety percent of the Florida mangroves are in southern Florida, in [Collier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collier_County,_Florida), [Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_County,_Florida), [Miami-Dade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_County,_Florida) and [Monroe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monroe_County,_Florida) counties.

### Reef

Main article: [Florida Reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Reef)



Fish and corals in [John Pennekamp Coral Reef State Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Pennekamp_Coral_Reef_State_Park) near [Key Largo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_Largo)

The Florida Reef is the only living coral barrier reef in the continental United States.[^cite_note-124] It is also the third-largest coral barrier reef system in the world, after the [Great Barrier Reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Barrier_Reef) and the [Belize Barrier Reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belize_Barrier_Reef).[^cite_note-125] The reef lies a little bit off of the coast of the Florida Keys. A lot of the reef lies within [John Pennekamp Coral Reef State Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Pennekamp_Coral_Reef_State_Park), which was the first [underwater park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underwater_park) in the United States.[^cite_note-126] The park contains a lot of tropical vegetation, marine life, and seabirds.[^cite_note-127]

The Florida Reef extends into other parks and sanctuaries as well including [Dry Tortugas National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_Tortugas_National_Park), [Biscayne National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biscayne_National_Park), and the [Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Keys_National_Marine_Sanctuary). Almost 1,400 species of marine plants and animals, including more than 40 species of [stony corals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stony_corals) and 500 species of fish, live on the Florida Reef.[^cite_note-128] The Florida Reef, being a delicate ecosystem like other coral reefs, faces many threats including [overfishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overfishing), plastics in the ocean, [coral bleaching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral_bleaching), rising sea levels, and changes in [sea surface temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_surface_temperature).

American flamingos in [South Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Florida)



An [American alligator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_alligator) and an invasive [Burmese python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burmese_python) in [Everglades National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everglades_National_Park)

Florida is a low per capita energy user.[^cite_note-Energy_Consumption_by_Source_and_Total_Consumption_per_Capita,_Ranked_by_State,_2004-129] In 2008, approximately 4% of energy in Florida was generated through renewable resources. In 2008, Florida's energy production was 6% of the U.S. total energy output. Its production of pollutants was lower, with 6% of national [nitrogen oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_oxide) production, 5% for [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide), and 4% for [sulfur dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur_dioxide).[^cite_note-DoE-profile-130] Wildfires in Florida occur at all times of the year.[^cite_note-131]

All potable water resources have been controlled by the state government through five regional water authorities since 1972.[^cite_note-132]

[Red tide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_tide) has been an issue on the southwest coast of Florida, as well as other areas. While there has been a great deal of conjecture over the cause of the toxic algae bloom, there is no evidence that it is being caused by pollution or that there has been an increase in the duration or frequency of red tides.[^cite_note-Tide's_toxins_trouble_lungs_ashore-133] Red tide is now killing off wildlife or [Tropical fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_fish) and coral reefs putting all in danger.[^cite_note-134]

The [Florida panther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_panther) is close to extinction. A record 23 were killed in 2009, mainly by automobile collisions, leaving about 100 individuals in the wild. The [Center for Biological Diversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_Biological_Diversity) and others have therefore called for a special [protected area](100406.md) for the panther to be established.[^cite_note-Record_number_of_panthers_killed_by_vehicles_in_2009-135] [Manatees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Indian_manatee) are also dying at a rate higher than their reproduction.[^cite_note-136] [American flamingos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_flamingos) are rare to see in Florida due to being hunted in the 1900s, where it was to a point considered completely extirpated. Now the flamingos are reproducing toward making a comeback to [South Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Florida) since it is adamantly considered native to the state and also are now being protected.[^cite_note-137][^cite_note-138]

Much of Florida has an elevation of less than 12 feet (3.7 m), including many populated areas. Therefore, it is susceptible to [rising sea levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_sea_level_rise) associated with [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming).[^cite_note-139] The Atlantic beaches that are vital to the state's economy are being washed out to sea due to rising sea levels caused by climate change. The Miami Beach area, close to the continental shelf, is running out of accessible offshore sand reserves.[^cite_note-140] Elevated temperatures can damage coral reefs, causing [coral bleaching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral_bleaching). The first recorded bleaching incident on the Florida Reef was in 1973. Incidents of bleaching have become more frequent in recent decades, in correlation with a rise in [sea surface temperatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_surface_temperature). [White band disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_band_disease) has also adversely affected corals on the Florida Reef.[^cite_note-141]

### Geology

Main article: [Geology of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology_of_Florida)



The [Florida Keys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Keys) as seen from a satellite, in December 2003

[^cite_note-142]

The Florida peninsula is a porous [plateau](538005.md) of [karst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karst) [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone) sitting atop [bedrock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedrock), known as the [Florida Platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Platform).

The largest deposits of [potash](56509.md) in the United States are found in Florida. The largest deposits of [rock phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorite) in the country are found in Florida.[^cite_note-Industry_overview-143] Most of this is in [Bone Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone_Valley).[^cite_note-144]

Extended systems of underwater [caves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cave), [sinkholes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinkholes) and [springs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_\(hydrosphere\)) are found throughout the state and supply most of the water used by residents.[^cite_note-145] The limestone is topped with [sandy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sand) soils deposited as ancient beaches over millions of years as global sea levels rose and fell. During the [last glacial period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_glacial_period), lower sea levels and a drier climate revealed a much wider peninsula, largely [savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate_grasslands,_savannas,_and_shrublands).[^cite_note-Florida's_Geological_History-146] While there are sinkholes in much of the state, modern sinkholes have tended to be in West-[Central Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Florida).[^cite_note-147][^cite_note-148]

Everglades National Park covers 1,509,000 acres (6,110 km<sup>2</sup>), throughout [Dade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_County,_Florida), [Monroe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monroe_County,_Florida), and [Collier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collier_County,_Florida) counties in Florida.[^cite_note-149] The [Everglades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everglades), an enormously wide, slow-flowing river encompasses the southern tip of the peninsula. Sinkhole damage claims on property in the state exceeded a total of \$2 billion from 2006 through 2010.[^cite_note-150] [Winter Park Sinkhole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_Park,_Florida#The_Winter_Park_Sinkhole), in central Florida, appeared May 8, 1981. It was approximately 350 feet (107 m) wide and 75 feet (23 m) deep. It was one of the largest recent sinkholes to form in the United States. It is now known as Lake Rose.[^cite_note-151]

The [Econlockhatchee River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econlockhatchee_River) (Econ River for short) is an 54.5-mile-long (87.7 km)[^cite_note-152] north-flowing [blackwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackwater_river) tributary of the [St. Johns River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Johns_River), the longest river in the [U.S. state](18618239.md) of Florida. The Econ River flows through [Osceola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osceola_County,_Florida), [Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_County,_Florida), and [Seminole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminole_County,_Florida) counties in [Central Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Florida), just east of the [Orlando Metropolitan Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlando_Metropolitan_Area) (east of [State Road 417](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_State_Road_417)). It is a designated [Outstanding Florida Waters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outstanding_Florida_Waters).[^cite_note-153]

Earthquakes are rare because Florida is not located near any [tectonic plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics) boundaries.[^cite_note-154]

Florida is a highly urbanized state, with 89 percent of its population living in urban areas in 2000, compared to 79 percent across the U.S.[^cite_note-157]

Florida's population was 21,477,737 on July 1, 2019, a 14.24% increase since the [2010 United States census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_States_Census).[^cite_note-164] The population of Florida in the 2010 census was 18,801,310.[^cite_note-165] Florida was the seventh fastest-growing state in the U.S. in the 12-month period ending July 1, 2012.[^cite_note-166] In 2010, the [center of population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_population) of Florida was located between [Fort Meade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Meade,_Florida) and [Frostproof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frostproof,_Florida). The center of population has moved less than 5 miles (8 km) to the east and approximately 1 mile (1.6 km) to the north between 1980 and 2010 and has been located in [Polk County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polk_County,_Florida) since the [1960 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1960_United_States_census).[^cite_note-167]

The population exceeded 19.7 million by December 2014, surpassing the population of the state of [New York](8210131.md) for the first time, making Florida the third most populous state.[^cite_note-168][^cite_note-169] The Florida population was 21,477,737 residents or people according to the U.S. Census Bureau's 2019 Population Estimates Program.[^cite_note-170] By the 2020 census, its population increased to 21,538,187.

In 2010, [undocumented immigrants](7124412.md) constituted an estimated 5.7% of the population. This was the sixth highest percentage of any U.S. state.[^cite_note-New_Arizona_law_puts_police_in_'tenuous'_spot-171][^cite_note-172] There were an estimated 675,000 illegal immigrants in the state in 2010.[^cite_note-173] Florida has banned [sanctuary cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanctuary_city).[^cite_note-174]

The top countries of origin for Florida's immigrants were [Cuba](5042481.md), [Haiti](13373.md), [Colombia](5222.md), Mexico and [Jamaica](15660.md) in 2018.[^cite_note-\:0-175]

In 2010, 6.9% of the population (1,269,765) considered themselves to be of only [American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_people) ancestry (regardless of race or ethnicity).[^cite_note-HisLatFlo-161] Many of these were of English or [Scotch-Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scots-Irish_American) descent, whose families have lived in the state for so long they choose to identify as having "American" ancestry or do not know their ancestry.[^cite_note-183][^cite_note-184][^cite_note-185][^cite_note-186][^cite_note-187][^cite_note-188] In the 1980 [United States census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_census), the largest ancestry group reported in Florida was English with 2,232,514 Floridians claiming they were of English or mostly [English American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_American) ancestry.[^cite_note-189] Some of their ancestry dated to the original [Thirteen Colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies).

As of 2010, those of (non-Hispanic white) European ancestry accounted for 57.9% of Florida's population. Out of the 57.9%, the largest groups were 12.0% [German](152735.md) (2,212,391), 10.7% [Irish](775859.md) (1,979,058), 8.8% English (1,629,832), 6.6% [Italian](385155.md) (1,215,242), 2.8% [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_people) (511,229), and 2.7% [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_people) (504,641).[^cite_note-HisLatFlo-161] [White Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_American) of all European backgrounds are present in all areas of the state. In 1970, non-Hispanic whites constituted nearly 80% of Florida's population.[^cite_note-census-178] Those of [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_American) and [Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_American) ancestry are present in large numbers in all the urban/suburban areas across the state. Some native white Floridians, especially those who have descended from long-time Florida families, may refer to themselves as "[Florida crackers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_cracker)"; others see the term as a derogatory one. Like whites in most other states of the southern U.S., they descend mainly from English and [Scots-Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scots-Irish_American) settlers, as well as some other [British American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_American) settlers.[^cite_note-190]

As of 2010, those of Hispanic or Latino ancestry accounted for 22.5% (4,223,806) of Florida's population. Out of the 22.5%, the largest groups were 6.5% (1,213,438) [Cuban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Americans), and 4.5% (847,550) [Puerto Rican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stateside_Puerto_Ricans).[^cite_note-HisLatFlo-161] Florida's [Hispanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanics_in_the_United_States) population includes large communities of Cuban Americans in Miami and Tampa, [Puerto Ricans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Ricans_in_the_United_States) in Orlando and Tampa, and Mexican/Central American migrant workers. The Hispanic community continues to grow more affluent and mobile. Florida has a large and diverse Hispanic population, with Cubans and Puerto Ricans being the largest groups in the state. Nearly 80% of Cuban Americans live in Florida, especially South Florida where there is a long-standing and affluent Cuban community.[^cite_note-191] Florida has the second-largest Puerto Rican population after New York, as well as the fastest-growing in the U.S.[^cite_note-192] Puerto Ricans are more widespread throughout the state, though the heaviest concentrations are in the Orlando area of Central Florida.[^cite_note-193] Florida has one of the largest and most diverse Hispanic/Latino populations in the country, especially in [South Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Florida) around Miami, and to a lesser degree Central Florida. Aside from the dominant Cuban and Puerto Rican populations, there are also large populations of Mexicans, Colombians, Venezuelans and Dominicans, among numerous other groups, as most Latino groups have sizable numbers in the state.

As of 2010, those of African ancestry accounted for 16.0% of Florida's population, which includes [African Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_American). Out of the 16.0%, 4.0% (741,879) were [West Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Indian) or [Afro-Caribbean American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Caribbean_American).[^cite_note-HisLatFlo-161] During the early 1900s, [black people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_people) made up nearly half of the state's population.[^cite_note-1870census-194] In response to segregation, disfranchisement and agricultural depression, many African Americans migrated from Florida to northern cities in the [Great Migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Migration_\(African_American\)), in waves from 1910 to 1940, and again starting in the later 1940s. They moved for jobs, better education for their children and the chance to vote and participate in society. By 1960, the proportion of African Americans in the state had declined to 18%.[^cite_note-Historical_Census_Browser:_1960_US_Census-195] Conversely, large numbers of northern [whites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_people) moved to the state.[^cite_note-196] Today, large concentrations of black residents can be found throughout Florida. Aside from blacks descended from African slaves brought to the southern U.S., there are also large numbers of blacks of [West Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Indian_American), [recent African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_immigration_to_the_United_States), and [Afro-Latino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Hispanic_and_Latino_Americans) immigrant origins, especially in the Miami/South Florida area.[^cite_note-197] Florida has the largest West Indian population of any state, originating from many Caribbean countries, with [Haitian Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Americans) being the most numerous.

In 2016, Florida had the highest percentage of West Indians in the United States at 4.5%, with 2.3% (483,874) from [Haitian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitians) ancestry, 1.5% (303,527) [Jamaican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaicans), and 0.2% (31,966) [Bahamian](19848165.md), with the other West Indian groups making up the rest.[^cite_note-198]

As of 2010, those of Asian ancestry accounted for 2.4% of Florida's population.[^cite_note-HisLatFlo-161][^cite_note-FLpop-199]

As of 2011, Florida contains the highest percentage of people over 65 (17.3%) in the U.S.[^cite_note-200] There were 186,102 military retirees living in the state in 2008.[^cite_note-HisLatFlo-161] About two-thirds of the population was born in another state, the second-highest in the U.S.[^cite_note-201]

In 2020, [Hispanic and Latinos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic_and_Latino) of any race(s) made up 26.5% of the population, while [Native Hawaiians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Hawaiian) [and Pacific Islanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Islander) made up 0.1% of all Broward County residents.[^cite_note-202]

In 2024, the state's non-Hispanic white population fell to 49.1%, making Florida the [9th state to reach majority-minority status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majority_minority_in_the_United_States).[^cite_note-CensusACS2024-203]

## Governance

Main article: [Government of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Florida)

See also: [List of governors of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_governors_of_Florida), [Florida's congressional delegations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida's_congressional_delegations), [List of United States senators from Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_United_States_senators_from_Florida), and [Florida Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Cabinet)



The old and new [Florida State Capitol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_State_Capitol) in [Tallahassee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallahassee,_Florida)

The basic structure, duties, function, and operations of the government of the State of Florida are defined by the [Florida Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Constitution), which establishes the basic law of the state and guarantees various rights and freedoms of the people. As with the American federal government and all other state governments, Florida's government consists of three separate branches: executive, legislative, and judicial. The legislature enacts bills, which, if signed by the [governor](233840.md), become [law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Statutes).

The [Florida Legislature](429346.md) comprises the [Florida Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Senate), which has 40 members, and the [Florida House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_House_of_Representatives), which has 120 members. The governor of Florida is [Ron DeSantis](36729527.md). The [Florida Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Supreme_Court) consists of a [chief justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Justice_of_Florida) and six justices.

Florida has 67 [counties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_\(United_States\)). Some reference materials may show only 66 because [Duval County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duval_County,_Florida) is consolidated with the [City of Jacksonville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Jacksonville). There are 379 cities in Florida (out of 411) that report regularly to the Florida Department of Revenue, but there are other incorporated municipalities that do not. The primary revenue source for cities and counties is property tax; properties with unpaid taxes are subject to [tax sales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_sale), which are held at the county level in May and are highly popular, due to the extensive use of online bidding sites.

The state government's primary revenue source is sales tax. Florida is [one of eight states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_income_tax#States_with_no_individual_income_tax) that do not impose a personal [income tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_tax).

There were 800 [federal corruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Corrupt_Practices_Act) convictions from 1988 to 2007, more than any other state.[^cite_note-211]

In a 2020 study, Florida was ranked as the 11th hardest state for citizens to vote in.[^cite_note-212] In April 2022, the legislature passed and the governor signed a new election law prohibiting Floridians from using [ranked-choice voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranked-choice_voting_in_the_United_States) in all federal, state and municipal elections.[^cite_note-213]

Florida retains the [death penalty](5902.md). Authorized methods of execution include the [electric chair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_chair) and [lethal injection](62745.md).[^cite_note-214] Since the resumption of executions by the U.S. Supreme Court in 1976 128 convicted murderers were executed in the state of Florida the third most of all states, all at [Florida State Prison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_State_Prison).[^cite_note-215] As of March 3, 2026, 248 offenders are awaiting execution.[^cite_note-216]

### Elections history

Further information: [Elections in Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_Florida), [Politics of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_Florida), [Political party strength in Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_party_strength_in_Florida), and [United States presidential elections in Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_presidential_elections_in_Florida)

From 1952 to 1964, most voters were registered Democrats, but the state voted for the Republican presidential candidate in every election except for [1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_United_States_presidential_election). The following year, Congress passed and President [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson) signed the [Voting Rights Act of 1965](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act_of_1965), providing for oversight of state practices and enforcement of constitutional voting rights for African Americans and other minorities in order to prevent the discrimination and disenfranchisement which had excluded most of them for decades from the political process.

From the 1930s through much of the 1960s, Florida was essentially a one-party state dominated by white conservative Democrats, who together with other Democrats of the [Solid South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_South), exercised considerable control in Congress. They have gained slightly less federal money from national programs than they have paid in taxes.[^cite_note-217] Since the 1970s, conservative white voters in the state have largely shifted from the Democratic to the Republican Party. Though the majority of registered voters in Florida were Democrats,[^cite_note-218] it continued to support Republican presidential candidates through 2004, except in [1976](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976_United_States_presidential_election) and [1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_United_States_presidential_election), when the Democratic nominee was from [the South](179553.md).

In the [2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_United_States_presidential_election) and [2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_United_States_presidential_election) presidential elections, [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) carried the state as a northern Democrat, attracting high voter turnout, especially among the young, independents, and minority voters, of whom [Hispanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanics) comprise an increasingly large proportion. 2008 marked the first time since 1944, when [Franklin D. Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) carried the state for the fourth time, that Florida was carried by a Northern Democrat for president.

The first post-[Reconstruction era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_era) Republican elected to Congress from Florida was [William C. Cramer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_C._Cramer) in 1954 from Pinellas County on the Gulf Coast,[^cite_note-William_C._Cramer,_81,_a_Leader_Of_G.O.P._Resurgence_in_South-219] where demographic changes were underway. In this period, African Americans were still [disenfranchised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disfranchisement_after_Reconstruction_era) by the state's constitution and discriminatory practices; in the 19th century, they had made up most of the Republican Party. Cramer built a different Republican Party in Florida, attracting local white conservatives and transplants from northern and midwestern states. In 1966, [Claude R. Kirk Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_R._Kirk_Jr.) was elected as the first post-Reconstruction Republican governor, in an upset election.[^cite_note-Claude_Roy_Kirk_Jr.-220] In 1968, [Edward J. Gurney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_J._Gurney), also a white conservative, was elected as the state's first post-reconstruction Republican US senator.[^cite_note-E._J._Gurney,_82,_Senator_Who_Backed_Nixon-221] In 1970, Democrats took the governorship and the open US Senate seat and maintained dominance for years.

Florida is sometimes considered a bellwether state in presidential elections because every candidate who won the state from 1996 until 2016 won the election.[^cite_note-222] The 2020 election broke that streak when [Donald Trump](4848272.md) won Florida but lost the election.

In 1998, Democratic voters dominated areas of the state with a high percentage of racial minorities and transplanted white liberals from the northeastern United States, known colloquially as "snowbirds". [South Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Florida) and the [Miami metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_metropolitan_area) became dominated by both racial minorities and white liberals. Because of this, the area has consistently voted as one of the most Democratic areas of the state. The Daytona Beach area is similar demographically and the city of Orlando has a large Hispanic population, which has often favored Democrats. Republicans, made up mostly of white conservatives, have dominated throughout much of the rest of Florida, including Jacksonville and the panhandle and particularly in the more rural and suburban areas. This is characteristic of its voter base throughout the [Deep South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_South).[^cite_note-The_New_York_Times-223]

The fast-growing [I-4 corridor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I-4_corridor) area, which runs through [Central Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Florida) and connects the cities of [Daytona Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytona_Beach,_Florida), [Orlando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlando,_Florida), and [Tampa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tampa,_Florida)/[St. Petersburg](84504.md), has had a fairly even breakdown of Republican and Democratic voters. The area has often been seen as a merging point of the conservative northern portion of the state and the liberal southern portion, making it the biggest swing area in the state. Since the late 20th century, the voting results in this area, containing 40% of Florida voters, has often determined who will win the state in federal presidential elections.[^cite_note-The_Washington_Times-224]

Historically, the Democratic Party maintained an edge in voter registration, both statewide and in the state's three most populous counties, [Miami-Dade County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_County), [Broward County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broward_County,_Florida), and [Palm Beach County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm_Beach_County).[^cite_note-Voter_Registration_by_Party_Affiliation_and_County-225]

#### 2000–present

In 2000, [George W. Bush](3414021.md) won the [U.S. presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_United_States_presidential_election) by a margin of 271–266 in the [Electoral College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_College_\(United_States\)).[^cite_note-archives2000-226] Of the 271 electoral votes for Bush, 25 were cast by electors from Florida.[^cite_note-archives1-227] The Florida results were contested and a recount was ordered by the court, with the results settled in a [Supreme Court](31737.md) decision, *[Bush v. Gore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bush_v._Gore)*.

Reapportionment following the 2010 United States census gave the state two more seats in the House of Representatives.\[^cite_note-[www.tampabay.com-228](http://www.tampabay.com-228/)] The legislature's redistricting, announced in 2012, was quickly challenged in court, on the grounds that it had unfairly benefited Republican interests. In 2015, the Florida Supreme Court ruled on appeal that the congressional districts had to be redrawn because of the legislature's violation of the Fair District Amendments to the state constitution passed in 2010; it accepted a new map in early December 2015.

The political make-up of congressional and legislative districts has enabled Republicans to control the governorship and most statewide elective offices, and 17 of the state's 27 seats in the 2012 [House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections_in_Florida,_2012).[^cite_note-229] Florida was listed as a [swing state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_state) in presidential elections from 1952 till 2024, voting for the losing candidate only twice in that period of time. Today, it is considered a reliably Republican state in state and national elections.[^cite_note-politico.com-230] [^cite_note-Florida1-231]

In the closely contested [2000 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_United_States_presidential_election_in_Florida), the state played a pivotal role.[^cite_note-archives2000-226][^cite_note-archives1-227][^cite_note-232][^cite_note-233][^cite_note-Study_of_Disputed_Florida_Ballots_Finds_Justices_Did_Not_Cast_the_Deciding_Vote-234][^cite_note-235] Out of more than 5.8 million votes for the two main contenders Bush and [Al Gore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Gore), around 500 votes separated the two candidates for the all-decisive Florida electoral votes that landed Bush the election win. Florida's [felony disenfranchisement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felony_disenfranchisement) law is more severe than most European nations or other American states. A 2002 study in the *[American Sociological Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Sociological_Review)* concluded that "if the state's 827,000 disenfranchised felons had voted at the same rate as other Floridians, Democratic candidate Al Gore would have won Florida—and the presidency—by more than 80,000 votes."[^cite_note-236]

In 2008, delegates of both the [Republican Florida primary election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Party_\(United_States\)_presidential_primaries,_2008#GOP_February_5_rule) and [Democratic Florida primary election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Party_\(United_States\)_presidential_primaries,_2008#Nullified_primaries) were stripped of half of their votes when the conventions met in August due to violation of both parties' national rules.

In the 2010 elections, Republicans solidified their dominance statewide, by winning the governor's mansion, and maintaining firm majorities in both houses of the state legislature. They won four previously Democratic-held seats to create a 19–6 Republican majority delegation representing Florida in the federal House of Representatives.

In 2010, more than 63% of state voters approved the initiated Amendments 5 and 6 to the state constitution, to ensure more fairness in districting. These have become known as the Fair District Amendments. As a result of the [2010 United States census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_States_census), Florida gained two House of Representative seats in 2012.\[^cite_note-[www.tampabay.com-228](http://www.tampabay.com-228/)] The legislature issued revised congressional districts in 2012, which were immediately challenged in court by supporters of the above amendments.

The court ruled in 2014, after lengthy testimony, that at least two districts had to be redrawn because of gerrymandering. After this was appealed, in July 2015 the Florida Supreme Court ruled that lawmakers had followed an illegal and unconstitutional process overly influenced by party operatives, and ruled that at least eight districts had to be redrawn. On December 2, 2015, a 5–2 majority of the Court accepted a new map of congressional districts, some of which was drawn by challengers. Their ruling affirmed the map previously approved by [Leon County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_County,_Florida) Judge Terry Lewis, who had overseen the original trial. It particularly made changes in South Florida.[^cite_note-237]

According to [The Sentencing Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sentencing_Project), the effect of Florida's felony disenfranchisement law is such that in 2014, "\[m]ore than one in ten Floridians—and nearly one in four African-American Floridians—are \[were] shut out of the polls because of felony convictions", although they had completed sentences and parole/probation requirements.[^cite_note-239]

The state switched back to the GOP in the [2016 presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_States_presidential_election_in_Florida), and again in [2020](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_presidential_election_in_Florida), when [Donald Trump](4848272.md) headed the party's ticket both times. 2020 marked the first time Florida sided with the eventual loser of the presidential election since [1992](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_United_States_presidential_election_in_Florida).

In the [2018 elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_United_States_elections), the ratio of Republican to Democratic representation fell from 16\:11 to 14\:13. The [U.S. Senate election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_United_States_Senate_election_in_Florida) between Democratic incumbent senator [Bill Nelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Nelson) and then governor [Rick Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rick_Scott) was close, with 49.93% voting for the incumbent and 50.06% voting for the former governor. Republicans also held onto the governorship in a [close race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Florida_gubernatorial_election) between Republican candidate [Ron DeSantis](36729527.md) and Democratic candidate [Andrew Gillum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Gillum), with 49.6% voting for DeSantis and 49.3% voting for Gillum. In [2022](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_United_States_elections), incumbent Governor DeSantis [won reelection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Florida_gubernatorial_election) by a [landslide](41053306.md) against Democrat [Charlie Crist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Crist). The unexpectedly large margin of victory led many pundits to question Florida's perennial status as a swing state, and instead identify it as a [red state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_states_and_blue_states).[^cite_note-240]

In November 2021, for the first time in Florida's history, the total number of registered Republican voters exceeded the number of registered Democrats.[^cite_note-241]

Today, it is considered a reliably Republican state in state and national elections.[^cite_note-politico.com-230]

### Statutes

See also: [Law of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Florida)



The [Florida Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Florida) building in [Tallahassee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallahassee,_Florida)

In 1972, the state made [personal injury protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_injury_protection) auto insurance mandatory for drivers, becoming the second in the U.S. to enact a [no-fault insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No-fault_insurance) law.[^cite_note-242] The ease of receiving payments under this law is seen as precipitating a major increase in insurance fraud.[^cite_note-243] Auto insurance fraud was the highest in the U.S. in 2011, estimated at close to \$1 billion.[^cite_note-244] Fraud is particularly centered in the Miami-Dade and Tampa areas.[^cite_note-245][^cite_note-246][^cite_note-247]

[Capital punishment](5902.md) is applied in Florida.[^cite_note-248] If a person committing a predicate felony directly contributed to the death of the victim then the person will be charged with murder in the first degree. The only two sentences available for that statute are life imprisonment and the death penalty. If a person commits a predicate felony, but was not the direct contributor to the death of the victim then the person will be charged with murder in the second degree. The maximum prison term is life.[^cite_note-The_Florida_Statutes.-249][^cite_note-guidelines-250] In 1995, the legislature modified Chapter 921 to provide that felons should serve at least 85% of their sentence.[^cite_note-251][^cite_note-252]

Florida approved its [lottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Lottery) by amending the constitution in 1984. It approved slot machines in Broward and [Miami-Dade County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Dade_County) in 2004. It has disapproved casinos (outside of sovereign [Seminole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminole) and [Miccosukee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miccosukee) tribal areas) three times: 1978, 1986, and 1994.[^cite_note-253]

## Economy

Main articles: [Economy of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Florida) and [Agriculture in Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_in_Florida)



The [Brickell Financial District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brickell_Financial_District) in [Miami](53846.md) contains the largest concentration of international banks in the United States.



Visitors enjoying the beach at [Pensacola Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pensacola_Beach)

In 2025, the economy of the state of Florida was the [fourth-largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_GDP) in the United States, with a \$1.834 trillion [gross state product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_state_product) (GSP) and Florida's per capita personal income was \$76,440.[^cite_note-254] If Florida were a sovereign nation (2024), it would rank as the [world's 15th-largest economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_\(nominal\)) according to the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md), ahead of [Spain](26667.md) and behind [South Korea](27019.md).[^cite_note-GDPByState-255][^cite_note-256][^cite_note-257] In 2025, Florida ranked third on *[CNBC](223441.md)*'s list of top states for business, which took into account overall economy, infrastructure, cost of doing business, among other factors.[^cite_note-258]

In the 20th century, tourism, industry, construction, international banking, biomedical and life sciences, healthcare research, simulation training, aerospace and defense, and commercial space travel have contributed to the state's economic development.[^cite_note-259]

Tourism is a large portion of Florida's economy. Florida is home to the world's most visited theme park, the [Magic Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_Kingdom).[^cite_note-260] Florida is also home to the largest single-site employer in the United States, [Walt Disney World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Disney_World).[^cite_note-261] [PortMiami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PortMiami) is the largest [passenger port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passenger_port) in the world and one of the largest [cargo ports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cargo_port) in the United States.[^cite_note-262]

Agriculture is another large part of the Florida economy. Florida is the number one grower of oranges for juice,[^cite_note-263] mangoes,[^cite_note-264] fresh tomatoes,[^cite_note-265] sugar,[^cite_note-266] sweet corn, green beans,[^cite_note-267] beans, cucumbers, watermelons, and more.[^cite_note-stacker.com-268] Florida is also the second biggest producer of strawberries, avocados, grapefruit, and peppers in the U.S.[^cite_note-stacker.com-268][^cite_note-269]

Other large sectors of Florida's economy include finance, government and military (especially in Jacksonville and Pensacola),[^cite_note-270] healthcare, aerospace (especially in the [Space Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Coast)), mining (especially for phosphate in [Bone Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone_Valley)), fishing, trade, real estate, and tech (especially in Miami, Orlando, and Tampa in the 2020s).

In 2025, small businesses made up 99.8% of businesses in Florida and employed 39.6% of the state's work force.[^cite_note-271] According to *Business Observer*, Florida ranked first among U.S. states for business formations in 2025, with 698,000 new businesses formed within the state that year.[^cite_note-272]

As of May 2025, the state's unemployment rate was 3.7%.[^cite_note-273]

#### Highways

Further information: [Florida State Highway System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_State_Highway_System), [List of state roads in Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_state_roads_in_Florida), and [County roads in Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_roads_in_Florida)

Florida's highway system contains 1,495 mi (2,406 km) of interstate highway, and 10,601 mi (17,061 km) of non-interstate highway, such as state highways and U.S. Highways. Florida's [interstates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_Highway_System), [state highways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_State_Highway_System), and [U.S. Highways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Highways) are maintained by the [Florida Department of Transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Department_of_Transportation).[^cite_note-274]

In 2011, there were about 9,000 [retail gas stations](61260.md) in the state. Floridians consumed 21 million gallons of gasoline daily in 2011, ranking it third in national use behind California and Texas.[^cite_note-275] As of 2024, motorists in Florida have one of the highest rates of car insurance in the U.S.[^cite_note-276][^cite_note-277] 24% are uninsured.[^cite_note-278]

Drivers between 15 and 19 years of age averaged 364 car crashes a year per ten thousand licensed Florida drivers in 2010. Drivers 70 and older averaged 95 per 10,000 during the same time frame. A spokesperson for the non-profit [Insurance Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insurance_Institute_for_Highway_Safety) stated "Older drivers are more of a threat to themselves."[^cite_note-279]

Intercity bus travel, which utilizes Florida's highway system, is provided by [Greyhound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greyhound_Lines), [Megabus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megabus_\(North_America\)), and [Amtrak Thruway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amtrak_Thruway).

Before the construction of routes under the [Federal Aid Highway Act of 1956](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Aid_Highway_Act_of_1956), Florida began construction of a long cross-state [toll road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toll_road), [Florida's Turnpike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida's_Turnpike). The first section, from [Fort Pierce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Pierce,_Florida) south to the [Golden Glades Interchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Glades_Interchange) was completed in 1957. After a second section north through Orlando to [Wildwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildwood,_Florida) (near present-day [The Villages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Villages,_Florida)), and a [southward extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestead_Extension_of_Florida's_Turnpike) around Miami to [Homestead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestead,_Florida), it was finished in 1974.

## Healthcare

See also: [List of hospitals in Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_hospitals_in_Florida) and [Abortion in Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abortion_in_Florida)



[Jackson Memorial Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackson_Memorial_Hospital) in the [Miami Health District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_District_\(Miami\)), the primary teaching hospital of the [Miller School of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_School_of_Medicine) at the [University of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Miami)

There were 2.7 million [Medicaid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicaid) patients in Florida in 2009.[^cite_note-County_Medicaid_tab_rises,_could_get_worse-290] The cost of caring for 2.3 million clients in 2010 was \$18.8 billion.[^cite_note-291]   This is nearly 30% of Florida's budget.[^cite_note-292] Medicaid paid for 42.2% of all births in Florida in 2023.[^cite_note-293] The state has a [program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Medicaid_waiver) for those not covered by Medicaid.

In 2013, Florida refused to participate in providing coverage for the uninsured under the [Affordable Care Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affordable_Care_Act), colloquially called Obamacare. The Florida legislature also refused to accept additional Federal funding for Medicaid, although this would have helped its constituents at no cost to the state. As a result, Florida is second only to Texas in the percentage of its citizens without health insurance.[^cite_note-294]

In 2022, the largest [hospital network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospital_network) in Florida is [HCA Healthcare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HCA_Healthcare)[^cite_note-295] and the second largest is [AdventHealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AdventHealth).[^cite_note-296][^cite_note-297] In 2023, the largest hospitals in Florida were [Jackson Memorial Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackson_Memorial_Hospital), [AdventHealth Orlando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AdventHealth_Orlando), [Tampa General Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tampa_General_Hospital), [UF Health Shands Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UF_Health_Shands_Hospital) and [Baptist Hospital of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptist_Hospital_of_Miami).[^cite_note-298]

[Mayo Clinic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayo_Clinic) hosts one of its three major U.S. campuses in Jacksonville. The practice specializes in treating difficult cases through [tertiary care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_care) and [destination medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_tourism#United_States).

Within Florida, certain cities are recognized for presenting challenges to allergy sufferers. For example, Sarasota has been ranked as 13th nationally for pollen counts in some assessments of US cities. Orlando is also frequently listed among the Top 20 "Allergy Capitals" in the United States, attributed in part to year-round allergens exacerbated by urban greenery. Miami is noted for elevated mold spore levels, particularly following hurricane events, which can worsen allergy symptoms for sensitive individuals.[^cite_note-allergy_cities_florida-299]

## Architecture

See also: [Architecture of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_Miami) and [Architecture of Jacksonville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_Jacksonville)



[Miami Art Deco District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Beach_Architectural_District) in [South Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Beach), built during the 1920s and 1930s

Florida has the largest collection of [Art Deco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Deco) and [Streamline Moderne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streamline_Moderne) buildings, both in the United States and in the entire world, most of which are located in the [Miami metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_metropolitan_area), especially [Miami Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Beach,_Florida)'s [Art Deco District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Beach_Architectural_District), constructed as the city was becoming a resort destination.[^cite_note-300] A unique architectural design found only in Florida is the post-World War II [Miami Modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Modern_Architecture), which can be seen in areas such as [Miami](53846.md)'s [MiMo Historic District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MiMo_Historic_District).[^cite_note-301]

Being of early importance as a regional center of banking and finance, the [architecture of Jacksonville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_Jacksonville) displays a wide variety of styles and design principles. Many of the state's earliest skyscrapers were constructed in Jacksonville, dating as far back as 1902,[^cite_note-302] and last holding a state height record from 1974 to 1981.[^cite_note-303] The city is endowed with one of the largest collections of [Prairie School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prairie_School) buildings outside of the Midwest.[^cite_note-304] Jacksonville is also noteworthy for its collection of [Mid-Century modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-Century_modern) architecture.[^cite_note-305]

Some sections of the state feature architectural styles including [Spanish revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Colonial_Revival_Style_architecture), [Florida vernacular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_cracker_architecture), and [Mediterranean Revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Revival_Style).[^cite_note-306] A notable collection of these styles can be found in [St. Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Augustine,_Florida), the oldest continuously occupied European-established settlement within the borders of the United States.[^cite_note-307]

### Primary and secondary education

Florida spent \$8,920 for each student in 2016, and was 43rd in the U.S. in expenditures per student.[^cite_note-310]

Florida's primary and secondary school systems are administered by the [Florida Department of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Department_of_Education). School districts are organized within county boundaries. Each school district has an elected [Board of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_of_Education) that sets policy, budget, goals, and approves expenditures. Management is the responsibility of a [Superintendent of schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superintendent_\(education\)).

The [Florida Department of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Department_of_Education) is required by law to train educators in teaching [English for Speakers of Other Languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language_learning_and_teaching) (ESOL).[^cite_note-311]

While Florida's public schools suffer from more than 5,000 unoccupied teacher positions, according to Karla Hernández, teacher and president of United Teachers of Dade, decisions made by the DeSantis administration will make the situation worse. She referred to its blocking of an [Advanced Placement African American studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AP_African_American_Studies) course,[^cite_note-312] book bans and removing some lessons in courses as "really scary moments in the state of Florida".[^cite_note-313]

In 2023, the state of Florida approved a public school curriculum including videos produced by conservative advocacy group [PragerU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PragerU), likening climate change skeptics to those who fought Communism and Nazism, implying renewable energy harms the environment, and saying global warming occurs naturally. DeSantis has called climate change "[leftwing](18499.md) stuff".[^cite_note-Guardian_20230810-314]

In August 2023, restrictions have been placed on the teaching of [Shakespearean plays and literature](32897.md) by Florida teachers in order to comply with state law.[^cite_note-315][^cite_note-316][^cite_note-317]

### Higher education

The [State University System of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_University_System_of_Florida) was founded in 1905, and is governed by the [Florida Board of Governors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Board_of_Governors). During the 2019 academic year, 346,604 students attended one of these twelve universities.[^cite_note-318] In 2016, Florida charged the second lowest tuition in the U.S. for four-year programs, at \$26,000 for in-state students and \$86,000 for out-of-state students; this compares with an average of \$34,800 for in-state students.[^cite_note-319]

As of 2020, three Florida universities are among the top 10 [largest universities by enrollment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_United_States_public_university_campuses_by_enrollment) in the United States: The [University of Central Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Central_Florida) in [Orlando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlando,_Florida) (2nd), the [University of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Florida) in [Gainesville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gainesville,_Florida) (4th), and [Florida International University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_International_University) in [Miami](53846.md) (8th).

The [Florida College System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_College_System) comprises 28 public community and state colleges with 68 campuses spread out throughout the state. In 2016, enrollment exceeded 813,000 students.[^cite_note-320]

The [Independent Colleges and Universities of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Colleges_and_Universities_of_Florida) is an association of 30 private, educational institutions in the state.[^cite_note-321] This Association reported that their member institutions served more than 158,000 students in the fall of 2020.[^cite_note-322]

The [University of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Miami) in [Coral Gables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral_Gables,_Florida) is one of the top private research universities in the U.S. Florida's first private university, [Stetson University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stetson_University) in [DeLand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DeLand,_Florida), was founded in 1883.

As of 2023, three universities in Florida are members of the [Association of American Universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_of_American_Universities): University of Florida, University of Miami and University of South Florida.[^cite_note-323]

## Sports

Main article: [Sports in Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_in_Florida)

See also: [Sports teams in Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_teams_in_Florida)



[Kaseya Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaseya_Center) in [Miami](53846.md)



[Amerant Bank Arena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amerant_Bank_Arena) in [Sunrise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunrise,_Florida)



[LoanDepot Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LoanDepot_Park) in [Little Havana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Havana), home field of the [Miami Marlins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Marlins) of [Major League Baseball](38776.md)



[Daytona International Speedway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytona_International_Speedway), home to various auto racing events, including the [Daytona 500](39021.md)

Florida has three [NFL](21211.md) teams, two [MLB](38776.md) teams, two [NBA](22093.md) teams, two [NHL](21809.md) teams, and two [MLS](71802.md) teams. Florida gained its first permanent major-league professional sports team in 1966 when the [American Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Football_League) added the [Miami Dolphins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Dolphins). Florida has given professional sports franchises some subsidies in the form of tax breaks since 1991.[^cite_note-324]

About half of all [Major League Baseball](38776.md) teams conduct [spring training](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_training) in the state, with teams informally organized into the "[Grapefruit League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grapefruit_League)". Throughout MLB history, other teams have held spring training in Florida.

[NASCAR](21114.md) (headquartered in [Daytona Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytona_Beach,_Florida)) begins all three of its major auto racing series in Florida at [Daytona International Speedway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytona_International_Speedway) in February, featuring the [Daytona 500](39021.md). Daytona also has the [Coke Zero Sugar 400](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_Zero_Sugar_400) NASCAR race weekend in August. NASCAR also has a race weekend at [Homestead-Miami Speedway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestead-Miami_Speedway) in [Homestead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestead,_Florida) in October. The [24 Hours of Daytona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/24_Hours_of_Daytona) is one of the world's most prestigious endurance auto races. The [Grand Prix of St. Petersburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Prix_of_St._Petersburg) and [Grand Prix of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Prix_of_Miami_\(Indycar\)) have held [IndyCar](30446232.md) races as well.

Florida is a major [golf](19568112.md) hub. The [PGA of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professional_Golfers'_Association_of_America) is headquartered in [Palm Beach Gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm_Beach_Gardens,_Florida), the [PGA Tour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PGA_Tour) is headquartered in [Ponte Vedra Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponte_Vedra_Beach,_Florida) (a Jacksonville suburb) and the [LPGA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LPGA) is headquartered in Daytona Beach. [The Players Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Players_Championship), [WGC-Cadillac Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WGC-Cadillac_Championship), [Arnold Palmer Invitational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Palmer_Invitational), [Honda Classic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honda_Classic) and [Valspar Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valspar_Championship) are PGA Tour rounds.

Florida has teams in all five American major league sports. Florida's most recent major-league team, [Inter Miami](41853326.md), began play in MLS in 2020.[^cite_note-325]

The [Miami Masters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Masters) is an [ATP World Tour Masters 1000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATP_World_Tour_Masters_1000) and [WTA Premier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WTA_Premier_tournaments) tennis event, whereas the [Delray Beach International Tennis Championships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delray_Beach_International_Tennis_Championships) is an [ATP World Tour 250](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATP_World_Tour_250) event.

There are minor league baseball, [football](18951490.md), basketball, [ice hockey](14790.md), soccer and [indoor football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indoor_American_football) teams based in Florida.[^cite_note-326] [Ben Hill Griffin Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Hill_Griffin_Stadium) is the largest football stadium in Florida, the [12th-largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_NCAA_Division_I_FBS_football_stadiums) stadium in [college football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_football), and the [18th-largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_stadiums_by_capacity) stadium in the world, as measured by its official [seating capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seating_capacity) of 88,548—though, it has often held over 90,000 for Florida's home football games.

Florida's universities have a number of [collegiate sport](60706.md) programs. Major college football programs include the [Florida State Seminoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_State_Seminoles) and [Miami Hurricanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Hurricanes) of the [Atlantic Coast Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Coast_Conference), and the [Florida Gators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Gators) of the [Southeastern Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeastern_Conference).[^cite_note-327] Since 1996, Florida has added four additional teams to the ranks of [Division I FBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA_Division_I_Football_Bowl_Subdivision): [UCF Knights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCF_Knights), [South Florida Bulls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Florida_Bulls), [Florida Atlantic Owls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Atlantic_Owls) and [FIU Panthers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FIU_Panthers).
