[Japan](15573.md) has a highly developed [mixed economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_economy). It is known as an [East Asian model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asian_model) for its public investment into strategic sectors.[^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29] Japan is the fourth-largest economy in the world [by nominal GDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_\(nominal\)) and the fifth-largest [by purchasing power parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_\(PPP\)) (PPP).[^cite_note-30] It constituted 3.7% of the world's economy on a nominal basis in 2024.[^cite_note-IMFWEOJP-31][^cite_note-32] A founding member of the [G7](1196634.md) and an early member of the [OECD](33853117.md), Japan was the first country in Asia to achieve [developed country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developed_country) status. In 2024, Japan was the sixth-largest in the world [as an importer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_imports) and eight-largest [as an exporter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_exports).[^cite_note-\:22-33][^cite_note-34] The country also has the world's [fourth-largest consumer market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_consumer_markets),[^cite_note-35] and consumption accounts for around 53% of GDP.[^cite_note-\:19-36]

Long having been an agricultural country, it has been estimated that Japan’s economy was among the top ten in the world by size before the industrial revolution started.[^cite_note-37] Industrialisation in Japan began in the 19th century with the [Meiji Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration), initially focusing on the [textile industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textile_industry) and later on heavy industries. The country rapidly built [its colonial empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Japan). After the defeat in the [Second World War](32927.md), Japan’s economy recovered, primarily propelled by its lucrative manufacturing exporting industries.[^cite_note-38] Japan was the [second largest economy in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_largest_historical_GDP) from the 1980s to 2010,[^cite_note-\:0-39] and on a nominal per capita basis, the most high-income among the G7 countries in the 1980s and 1990s.[^cite_note-40] Since the collapse of the [Japanese asset price bubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_asset_price_bubble) in the early 1990s, the country has experienced a prolonged period of [economic stagnation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_stagnation) referred to as the [Lost Decades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Decades).&#x20;

The country has the world's [second-largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_foreign-exchange_reserves) foreign-exchange reserves, worth \$1.4 trillion.[^cite_note-Japan_reserves-41] Japan has the [third-largest financial assets in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states_by_financial_assets), valued at \$12 trillion, or 8.6% of the global GDP total as of 2020.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-fin-43] Japan has a highly efficient [social security system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_in_Japan), which comprises [roughly 23.5% of GDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_state#Effects).[^cite_note-Kenworthy-4][^cite_note-Bradley_et_al.-44][^cite_note-Japan_welfare-3] The [Tokyo Stock Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Stock_Exchange) is the world's [fourth-largest stock exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_stock_exchanges) by [market capitalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_capitalisation) as of 2025.[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46] Japan has a highly service-dominated economy, making up 70% of GDP.[^cite_note-47] The [country's automobile industry](30864860.md) is the third largest in the world.[^cite_note-48] In 2022, Japan spent around 3.7% of GDP on research and development. As of 2025, 38 of the *[Fortune Global 500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_Global_500)* companies are based in Japan.[^cite_note-Global_500_2015-49]

## History

Main article: [Economic history of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_Japan)

The economic history of Japan is one of the most studied. Major milestones in modern Japan's economic progress include:

- The foundation of the [Tokugawa shogunate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_shogunate) based in [Edo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_\(Tokyo\)) (in 1603), initiating a period of internal economic development.

- The [Meiji Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration) (in 1868) leading to Japan becoming the first non-European modern world power.

- [Japan's defeat in World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrender_of_Japan) (in 1945), after which the island nation rose to become the world's second-largest economy.

- The [Lost Decades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Decades) (the 1990s, 2000s and 2010s), during which the country struggled to get out of deflation and extremely low or negative growth.

### Edo period (1603–1868)

Main article: [Economics of feudal Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics_of_feudal_Japan)



An 1856 [ukiyo-e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukiyo-e) depicting Echigoya, the current [Mitsukoshi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsukoshi)

The beginning of the [Edo period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_period) coincides with the last decades of the [Nanban trade period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanban_trade), marked by intense interaction with European powers. Japan built its first Western-style warships, such as the [*San Juan Bautista*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Juan_Bautista_\(ship\)), and commissioned around 350 [Red Seal Ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_seal_ships) for intra-Asian commerce. Japanese adventurers, such as [Yamada Nagamasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamada_Nagamasa), were active throughout Asia.[^cite_note-50]

To eradicate [Christian influence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianization), Japan entered a period of isolation called *[sakoku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakoku)* in the 1630s, which led to economic stability and mild progress. In the 1650s, [Japanese export porcelain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_export_porcelain) production increased significantly due to [a civil war in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_from_Ming_to_Qing), mainly in [Kyushu](16914.md).[^cite_note-51] This trade dwindled by the 1740s under renewed Chinese competition but resumed after Japan’s mid-19th century opening.

Economic development during the Edo period included [urbanisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanization), increased commodity shipping, and expanded domestic and foreign commerce. The construction trades, banking facilities, and merchant associations flourished. [*Daimyō*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daimyo)-led authorities (*han*) oversaw rising agricultural production and rural handicrafts. By the mid-18th century, Edo had a population of over 1 million, while Osaka and Kyoto each had more than 400,000 inhabitants, becoming centres for trade and [handicraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handicraft) production.[^cite_note-52] Rice, the economy’s base, was taxed at about 40% of the harvest and sold at the fudasashi market in Edo. *Daimyō* used [forward contracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forward_contract) similar to modern futures trading to sell rice before harvest.[^cite_note-53]

During the *sakoku* period, Japan studied Western sciences and techniques (*[rangaku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rangaku)*) through Dutch traders in Dejima, including geography, medicine, natural sciences, astronomy, and mechanical sciences. Japan reopened its economy to the West after being pressured by the [United States](3434750.md) twice in 1853 and 1854.[^cite_note-54]

### Meiji and Imperial Period (1868–1945)

Main article: [Economy of the Empire of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_Empire_of_Japan)

Since the mid-19th century, after the [Meiji Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration), the country was opened up to Western commerce and influence and went through a period of economic development that extended through to the [First World War](4764461.md).[^cite_note-55] Economic developments of the [prewar period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Japan) began with the "[Rich State and Strong Army Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukoku_ky%C5%8Dhei)" by the [Meiji government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_government). During "the [Meiji period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_period) (1868–1912), leaders inaugurated a new Western-based education system for all young people, sent thousands of students to"[^cite_note-\:3-56] Europe and the United States, "and hired more than 3,000 Westerners to teach modern science, mathematics, technology, and foreign languages in Japan"[^cite_note-\:3-56] (*[Oyatoi gaikokujin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oyatoi_gaikokujin)*). The government also built an extensive railway network, "improved roads, and inaugurated a land reform program to prepare the country for further development."[^cite_note-\:3-56]

To promote [industrialisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrialisation), "the government decided that, while it should help private business to allocate resources and to plan, the private sector was best equipped to stimulate economic growth. The greatest role of government was to help provide"[^cite_note-\:3-56] good economic conditions for business. "In short, government was to be the guide and business the producer. In the early Meiji period, the government built factories and shipyards that were sold to entrepreneurs at a fraction of their value. Many of these businesses grew rapidly into the larger [conglomerates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conglomerate_\(company\))"[^cite_note-\:3-56] such as [Mitsubishi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi). "Government emerged as chief promoter of [private enterprise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_enterprise), enacting a series of" pro-business policies.[^cite_note-57]

### Post-war period (1945–1991)

See also: [Japanese economic miracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_economic_miracle) and [Balance of payments accounts of Japan (1960–1990)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balance_of_payments_accounts_of_Japan_\(1960%E2%80%931990\))



Japanese exports partners in 2005

Japan underwent significant economic transformation and rapid recovery and growth, emerging from the devastation of the [Second World War](32927.md) to become a global economic powerhouse. The immediate post-war period saw Japan slowly recovering as a democratic nation under the [Allied Occupation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allied_Occupation_of_Japan). The [Korean War](16772.md) (1950–1953), which happened in its now divided former colony, boosted the economy, as Japan served as a major supply hub for U.S. forces. By the 1950s and 1960s, Japan’s economy had entered a period of high growth, often referred to as the '[Japanese Economic Miracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_economic_miracle)'. Key factors in this growth included government-industry cooperation, a strong work ethic, advanced technology, and a focus on export-oriented [manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing_in_Japan). Japan’s economy diversified from textiles to steel, shipbuilding, and eventually electronics and automobiles, with companies such as [Toyota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyota), [Sony](26989.md), [Hitachi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitachi) and [Honda](13729.md) becoming household names worldwide.[^cite_note-58]

In 1968, Japan became the world’s third-largest economy and then the second largest in the 1980s, surpassing the Soviet Union, a position it held until it was surpassed by China in 2010. The government played a crucial role through policies that promoted industrial expansion and technological advancement. Japan’s emphasis on quality control and continuous improvement (*[kaizen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaizen)*) further boosted its international competitiveness.[^cite_note-59] By the 1980s, Japan was leading in a wide range of industries, including automotive and consumer electronics, and was known for its formidable trade surplus and wealth.[^cite_note-60] However, the late 1980s also saw the infamous [Plaza Accord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_Accord) and the formation of an asset price bubble, with inflated real estate and stock market prices, setting the stage for the economic stagnation of the '[Lost Decades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Decades)' that followed.

Growth slowed markedly in the late 1990s, also termed the [Lost Decade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Decades), after the collapse of the [Japanese asset price bubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_asset_price_bubble). As a consequence Japan ran massive budget deficits (added trillions in yen to the Japanese financial system) to finance large [public works](323158.md) programs.

By 1998, Japan's public works projects still could not stimulate demand enough to end the economy's stagnation. In desperation, the Japanese government undertook "structural reform" policies intended to wring speculative excesses from the stock and real estate markets. Unfortunately, these policies led Japan into deflation on numerous occasions between 1999 and 2004. The Bank of Japan used [quantitative easing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantitative_easing) to expand the country's money supply in order to raise expectations of inflation and spur economic growth. Initially, the policy failed to induce any growth, but it eventually began to affect inflationary expectations. By late 2005, the economy finally began what seems to be a sustained recovery. GDP growth for that year was 2.8%, with an annualised fourth quarter expansion of 5.5%, surpassing the growth rates of the US and [European Union](1598533.md) during the same period.[^cite_note-63] Unlike previous recovery trends, domestic consumption has been the dominant factor of growth.

Despite having interest rates down near zero for a long period of time, the quantitative easing strategy did not succeed in stopping price deflation.[^cite_note-64] This led some economists, such as Paul Krugman, and some Japanese politicians, to advocate the generation of higher inflation expectations.[^cite_note-65] In July 2006, the zero-rate policy was ended. In 2008, the Japanese Central Bank still had the lowest interest rates in the developed world, but deflation had still not been eliminated[^cite_note-66] and the Nikkei 225 has fallen over approximately 50% (between June 2007 and December 2008). However, on 5 April 2013, the [Bank of Japan](188155.md) announced that it would be purchasing 60–70 trillion yen in bonds and securities in an attempt to eliminate deflation by doubling the money supply in Japan over the course of two years. Markets around the world have responded positively to the government's current proactive policies, with the [Nikkei 225](40293.md) adding more than 42% since November 2012.[^cite_note-67] *[The Economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Economist)* has suggested that improvements to bankruptcy law, land transfer law, and tax laws will aid Japan's economy. In recent years, Japan has been the top export market for almost 15 trading nations worldwide.

In December 2018, a [free trade agreement](2533562.md) between Japan and the [European Union](9317.md) was cleared to commence in February 2019. It creates the world's largest [free trade zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_trade_zone) valued at 1/3rd of global gross domestic product. This reduces tariffs on Japanese cars by 10%, duties by 30% on cheese and 10% on wines and opens service markets.[^cite_note-68]

### Reiwa period (2020–present)

### 2020–21 recession

In early January 2020, Japanese economy began to suffer from the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Japan). In early April, Japanese Prime Minister [Shinzo Abe](2634434.md) announced a [state of emergency](298608.md).[^cite_note-69][^cite_note-416emergency-70] Less than a quarter of Japanese people expected living conditions to improve in the coming decades.[^cite_note-71] In October 2020 during the pandemic, Japan and the [United Kingdom](31717.md) formally signed the first free-trade agreement post-[Brexit](41688778.md), which will boost trade by approximately £15.2 billion. It enables tariff-free trade on 99% of exports to Japan.[^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73] On 15 February 2021, the Nikkei average breached the 30k benchmark, the highest since November 1991.[^cite_note-30kbenchmark-74] It was due to strong corporate earnings, GDP data and optimism over the [COVID-19 vaccination program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_vaccination_in_Japan) in the country.[^cite_note-30kbenchmark-74]

### Post-recession (2021–present)

As of 2021, Japan has significantly higher [public debt](36392189.md) than other developed nations, at approximately 260% of GDP.[^cite_note-Nikkeimoneypushers-75][^cite_note-debttogdp-76] 45% of this debt is held by the Bank of Japan, and most of the remainder is also held domestically.[^cite_note-Nikkeimoneypushers-75] The Japanese economy faces considerable challenges posed by an [ageing and declining population](15015085.md), which peaked at 128.5 million people in 2010 and has fallen to 122.6 million people in 2024.[^cite_note-77] In 2024, the country's working age population consisted of approximately 59.6% of the total population, which was the lowest rate among all the OECD countries.[^cite_note-78] According to 2023 government projections, the country's population will fall to 87 million by 2070, with only 45 million of working age.[^cite_note-79]

At the end of March 2022, the [Ministry of Finance](870389.md) announced that the national debt reached precisely 1.017 million billion yen.[^cite_note-medium-80] The total public debt of the country, which includes debts contracted by local governments, represents 1.210 million billion yen (9,200 billion dollars) which is nearly 250% of Japan's GDP.[^cite_note-medium-80] Economist Kohei Iwahara said such an exceptional debt to GDP level is only possible because Japanese hold most of the debt: "“Japanese households hold most of their savings in bank accounts (48%) and these sums are used by commercial banks to buy Japanese government bonds. Thus, 85.7% of these bonds are held by Japanese investors.”[^cite_note-medium-80]

The [Bank of Japan](188155.md)'s main policy aim since the Lost Decades started had been to end [deflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflation) and eventually achieve 2% [inflation](38286.md).[^cite_note-japandebt-81] The increased international economic tension brought about by events such as the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md) finally helped the country achieve the much-anticipated inflation target of 2%, and the [negative interest policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_interest_rate) was ended in March 2024.[^cite_note-\:1-82][^cite_note-\:2-83] However, while other major economies focus on suppressing inflation by raising interest rates, Japan aims to firmly establish inflation by maintaining low rates. As a side effect, the [Japanese yen](34392.md) has become extremely weak, hitting a 37.5-year low of 161 yen/USD in July 2024.[^cite_note-84][^cite_note-85] Furthermore, the [real effective exchange rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_exchange_rate) in May 2024, when the 2020 average is set at 100, is 68.65, the lowest level since the start of the Bank of Japan statistics in January 1970, due to a combination of low inflation in Japan and a relatively low trade share.[^cite_note-86][^cite_note-87][^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89] This devaluation of the currency caused Japan to lose its status as the [world’s third largest economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_\(nominal\)) to [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Germany) in nominal terms, which was approximately half the size of the country's economy a decade earlier.[^cite_note-Japan_slips-90][^cite_note-McCurry-91][^cite_note-92]

Factors such as an apparent end to the 30-year struggle against deflation, improvements in corporate governance, and high corporate profits boosted the stock market. Consequently, both the [Nikkei 225](40293.md) and [TOPIX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TOPIX) indices surpassed the record highs they reached more than 30 years ago in 2024. The market capitalisation of the Tokyo Stock Exchange’s prime section exceeded a quadrillion yen for the first time in July 2024.[^cite_note-93] In June 2026, the Bank of Japan raised its short-term policy rate by 0.25 to "around 1 per cent", the highest policy rate since 1995. Analysts described the move as a milestone in monetary normalisation.[^cite_note-94]

## Macroeconomic trend

Driven by speculative investments and excessive lending, the [Japanese asset price bubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_asset_price_bubble) of the early 1990s burst, triggering a prolonged period of economic stagnation marked by deflation and persistently low or negative growth, now known as the [Lost Decades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Decades). From 1995 to 2023, the country’s GDP fell from \$5.5 trillion to \$4.2 trillion in nominal terms.[^cite_note-95] At the turn of the 21st century, the [Bank of Japan](188155.md) set out to encourage growth through a policy of [quantitative easing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantitative_easing), with the central bank purchasing government bonds at an unprecedented scale to address the persisting [deflationary pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflation).[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-japandebt-81] In 2016, the Bank of Japan introduced a [negative interest policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_interest_rate) to stimulate economic growth and combat persistent deflationary pressure. A combination of domestic policies and global economic conditions helped the country achieve its 2% inflation target, leading to the conclusion of the policy in 2024.[^cite_note-\:1-82][^cite_note-\:2-83]

Japan used to run a considerable [trade surplus](4810.md), but the decline of the manufacturing sector since the 1980s and increased fossil fuel imports after the [Fukushima nuclear accident](31162817.md) in 2011 have changed this trend in recent years.[^cite_note-97][^cite_note-98]

### Agriculture, livestock and forestry

Main article: [Agriculture, forestry, and fishing in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture,_forestry,_and_fishing_in_Japan)

See also: [Rice production in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice_production_in_Japan), [Cocoa production in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocoa_production_in_Japan), [Tea in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tea_in_Japan), [Beer in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beer_in_Japan), [Japanese wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_wine), [Agricultural protectionism in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_protectionism_in_Japan), [Cattle in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle_in_Japan), and [Animal welfare and rights in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_welfare_and_rights_in_Japan)



Rice is a very important crop in Japan as shown here in a [rice paddy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice_paddy) in [Tawaramoto, Nara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tawaramoto,_Nara).

The Japanese agricultural sector accounts for about 1.1% (2017) of the total country's GDP.[^cite_note-Japan_Country_Report-103] Only 12% of Japan's land is suitable for cultivation.[^cite_note-As_Farmers_Age,_Japan_Rethinks_Relationship_with_Food,_Fields-104][^cite_note-Trip_Report_-_Japan_Agricultural_Situation-105] Due to this lack of arable land, a system of terraces is used to farm in small areas.[^cite_note-Urbanites_Help_Sustain_Japan's_Historic_Rice_Paddy_Terraces-106] This results in one of the world's highest levels of crop yields per unit area, with an overall agricultural self-sufficiency rate of about 50% on fewer than 56,000 km<sup>2</sup> (14 million acres) cultivated.

Japan's small agricultural sector, however, is also highly subsidised and protected, with government regulations that favour small-scale cultivation instead of large-scale agriculture as practiced in North America.[^cite_note-As_Farmers_Age,_Japan_Rethinks_Relationship_with_Food,_Fields-104] There has been a growing concern about farming as the current farmers are aging with a difficult time finding successors.[^cite_note-How_will_Japan's_farms_survive?-107]

Rice accounts for almost all of Japan's cereal production.[^cite_note-Japan_-_Agriculture-108] Japan is the second-largest agricultural product importer in the world.[^cite_note-Japan_-_Agriculture-108] Rice, the most protected crop, is subject to tariffs of 777.7%.[^cite_note-Trip_Report_-_Japan_Agricultural_Situation-105][^cite_note-With_fewer,_bigger_plots_and_fewer_part-time_farmers,_agriculture_could_compete-109]

Although Japan is usually self-sufficient in rice (except for its use in making rice crackers and processed foods) and wheat, the country must import about 50% of its requirements of other grain and fodder crops and relies on imports for half of its supply of meat.[^cite_note-Japan_Immigration_Work_Permits_and_Visas-110][^cite_note-Japan_needs_imports_to_keep_itself_fed-111] Japan imports large quantities of wheat and [soybeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soybean).[^cite_note-Japan_-_Agriculture-108] Japan is the 5th largest market for the European Union's agricultural exports.[^cite_note-Agricultural_trade_in_2012:_EU-112] Over 90% of [mandarin oranges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandarin_orange) in Japan are grown domestically.[^cite_note-Japan_needs_imports_to_keep_itself_fed-111] [Apples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple) are also grown due to restrictions on apple imports.[^cite_note-Japan_may_warm_to_U.S._apples-113]

#### Fishery and whaling

Main articles: [Fishing industry in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing_industry_in_Japan) and [Whaling in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whaling_in_Japan)



Global fish catch in Japan

Japan ranked fourth in the world in 1996 in [tonnage of fish caught](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing_industry_by_country).[^cite_note-World_review_of_fisheries_and_aquaculture-114] Japan captured 4,074,580 metric tons of fish in 2005, down from 4,987,703 tons in 2000, 9,558,615 tons in 1990, 9,864,422 tons in 1980, 8,520,397 tons in 1970, 5,583,796 tons in 1960 and 2,881,855 tons in 1950.[^cite_note-Fish_capture_by_country-115] In 2003, the total aquaculture production was predicted at 1,301,437 tonnes.[^cite_note-Japan_National_Aquaculture_Sector_Overview-116] In 2010, Japan's total fisheries production was 4,762,469 fish.[^cite_note-World_fisheries_production,_by_capture_and_aquaculture,_by_country_(2010)-117] Offshore fisheries accounted for an average of 50% of the nation's total fish catches in the late 1980s although they experienced repeated ups and downs during that period.

Coastal fishing by small boats, set nets, or breeding techniques accounts for about one third of the industry's total production, while offshore fishing by medium-sized boats makes up for more than half the total production. Deep-sea fishing from larger vessels makes up the rest. Among the many species of seafood caught are sardines, [skipjack tuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skipjack_tuna), crab, shrimp, salmon, [pollock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollock), squid, clams, [mackerel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mackerel), [sea bream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_bream), [sauries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauries), tuna and [Japanese amberjack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_amberjack). Freshwater fishing, including salmon, trout and eel hatcheries and fish farms,[^cite_note-FRESHWATER_FISH_CULTURE_IN_JAPAN-118] takes up about 30% of Japan's fishing industry. Among the nearly 300 fish species in the rivers of Japan are native varieties of catfish, chub, herring and goby, as well as such freshwater crustaceans as crabs and crayfish.[^cite_note-List_of_Freshwater_Fishes_for_Japan-119] Marine and freshwater aquaculture is conducted in all 47 prefectures in Japan.[^cite_note-Japan_National_Aquaculture_Sector_Overview-116]

Japan maintains one of the world's largest fishing fleets and accounts for nearly 15% of the global catch,[^cite_note-120] prompting some claims that Japan's fishing is leading to depletion in fish stocks such as [tuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuna).[^cite_note-UN_tribunal_halts_Japanese_tuna_over-fishing-121] Japan has also sparked controversy by supporting quasi-commercial [whaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whaling_in_Japan).[^cite_note-Japanese_whaling_'science'_rapped-122]

### Industry

Main article: [Manufacturing in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing_in_Japan)

See also: [Industrial policy of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_policy_of_Japan)

Japanese manufacturing and industry is very diversified, with a variety of advanced industries that are highly successful. Industry accounts for 30.1% (2017) of the nation's GDP.[^cite_note-Japan_Country_Report-103] The country's manufacturing output is the third highest in the world.[^cite_note-123]

Industry is concentrated in several regions, with the [Kantō region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kant%C5%8D_region) surrounding Tokyo, (the [Keihin industrial region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keihin_region)) as well as the [Kansai region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansai_region) surrounding Osaka (the [Hanshin industrial region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanshin_Industrial_Region)) and the [Tōkai region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%8Dkai_region) surrounding Nagoya (the [Chūkyō–Tōkai industrial region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C5%ABky%C5%8D%E2%80%93T%C5%8Dkai_industrial_region)) the main industrial centers.[^cite_note-NRI_Tokai_Industry-124][^cite_note-Case_study_of_regional_university-industry_partnership_in_practice-125][^cite_note-Evaluating_the_Impacts_of_Regional_Cluster_Policies-126][^cite_note-Location_and_Role_of_Foreign_Firms_in_Regional_Innovation_Systems_in_Japan-127][^cite_note-Profile_of_Osaka/Kansai-128][^cite_note-Japan_in_the_21st_Century-129] Other industrial centers include the southwestern part of [Honshū](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honsh%C5%AB) and northern [Shikoku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shikoku) around the [Seto Inland Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seto_Inland_Sea) (the Setouchi industrial region); and the northern part of [Kyūshū](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ky%C5%ABsh%C5%AB) ([Kitakyūshū](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitaky%C5%ABsh%C5%AB)). In addition, a long narrow belt of industrial centers called the [Taiheiyō Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiheiy%C5%8D_Belt) is found between Tokyo and [Fukuoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukuoka,_Fukuoka), established by particular industries, that have developed as mill towns.

Japan enjoys high technological development in many fields, including [consumer electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_consumer_electronics_industry), [automobile manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automobile_industry_in_Japan), [semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor) manufacturing, [optical fibers](3372377.md), [optoelectronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optoelectronics), [optical media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_media), [facsimile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fax) and [copy machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photocopying), and fermentation processes in [food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermentation_\(food\)) and [biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermentation_\(biochemistry\)). #### Semiconductor

Main article: [Semiconductor industry in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor_industry_in_Japan)

This section is an excerpt from [Semiconductor industry in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor_industry_in_Japan).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Semiconductor_industry_in_Japan\&action=edit)]

The [Japanese semiconductor industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor_industry_in_Japan) once dominated the world's semiconductor market, with its revenue share reaching 50% in the 1980s. Various explanations have been proposed for this dominance by Japanese semiconductor manufacturers.[^cite_note-138] However, due to a combination of factors, including the shift towards [fabless manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabless_manufacturing) in the global semiconductor industry, lack of investment caused by the [country's stagnant economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Decades), high wages, [an appreciating yen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endaka), [rapid industrialisation in the rest of Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Asian_Tigers), and an agreement with the US to protect the American semiconductor industry, this had dwindled to approximately 10% by 2019.

The areas where the country still remains competitive are semiconductor manufacturing equipment ([Tokyo Electron](27745200.md) (TEL), [SCREEN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SCREEN_Holdings), [Advantest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advantest)), wafers ([Shin-Etsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shin-Etsu_Chemical), [SUMCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SUMCO)), [NAND flash memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_memory) ([Kioxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kioxia), formerly Toshiba Memory), and [power semiconductor devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_semiconductor_device) ([Renesas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renesas_Electronics), [Rohm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rohm)).[^cite_note-139] [Dynamic random-access memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_random-access_memory) is still developed and manufactured in Japan by the American company [Micron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micron_Technology), which acquired the Japanese firm [Elpida Memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micron_Memory_Japan). Since 2021, the [Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Economy,_Trade_and_Industry) has offered large subsidy packages to bring back advanced semiconductor development and manufacturing capabilities to the country. Notable projects backed by this initiative include [Rapidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapidus), [JASM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Advanced_Semiconductor_Manufacturing), and Kioxia's investment in 3D vertical NAND memory factories.[^cite_note-140]

#### Steel

Japan's steel industry is currently the world's fourth largest, focusing on high-quality steel production and primarily driven by major manufacturers such as [Nippon Steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nippon_Steel), [JFE Steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JFE_Steel), and [Kobe Steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kobe_Steel). Concentrated in regions such as Tokyo, Osaka-Kobe, and Kyushu, the sector is foundational to Japan's economy. Japan is a major exporter, with increasing competition from low-cost Chinese steel imports.[^cite_note-141]

#### Shipbuilding

Japan's shipbuilding industry is currently the world's third-largest, holding roughly 10% of the market, that focus on bulk carriers, LNG carriers, and tankers. Major players such as [Imabari Shipbuilding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imabari_Shipbuilding) and [Japan Marine United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Marine_United) (JMU) dominate the market, with a strong push toward modernization and potential government-backed consolidation to regain market share from China and South Korea.[^cite_note-142]

#### Pharmaceutical

Japan’s pharmaceutical market is the world's third-largest, valued at over 11.5 trillion yen in 2024, driven by an aging population, high demand for oncology and diabetes treatments, and a strong push toward generic drug usage. The industry is shifting from traditional medicine to biologics and specialized therapies, with major players such as [Takeda Pharmaceutical Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takeda_Pharmaceutical_Company) and [Daiichi Sankyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daiichi_Sankyo) that are focusing on R\&D for international expansion.[^cite_note-143]

Main article: [Housing in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing_in_Japan)

This section is an excerpt from [Housing in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing_in_Japan).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Housing_in_Japan\&action=edit)]

Various types of modern housing in Japan



Traditional-style [Sukiya-zukuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sukiya-zukuri)

[Housing in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing_in_Japan) includes modern and traditional styles. Two patterns of residences are predominant in contemporary Japan: the [single-family detached](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_detached_home) [house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House) and the [multiple-unit building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-family_residential), either owned by an individual or corporation and rented as apartments to tenants, or owned by occupants. Additional kinds of housing, especially for unmarried people, include boarding houses (which are popular among college students), dormitories (common in companies), and barracks (for members of the [Japan Self-Defense Forces](2785204.md), police and some other public employees).

An unusual feature of Japanese housing is that houses are presumed to have a limited lifespan, and are often torn down and rebuilt after a few decades, generally twenty years for wooden buildings and thirty years for concrete buildings – see [regulations](#Housing_regulations) for details. [Renovating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renovation) houses, rather than rebuilding them, is a relatively uncommon practice in Japan, though its prevalence is increasing, indicating that attitudes towards the use of older houses may be changing.[^cite_note-165]

Japan is located in a seismically active region, and earthquake resistance is a major consideration in housing.[^cite_note-166] The Building Standards Act sets minimum standards for building safety,[^cite_note-167] and seismic design requirements have been strengthened over time, including a major revision in 1981.[^cite_note-168] Post-disaster surveys after the 1995 Great Hanshin–Awaji Earthquake found that buildings constructed to older seismic standards generally suffered greater damage than those built to the 1981 standards.[^cite_note-169] Many detached houses are wood-frame construction, in which seismic performance depends on factors such as the amount and layout of shear walls and bracing.[^cite_note-170]

#### Education

Main article: [Education in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_Japan)

This section is an excerpt from [Education in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_Japan).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Education_in_Japan\&action=edit)]

[Education in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_Japan) is managed by the [Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Education,_Culture,_Sports,_Science_and_Technology) (MEXT) of [Japan](15573.md). [Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education) is [compulsory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compulsory_education) at the [elementary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_education) and [lower secondary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_school) levels, for a total of nine years.[^cite_note-171]

The contemporary Japanese education system is a product of historical reforms dating back to the [Meiji period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_era), which established modern educational institutions and systems.[^cite_note-172] This early start of modernisation enabled Japan to provide education at all levels in the native language ([Japanese](15606.md)),[^cite_note-173] rather than using the languages of powerful countries that could have had a strong influence in the region.[^cite_note-174] Current educational policies focus on promoting lifelong learning, advanced professional education, and internationalising higher education through initiatives such as accepting more international students, as the nation has a rapidly [ageing and shrinking population](15015085.md).[^cite_note-175][^cite_note-176]

Japanese students consistently achieve high rankings compared to other countries in reading, mathematics, and sciences according to [OECD](33853117.md) evaluations. In the 2018 [Programme for International Student Assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programme_for_International_Student_Assessment) (PISA), Japan ranked eighth globally, with an average score of 520 compared to the OECD average of 488.[^cite_note-177][^cite_note-Education_in_Japan_OECD_Student_performance-178][^cite_note-Education_in_Japan_\:2-179] Despite this relatively high performance, Japan’s spending on education as a percentage of GDP is 4.1%, below the OECD average of 5%.[^cite_note-Education_in_Japan_OECD_Student_performance2-180] However, the expenditure per student is relatively high. As of 2023, around 65% of Japanese aged 25 to 34 have attained some form of tertiary education, with a significant number holding degrees in science and engineering, fields crucial to Japan’s technology-driven economy.[^cite_note-Education_in_Japan_\:22-181] Japanese women surpass men in higher education attainment, with 59% holding university degrees compared to 52% of men. MEXT reports that 80.6% of 18-year-olds pursue higher education, with a majority attending universities.[^cite_note-Education_in_Japan_\:32-182]

#### Healthcare

Main article: [Healthcare in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthcare_in_Japan)

This section is an excerpt from [Health care system in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_care_system_in_Japan).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Health_care_system_in_Japan\&action=edit)]



[Tokyo Metropolitan Matsuzawa Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Metropolitan_Matsuzawa_Hospital)



[Japanese Red Cross Medical Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Red_Cross_Medical_Center) in [Hiroo, Shibuya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiroo,_Shibuya)



[NTT Medical Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NTT_Medical_Center_Tokyo) in Tokyo

The [health care system in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_care_system_in_Japan) provides different types of services, including screening examinations, [prenatal care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prenatal_care) and [infectious disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infectious_disease) control, with the patient accepting responsibility for 30% of these costs while the government pays the remaining 70%. Payment for personal medical services is offered by a [universal health care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_health_care) insurance system that provides relative equality of access, with fees set by a government committee. All residents of [Japan](15573.md) are required by the law to have health insurance coverage. People without employer insurance; insurance provided by the employer, where premiums are split between the employer and employee, can participate in a national [health insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_insurance) program, administered by local governments. Patients are free to select physicians or facilities of their choice and cannot be denied coverage.

Medical fees are strictly regulated by the government to keep them affordable. Depending on the family's income and the age of the insured, patients are responsible for paying 10%, 20%, or 30% of medical fees, with the government paying the remaining fee.[^cite_note-183] Also, monthly thresholds are set for each household, again depending on income and age, and medical fees exceeding the threshold are waived or reimbursed by the government.

Uninsured patients are responsible for paying 100% of their medical fees, but fees are waived for low-income households receiving a government subsidy.

Japan has one of the world's most modern communication networks, with strong mobile and internet growth. In 2008, 75 million Japanese consumers (82% of all internet users) used mobile phones to access the Internet. #### Tourism

Main article: [Tourism in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_Japan)



Himeji Castle, in [Himeji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himeji,_Hy%C5%8Dgo), [Hyōgo Prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hy%C5%8Dgo_Prefecture), is one of the most visited sights in Japan.

In 2012, Japan was the fifth most visited country in Asia and the Pacific, with over 8.3 million tourists.[^cite_note-Tourism_Highlights_2013_Edition-184] In 2013, due to the weaker yen and easier visa requirements for southwest Asian countries, Japan received a record 11.25 million visitors, which was higher than the government's projected goal of 10 million visitors.[^cite_note-Japan_marks_new_high_in_tourism-185][^cite_note-Attracting_more_tourists_to_Japan-186][^cite_note-Record_2013_tourism_in_Japan_despite_islands_spat,_nuclear_fallout-187] The government hopes to attract 40 million visitors a year by the [2020 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Summer_Olympics) in Tokyo.[^cite_note-Attracting_more_tourists_to_Japan-186] Some of the most popular visited places include the [Shinjuku](147734.md), [Ginza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginza), [Shibuya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibuya) and [Asakusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asakusa,_Tokyo) areas in [Tokyo](30057.md), and the cities of [Osaka](52230.md), [Kobe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kobe) and [Kyoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto), as well as [Himeji Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himeji_Castle).[^cite_note-Japan's_10_Most_Popular_Tourist_Attractions-188] [Hokkaido](58092.md) is also a popular winter destination for visitors with several ski resorts and luxury hotels being built there.[^cite_note-Attracting_more_tourists_to_Japan-186][^cite_note-Boom_time_for_Hokkaido_ski_resort_area-189]

Japan's economy is less dependent on international tourism than those of other G7 countries and OECD countries in general; from 1995 to 2014, it was by far the least visited country in the [G7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_Seven) despite being the second largest country in the group,[^cite_note-190] and as of 2013 was one of the least visited countries in the [OECD](33853117.md) on a per capita basis.[^cite_note-191] In 2013, international tourist receipts was 0.3% of Japan's GDP, while the corresponding figure was 1.3% for the United States and 2.3% for France.[^cite_note-192][^cite_note-193]

#### Energy

Main article: [Energy in Japan](2658458.md)

See also: [Renewable energy in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_energy_in_Japan)

In 2005, one half of Japan's energy was produced from petroleum, a fifth from coal, and 14% from natural gas.[^cite_note-196] [Nuclear power in Japan](4131940.md) made a quarter of electricity production but due to the [Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_Daiichi_nuclear_disaster) there has been a large desire to end Japan's nuclear power program.[^cite_note-197][^cite_note-198] In September 2013, Japan closed its last 50 nuclear power plants nationwide, causing the nation to be nuclear free.[^cite_note-Japan_goes_nuclear-free_indefinitely-199] The country has since then opted to restart a few of its nuclear reactors.[^cite_note-200]

About 84% of Japan's energy is imported from other countries.[^cite_note-Nuclear_Power_in_Japan-201][^cite_note-Japan's_Energy_Supply_Situation_and_Basic_Policy-202] Japan is the world's largest liquefied natural gas importer, second largest coal importer, and third largest net oil importer.[^cite_note-203] Given its heavy dependence on imported energy, Japan has aimed to diversify its sources.[^cite_note-Fukushima_Watch:_Some_Power_Companies_in_Black_without_Nuclear_Restarts-204] Since the oil shocks of the 1970s, Japan has reduced dependence on petroleum as a source of energy from 77.4% in 1973 to about 43.7% in 2010 and increased dependence on natural gas and nuclear power.[^cite_note-205] In September 2019, Japan will invest 10 billion on liquefied natural gas projects worldwide, in a strategy to boost the global LNG market and reinforce the security of energy supply.[^cite_note-206]

Other important energy source includes coal, and [hydroelectricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroelectricity_in_Japan) is Japan's biggest renewable energy source.[^cite_note-207][^cite_note-208] Japan's solar market is also currently booming.[^cite_note-Kyocera_Boosts_Solar_Sales_Goal_on_Higher_Demand_in_Japan-209] Kerosene is also used extensively for home heating in portable heaters, especially farther north.[^cite_note-Japan_kerosene_heater_sales_surge_on_power_worries-210] Many taxi companies run their fleets on liquefied natural gas.[^cite_note-211] A recent success towards greater [fuel economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_economy_in_automobiles) was the introduction of mass-produced [hybrid vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_vehicle).[^cite_note-Prius_No._1_in_Japan_sales_as_green_interest_grows-212] Prime Minister [Shinzō Abe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinz%C5%8D_Abe), who was working on Japan's economic revival, signed a treaty with [Saudi Arabia](349303.md) and [UAE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UAE) about the rising prices of oil, ensuring Japan's stable deliveries from that region.[^cite_note-Japan,_Saudi_Arabia_agree_on_security,_energy_cooperation-213][^cite_note-Abe_clinches_nuclear_technology_deal_with_Abu_Dhabi-214]

##### Electrical

Main article: [Electricity sector in Japan](31014585.md)

This section is an excerpt from [Electricity sector in Japan](31014585.md).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Electricity_sector_in_Japan\&action=edit)]

| --- | --- |



Electricity production in Japan by source.

The [electric power industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power_industry) in Japan covers the generation, transmission, distribution, and sale of [electric energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_energy) in [Japan](15573.md). Japan's electricity demand was approximately 805.9 terawatt-hours (TWh) in 2024.[^cite_note-215] Before the 2011 [Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_Daiichi_nuclear_disaster), about a quarter of electricity in the country was generated by nuclear power. In the following years, most nuclear power plants have been on hold, being replaced mostly by coal and natural gas. Solar power is a growing source of electricity, and Japan has the third largest solar installed capacity with about 50 GW as of 2017. Japan's electricity production is characterized by a diverse energy mix, including nuclear, fossil fuels, renewable energy, and hydroelectric power. Japan has the second largest [pumped-hydro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pumped-hydro) storage installed capacity in the world after China.

The electrical grid in Japan is isolated, with no international connections, and consists of four [wide area synchronous grids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wide_area_synchronous_grid). Unusually, the Eastern and Western grids run at different frequencies (50 and 60 Hz respectively) and are connected by [HVDC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HVDC) connections. This considerably limits the amount of electricity that can be transmitted between the north and south of the country.

#### Transport

Main article: [Transport in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_in_Japan)

See also: [Road transport in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road_transport_in_Japan), [Rail transport in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Japan), [List of airports in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_airports_in_Japan), and [Category\:Ports and harbors of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Ports_and_harbors_of_Japan)

Japan's spendings on roads has been considered large.[^cite_note-Japan's_Road_to_Deep_Deficit_Is_Paved_With_Public_Works-216] The 1.2 million kilometers of paved road are one of the major means of transportation.[^cite_note-217] Japan has [left-hand traffic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-_and_left-hand_traffic).[^cite_note-218] A single network of speed, divided, limited-access toll roads connects major cities and are operated by [toll-collecting enterprises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road_pricing).[^cite_note-219] New and used cars are inexpensive, and the Japanese government has encouraged people to buy [hybrid vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_vehicle).[^cite_note-Prius_No._1_in_Japan_sales_as_green_interest_grows-212] Car ownership fees and fuel levies are used to promote energy-efficiency.[^cite_note-Prius_No._1_in_Japan_sales_as_green_interest_grows-212]

[Rail transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Japan) is a major means of transport in Japan. [Dozens of Japanese railway companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_railway_companies_in_Japan) compete in regional and local passenger transportation markets; for instance, 6 passenger [JR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Railways_Group) enterprises, [Kintetsu Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kintetsu_Railway), [Seibu Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seibu_Railway), and [Keio Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keio_Corporation).[^cite_note-220] Often, strategies of these enterprises contain [real estate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_estate) or [department stores next to stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_stores_in_Japan), and many major stations have major department stores near them.[^cite_note-Osaka_dept_stores_locked_in_scrap_for_survival-221] The Japanese cities of [Fukuoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukuoka_Subway), [Kobe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kobe_Municipal_Subway), [Kyoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto_Municipal_Transportation_Bureau), [Nagoya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagoya_Subway), [Osaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osaka_Municipal_Subway), [Sapporo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sapporo_Subway), [Sendai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sendai_Subway), [Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Subway) and [Yokohama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yokohama_Subway) all have [subway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapid_transit) systems. Some 250 high-speed [Shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinkansen) trains connect major cities.[^cite_note-222] All trains are known for punctuality, and a delay of 90 seconds can be considered late for some train services.[^cite_note-223]

There are 98 passenger and 175 total [airports in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_airports_in_Japan), and flying is a popular way to travel.[^cite_note-224][^cite_note-225] The largest domestic airport, [Tokyo International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haneda_Airport), is [Asia's second busiest airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World's_busiest_airports_by_passenger_traffic).[^cite_note-226] The largest international gateways are [Narita International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narita_International_Airport) (Tokyo area), [Kansai International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansai_International_Airport) (Osaka/Kobe/Kyoto area), and [Chūbu Centrair International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C5%ABbu_Centrair_International_Airport) (Nagoya area).[^cite_note-Narita_airport_prepares_for_battle_with_Asian_hubs-227]

The largest ports in Japan include [Nagoya Port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagoya_Port), the [Port of Yokohama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Yokohama), the [Port of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Tokyo) and the [Port of Kobe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Kobe).[^cite_note-228]

#### Water supply and sanitation

Main article: [Water supply and sanitation in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Japan)

This section is an excerpt from [Water supply and sanitation in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Japan).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Japan\&action=edit)]

[Water supply and sanitation in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Japan) is characterized by numerous achievements and some challenges. The country has achieved universal access to water supply and sanitation, has one of the lowest levels of water distribution losses in the world, regularly exceeds its own strict standards for the quality of drinking water and treated waste water, uses an effective national system of performance [benchmarking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benchmarking) for water and sanitation utilities, makes extensive use of both advanced and appropriate technologies such as the *jōkasō* on-site sanitation system, and has pioneered the [payment for ecosystem services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payment_for_ecosystem_services) before the term was even coined internationally. Some of the challenges are a decreasing population, declining investment, fiscal constraints, ageing facilities, an ageing workforce, a fragmentation of service provision among thousands of municipal utilities, and the vulnerability of parts of the country to droughts that are expected to become more frequent due to climate change.

## Labour force

Main article: [Labour market of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_market_of_Japan)

See also: [Labour unions in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_unions_in_Japan) and [Japanese labour law](6712884.md)



Unemployment rate of Japan.

[^cite_note-OECDemp-235]

&#x20;Red line is G7 average.\
&#x20;15-24 age (thin line) is [youth unemployment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Youth_unemployment).&#x20;

The unemployment rate in December 2013 was 3.7%, down 1.5 percentage points from the claimed unemployment rate of 5.2% in June 2009 due to the strong economic recovery.[^cite_note-236][^cite_note-Japan's_Jobless_Rate_Five-Year_High-237][^cite_note-Japan's_Unemployment_Rate_Drops_to_Six-Year_Low-238] As of 2019 Japan's unemployment rate was the lowest in the G7.[^cite_note-239] Its employment rate for the working-age population (15–64) was the highest in the G7.[^cite_note-240]

In 2008 Japan's labour force consisted of some 66 million workers—40% of whom were women—and was rapidly shrinking.[^cite_note-241] One major long-term concern for the Japanese labour force is its low birthrate.[^cite_note-Japan's_Biggest_Challenge-242] In 2005, the number of deaths in Japan exceeded the number of births, indicating that the decline in population had already started.[^cite_note-243] While one countermeasure for a declining birthrate would be to increase [immigration](42161679.md), Japan has struggled to attract potential migrants despite immigration laws being relatively lenient (especially for high-skilled workers) compared to other developed countries.[^cite_note-244] This is also apparent when looking at Japan's work visa programme for "specified skilled worker", which had less than 3,000 applicants, despite an annual goal of attracting 40,000 overseas workers, suggesting Japan faces major challenges in attracting migrants compared to other developed countries regardless of its immigration policies.[^cite_note-245] A Gallup poll found that few potential migrants wished to migrate to Japan compared to other G7 countries, consistent with the country's low migrant inflow.[^cite_note-246][^cite_note-247]

In 1989, the predominantly public sector union confederation, SOHYO (General Council of Trade Unions of Japan), merged with RENGO (Japanese Private Sector Trade Union Confederation) to form the [Japanese Trade Union Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RENGO). Labour union membership is about 12 million.

## Law and government

See also: [Monetary and fiscal policy of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetary_and_fiscal_policy_of_Japan), [Taxation in Japan](186403.md), [National debt of Japan](36392189.md), [Government-business relations in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government-business_relations_in_Japan), and [Japanese public corporations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_public_corporations)

Japan ranks 27th of 185 countries in the [ease of doing business index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ease_of_doing_business_index) 2013.[^cite_note-Economy_Rankings-248]

[Japan](186403.md) has [one of the smallest tax rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_tax_revenue_as_percentage_of_GDP) in the developed world.[^cite_note-oecd2008-249] After deductions, the majority of workers are free from [personal income taxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_income_tax). [Consumption tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumption_tax) rate is 10%, while [corporate tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_tax) rates are high, second highest corporate tax rate in the world, at 36.8%.[^cite_note-oecd2008-249][^cite_note-US_displacing_Japan_as_No_1_for_highest_corp_taxes-250][^cite_note-U.S._corporate_tax_rate:_No._1_in_the_world-251] However, the [House of Representatives](217778.md) has passed a bill which increased the consumption tax to 10% in October 2015.[^cite_note-Lower_House_passes_bills_to_double_consumption_tax-252] The government has also decided to reduce corporate tax and to phase out automobile tax.[^cite_note-Big_firms_to_see_tax_cut_on_social_expenses-253][^cite_note-Japan_to_Phase_Out_Automobile_Tax-254]

In 2016, the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md) encouraged Japan to adopt an income policy that pushes firms to raise employee wages in combination with reforms to tackle the labor market dual tiered employment system to drive higher wages, on top of monetary and fiscal stimulus. Shinzo Abe has encouraged firms to raise wages by at least three per cent annually (the inflation target plus average productivity growth).[^cite_note-Overhyped,_underappreciated-255][^cite_note-256][^cite_note-257]

[Shareholder activism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shareholder_activism) is rare despite the fact that the corporate law gives shareholders strong powers over managers.[^cite_note-Shareholder_Activism_in_Japan?_Not_Yet-258] Under Prime Minister [Shinzō Abe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinz%C5%8D_Abe), corporate governance reform has been a key initiative to encourage economic growth. In 2012, only around 40% of leading Japanese companies had any independent directors; by 2016, the majority had begun to appoint independent directors.[^cite_note-Overhyped,_underappreciated-255][^cite_note-259]

The government's liabilities include [the second largest public debt of any nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_public_debt) with debt of over one quadrillion yen, or 8,535,340,000,000 in USD.[^cite_note-260][^cite_note-Japan's_Debt_About_3_Times_Larger_Than_ASEAN's_GDP-261][^cite_note-Japan_Gets_to_Know_a_Quadrillion_as_Debt_Hits_New_High-262] Former Prime Minister [Naoto Kan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naoto_Kan) has called the situation 'urgent'.[^cite_note-263]

Japan's central bank has [the second largest foreign-exchange reserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_foreign_exchange_reserves) after China, with over one trillion US Dollars in foreign reserves.[^cite_note-Japan's_forex_reserves_up-264]

### Welfare

See also: [Welfare in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_in_Japan), [National Pension (Japan)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Pension_\(Japan\)), [Minimum wage in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_wage_in_Japan), [Universal basic income in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_basic_income_in_Japan), and [Unemployment insurance in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unemployment_insurance_in_Japan)

This section is an excerpt from [Welfare in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_in_Japan).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Welfare_in_Japan\&action=edit)]

[Social welfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_in_Japan), assistance for the ill or otherwise disabled and the old, has long been provided in [Japan](15573.md) by both the government and private companies. Beginning in the 1920s, the Japanese government enacted a series of welfare programs, based mainly on European models, to provide medical care and financial support. During the post-war period, a comprehensive system of social security was gradually established.[^cite_note-265][^cite_note-266] Universal health insurance and a pension system were established in 1960.[^cite_note-267]

The futures of [health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health) and [welfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_welfare) systems in Japan are being shaped by the [rapid aging of the population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elderly_people_in_Japan). The mixture of public and private funding has created complex pension and insurance systems, meshing with Japanese traditional calls for support within the family and by the local community for welfare recipients.

### Overview

*[Nemawashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nemawashi)* (根回し), 'consensus building', in Japanese culture is an informal process of quietly betting the foundation for some proposed change or project, by talking to the people concerned, gathering support and feedback, and so forth. It is considered an important element in any major change, before any formal steps are taken, and successful *nemawashi* enables changes to be carried out with the consent of all sides.

Japanese companies are known for management methods such as "[The Toyota Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Toyota_Way)". *[Kaizen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaizen)* ([改善](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%94%B9%E5%96%84), Japanese for 'improvement') is a Japanese philosophy that focuses on continuous improvement throughout all aspects of life. When applied to the workplace, *kaizen* activities continually improve all functions of a business, from manufacturing to management and from the CEO to the assembly line workers.[^cite_note-269] By improving standardised activities and processes, Kaizen aims to eliminate waste (see [Lean manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lean_manufacturing)). *Kaizen* was first implemented in several Japanese businesses during the country's recovery after World War II, including Toyota, and has since spread to businesses throughout the world.[^cite_note-270] The corporate application of the *kaizen* system has been criticised for neglecting or harming the quality of life of workers, particularly via the implementation of long working hours. However, according to the OECD, Japan's average for annual hours worked per employee is lower than the OECD average and middling among G7 countries.[^cite_note-271]

Some companies have powerful enterprise unions and *[shuntō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shunt%C5%8D)*. The [*nenko* system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nenko_system) or *nenko joretsu* as it is called in Japan, is the Japanese system of preferring to promote employees close to [retirement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retirement). This is done to prioritise experience in executive positions and to allow older employees to achieve a higher salary level before retirement. The *nenko* system has been criticised for leaving younger employees at a disadvantage against older employees who may be less capable.

Relationships between government bureaucrats and companies are often close. [Amakudari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amakudari) (天下り, *amakudari*; 'descent from heaven') is the institutionalised practice where Japanese senior bureaucrats retire to high-profile positions in the private and public sectors. The practice is increasingly viewed as corrupt and a limitation on efforts to reduce ties between the private sector and the state that prevent economic and political reforms. [Lifetime employment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lifetime_employment) (*shūshin koyō*) and seniority-based career advancement have been common in the [Japanese work environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_work_environment).[^cite_note-oecd2008-249][^cite_note-272] These practices have become less common in recent years.[^cite_note-273]

[Salaryman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salaryman) (サラリーマン, *_Sararīman_*; salaried man) refers to someone whose income is [salary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salary)-based; particularly those working for corporations. [Coined in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasei_eigo), the word's frequent use by Japanese corporations and its prevalence in Japanese [manga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manga) and [anime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anime) has led to its adoption into and common usage within the English language to refer to Japanese [white-collar workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-collar_workers), and it can be found in many English-language books and articles pertaining to Japanese culture. Immediately following World War II, becoming a salaryman was viewed as a gateway to a stable, middle-class lifestyle. In modern use, the term carries associations of long working hours, low prestige in the corporate hierarchy, absence of significant sources of income other than salary, [wage slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wage_slavery), and *[karōshi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kar%C5%8Dshi)*. The term *salaryman* refers almost exclusively to males.

An [office lady](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_lady), often abbreviated OL (Japanese: オーエル *Ōeru*), is a female [office worker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_worker) in Japan who performs generally [pink collar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pink_collar) tasks such as serving tea and doing [secretarial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary) or [clerical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clerk_\(position\)) work. Like many unmarried Japanese, OLs often live with their parents well into [early adulthood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_adulthood). Office ladies are usually [full-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full-time_job) permanent staff, with little opportunity for [promotion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promotion_\(rank\)), and there is often a tacit expectation that they leave their jobs if they get married.

[Freeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeter) (フリーター, *furītā*) is a Japanese expression for people between the age of 15 and 34 who lack full-time employment or are unemployed, excluding homemakers and students.[^cite_note-274] They may also be described as *[underemployed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underemployment)* or [freelance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freelance) workers. These people do not start a [career](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Career) after [high school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_education_in_Japan) or [university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_universities_in_Japan) but instead usually live as [parasite singles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasite_single) with their parents and earn some money with low skilled and low paid jobs. Low income makes it difficult for freeters to start a family, and lacking qualifications in the form of skilled labor experience makes it difficult for them to start a career at a later point in life.

[Karōshi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kar%C5%8Dshi) (過労死, *_karōshi_*), which can be translated quite literally from Japanese as "[death from overwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karoshi)", is occupational sudden death. The major medical causes of karōshi deaths are heart attack and stroke due to stress.[^cite_note-275]

[Sōkaiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C5%8Dkaiya) (総会屋, *sōkaiya*), (sometimes also translated as 'corporate bouncers', 'meeting-men', or 'corporate blackmailers') are a form of specialised [racketeer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racketeer) unique to Japan, and often associated with the [yakuza](44774.md), who extort money from or blackmail companies by threatening to publicly [humiliate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humiliation) companies and their management, usually in their annual meeting (総会, *sōkai*). [Sarakin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarakin) (サラ金) is a Japanese term for 'moneylender' or '[loan shark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loan_shark)'. It is a contraction of the Japanese words for '[salaryman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salaryman)' and 'cash'. Around 14 million people, or 10% of the Japanese population, have borrowed from a *sarakin*. In total, there are about 10,000 firms (down from 30,000 a decade ago); however, the top seven firms make up 70% of the market. The value of outstanding loans totals 100 billion. The biggest *sarakin* are publicly traded and often allied with [big banks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_banks_in_Japan).[^cite_note-economist-276]

The first "Western-style" [department store in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_stores_in_Japan) (called デパート, *depāto*) was [Mitsukoshi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsukoshi), founded in 1904, which has its root as a [kimono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimono) store called Echigoya from 1673. When the roots are considered, however, [Matsuzakaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matsuzakaya) has an even longer history, dating back to 1611 and becoming a department store in 1910. In 1924, Matsuzakaya store in [Ginza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginza) allowed street shoes to be worn indoors, something innovative at the time.[^cite_note-277] These former kimono shop department stores dominated the market in its earlier history. They sold luxurious products which contributed to them having sophisticated atmospheres. From the 1920s, [private railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_railway) operators started to build department stores directly linked to their lines' [terminuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_train_station). [Seibu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seibu_Department_Stores) and [Hankyu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hankyu_Department_Stores) are examples of this practice. From the 1980s onwards, Japanese department stores have faced fierce competition from supermarkets and convenience stores, and they have gradually declined in prominence. Still, *depāto* are seen as bastions of cultural conservatism in the country. Gift certificates for department stores are frequently given as presents in Japan. Department stores in Japan generally offer a wide range of services which can include [foreign exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_exchange_market), travel reservations, and ticket sales for local concerts and other events.

## *Keiretsu* and *Zaibatsu*

Main articles: [Keiretsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keiretsu) and [Zaibatsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaibatsu)

A *[keiretsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keiretsu)* (系列; 'system' or 'series') is a set of companies with interlocking business relationships and shareholdings. It is a type of [business group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_group). The prototypical *keiretsu* appeared in Japan during the "[economic miracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_post-war_economic_miracle)" following World War II. Before Japan's surrender, Japanese industry was controlled by large family-controlled [vertical monopolies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_monopoly) called *[zaibatsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaibatsu)*. The [Allies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II) dismantled the *zaibatsu* in the late 1940s, but the companies formed from the dismantling of the *zaibatsu* were reintegrated. The dispersed corporations were re-interlinked through share purchases to form horizontally integrated alliances across many industries. Where possible, *keiretsu* companies would also supply one another, making the alliances vertically integrated as well. In this period, official government policy promoted the creation of robust trade corporations that could withstand pressures from intensified world trade competition.[^cite_note-278]

The major *keiretsu* were each centered on one bank, which lent money to the *keiretsu*'s member companies and held equity positions in the companies. Each central bank had great control over the companies in the *keiretsu* and acted as a monitoring entity and as an emergency bail-out entity. One effect of this structure was to minimise the presence of [hostile takeovers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hostile_takeover) in Japan, because no entities could challenge the power of the banks.[^cite_note-279]

There are two types of *keiretsu*: vertical and horizontal. Vertical *keiretsu* illustrates the organisation and relationships within a company (for example all factors of production of a certain product are connected), while a horizontal *keiretsu* shows relationships between entities and industries, normally centered on a bank and trading company. Both are complexly woven together and sustain each other.[^cite_note-280]

The Japanese recession in the 1990s had profound effects on the *keiretsu*. Many of the largest banks were hit hard by bad loan portfolios and forced to merge or go out of business. This had the effect of blurring the lines between the *keiretsu*: [Sumitomo Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumitomo_Bank) and [Mitsui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsui) Bank, for instance, became [Sumitomo Mitsui Banking Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumitomo_Mitsui_Banking_Corporation) in 2001, while [Sanwa Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanwa_Bank) (the banker for the Hankyu-Toho Group) became part of Bank of Tokyo-Mitsubishi UFJ, now known as [MUFG Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MUFG_Bank). Additionally, many companies from outside the keiretsu system, such as [Sony](26989.md), began outperforming their counterparts within the system.

Generally, these causes gave rise to a strong notion in the business community that the old *keiretsu* system was not an effective business model, and led to an overall loosening of *keiretsu* alliances. While the *keiretsu* still exist, they are not as centralised or integrated as they were before the 1990s. This, in turn, has led to a growing [corporate acquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_acquisition) industry in Japan, as companies are no longer able to be easily "bailed out" by their banks, as well as rising [derivative litigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative_litigation) by more independent shareholders.
