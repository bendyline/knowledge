# The Reverend

"Reverend" and "Rev." redirect here. For other uses, see [Reverend (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverend_\(disambiguation\)) and [Rev. (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rev._\(disambiguation\)).



Profile of [John Wesley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wesley), a major religious leader of the 18th century. He is styled *The Rev<sup>d</sup>.*, an abbreviation of "The Reverend".

**The Reverend** (abbreviated as **The Revd**, **The Rev'd** or **The Rev**) is an [honorific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorific) [style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Style_\(form_of_address\)) given to certain (primarily [Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Christian)) [Christian clergy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_clergy) and [ministers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_minister). There are sometimes differences in the way the style is used in different countries and church traditions. *The Reverend* is correctly called a *style*, but is sometimes referred to as a [title](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title), form of address, or title of respect.[^cite_note-ldoceonline-1]&#x20;

## Etymology

The term is an [anglicisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglicisation) of the Latin *reverendus*, the style originally used in Latin documents in medieval Europe. It is the [gerundive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerundive) or future passive participle of the verb *revereri* ("to respect; to revere"), meaning "\[one who is] to be revered/must be respected". *The Reverend* is therefore equivalent to *[the Honourable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Honourable)* or *[the Venerable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Venerable)*.&#x20;

Originating as a general term of respectful address in the 15th century, it became particularly associated with clergy by the 17th century,[^cite_note-2] with variations associated with certain ranks in the church. It is paired with a modifier or noun for some offices in some traditions: [Lutheran](23371382.md) archbishops, [Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican) archbishops, and most [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic) bishops are usually styled *[The Most Reverend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Most_Reverend)* (*reverendissimus*); other Lutheran bishops, Anglican bishops, and Catholic bishops are styled *[The Right Reverend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Right_Reverend)*.[^cite_note-Lutheran-3][^cite_note-Crockford's-4][^cite_note-C-5]&#x20;

The forms *His Reverence* and *Her Reverence* are also sometimes used, along with their parallel in direct address, *Your Reverence*.[^cite_note-OED2017-6]&#x20;

## Usage

In traditional and formal English usage it is still considered incorrect to drop the definite article, *the*, before *Reverend*. In practice, however, the *the* is often not used in both written and spoken English. When the style is used within a sentence, *the* is correctly in lower-case.[^cite_note-7] Abbreviations for *Reverend* include *Rev.*, *Revd* (or *Rev<sup>d</sup>*), and *Rev'd*.&#x20;

*The Reverend* is traditionally used as an adjectival form with first names (or initials) and surname, e.g. "the Reverend John Smith" or "the Reverend J. F. Smith"; if the first names (or initials) are unknown, the correct form is "The Reverend Mr./Mrs./Miss/Ms. Smith".[^cite_note-Crockford's-4] Use of the prefix with the surname alone, e.g. "The Reverend Smith", is incorrect (a [solecism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solecism)) in formal usage.[^cite_note-thinkquest-8][^cite_note-Crockford's-4] The style normally precedes titles such as [Canon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_\(title\)) or [Doctor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_\(title\)).[^cite_note-Crockford's-4]&#x20;

In the 20th and 21st centuries, it has been increasingly common for the term to be used as a noun and for clergy to be referred to as being either *a reverend* or *the reverend*, or to be addressed as simply *reverend*. This has traditionally been considered incorrect on the basis that it is equivalent to referring to a judge as being *an honourable* or an adult man as *a mister*, both of which are also grammatically improper.[^cite_note-thinkquest-8][^cite_note-9] It is likewise incorrect to form the plural *reverends*. Some dictionaries,[^cite_note-10] however, do place the noun rather than the adjective as the word's principal form, owing to an increasing use of the word as a noun among people with no religious background or knowledge of traditional styles of ecclesiastical address. When several clergy are referred to, they are often styled individually (e.g. "The Reverend John Smith and the Reverend Henry Brown"); in a list of clergy, *the Revv* is sometimes put before the list of names, especially in the Catholic Church in Britain and Ireland.[^cite_note-11]&#x20;

In a unique case, *the Reverend* was used to refer to a church [consistory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consistory_\(Protestantism\)), a local administrative body. "The Reverend Coetus" and "the Reverend Assembly" were used to refer to the collective body of local officials during the transformation of the [Dutch Reformed Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Reformed_Church) in the mid-18th century.[^cite_note-Thompson1882-12]&#x20;

### Other religions

The use of the Christian term "Reverend" for the [rabbi](51273.md) of a congregation was common in [Reform Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reform_Judaism) in the 19th and early 20th centuries; however, the [Central Conference of American Rabbis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Conference_of_American_Rabbis) deprecated this usage in 1897.[^cite_note-13]&#x20;

The style is also sometimes used by leaders in other religions such as [Buddhism](3267529.md).[^cite_note-14]&#x20;
