[Germany](11867.md) is a major electricity producer and consumer. It has [the largest economy in the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_\(nominal\)). The country produced 488.5 [TWh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TWh) of electricity in 2024,[^cite_note-BDEW2024-1] with 59.4% from [renewable energy sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_energy_sources).[^cite_note-4] Germany's [electrical grid](20344155.md) is part of the [synchronous grid of continental Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchronous_grid_of_continental_Europe).

Germany is undergoing an [energy transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transition) (*[Energiewende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energiewende)*) towards renewable energy, in particular [solar](13690575.md) and [wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power), and away from nuclear and [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel). It completed its [nuclear phase-out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_phase-out) in 2023, and is in the process of phasing out [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) and [fossil gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_gas). The country plans to [phase out coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_phase-out) by 2038 or earlier.[^cite_note-coalshutdown-5] In 2023, 31.1% of electricity was produced from [wind power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power), 12.1% from [solar power](13690575.md), 8.4% from [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass) and the remaining 3.4% from hydropower and other renewables, for a total of 55% share of renewable energy sources in total electricity generation.[^cite_note-\:1-6] In 2024, an average of 363 grams of CO<sub>2</sub> was emitted per kilowatt hour of electricity consumed in Germany (compared to 433 g/kWh in 2022).[^cite_note-7] Germany's emissions in 2022 represent a 40% reduction compared to 1990, the year of the [German reunification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_reunification). Germany once again met its target under the European Union's Effort Sharing Regulation (ESR) in 2022.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9]

As part of its energy transition towards renewable energy, Germany's installed capacity for electric generation increased from 121 [gigawatts](21347693.md) (GW) in 2000 to 218 GW in 2019, an 80% increase, while electricity generation increased only 5% in the same period, due to the lower [capacity factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacity_factor) of renewable energy sources.[^cite_note-10]

## International electricity trade

In 2021, Germany exported 57,000 GWh of electricity and imported 39,600 GWh.[^cite_note-11] By 2024, Germany's electricity exports were recorded at 57,400 GWh. Germany remains the second largest exporter of electricity after [France](5843419.md), representing about 10% of electricity exports worldwide.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13] Germany has [grid interconnections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchronous_grid_of_Continental_Europe) with neighboring countries representing 10% of domestic capacity.[^cite_note-14]<sup>: 5 </sup>

## Mode of production



### Coal

See also: [Fossil-fuel phase-out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil-fuel_phase-out) and [List of power stations in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_power_stations_in_Germany)

In 2008, power from coal supplied 291 TWh or 46% of Germany's overall production of 631 TWh, but this dropped to 118 TWh (24%) in 2020.[^cite_note-\:1:-20] In 2010 Germany was still one of the world's largest consumers of [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) at 4th place behind [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_power_in_China) (2,733 TWh), [USA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_power_in_the_United_States) (2,133 TWh) and [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_mining_in_India) (569 TWh).[^cite_note-IEA2010-21] By 2019 it had fallen to 8th, behind smaller countries such as [South Korea](27019.md) and [South Africa](17416221.md).[^cite_note-22]

Germany has shut its last hardcoal mine in December 2018.[^cite_note-23] Germany still has three large open pit mines for lignite: [Garzweiler surface mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garzweiler_surface_mine) near [Köln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ln), the [Lausitzer Braunkohlerevier](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lausitzer%20Braunkohlerevier) and the [Oberlausitzer Bergbaurevier](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oberlausitzer%20Bergbaurevier) both near the [Polish border](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oder%E2%80%93Neisse_line).

In January 2019 the German [Commission on Growth, Structural Change and Employment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commission_on_Growth,_Structural_Change_and_Employment) initiated Germany's plans to entirely phase out and shut down the 84 remaining [coal-fired plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal-fired_plant) on its territory by 2038.[^cite_note-coalshutdown-5] This is called *Kohleausstieg* ([Coal phase-out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_phase-out)).[^cite_note-24]

### Nuclear power

Main article: [Nuclear power in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_Germany)

In 1969, [Siemens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens) and [AEG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AEG_\(German_company\)) founded [Kraftwerk Union AG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kraftwerk_Union) (KWU). In 1977, Siemens got 100% of the shares. KWU was the only significant nuclear constructor in Germany. In 2000, the nuclear share was 3% of Siemens' business.[^cite_note-25] In 2001, Siemens Nuclear Power (SNP), the nuclear division of Siemens, merged with [Framatome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framatome) to form the nuclear technology company [Framatome ANP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_France's_civil_nuclear_program).[^cite_note-26] In March 2011, after the Fukushima accident had begun, Siemens sold its stake in Areva NP to [Areva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Areva).[^cite_note-27]

The installed [nuclear power](22153.md) capacity in Germany was 20 GW in 2008 and 21 GW in 2004. The production of nuclear power was 148 TWh in 2008 (sixth top by 5.4% of world total) and 167 TWh in 2004 (fourth top by 6.1% of world total).[^cite_note-IEA2010-21][^cite_note-IEA2006-28]

In 2009, nuclear power production saw a 19% reduction compared to 2004, and its share had declined smoothly over time from 27% to 23%. The share of renewable and electricity increased as well as fossil fuels such as natural gas and lignite burning, substituting for nuclear power.[^cite_note-SverigeE-15]

In 2011, following the [Fukushima nuclear accident](31162817.md), the [second Merkel cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Merkel_cabinet) established a firm phase-out policy for nuclear power (*[Atomausstieg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomausstieg?action=edit\&redlink=1)*). That same year, eight [nuclear reactors](22151.md) were permanently shut down.[^cite_note-29] These included Unterweser, Krümmel, Biblis A and B, Philippsburg 1, Isar 1, Neckarwestheim 1, and Brunsbüttel.

While the remaining nine reactors were scheduled for decommissioning by the end of 2022, the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine) and the subsequent energy crisis led to a brief policy adjustment. To mitigate potential energy shortages caused by the loss of Russian gas supplies, the final three units—Isar 2, Emsland, and Neckarwestheim 2—remained operational until 15 April 2023, when they were officially disconnected from the grid.[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31]

### Renewable electricity

Main article: [Renewable energy in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_energy_in_Germany)



Germany renewable electricity production by source, 1990-2023



German renewable energy production by source, 2000–2017



&#x20;Wind turbines in [Baltic Sea](3335.md) in 2013

[Germany](11867.md) has been described as "the world's first major [renewable energy](25784.md) economy."[^cite_note-32] The sector is primarily based on wind, solar, and biomass. Germany held the world's largest installed [photovoltaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photovoltaics) capacity until 2014, and by 2016, it ranked third globally with 40 GW. Similarly, wind power capacity grew to 50 GW by 2016, including over 4 GW from offshore installations.[^cite_note-33] Expansion accelerated significantly in the following decade; by the beginning of 2026, installed solar capacity reached 100 GW, while total wind capacity climbed to 73 GW, driven by a record expansion in both onshore and offshore sectors.[^cite_note-34]

Former Chancellor [Angela Merkel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Merkel), along with a vast majority of her compatriots, maintained that "as the first big industrialized nation, we can achieve such a transformation toward efficient and renewable energies, with all the opportunities that brings for exports, developing new technologies and jobs."[^cite_note-35] Following this vision, the share of renewable electricity in gross electricity consumption rose from 3.4% in 1990 to exceed 10% by 2005, 30% by 2015, and 50% by 2023. By the end of 2024, renewable sources accounted for approximately 56% of Germany's electricity generation.[^cite_note-BMW-erneuerbare-energien-36][^cite_note-37] However, the transition in the transport and heating sectors remained considerably slower than in the power sector.

By the end of 2015, there were more than 23,000 [wind turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_turbines) and 1.4 million [solar PV systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_PV_systems) distributed across Germany.[^cite_note-38] Following a decade of accelerated expansion, these numbers grew significantly; by early 2026, the country hosted approximately 30,000 onshore wind turbines and over 4 million solar PV systems, the latter driven by a surge in residential installations and commercial solar farms.[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40] Employment in the sector has followed a similar upward trajectory. From 160,500 jobs in 2004 and around 370,000 in 2010, the renewable energy workforce expanded to over 450,000 people by 2025, particularly within small and medium-sized enterprises.[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42] Historically, about two-thirds of these jobs were attributed to the [Renewable Energy Sources Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Renewable_Energy_Act).[^cite_note-43]

Germany's federal government is working to increase [renewable energy commercialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_energy_commercialization),[^cite_note-BMU2011_2-44] with a particular focus on [offshore wind farms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offshore_wind_farm).[^cite_note-Spiegel-45] A major challenge is the development of sufficient network capacities for transmitting the power generated in the North Sea to the large industrial consumers in southern parts of the country.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-46] Germany's energy transition, the *[Energiewende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energiewende)*, designates a significant change in [energy policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_policy) from 2011. The term encompasses a reorientation of policy from demand to supply and a shift from centralized to distributed generation (for example, producing heat and power in very small cogeneration units), which should replace [overproduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overproduction) and avoidable energy consumption with energy-saving measures and increased efficiency. At the end of 2020, Germany had 2.3 GWh of home battery storage, often in conjunction with solar panels,[^cite_note-47] and home storage increased to 15.4 GWh at the end of 2024 while large-scale storage was 2.3 GWh.[^cite_note-48] 72 GW of Germany's 100 GW of solar power, and 47 GW (2/3) of wind power, received a fixed feed-in tariff, and had thus little incentive to self-[curtail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtailment_\(electricity\)) during surplus power when grid price is zero or below.[^cite_note-ffe2024-49]

[Large-scale batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battery_energy_storage_system) can take 2 years to build, while a 30 MW battery can take a year.[^cite_note-50] In 2024, Germany had more than 400 hours of [negative electricity prices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_pricing#Electricity), supplying low cost input for storage.[^cite_note-ffe2024-49][^cite_note-51]

## Electricity prices

German electricity prices in 2020 were 31.47 euro cents per kW⋅h for residential customers (an increase of 126% since 2000),[^cite_note-\:0-52] and 17.8 euro cents per kW⋅h for non-residential customers (21.8 with taxes).[^cite_note-eurostat-53][^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55] During and after the [Global energy crisis (2021–2023)](68934947.md), gas prices and electricity prices increased significantly.[^cite_note-ffe2024-49]

There are some special features in the transmission network of Germany. Also they have no direct influence on operation, they are remarkable under technical viewpoints.

#### Garland-type communication cables

Many powerlines in Baden-Württemberg, which were built by Energie-Versorgung-Schwaben (EVS, now part of EnBW) are equipped with a communication cable, which hangs like a garland on the ground conductor. Some of these lines have also a second communication cable hanging on an auxiliary wire, which is usually fixed on the pinnacle of the pylon below the ground conductor. Such devices are usually installed on lines with voltages of 110 kV and more, but there existed also a 20 kV-line near Eberdingen, which had a communication cable fixed like a garland on a conductor rope. Although communication cables fixed like a garland where replaced in the last decades by free-hanging communication cables many of these devices are still in use. If a downlink of the communication cable from the suspension level to the ground is necessary, which is for example the case at amplifier stations, lines built by former Energie-Versorgung-Schwaben (EVS) use therefore a cable in the centre of the tower strung by a pond instead of a cable fixed at the tower structure. This construction type can be found as well on lines using garland-type communication cables as on lines using freely span communication cables.

#### Powerline crossings of Elbe river near Stade



Elbe Crossing 1 (center) and 2 (right, one mast only)

There are two powerline crossings of the Elbe river near Stade, whose pylons are among the tallest structures in Europe.

**Elbe Crossing 1** is a group of masts providing an overhead crossing of a 220 kV three-phase [alternating current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_current) electric powerline across the River [Elbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elbe).[^cite_note-58] Constructed between 1959 and 1962 as part of the line from [Stade](306032.md) to [Hamburg](13467.md) north, it consists of four masts. Each of the two portal masts is a guyed mast 50 metres (160 ft) in height with a crossbeam at a height of 33 metres (108 ft). One of these masts stands on the [Schleswig-Holstein](27126.md) bank of the Elbe and the other on the [Lower Saxony](18435.md) bank. Two identical carrying masts 189 metres (620 ft) in height, each weighing 330 tonnes (320 long tons; 360 short tons), ensure the necessary passage height of 75 metres (246 ft) over the Elbe. One stands on the island of [Lühesand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%BChesand), the other in the [Buhnenfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buhnenfeld?action=edit\&redlink=1) on the Schleswig-Holstein side.

Because of the swampy terrain, each mast's foundation is built on pilings driven into the ground. The Lühesand portal mast rests on 41 pilings and the one on the Buhnenfeld on 57. In contrast to the usual construction of such [lattice-steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_tower) [transmission towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_tower), the direction of the line passes diagonally over the square ground cross section of the pylon, resulting in savings in material. The two crossbeams for the admission of the six conductor cables are at a height of 166 metres (545 ft) and 179 metres (587 ft). The mast on the Buhnenfeld bears at a height of 30 metres (98 ft) a radar facility belonging to the Water and Navigation Office of the [Port of Hamburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamburg_Harbour). Each portal mast has stairs and gangways for maintenance of flight safety beacons, and has a hoist for heavy loads.

**Elbe Crossing 2** is a group of [transmission towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_tower) providing overhead lines for four 380 [kV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volt) [three-phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-phase_electric_power) [alternating current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_current) (AC) circuits across the [German](11867.md) river [Elbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elbe).[^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60] It was constructed between 1976 and 1978 to supplement [Elbe Crossing 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elbe_Crossing_1), and consists of four towers:

- A 76-metre-tall anchor pylon located in [Lower Saxony](18435.md), on the Elbe's southern banks.

- Two carrying pylons, each 227 metres (745 ft) tall. One is located on the island of [Lühesand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%BChesand) and the other is near [Hetlingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hetlingen) in [Schleswig-Holstein](27126.md), on the northern shore.

These pylons are the tallest pylons in Europe and the sixth tallest of the world. They stand on 95 piers because of the unfavorable building ground. The base of each pylon measures 45 by 45 metres (148 ft × 148 ft) and each pylon weighs 980 tonnes (960 long tons; 1,080 short tons). Crossbeams, which hold up the power cables, are located at heights of 172 (564), 190 (620) and 208 metres (682 ft). The crossbeams [span](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Span_\(architecture\)) 56 metres or 184 feet (lowest crossbeam), 72 metres or 236 feet (middle crossbeam) and 57 metres or 187 feet (highest crossbeam). Each pylon has a self-propelled climbing [elevator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevator) for maintenance of the [aircraft warning lights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_warning_lights); each elevator runs inside a steel tube in the centre of the mast, around which there is a spiral staircase.

- A 62-metre-tall (203 ft) anchor pylon on the Schleswig-Holstein side.

The enormous height of the two carrying pylons ensures that the passage height requirement of 75 metres (246 ft) over the Elbe demanded by German authorities is met. The height requirement ensures that large ships are able to enter [Hamburg](13467.md)'s [deep-water port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamburg_Harbour).

#### Electricity pylons



Donaumast pylon

Unlike in most other countries, there are only few three-level or delta pylons. Instead, two-level lattice pylons called [de\:Donaumast](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donaumast) are widely used. These carry two cables on the upper and four cables on the lower crossarm. Particularly in eastern Germany one-level pylons were used too. Power lines with less than 100 kV mostly run underground today. Unlike in the US and many other countries, roadside medium-voltage overhead lines do not exist.[^cite_note-61]

A 302 metres tall cooling tower of [Scholven Power Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholven_Power_Station) at Gelsenkirchen, which is used by four units of these thermal power station is equipped with three booms carrying the conductors of a 220 kV-circuit leaving one of these units.



Pylon with observation platform near Hürth (2005)

From 1977 to 2010 a [74.84 metres high strainer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%BCrth#Facility_4101,_Tower_93) of powerline Oberzier-Niedersechtem was equipped with a public observation deck in a height of 27 metres, which was accessible by a staircase. After too much vandalism occurred, which endangered also the integrity of the pylon, this observation deck was removed.



Radio relay tower on Goose Neck

As in many other countries, power companies in Germany use radio relay links for data transmissions. In most cases the antennas used therefore are installed on lattice towers, but at some sites concrete towers are used for this purpose. The 87 metres tall radio relay tower on [Goose Neck mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goose_Neck_Tower) (German: Gänsehals) near Bell is the only of these towers equipped with a public observation deck. It is situated in a height of 24 metres and accessible by a staircase.
