**Uranium** is a [chemical element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element); it has [symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_symbol) **U** and [atomic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_number) 92. It is a silvery-grey [metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal) in the [actinide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actinide) series of the [periodic table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table). A uranium atom has 92 [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton) and 92 [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron), of which 6 are [valence electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_electron). Uranium [radioactively decays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_decay), usually by emitting an [alpha particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_particle). The [half-life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-life) of this decay varies between 159,200 and 4.5 billion years for different [isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopes_of_uranium), making them useful for dating the [age of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_the_Earth). The most common isotopes in [natural uranium](2836053.md) are [uranium-238](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-238) (which has 146 [neutrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron) and accounts for over 99% of uranium on Earth) and [uranium-235](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-235) (which has 143 neutrons). Uranium has the highest [atomic weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_weight) of the [primordially](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primordial_nuclide) occurring elements. Its [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) is about 70% higher than that of [lead](17747.md) and slightly lower than that of [gold](12240.md) or [tungsten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten). It occurs naturally in low concentrations of a few [parts per million](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts-per_notation#Parts-per_expressions) in soil, rock and water, and is commercially [extracted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_mining) from uranium-bearing [minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral) such as [uraninite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uraninite).[^cite_note-10]

Many contemporary uses of uranium exploit its unique [nuclear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus) properties. Uranium is used in [nuclear power plants](207347.md) and [nuclear weapons](21785.md) because it is the only naturally occurring element with a [fissile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fissile) isotope – uranium-235 – present in non-trace amounts. However, because of the low abundance of uranium-235 in natural uranium (which is overwhelmingly uranium-238), uranium needs to undergo [enrichment](37555.md) so that enough uranium-235 is present. Uranium-238 is fissionable by fast neutrons and is [fertile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertile_material), meaning it can be [transmuted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_transmutation) to fissile [plutonium-239](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium-239) in a [nuclear reactor](22151.md). Another fissile isotope, [uranium-233](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-233), can be produced from natural [thorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorium) and is studied for future industrial use in nuclear technology. Uranium-238 has a small probability for [spontaneous fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_fission) or even induced fission with fast neutrons; uranium-235, and to a lesser degree uranium-233, have a much higher fission cross-section for slow neutrons. In sufficient concentration, these isotopes maintain a sustained [nuclear chain reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_chain_reaction). This generates the heat in nuclear power reactors and produces the fissile material for nuclear weapons. The primary civilian use for uranium harnesses the heat energy to produce electricity. [Depleted uranium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depleted_uranium) (<sup>238</sup>U) is used in [kinetic energy penetrators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy_penetrator) and [armor plating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle_armour).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001479-11]

The [1789 discovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_the_chemical_elements) of uranium in the mineral [pitchblende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitchblende) is credited to [Martin Heinrich Klaproth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Heinrich_Klaproth), who named the new element after the recently discovered planet [Uranus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranus). [Eugène-Melchior Péligot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne-Melchior_P%C3%A9ligot) was the first person to isolate the metal, and its radioactive properties were discovered in 1896 by [Henri Becquerel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Becquerel). Research by [Otto Hahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Hahn), [Lise Meitner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lise_Meitner), [Enrico Fermi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi) and others (including [J. Robert Oppenheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Robert_Oppenheimer)), beginning in 1934, led to its use as a fuel in the [nuclear power](22153.md) industry and in [Little Boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Boy), the [first nuclear weapon used in war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bombings_of_Hiroshima_and_Nagasaki). An ensuing [arms race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arms_race) during the [Cold War](325329.md) between the [United States](3434750.md) and the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) produced tens of thousands of nuclear weapons that used uranium metal and uranium-derived plutonium-239. Dismantling of these weapons and related nuclear facilities is carried out within various [nuclear disarmament](22165.md) programs and costs billions of dollars. Weapon-grade uranium obtained from nuclear weapons is diluted with uranium-238 and reused as fuel for nuclear reactors. [Spent nuclear fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spent_nuclear_fuel) forms [radioactive waste](37257.md), which mostly consists of uranium-238 and poses a significant threat to health [and to the environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_in_the_environment).

## Characteristics

A diagram showing a chain transformation of uranium-235 to uranium-236 to barium-141 and krypton-92

A neutron-induced nuclear fission event involving uranium-235

Uranium is a silvery white, weakly radioactive [metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal). It has a [Mohs hardness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardnesses_of_the_elements_\(data_page\)) of 6, sufficient to scratch glass and roughly equal to that of [titanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanium), [rhodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodium), [manganese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese) and [niobium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niobium). It is [malleable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malleable), [ductile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ductile), slightly [paramagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramagnetic), strongly [electropositive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electropositive) and a [poor electrical conductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_conductivity).[^cite_note-SciTechEncy-12][^cite_note-LANL-13] Uranium metal has a very high [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) of 19.1 g/cm<sup>3</sup>,[^cite_note-14] denser than [lead](17747.md) (11.3 g/cm<sup>3</sup>),[^cite_note-15] but slightly less dense than [tungsten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten) and [gold](12240.md) (19.3 g/cm<sup>3</sup>).[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17]

Uranium metal reacts with almost all non-metallic elements (except [noble gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_gas)) and their [compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound), its reactivity increasing with temperature.[^cite_note-ColumbiaEncy-18] [Hydrochloric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid) and [nitric acids](21655.md) dissolve uranium, but non-oxidizing acids other than hydrochloric acid attack the element very slowly.[^cite_note-SciTechEncy-12] When finely divided, it can react with cold water; in air, uranium metal becomes coated with a dark layer of [uranium dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_dioxide).[^cite_note-LANL-13] Uranium in ores is extracted chemically and converted into uranium dioxide or other chemical forms usable in industry.

In 1938, [Otto Hahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Hahn) and [Fritz Strassman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Strassman) discovered that [barium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barium) was a product of bombarding [uranium-235](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-235) with neutrons, and a year later [Lise Meitner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lise_Meitner) and [Otto Robert Frisch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Robert_Frisch) developed the theory of [nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission) to explain this new phenomenon, making U-235 the first [fissile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fissile) isotope to be discovered.[^cite_note-19] On bombardment with slow neutrons, uranium-235 most of the time splits into two smaller [nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus), releasing nuclear [binding energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binding_energy) and more neutrons. If too many of these neutrons are absorbed by other uranium-235 nuclei, a [nuclear chain reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_chain_reaction) occurs that results in a burst of heat or (in some circumstances) an explosion. In a nuclear reactor, such a chain reaction is slowed and controlled by a [neutron poison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_poison), absorbing some of the free neutrons. Such neutron-absorbent materials are often part of reactor [control rods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_rod) (see [nuclear reactor physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_reactor_physics) for a description of this process of reactor control). Other naturally occurring isotopes such as [uranium-238](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-238) are [fissionable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fissile_material#Fissile_vs_fissionable), but not fissile, meaning that they only undergo fission when absorbing high energy (fast) neutrons.[^cite_note-20]

As little as 15 lb (6.8 kg) of uranium-235 can be used to make an atomic bomb.[^cite_note-EncyIntel-21] The nuclear weapon detonated over [Hiroshima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiroshima), Japan, named [Little Boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Boy), relied on uranium fission. However, the first nuclear bomb (the *Gadget* used at [Trinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_\(nuclear_test\))) and the bomb that was detonated over Nagasaki, Japan ([Fat Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fat_Man)), were both plutonium bombs.

## Applications

### Military

Shiny metallic cylinder with a sharpened tip. The overall length is 9 cm and diameter about 2 cm.

Various militaries use depleted uranium as high-density penetrators.

The major application of uranium in the military sector is in high-density penetrating projectiles. This ammunition consists of [depleted uranium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depleted_uranium) (DU) alloyed with 1–2% other elements, such as [titanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanium) or [molybdenum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molybdenum).[^cite_note-24] At high impact speed, the density, hardness, and [pyrophoricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrophoricity) of the projectile enable the destruction of heavily armored targets. Tank armor and other removable [vehicle armor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle_armor) can also be hardened with depleted uranium plates. The use of depleted uranium became politically and environmentally contentious after the use of such munitions by the US, UK and other countries during wars in the Persian Gulf and the Balkans raised health questions concerning uranium compounds left in the soil (see [Gulf War syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War_syndrome)).[^cite_note-EncyIntel-21]

Depleted uranium is also used as a shielding material in some containers used to store and transport radioactive materials. While the metal itself is radioactive, its high density makes it more effective than [lead](17747.md) in halting radiation from strong sources such as [radium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radium).[^cite_note-SciTechEncy-12] Other uses of depleted uranium include counterweights for aircraft control surfaces, as ballast for missile [re-entry vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_reentry) and as a shielding material.[^cite_note-LANL-13] Due to its high density, this material is found in [inertial guidance systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_guidance_system) and in [gyroscopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyroscopic) [compasses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass).[^cite_note-LANL-13] Depleted uranium is preferred over similarly dense metals for its ability to be easily machined and cast, as well as its relatively low cost.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001480-25] The main risk of exposure to depleted uranium is chemical poisoning by [uranium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_oxide) rather than radioactivity (uranium being only a weak [alpha emitter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_decay)).

During the later stages of [World War II](32927.md), the entire [Cold War](325329.md), and to a lesser extent afterwards, uranium-235 has been used as the fissile explosive material to produce nuclear weapons. Initially, two major types of fission bombs were built: a relatively simple device that uses uranium-235 and a more complicated mechanism that uses [plutonium-239](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium-239) derived from uranium-238. Later, a much more complicated and far more powerful type of fission/fusion bomb ([thermonuclear weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermonuclear_weapon)) was built, that uses a plutonium-based device to cause a mixture of [tritium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tritium) and [deuterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deuterium) to undergo [nuclear fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fusion). Such bombs are jacketed in a non-fissile (unenriched) uranium case, and they derive more than half their power from the fission of this material by [fast neutrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_neutron) from the nuclear fusion process.[^cite_note-26]

### Civilian

The main use of uranium in the civilian sector is to fuel [nuclear power plants](207347.md). One kilogram of uranium-235 can theoretically produce about 20 [terajoules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terajoules) of energy (2×10<sup>13</sup> [joules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule)), assuming complete fission; as much [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) as 1.5 million kilograms (1,500 metric [tonnes](31185.md)) of [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001479-11]

Commercial nuclear power plants use fuel that is typically enriched to around 3% uranium-235.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001479-11] The [CANDU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CANDU_reactor) and [Magnox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnox) designs are the only commercial reactors capable of using unenriched uranium fuel. Fuel used for [United States Navy](20518076.md) reactors is typically highly enriched in [uranium-235](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-235) (the exact values are [classified](252857.md)). Before (and, occasionally, after) the discovery of radioactivity, uranium was primarily used in small amounts for yellow glass and pottery glazes, such as [uranium glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_glass) and in [Fiestaware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiesta_\(dinnerware\)).[^cite_note-27]

The discovery and isolation of [radium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radium) in uranium ore (pitchblende) by [Marie Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Curie) sparked the development of uranium mining to extract the radium, which was used to make glow-in-the-dark paints for clock and aircraft dials.[^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29] This left a prodigious quantity of uranium as a waste product, since it takes three tonnes of uranium to extract one gram of radium. This waste product was diverted to the glazing industry, making uranium glazes very inexpensive and abundant. Besides the pottery glazes, [uranium tile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_tile) glazes accounted for the bulk of the use, including common bathroom and kitchen tiles which can be produced in green, yellow, [mauve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauve), black, blue, red and other colors.

Uranium was also used in [photographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photography) chemicals (especially [uranium nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_nitrate) as a [toner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photographic_print_toning)),[^cite_note-LANL-13] in lamp filaments for [stage lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stage_lighting) bulbs,[^cite_note-epa-30] to improve the appearance of [dentures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dentures),[^cite_note-31] and in the leather and wood industries for stains and dyes. Uranium salts are [mordants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordant) of silk or wool. [Uranyl acetate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranyl_acetate) and [uranyl formate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranyl_formate) are used as electron-dense "stains" in [transmission electron microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_electron_microscopy), to increase the contrast of biological specimens in ultrathin sections and in [negative staining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_staining) of [viruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus), isolated [cell organelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_organelle) and [macromolecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macromolecule).

The discovery of the radioactivity of uranium ushered in additional scientific and practical uses of the element. The long [half-life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-life) of uranium-238 (4.47×10<sup>9</sup> years) makes it well-suited for use in estimating the age of the earliest [igneous rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igneous_rock) and for other types of [radiometric dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiometric_dating), including [uranium–thorium dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium%E2%80%93thorium_dating), [uranium–lead dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium%E2%80%93lead_dating) and [uranium–uranium dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium%E2%80%93uranium_dating). Uranium metal is used for [X-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray) targets in the making of high-energy X-rays.[^cite_note-LANL-13]

## History

### Pre-discovery use

The use of [pitchblende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitchblende), uranium in its natural [oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxide) form, dates back to at least the year 79 AD, when it was used in the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) to add a yellow color to [ceramic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceramic) glazes.[^cite_note-LANL-13] Yellow glass with 1% uranium oxide was found in a Roman villa on Cape [Posillipo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posillipo) in the [Gulf of Naples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Naples), Italy, by R. T. Gunther of the [University of Oxford](31797.md) in 1912.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001482-32] Starting in the late [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), pitchblende was extracted from the [Habsburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburg) silver mines in [Joachimsthal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%A1chymov), [Bohemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemia) (now Jáchymov in the Czech Republic) in the [Ore Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ore_Mountains), and was used as a coloring agent in the local [glassmaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass) industry.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001477-33] In the early 19th century, the world's only known sources of uranium ore were these mines.

### Discovery



The planet [Uranus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranus), which uranium is named after

The [discovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_the_chemical_elements) of the element is credited to the German chemist [Martin Heinrich Klaproth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Heinrich_Klaproth). While he was working in his experimental laboratory in [Berlin](3354.md) in 1789, Klaproth was able to precipitate a yellow compound (likely [sodium diuranate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_diuranate)) by dissolving [pitchblende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitchblende) in [nitric acid](21655.md) and neutralizing the solution with [sodium hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_hydroxide).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001477-33] Klaproth assumed the yellow substance was the oxide of a yet-undiscovered element and heated it with [charcoal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charcoal) to obtain a black powder, which he thought was the newly discovered metal itself (in fact, that powder was an [oxide of uranium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxide_of_uranium)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001477-33][^cite_note-34] He named the newly discovered element "Uranit" after the planet [Uranus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranus) (named after the primordial [Greek god of the sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranus_\(mythology\))), which had been discovered eight years earlier by [William Herschel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Herschel).[^cite_note-35] He later renamed it "Uranium" to conform to the naming standard.[^cite_note-36]

In 1841, [Eugène-Melchior Péligot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne-Melchior_P%C3%A9ligot), Professor of Analytical Chemistry at the [Conservatoire National des Arts et Métiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservatoire_National_des_Arts_et_M%C3%A9tiers) (Central School of Arts and Manufactures) in [Paris](22989.md), isolated the first sample of uranium metal by heating [uranium tetrachloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_tetrachloride) with [potassium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001477-33][^cite_note-37]

Two fuzzy black features on a fuzzy white paper-like background. There is a handwriting at the top of the picture.

[Henri Becquerel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Becquerel) discovered [radioactivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactivity) by exposing a [photographic plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photographic_plate) to uranium in 1896.

[Henri Becquerel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Becquerel) discovered radioactivity by using uranium in 1896.[^cite_note-ColumbiaEncy-18] Becquerel made the discovery in Paris by leaving a sample of a uranium salt, K<sub>2</sub>UO<sub>2</sub>(SO<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub> (potassium uranyl sulfate), on top of an unexposed [photographic plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photographic_plate) in a drawer and noting that the plate had become "fogged".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001478-38] He determined that a form of invisible light or rays emitted by uranium had exposed the plate.

During World War I when the [Central Powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Powers) suffered a shortage of molybdenum to make artillery gun barrels and high speed tool steels, they routinely used [ferrouranium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrouranium) alloy as a substitute, as it presents many of the same physical characteristics as molybdenum. When this practice became known in 1916 the US government requested several prominent universities to research the use of uranium in manufacturing and metalwork. Tools made with these formulas remained in use for several decades,[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40] until the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project) and the [Cold War](325329.md) placed a large demand on uranium for fission research and weapon development.

### Fission research



Cuboids of uranium produced during the Manhattan Project

A team led by [Enrico Fermi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi) in 1934 found that bombarding uranium with neutrons produces [beta rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_decay) ([electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) or [positrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron) from the elements produced; see [beta particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_particle)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968773-41] The fission products were at first mistaken for new elements with atomic numbers 93 and 94, which the Dean of the [Sapienza University of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sapienza_University_of_Rome), [Orso Mario Corbino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orso_Mario_Corbino), named [ausenium and hesperium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ausenium_and_hesperium), respectively.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45] The experiments leading to the discovery of uranium's ability to fission (break apart) into lighter elements and release [binding energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binding_energy) were conducted by [Otto Hahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Hahn) and [Fritz Strassmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Strassmann)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968773-41] in Hahn's laboratory in Berlin. [Lise Meitner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lise_Meitner) and her nephew, physicist [Otto Robert Frisch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Robert_Frisch), published the physical explanation in February 1939 and named the process "[nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission)".[^cite_note-46] Soon afterward, Fermi hypothesized that fission of uranium might release enough neutrons to sustain a fission reaction. Confirmation of this hypothesis came in 1939, and later work found that on average about 2.5 neutrons are released by each fission of uranium-235.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968773-41] Fermi urged [Alfred O. C. Nier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_O._C._Nier) to separate uranium isotopes for determination of the fissile component, and on 29 February 1940, Nier used an instrument he built at the [University of Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Minnesota) to separate the world's first [uranium-235](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-235) sample in the Tate Laboratory. Using [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pupin_Hall)'s [cyclotron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclotron), [John Dunning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_R._Dunning) confirmed the sample to be the isolated fissile material on 1 March.[^cite_note-47] Further work found that the far more common uranium-238 isotope can be [transmuted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_transmutation) into plutonium, which, like uranium-235, is also fissile by thermal neutrons. These discoveries led numerous countries to begin working on the development of nuclear weapons and [nuclear power](22153.md). Despite fission having been discovered in Germany, the *[Uranverein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranverein)* ("uranium club") Germany's wartime project to research nuclear power and/or weapons was hampered by limited resources, infighting, the exile or non-involvement of several prominent scientists in the field and several crucial mistakes such as failing to account for impurities in available graphite samples which made it appear less suitable as a [neutron moderator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_moderator) than it is in reality. Germany's attempts to build a [natural uranium](2836053.md) / [heavy water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_water) reactor had not come close to reaching criticality by the time the Americans reached [Haigerloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haigerloch), the site of the last German wartime reactor experiment.[^cite_note-48]

On 2 December 1942, as part of the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project), another team led by Enrico Fermi was able to initiate the first artificial self-sustained [nuclear chain reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_chain_reaction), [Chicago Pile-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Pile-1). An initial plan using enriched uranium-235 was abandoned as it was as yet unavailable in sufficient quantities.[^cite_note-49] Working in a lab below the stands of [Stagg Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagg_Field) at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago), the team created the conditions needed for such a reaction by piling together 360 tonnes of [graphite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphite), 53 tonnes of [uranium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_oxide), and 5.5 tonnes of uranium metal, most of which was supplied by [Westinghouse Lamp Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westinghouse_Lamp_Plant) in a makeshift production process.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968773-41][^cite_note-50]

### Nuclear weaponry

White fragmentred mushroom-like smoke cloud evolving from the ground.

[Mushroom cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mushroom_cloud) over Hiroshima after the dropping of the uranium-fired '[Little Boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Boy)'

Two types of atomic bomb were developed by the United States during [World War II](32927.md): a uranium-based device (codenamed "Little Boy") whose fissile material was highly [enriched uranium](37555.md), and a plutonium-based device (see [Trinity test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_test) and "Fat Man") whose plutonium was derived from uranium-238. Little Boy became the first nuclear weapon used in war when it was detonated over [Hiroshima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiroshima), [Japan](15573.md), on 6 August 1945. Exploding with a yield equivalent to 12,500 tonnes of [TNT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TNT), the blast and thermal wave of the bomb destroyed nearly 50,000 buildings and killed about 75,000 people (see [Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bombings_of_Hiroshima_and_Nagasaki)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001478-38]

In 1943 the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project) contracted two private companies, [Union Carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Carbide) and [Chevron](284749.md), to quietly compile a survey of uranium deposits around the world. As the survey results came in, two geology professors studied the results and suggested general guidelines for new sources, including uranium associated with gold mines in [the Rand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witwatersrand) area in [South Africa](17416221.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGroves1962180–184-51]

Initially it was believed that uranium was relatively rare, and that [nuclear proliferation](22158.md) could be avoided by simply buying up all known uranium stocks, but within a decade large deposits of it were discovered in many places around the world.[^cite_note-52]

### Reactors

An industrial room with four large illuminated light bulbs hanging down from a bar.

Four light bulbs lit with electricity generated from the first artificial electricity-producing nuclear reactor, [EBR-I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_Breeder_Reactor_I) (1951)

The [X-10 Graphite Reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-10_Graphite_Reactor) at [Oak Ridge National Laboratory](38147.md) (ORNL) in Oak Ridge, Tennessee, formerly known as the Clinton Pile and X-10 Pile, was the world's second artificial nuclear reactor (after Enrico Fermi's Chicago Pile) and was the first reactor designed and built for continuous operation. [Argonne National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argonne_National_Laboratory)'s [Experimental Breeder Reactor I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_Breeder_Reactor_I), located at the Atomic Energy Commission's National Reactor Testing Station near [Arco, Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arco,_Idaho), became the first nuclear reactor to create electricity on 20 December 1951.[^cite_note-53] Initially, four 150-watt light bulbs were lit by the reactor, but improvements eventually enabled it to power the whole facility (later, the town of Arco became the first in the world to have all its [electricity](9550.md) come from nuclear power generated by [BORAX-III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BORAX-III), another reactor designed and operated by Argonne National Laboratory).[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55] The world's first commercial scale nuclear power station, [Obninsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obninsk_Nuclear_Power_Plant) in the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union), began generation with its reactor AM-1 on 27 June 1954. Other early nuclear power plants were [Calder Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calder_Hall_nuclear_power_station) in England, which began generation on 17 October 1956,[^cite_note-56] and the [Shippingport Atomic Power Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shippingport_Atomic_Power_Station) in [Pennsylvania](23332.md), which began on 26 May 1958. Nuclear power was used for the first time for propulsion by a [submarine](28825.md), the [USS *Nautilus*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Nautilus_\(SSN-571\)), in 1954.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968773-41][^cite_note-57]

### Prehistoric naturally occurring fission

Main article: [Natural nuclear fission reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_nuclear_fission_reactor)

In 1972, French physicist [Francis Perrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Perrin_\(physicist\)) discovered fifteen ancient and no longer active natural nuclear fission reactors in three separate ore deposits at the [Oklo mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklo_mine) in [Gabon](12027.md), Africa, collectively known as the [Oklo Fossil Reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_nuclear_fission_reactor). The ore deposit is 1.7 billion years old; then, uranium-235 constituted about 3% of uranium on Earth.[^cite_note-OCRWM-58] This is high enough to permit a sustained chain reaction, if other supporting conditions exist. The capacity of the surrounding sediment to contain the health-threatening [nuclear waste](37257.md) products has been cited by the U.S. federal government as supporting evidence for the feasibility to store spent nuclear fuel at the [Yucca Mountain nuclear waste repository](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yucca_Mountain_nuclear_waste_repository).[^cite_note-OCRWM-58]

### Contamination and the Cold War legacy

A graph showing evolution of number of nuclear weapons in the US and USSR and in the period 1945–2005. US dominates early and USSR later years with and crossover around 1978.

U.S. and USSR/Russian nuclear weapons stockpiles, 1945–2005

Above-ground [nuclear tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_testing) by the Soviet Union and the United States in the 1950s and early 1960s and by [France](5843419.md) into the 1970s and 1980s[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001480-25] spread a significant amount of [fallout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fallout) from uranium [daughter isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daughter_isotope) around the world.[^cite_note-59] Additional fallout and pollution occurred from several [nuclear accidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_and_radiation_accidents).[^cite_note-60]

Uranium miners have a higher incidence of [cancer](105219.md). An excess risk of lung cancer among [Navajo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navajo_people) uranium miners, for example, has been documented and linked to their occupation.[^cite_note-Gilliland_et_al_2000-61] The [Radiation Exposure Compensation Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_Exposure_Compensation_Act), a 1990 law in the US, required \$100,000 in "compassion payments" to uranium miners diagnosed with cancer or other respiratory ailments.[^cite_note-ajph.org-62]

During the [Cold War](325329.md) between the Soviet Union and the United States, huge stockpiles of uranium were amassed and tens of thousands of nuclear weapons were created using enriched uranium and plutonium made from uranium. After the [break-up of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collapse_of_the_Soviet_Union_\(1985%E2%80%931991\)#Dissolution_of_the_USSR) in 1991, an estimated 600 short tons (540 metric tons) of highly enriched weapons grade uranium (enough to make 40,000 nuclear warheads) had been stored in often inadequately guarded facilities in the [Russian Federation](25391.md) and several other former Soviet states.[^cite_note-EncyIntel-21] Police in [Asia](689.md), [Europe](9239.md), and [South America](26769.md) on at least 16 occasions from 1993 to 2005 have [intercepted shipments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_espionage) of smuggled bomb-grade uranium or plutonium, most of which was from ex-Soviet sources.[^cite_note-EncyIntel-21] From 1993 to 2005 the [Material Protection, Control, and Accounting Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Material_Protection,_Control,_and_Accounting_Program), operated by the [federal government of the United States](195149.md), spent about US\$550 million to help safeguard uranium and plutonium stockpiles in Russia. This money was used for improvements and security enhancements at research and storage facilities.[^cite_note-EncyIntel-21]

Safety of nuclear facilities in Russia has been significantly improved since the stabilization of political and economical turmoil of the early 1990s. For example, in 1993 there were 29 incidents ranking above level 1 on the [International Nuclear Event Scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Nuclear_Event_Scale), and this number dropped under four per year in 1995–2003. The number of employees receiving annual radiation doses above 20 [mSv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sievert), which is equivalent to a single full-body [CT scan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CT_scan),[^cite_note-63] saw a strong decline around 2000. In November 2015, the Russian government approved a federal program for nuclear and radiation safety for 2016 to 2030 with a budget of 562 billion rubles (ca. 8 billion [USD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USD)). Its key issue is "the deferred liabilities accumulated during the 70 years of the nuclear industry, particularly during the time of the Soviet Union". About 73% of the budget will be spent on decommissioning aged and obsolete nuclear reactors and nuclear facilities, especially those involved in state defense programs; 20% will go in processing and disposal of nuclear fuel and radioactive waste, and 5% into monitoring and ensuring of nuclear and radiation safety.[^cite_note-64]

## Occurrence

Uranium is a [naturally occurring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_abundance) element found in low levels in all rock, soil, and water. It is the highest-numbered element found naturally in significant quantities on Earth and is almost always found combined with other elements.[^cite_note-LANL-13] Uranium is the [48th most abundant element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abundance_of_elements_in_Earth's_crust) in the Earth's crust.[^cite_note-65] The decay of uranium, [thorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorium), and [potassium-40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium-40) in Earth's [mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantle_\(geology\)) is thought to be the main source of heat[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67] that keeps the Earth's [outer core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_of_the_Earth) in the liquid state and drives [mantle convection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantle_convection), which in turn drives [plate tectonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics).

Uranium's [concentration in the Earth's crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abundance_of_elements_in_Earth's_crust) is (depending on the reference) 2 to 4 parts per million,[^cite_note-SciTechEncy-12][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001480-25] or about 40 times as abundant as [silver](27119.md).[^cite_note-ColumbiaEncy-18] The Earth's crust from the surface to 25 km (15 mi) down is calculated to contain 10<sup>17</sup> kg (2×10<sup>17</sup> lb) of uranium while the [oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean) may contain 10<sup>13</sup> kg (2×10<sup>13</sup> lb).[^cite_note-SciTechEncy-12] The concentration of uranium in soil ranges from 0.7 to 11 parts per million (up to 15 parts per million in farmland soil due to use of phosphate [fertilizers](37401.md) containing uranium impurities),[^cite_note-68] and its concentration in sea water is 3 parts per billion.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001480-25]

Uranium is more plentiful than [antimony](898.md), [tin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin), [cadmium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadmium), [mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_\(element\)), or silver, and it is about as abundant as [arsenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsenic) or [molybdenum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molybdenum).[^cite_note-LANL-13][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001480-25] Uranium is found in hundreds of minerals, including:

### Origin

Like all elements with [atomic weights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_weight) higher than that of [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron), uranium is only naturally formed by the [r-process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R-process) (rapid neutron capture) in [supernovae](27680.md) and [neutron star mergers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star_merger).[^cite_note-69] Primordial thorium and uranium are only produced in the r-process, because the [s-process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-process) (slow neutron capture) is too slow and cannot pass the gap of instability after bismuth.[^cite_note-B2FH-70][^cite_note-71] Besides the two extant primordial uranium isotopes, <sup>235</sup>U and <sup>238</sup>U, the r-process also produced significant quantities of [<sup>236</sup>U](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-236), which has a shorter half-life and so is an [extinct radionuclide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinct_radionuclide), having long since decayed completely to <sup>232</sup>Th. Further uranium-236 was produced by the decay of [<sup>244</sup>Pu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium-244), accounting for the observed higher-than-expected abundance of thorium and lower-than-expected abundance of uranium.[^cite_note-thoruranium-72] While the natural abundance of uranium has been supplemented by the decay of extinct [<sup>242</sup>Pu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium-242) (half-life 375,000 years) and <sup>247</sup>Cm (half-life 16 million years), producing <sup>238</sup>U and <sup>235</sup>U respectively, this occurred to an almost negligible extent due to the shorter half-lives of these parents and their lower production than <sup>236</sup>U and <sup>244</sup>Pu, the parents of thorium: the <sup>247</sup>Cm/<sup>235</sup>U ratio at the formation of the Solar System was (7.0±1.6)×10<sup>−5</sup>.[^cite_note-73]

Some bacteria, such as *[Shewanella putrefaciens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shewanella_putrefaciens)*, *[Geobacter metallireducens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geobacter_metallireducens)* and some strains of *[Burkholderia fungorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burkholderia_fungorum)*, can use uranium for their growth and convert U(VI) to U(IV).[^cite_note-74][^cite_note-75] Recent research suggests that this pathway includes reduction of the soluble U(VI) via an intermediate U(V) pentavalent state.[^cite_note-Renshaw-76][^cite_note-Vitesse-77]  Other organisms, such as the [lichen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lichen) *Trapelia involuta* or [microorganisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microorganism) such as the [bacterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacterium) *[Citrobacter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citrobacter)*, can absorb concentrations of uranium that are up to 300 times the level of their environment.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001476_and_482-78] *Citrobacter* species absorb [uranyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranyl) ions when given [glycerol phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycerol_phosphate) (or other similar organic phosphates). After one day, one gram of bacteria can encrust themselves with nine grams of uranyl phosphate crystals; this creates the possibility that these organisms could be used in [bioremediation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioremediation) to [decontaminate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_contamination) uranium-polluted water.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001477-33][^cite_note-79] The proteobacterium *[Geobacter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geobacter)* has also been shown to bioremediate uranium in ground water.[^cite_note-AndersonVrionis2003-80] The mycorrhizal fungus *[Glomus intraradices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glomus_intraradices)* increases uranium content in the roots of its symbiotic plant.[^cite_note-81]

In nature, uranium(VI) forms highly soluble carbonate complexes at alkaline pH. This leads to an increase in mobility and availability of uranium to groundwater and soil from nuclear wastes which leads to health hazards. However, it is difficult to precipitate uranium as phosphate in the presence of excess carbonate at alkaline pH. A *[Sphingomonas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphingomonas)* sp. strain BSAR-1 has been found to express a high activity [alkaline phosphatase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkaline_phosphatase) (PhoK) that has been applied for bioprecipitation of uranium as uranyl phosphate species from alkaline solutions. The precipitation ability was enhanced by overexpressing PhoK protein in *[E. coli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._coli)*.[^cite_note-82]

[Plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant) absorb some uranium from soil. Dry weight concentrations of uranium in plants range from 5 to 60 parts per billion, and ash from burnt wood can have concentrations up to 4 parts per million.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001477-33] Dry weight concentrations of uranium in [food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food) plants are typically lower with one to two micrograms per day ingested through the food people eat.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001477-33]

### Production and mining

Main article: [Uranium mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_mining)

Worldwide production of uranium in 2024 was 60,213 [tonnes](31185.md), of which 23,270 t (39%) was mined in [Kazakhstan](16642.md). Other important uranium mining countries are [Canada](5042916.md) (14,309 t), [Namibia](21292.md) (7,333 t), [Australia](4689264.md) (4,598 t), [Uzbekistan](31853.md) (4,000 t), and [Russia](25391.md) (2,738 t).[^cite_note-WNA-WUM-83]

Uranium ore is mined in several ways: [open pit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-pit_mining), [underground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underground_mining_\(soft_rock\)), [in-situ leaching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In-situ_leach), and [borehole mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borehole_mining).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001479-11] Low-grade uranium ore mined typically contains 0.01 to 0.25% uranium oxides. Extensive measures must be employed to extract the metal from its ore.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968774-84] High-grade ores found in [Athabasca Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athabasca_Basin) deposits in [Saskatchewan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saskatchewan), Canada can contain up to 23% uranium oxides on average.[^cite_note-85] Uranium ore is crushed and rendered into a fine powder and then leached with either an [acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid) or [alkali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali). The [leachate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leachate) is subjected to one of several sequences of precipitation, solvent extraction, and ion exchange. The resulting mixture, called [yellowcake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellowcake), contains at least 75% uranium oxides U<sub>3</sub>O<sub>8</sub>. Yellowcake is then [calcined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcined) to remove impurities from the milling process before refining and conversion.[^cite_note-86]

Commercial-grade uranium can be produced through the [reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) of uranium [halides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halide) with [alkali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali_metal) or [alkaline earth metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkaline_earth_metal).[^cite_note-LANL-13] Uranium metal can also be prepared through [electrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolysis) of KUF<sup></sup>\
<sub>5</sub> or [UF<sup></sup>\
<sub>4</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_tetrafluoride), dissolved in molten [calcium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_chloride) (CaCl<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>) and [sodium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chloride) ([Na](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium)Cl) solution.[^cite_note-LANL-13] Very pure uranium is produced through the [thermal decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_decomposition) of uranium halides on a hot filament.[^cite_note-LANL-13]

### Resources and reserves



Uranium price 1990–2022.

It is estimated that 6.1 million tonnes of uranium exists in ores that are economically viable at US\$130 per kg of uranium,[^cite_note-res-88] while 35 million tonnes are classed as mineral resources (reasonable prospects for eventual economic extraction).[^cite_note-IAEAResourcesDemand-89]

Australia has 28% of the world's known uranium ore reserves[^cite_note-res-88] and the world's largest single uranium deposit is located at the [Olympic Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Dam,_South_Australia) Mine in [South Australia](26716.md).[^cite_note-90] There is a significant reserve of uranium in [Bakouma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakouma), a [sub-prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-prefecture) in the [prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefecture) of [Mbomou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbomou) in the [Central African Republic](5478.md).[^cite_note-91]

Some uranium also originates from dismantled nuclear weapons.[^cite_note-92] For example, in 1993–2013 Russia supplied the United States with 15,000 tonnes of low-enriched uranium within the [Megatons to Megawatts Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megatons_to_Megawatts_Program).[^cite_note-93]

An additional 4.6 billion tonnes of uranium are estimated to be dissolved in [sea water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_water) ([Japanese](15573.md) scientists in the 1980s showed that extraction of uranium from sea water using [ion exchangers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion_exchange) was technically feasible).[^cite_note-UseaWater-94][^cite_note-stanfordCohen-95] There have been experiments to extract uranium from sea water,[^cite_note-96] but the yield has been low due to the carbonate present in the water. In 2012, [ORNL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ORNL) researchers announced the successful development of a new absorbent material dubbed HiCap which performs surface retention of solid or gas molecules, atoms or ions and also effectively removes toxic metals from water, according to results verified by researchers at [Pacific Northwest National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Northwest_National_Laboratory).[^cite_note-97][^cite_note-98]

In 2005, ten countries accounted for the majority of the world's concentrated uranium oxides: [Canada](5042916.md) (27.9%), [Australia](4689264.md) (22.8%), [Kazakhstan](16642.md) (10.5%), [Russia](25391.md) (8.0%), [Namibia](21292.md) (7.5%), [Niger](21373.md) (7.4%), [Uzbekistan](31853.md) (5.5%), the [United States](3434750.md) (2.5%), [Argentina](18951905.md) (2.1%) and [Ukraine](31750.md) (1.9%).[^cite_note-100] In 2008, Kazakhstan was forecast to increase production and become the world's largest supplier of uranium by 2009;[^cite_note-101][^cite_note-102] Kazakhstan has dominated the world's uranium market since 2010. In 2021, its share was 45.1%, followed by Namibia (11.9%), Canada (9.7%), Australia (8.7%), Uzbekistan (7.2%), Niger (4.7%), Russia (5.5%), China (3.9%), India (1.3%), Ukraine (0.9%), and South Africa (0.8%), with a world total production of 48,332 tonnes.[^cite_note-WNA-WUM-83] Most uranium was produced not by conventional underground mining of ores (29% of production), but by [in-situ leaching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In-situ_leach) (66%).[^cite_note-WNA-WUM-83][^cite_note-103]

In the late 1960s, UN geologists discovered major uranium deposits and other rare mineral reserves in [Somalia](27358.md). The find was the largest of its kind, with industry experts estimating the deposits at over 25% of the world's then known uranium reserves of 800,000 tons.[^cite_note-Bufais-104]

The ultimate available supply is believed to be sufficient for at least the next 85 years,[^cite_note-IAEAResourcesDemand-89] though some studies indicate underinvestment in the late twentieth century may produce supply problems in the 21st century.[^cite_note-MITfuelSupply-105] Uranium deposits seem to be log-normal distributed. There is a 300-fold increase in the amount of uranium recoverable for each tenfold decrease in ore grade.[^cite_note-106] In other words, there is little high grade ore and proportionately much more low grade ore available.

The most common forms of uranium oxide are [triuranium octoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triuranium_octoxide) (U<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub>O<sup></sup>\
<sub>8</sub>) and UO<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>.[^cite_note-ANL-Chem-109] Both oxide forms are solids that have low solubility in water and are relatively stable over a wide range of environmental conditions. Triuranium octoxide is (depending on conditions) the most stable compound of uranium and is the form most commonly found in nature. Uranium dioxide is the form in which uranium is most commonly used as a nuclear reactor fuel.[^cite_note-ANL-Chem-109] At ambient temperatures, UO<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub> will gradually convert to U<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub>O<sup></sup>\
<sub>8</sub>. Because of their stability, uranium oxides are generally considered the preferred chemical form for storage or disposal.[^cite_note-ANL-Chem-109]

#### Aqueous chemistry



Uranium in its oxidation states III, IV, V, VI

Salts of many [oxidation states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation_state) of uranium are water-[soluble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solubility) and may be studied in [aqueous solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueous_solution). The most common ionic forms are U<sup>3+</sup>\
<sub></sub> (brown-red), U<sup>4+</sup>\
<sub></sub> (green), UO<sup>+</sup>\
<sub>2</sub> (unstable), and [UO<sup>2+</sup>\
<sub>2</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranyl) (yellow), for U(III), U(IV), U(V), and U(VI), respectively.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968778-110] A few [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid) and semi-metallic compounds such as UO and [US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_monosulfide) exist for the formal oxidation state uranium(II), but no simple ions are known to exist in solution for that state. Ions of U<sup>3+</sup>\
<sub></sub> liberate [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) from [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) and are therefore considered to be highly unstable. The UO<sup>2+</sup>\
<sub>2</sub> ion represents the uranium(VI) state and is known to form compounds such as [uranyl carbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranyl_carbonate), [uranyl chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranyl_chloride) and [uranyl sulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranyl_sulfate). UO<sup>2+</sup>\
<sub>2</sub> also forms [complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_\(chemistry\)) with various [organic](22203.md) [chelating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chelation) agents, the most commonly encountered of which is [uranyl acetate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranyl_acetate).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968778-110]

Unlike the uranyl salts of uranium and [polyatomic ion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyatomic_ion) uranium-oxide cationic forms, the [uranates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranate), salts containing a polyatomic uranium-oxide anion, are generally not water-soluble.[^cite_note-111]

#### Carbonates

The interactions of carbonate anions with uranium(VI) cause the [Pourbaix diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pourbaix_diagram) to change greatly when the medium is changed from water to a carbonate containing solution. While the vast majority of carbonates are insoluble in water (students are often taught that all carbonates other than those of alkali metals are insoluble in water), uranium carbonates are often soluble in water. This is because a U(VI) cation is able to bind two terminal oxides and three or more carbonates to form anionic complexes.

#### Effects of pH

The uranium fraction diagrams in the presence of carbonate illustrate this further: when the pH of a uranium(VI) solution increases, the uranium is converted to a hydrated uranium oxide hydroxide; At high pH, it becomes an anionic hydroxide complex.[^cite_note-medusa-112]

When carbonate is added, uranium is converted to a series of carbonate complexes if the pH is increased. One effect of these reactions is increased solubility of uranium in the pH range 6 to 8, a fact that has a direct bearing on the long term stability of spent uranium dioxide nuclear fuels.[^cite_note-medusa-112]

### Hydrides, carbides and nitrides

Uranium metal heated to 250 to 300 °C (482 to 572 °F) reacts with [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) to form [uranium hydride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_hydride). Even higher temperatures will reversibly remove the hydrogen. This property makes uranium hydrides convenient starting materials to create reactive uranium powder along with various uranium [carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbide), [nitride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitride), and [halide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halide) compounds.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968782-113] Two crystal modifications of uranium hydride exist: an α form that is obtained at low temperatures and a β form that is created when the formation temperature is above 250 °C.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968782-113]

[Uranium carbides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_carbide) and [uranium nitrides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_nitride) are both relatively [inert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemically_inert) [semimetallic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semimetal) compounds that are minimally soluble in [acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid), react with water, and can ignite in [air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air) to form U<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub>O<sup></sup>\
<sub>8</sub>.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968782-113] Carbides of uranium include uranium monocarbide (U[C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon)), uranium dicarbide (UC<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>), and diuranium tricarbide (U<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>C<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub>). Both UC and UC<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub> are formed by adding carbon to molten uranium or by exposing the metal to [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide) at high temperatures. Stable below 1800 °C, U<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>C<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub> is prepared by subjecting a heated mixture of UC and UC<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub> to mechanical stress.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968780-114] Uranium nitrides obtained by direct exposure of the metal to [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen) include uranium mononitride (UN), uranium dinitride (UN<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>), and diuranium trinitride (U<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>N<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub>).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968780-114]

### Halides

[Uranium hexafluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_hexafluoride) is the feedstock used to separate uranium-235 from natural uranium.

All uranium fluorides are created using [uranium tetrafluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_tetrafluoride) (UF<sup></sup>\
<sub>4</sub>); UF<sup></sup>\
<sub>4</sub> itself is prepared by hydrofluorination of uranium dioxide.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968782-113] Reduction of UF<sup></sup>\
<sub>4</sub> with hydrogen at 1000 °C produces [uranium trifluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_trifluoride) (UF<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub>). Under the right conditions of temperature and pressure, the reaction of solid UF<sup></sup>\
<sub>4</sub> with gaseous [uranium hexafluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_hexafluoride) (UF<sup></sup>\
<sub>6</sub>) can form the intermediate fluorides of U<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>F<sup></sup>\
<sub>9</sub>, U<sup></sup>\
<sub>4</sub>F<sup></sup>\
<sub>17</sub>, and [UF<sup></sup>\
<sub>5</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_pentafluoride).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968782-113]

At room temperatures, UF<sup></sup>\

This compound can be prepared from uranium dioxide and uranium hydride by the following process:[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968782-113]

UO<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub> + 4 HF → UF<sup></sup>\
<sub>4</sub> + 2 H<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>O (500 °C, endothermic)

UF<sup></sup>\
<sub>4</sub> + F<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub> → UF<sup></sup>\
<sub>6</sub> (350 °C, endothermic)

The resulting UF<sup></sup>\

Uranium hexafluorides (IV) and (V) can be used to make several [hexafluorouranates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexafluorouranate), as they are anions (UF<sub>6</sub><sup>-</sup> and UF<sub>6</sub><sup>2-</sup>). They bond with [alkali metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali_metal), certain [transition metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_metal), and other non-metal compounds.[^cite_note-115][^cite_note-116]

One method of preparing [uranium tetrachloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_tetrachloride) (UCl<sup></sup>\

[Bromides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bromide) and [iodides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iodide) of uranium are formed by direct reaction of, respectively, [bromine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bromine) and [iodine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iodine) with uranium or by adding UH<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub> to those element's acids.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968782-113] Known examples include: [UBr<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium\(III\)_bromide), [UBr<sup></sup>\
<sub>4</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium\(IV\)_bromide), [UI<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium\(III\)_iodide), and [UI<sup></sup>\
<sub>4</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium\(IV\)_iodide). UI<sup></sup>\
<sub>5</sub> has never been prepared. Uranium oxyhalides are water-soluble and include [UO<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>F<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranyl_fluoride), UOCl<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>, [UO<sup></sup>\

<sub>2</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranyl_bromide). Stability of the oxyhalides decrease as the [atomic weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_weight) of the component halide increases.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968782-113]

## Isotopes

Main article: [Isotopes of uranium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopes_of_uranium)

Uranium, like all elements with an atomic number greater than 82, has no [stable isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_isotope). All isotopes of uranium are [radioactive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive) because the [strong nuclear force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_nuclear_force) does not prevail over [electromagnetic repulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_repulsion) in nuclides containing more than 82 protons.[^cite_note-beiser-117] Nevertheless, the two most stable isotopes, <sup>238</sup>U and <sup>235</sup>U, have [half-lives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-life) long enough to occur in nature as [primordial radionuclides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primordial_radionuclide), with measurable quantities having survived since the formation of the Earth.[^cite_note-primU-118] These two [nuclides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclide), along with [thorium-232](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorium-232), are the only confirmed primordial nuclides heavier than nearly-stable [bismuth-209](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bismuth-209).[^cite_note-NUBASE2020-9][^cite_note-pu244-119]

[Natural uranium](2836053.md) consists of three major isotopes: uranium-238 (99.28% natural abundance), uranium-235 (0.71%), and uranium-234 (0.0054%). There are also five other trace isotopes: uranium-240, a decay product of [plutonium-244](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium-244);[^cite_note-pu244-119] uranium-239, which is formed when <sup>238</sup>U undergoes spontaneous fission, releasing neutrons that are captured by another <sup>238</sup>U atom; uranium-237, which is formed when <sup>238</sup>U captures a neutron but emits two more, which then decays to [neptunium-237](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptunium-237); [uranium-236](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-236), which occurs in trace quantities due to neutron capture on <sup>235</sup>U and as a decay product of plutonium-244;[^cite_note-pu244-119] and finally, [uranium-233](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-233), which is formed in the [decay chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decay_chain) of neptunium-237. Additionally, [uranium-232](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-232) would be produced by the [double beta decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_beta_decay) of natural [thorium-232](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorium-232), though this energetically possible process has never been observed.[^cite_note-Tretyak2002-122]

Uranium-238 is the most stable isotope of uranium, with a half-life of about 4.463×10<sup>9</sup> years,[^cite_note-NUBASE2020-9] roughly the [age of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_the_Earth). Uranium-238 is predominantly an alpha emitter, decaying to thorium-234. It ultimately decays through the [uranium series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_series), which has 18 members, into [lead-206](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead-206).[^cite_note-ColumbiaEncy-18] Uranium-238 is not fissile, but is a fertile isotope, because after [neutron activation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_activation) it can be converted to plutonium-239, another fissile isotope. Indeed, the <sup>238</sup>U nucleus can absorb one neutron to produce the radioactive isotope [uranium-239](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-239). <sup>239</sup>U decays by [beta emission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_emission) to [neptunium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptunium)-239, also a beta-emitter, that decays in its turn, within a few days into plutonium-239. <sup>239</sup>Pu was used as fissile material in the first [atomic bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bomb) detonated in the "[Trinity test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_test)" on 16 July 1945 in [New Mexico](21649.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeaborg1968773-41]

Uranium-235 has a half-life of about 7.04×10<sup>8</sup> years; it is the next most stable uranium isotope after <sup>238</sup>U and is also predominantly an alpha emitter, decaying to thorium-231.[^cite_note-NUBASE2020-9] Uranium-235 is important for both [nuclear reactors](22151.md) and [nuclear weapons](21785.md), because it is the only uranium isotope existing in nature on Earth in significant amounts that is fissile. This means that it can be split into two or three fragments ([fission products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fission_products)) by thermal neutrons.[^cite_note-ColumbiaEncy-18] The decay chain of <sup>235</sup>U, which is called the [actinium series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actinium_series), has 15 members and eventually decays into lead-207.[^cite_note-ColumbiaEncy-18] The constant rates of decay in these decay series makes the comparison of the ratios of parent to [daughter elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decay_product) useful in radiometric dating.

Uranium-236 has a half-life of 2.342×10<sup>7</sup> years[^cite_note-NUBASE2020-9] and is not found in significant quantities in nature. The half-life of uranium-236 is too short for it to be primordial, though it has been identified as an [extinct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinct_radionuclide) progenitor of its alpha decay daughter, thorium-232.[^cite_note-thoruranium-72] Uranium-236 occurs in [spent nuclear fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spent_nuclear_fuel) when neutron capture on <sup>235</sup>U does not induce fission, or as a decay product of [plutonium-240](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium-240). Uranium-236 is not fertile, as three more neutron captures are required to produce fissile <sup>239</sup>Pu, and is not itself fissile; as such, it is considered long-lived radioactive waste.[^cite_note-236U-123]

Uranium-234 is a member of the uranium series and occurs in equilibrium with its progenitor, <sup>238</sup>U; it undergoes alpha decay with a half-life of 245,500 years[^cite_note-NUBASE2020-9] and decays to lead-206 through a series of relatively short-lived isotopes.

Uranium-233 undergoes alpha decay with a half-life of 160,000 years and, like <sup>235</sup>U, is fissile.[^cite_note-LANL-13] It can be bred from [thorium-232](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorium-232) via neutron bombardment, usually in a nuclear reactor; this process is known as the [thorium fuel cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorium_fuel_cycle). Owing to the fissility of <sup>233</sup>U and the greater natural abundance of thorium (three times that of uranium),[^cite_note-iaea2022-124] <sup>233</sup>U has been investigated for use as nuclear fuel as a possible alternative to <sup>235</sup>U and <sup>239</sup>Pu,[^cite_note-Forsburg_1999-125] though is not in widespread use as of 2022.[^cite_note-iaea2022-124] The decay chain of uranium-233 forms part of the [neptunium series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptunium_series) and ends at nearly-stable bismuth-209 (half-life 2.01×10<sup>19</sup> years)[^cite_note-NUBASE2020-9] and stable [thallium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thallium)-205.

[Uranium-232](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-232) is an alpha emitter with a half-life of 68.9 years.[^cite_note-NUBASE2020-9] This isotope is produced as a byproduct in production of <sup>233</sup>U and is considered a nuisance, as it is not fissile and decays through short-lived alpha and [gamma emitters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_radiation) such as [<sup>208</sup>Tl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thallium-208).[^cite_note-Forsburg_1999-125] It is also expected that thorium-232 should be able to undergo [double beta decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_beta_decay), which would produce uranium-232, but this has not yet been observed experimentally.[^cite_note-NUBASE2020-9]

All isotopes from <sup>232</sup>U to <sup>236</sup>U inclusive have minor [cluster decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cluster_decay) branches (less than 10<sup>−10</sup>%), and all these bar <sup>233</sup>U, in addition to <sup>238</sup>U, have minor [spontaneous fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_fission) branches;[^cite_note-NUBASE2020-9] the greatest [branching ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branching_ratio) for spontaneous fission is about 5×10<sup>−5</sup>% for <sup>238</sup>U, or about one in every two million decays.[^cite_note-126] The shorter-lived trace isotopes <sup>237</sup>U and <sup>239</sup>U exclusively undergo [beta decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_decay), with respective half-lives of 6.752 days and 23.45 minutes.[^cite_note-NUBASE2020-9]

In total, 28 isotopes of uranium have been identified, ranging in [mass number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_number) from 214[^cite_note-127] to 242, with the exception of 220.[^cite_note-NUBASE2020-9][^cite_note-U241-128] Among the uranium isotopes not found in natural samples or nuclear fuel, the longest-lived is <sup>230</sup>U, an alpha emitter with a half-life of 20.23 days.[^cite_note-NUBASE2020-9] This isotope has been considered for use in [targeted alpha-particle therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Targeted_alpha-particle_therapy) (TAT).[^cite_note-230Pa-129] All other isotopes have half-lives shorter than one hour, except for <sup>231</sup>U (half-life 4.2 days) and <sup>240</sup>U (half-life 14.1 hours).[^cite_note-NUBASE2020-9] The shortest-lived known isotope is <sup>221</sup>U, with a half-life of 660 nanoseconds, and it is expected that the hitherto unknown <sup>220</sup>U has an even shorter half-life.[^cite_note-Khuyagbaatar-130] The proton-rich isotopes lighter than <sup>232</sup>U primarily undergo alpha decay, except for <sup>229</sup>U and <sup>231</sup>U, which decay to [protactinium isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopes_of_protactinium) via [positron emission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron_emission) and [electron capture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_capture), respectively; the neutron-rich <sup>240</sup>U, <sup>241</sup>U, and <sup>242</sup>U undergo [beta decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_decay) to form [neptunium isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopes_of_neptunium).[^cite_note-NUBASE2020-9][^cite_note-U241-128]

### Enrichment

Main article: [Enriched uranium](37555.md)

A photo of a large hall filled with arrays of long white standing cylinders.

Cascades of [gas centrifuges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_centrifuge) are used to enrich uranium ore to concentrate its fissionable isotopes.

In nature, uranium is found as uranium-238 (99.2742%) and uranium-235 (0.7204%).[^cite_note-NUBASE2020-9] [Isotope separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope_separation) concentrates (enriches) the fissile uranium-235 for nuclear weapons and most nuclear power plants, except for [gas cooled reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_cooled_reactor) and [pressurized heavy water reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressurized_heavy_water_reactor). Most neutrons released by a fissioning atom of uranium-235 must impact other uranium-235 atoms to sustain the [nuclear chain reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_chain_reaction). The concentration and amount of uranium-235 needed to achieve this is called a '[critical mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_mass)'.[^cite_note-131]

To be considered 'enriched', the uranium-235 fraction should be between 3% and 5%.[^cite_note-132] This process produces huge quantities of uranium that is depleted of uranium-235 and with a correspondingly increased fraction of uranium-238, called depleted uranium or 'DU'. To be considered 'depleted', the <sup>235</sup>U concentration should be no more than 0.3%.[^cite_note-paducah-133] The price of uranium has risen since 2001, so enrichment tailings containing more than 0.35% uranium-235 are being considered for re-enrichment, driving the price of [depleted uranium hexafluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depleted_uranium_hexafluoride) above \$130 per kilogram in July 2007 from \$5 in 2001.[^cite_note-paducah-133]

The [gas centrifuge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_centrifuge) process, where gaseous [uranium hexafluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_hexafluoride) (UF<sup></sup>\
<sub>6</sub>) is separated by the difference in molecular weight between <sup>235</sup>UF<sub>6</sub> and <sup>238</sup>UF<sub>6</sub> using high-speed [centrifuges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrifuge), is the cheapest and leading enrichment process.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001478-38] The [gaseous diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaseous_diffusion) process had been the leading method for enrichment and was used in the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project). In this process, uranium hexafluoride is repeatedly [diffused](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion) through a [silver](27119.md)-[zinc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc) membrane, and the different isotopes of uranium are separated by diffusion rate (since uranium-238 is heavier it diffuses slightly slower than uranium-235).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001478-38] The [molecular laser isotope separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_laser_isotope_separation) method employs a [laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser) beam of precise energy to sever the bond between uranium-235 and fluorine. This leaves uranium-238 bonded to fluorine and allows uranium-235 metal to precipitate from the solution.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001479-11] An alternative laser method of enrichment is known as [atomic vapor laser isotope separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_vapor_laser_isotope_separation) (AVLIS) and employs visible [tunable lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunable_laser) such as [dye lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dye_laser).[^cite_note-134] Another method used is liquid thermal diffusion.[^cite_note-SciTechEncy-12]

The only significant deviation from the <sup>235</sup>U to <sup>238</sup>U ratio in any known natural samples occurs in [Oklo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklo), [Gabon](12027.md), where [natural nuclear fission reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_nuclear_fission_reactor) consumed some of the <sup>235</sup>U some two billion years ago when the ratio of <sup>235</sup>U to <sup>238</sup>U was more akin to that of [low enriched uranium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_enriched_uranium) allowing regular ("light") water to act as a [neutron moderator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_moderator) akin to the process in humanmade [light water reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_water_reactor). The existence of such natural fission reactors which had been theoretically predicted beforehand was proven as the slight deviation of <sup>235</sup>U concentration from the expected values were discovered during [uranium enrichment](37555.md) in France. Subsequent investigations to rule out any nefarious human action (such as stealing of <sup>235</sup>U) confirmed the theory by finding isotope ratios of common [fission products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fission_product) (or rather their stable daughter nuclides) in line with the values expected for fission but deviating from the values expected for non-fission derived samples of those elements.[^cite_note-135]

## Human exposure

A person can be exposed to uranium (or its [radioactive daughters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decay_product), such as [radon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon)) by inhaling dust in air or by ingesting contaminated water and food. The amount of uranium in air is usually very small; however, people who work in factories that process [phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate) [fertilizers](37401.md) containing uranium impurities, live near government facilities that made or tested nuclear weapons, live or work near a modern battlefield where depleted uranium [weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weapons) have been used, or live or work near a [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal)-fired power plant, facilities that mine or process uranium ore, or enrich uranium for reactor fuel, may have increased exposure to uranium.[^cite_note-EPA-Rad-136][^cite_note-ATSDR-ToxFAQ-137] Houses or structures that are over uranium deposits (either natural or man-made slag deposits) may have an increased incidence of exposure to radon gas.

The health impacts of natural and of depleted uranium are chemical rather than due to radiation.[^cite_note-ATSDR-ToxFAQ-137] The [Occupational Safety and Health Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_Safety_and_Health_Administration) (OSHA) has set the [permissible exposure limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permissible_exposure_limit) for uranium exposure in the workplace as 0.25 mg/m<sup>3</sup> over an 8-hour workday. The [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_for_Occupational_Safety_and_Health) (NIOSH) has set a [recommended exposure limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recommended_exposure_limit) (REL) of 0.2 mg/m<sup>3</sup> over an 8-hour workday and a short-term limit of 0.6 mg/m<sup>3</sup>. At 10 mg/m<sup>3</sup>, uranium is [immediately dangerous to life and health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IDLH).[^cite_note-138]

Most ingested uranium is excreted during [digestion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digestion). Only 0.5% is absorbed when insoluble forms of uranium, such as its oxide, are ingested, whereas absorption of the more soluble [uranyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranyl) ion can be up to 5%.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001477-33] However, soluble uranium compounds tend to quickly pass through the body, whereas insoluble uranium compounds, especially when inhaled by way of dust into the [lungs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lung), pose a more serious exposure hazard. After entering the bloodstream, the absorbed uranium tends to [bioaccumulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioaccumulation) and stay for many years in [bone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone) tissue because of uranium's affinity for phosphates.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001477-33] Incorporated uranium becomes uranyl ions, which accumulate in bone, liver, kidney, and reproductive tissues.[^cite_note-139]

Elements of high atomic number like uranium exhibit phantom or secondary radiotoxicity through absorption of natural background gamma and X-rays and re-emission of photoelectrons, which in combination with the high affinity of uranium to the phosphate moiety of DNA cause increased single and double strand DNA breaks.[^cite_note-140]

Uranium is not absorbed through the skin, and [alpha particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_particle) released by uranium cannot penetrate the skin.[^cite_note-epa-30]

Uranium can be decontaminated from steel surfaces[^cite_note-Francis-141] and [aquifers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquifer).[^cite_note-142][^cite_note-143]

### Effects and precautions

Normal functioning of the [kidney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidney), [brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain), [liver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liver), [heart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart), and other systems can be affected by uranium exposure, because, besides being weakly radioactive, uranium is a [toxic metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal_toxicity).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2001477-33][^cite_note-Craft04-144][^cite_note-ATSDR-145] Uranium is also a [reproductive toxicant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproductive_toxicant).[^cite_note-Hindin2005-146][^cite_note-147] Radiological effects are generally local because alpha radiation, the primary form of <sup>238</sup>U decay, has a very short range, and will not penetrate skin. Alpha radiation from inhaled uranium has been demonstrated to cause lung cancer in exposed nuclear workers.[^cite_note-148] The [Centers for Disease Control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centers_for_Disease_Control) have published one study stating that neither natural nor depleted uranium have been classified with respect to carcinogenicity.[^cite_note-ATSDR-PHS-149] Exposure to its decay products, especially [radon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon), is a significant health threat, and uranium processing produces wastes contaminated with radium which in turn produces radon gas.[^cite_note-150] Because of its long half-life, purified uranium will not produce significant amounts of daughter nuclides for millions of years. Exposure to [strontium-90](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strontium-90), [iodine-131](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iodine-131), and other fission products is unrelated to uranium exposure, but may result from medical procedures or exposure to spent reactor fuel or fallout from nuclear weapons.[^cite_note-151]

Although accidental inhalation exposure to a high concentration of [uranium hexafluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_hexafluoride) has resulted in human fatalities, those deaths were associated with the generation of highly toxic hydrofluoric acid and [uranyl fluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranyl_fluoride) rather than with uranium itself.[^cite_note-152] Finely divided uranium metal presents a fire hazard because uranium is [pyrophoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrophoricity); small grains will ignite spontaneously in air at room temperature.[^cite_note-LANL-13]

Uranium metal is commonly handled with gloves as a sufficient precaution.[^cite_note-DOH.WA-153] Uranium concentrate is handled and contained so as to ensure that people do not inhale or ingest it.[^cite_note-DOH.WA-153]
