# World energy supply and consumption



Global energy consumption, measured in [exajoules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exajoules) per year: Coal, oil, and natural gas remain the primary global energy sources even as [renewables](25784.md) have begun rapidly increasing.

[^cite_note-1]



Primary energy consumption by source (worldwide) from 1965 to 2020

[^cite_note-2]

**World energy supply and consumption** refers to the global supply of [energy resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_energy_resources) and the [consumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_consumption) of those resources. The system of global energy supply consists of the [development](533487.md), [refinement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refineries), and trade of energy. Energy supplies may exist in various forms such as *raw resources* or *more processed and refined* forms of energy. The raw energy resources include for example [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal), [unprocessed oil and gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_and_gas), [uranium](31743.md). In comparison, the refined forms of energy include for example [refined oil](195137.md) that can be used as [fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuels) or to generate [electricity](6168349.md). Energy resources may be used in various different ways, depending on the specific resource (e.g. coal), and intended end use (industrial, residential, etc.). Energy production and consumption play a significant role in the [global economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy). It is needed in industry and global [transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation). The total energy supply chain, from production to final consumption, involves many activities that cause a loss of [useful energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Useful_energy).[^cite_note-3]

Total energy consumption tends to increase by about 1–2% per year.[^cite_note-4] As of 2022, 80% of energy consumption is derived from fossil fuels.[^cite_note-Enerdata-5] More recently, [renewable energy](25784.md) has been growing rapidly, averaging about a 20% increase per year in the 2010s.[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7]

Two key problems with energy production and consumption are [greenhouse gas emissions](9528025.md) and [environmental pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollution). Of the approximately 50 billion tonnes of worldwide annual greenhouse gas emissions,[^cite_note-8] 36 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide was a result of energy use (almost all from fossil fuels) in 2021.[^cite_note-9] Many scenarios have been envisioned to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, usually by the name of [net zero emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_zero).&#x20;

There is a clear connection between energy consumption per capita, and GDP per capita. As such, richer and more industrialized countries consume significantly more energy per person when compared to lower-income countries.[^cite_note-10]

The [Gulf States](3088555.md) and [Russia](25391.md) are major energy exporters, due to their large petroleum and natural gas reserves. Their customers include the [European Union](9317.md) and [China](5405.md).

A significant lack of energy supplies is called an [energy crisis](261316.md).

[Primary energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_energy) refers to the first form of energy encountered, as raw resources collected directly from energy production, before any conversion or transformation of the energy occurs.

Energy production is usually classified as:

- Fossil, using [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal), [crude oil](23195.md), and [natural gas](22131.md);

- Nuclear, using [uranium](31743.md);

- [Renewable](25784.md), using [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass), [geothermal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geothermal_energy), [hydropower](14073.md), [solar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_energy), [wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power), [tidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_power), [wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_power), among others.

Primary energy assessment by IEA follows certain rules[^cite_note-13] to ease measurement of different kinds of energy. These rules are controversial. Water and air flow energy that drives hydro and wind turbines, and sunlight that powers solar panels, are not taken as PE, which is set at the electric energy produced. But fossil and nuclear energy are set at the reaction heat, which is about three times the electric energy. This measurement difference can lead to underestimating the economic contribution of renewable energy.[^cite_note-14]

Enerdata displays data for "Total energy / production: Coal, Oil, Gas, Biomass, Heat and Electricity" and for "Renewables / % in electricity production: Renewables, non-renewables".[^cite_note-Enerdata-5]

Renewable is Biomass plus Heat plus renewable percentage of Electricity production (hydro, wind, solar). Nuclear is nonrenewable percentage of Electricity production. Primary energy sources are transformed by the energy sector to generate energy carriers.

Energy resources must be processed in order to make them suitable for final consumption. For example, there may be various impurities in raw coal mined or raw natural gas that was produced from an oil well that may make it unsuitable to be burned in a power plant.

Primary energy is converted in many ways to [energy carriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_carrier), also known as secondary energy:[^cite_note-17]

- Coal mainly goes to [thermal power stations](2549543.md). [Coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_\(fuel\)) is derived by destructive distillation of bituminous coal.

- Crude oil goes mainly to [oil refineries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_refineries)

- Natural-gas goes to [natural-gas processing](8862061.md) plants to remove contaminants such as water, carbon dioxide and hydrogen sulfide, and to adjust the heating value. It is used as fuel gas, also in thermal power stations.

- Nuclear reaction heat is used in thermal power stations.

- [Biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass) is used directly or converted to [biofuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofuel).

Electricity [generators](9540.md) are driven by [steam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_turbine) or [gas turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_turbine) in a [thermal plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_plant), or [water turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_turbine) in a [hydropower](14073.md) station, or [wind turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_turbine), usually in a [wind farm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_farm). The invention of the [solar cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_cell) in 1954 started electricity generation by solar panels, connected to a [power inverter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_inverter). Mass production of panels around the year 2000 made this economic.

Further information: [Energy quality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_quality) and [Fuel cell § Theoretical maximum efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_cell#Theoretical_maximum_efficiency)

There are different [qualities of energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_quality). Heat, especially at a relatively low temperature, is low-quality energy of random motion, whereas electricity is high-quality energy that flows smoothly through wires. It takes around 3 kWh of heat to produce 1 kWh of electricity. But by the same token, a kilowatt-hour of this high-quality electricity can be used to pump several kilowatt-hours of heat into a building using a heat pump. It turns out that the loss of useful energy incurred in thermal electricity plants is very much more than the loss due to, say, resistance in power lines, because of quality differences. Electricity can also be used in many ways in which heat cannot.&#x20;

In fact, the loss in thermal plants is due to poor conversion of chemical energy of fuel to motion by combustion. Otherwise chemical energy of fuel is not inherently low-quality; for example, conversion of chemical energy to electricity in batteries can approach 100%. So energy loss in thermal plants is real loss.

One way to account for this difference and to compare them is via the substitution method. High quality energy is measured according to the amount of low-quality energy that would be needed to produce it.

*Total final consumption* (TFC) is the worldwide consumption of energy by end-users (whereas primary energy consumption (Eurostat)[^cite_note-27] or total energy supply (IEA) is total energy demand and thus also includes what the energy sector uses itself and transformation and distribution losses). This energy consists of fuel (78%) and electricity (22%). The tables list amounts, expressed in million tonnes of oil equivalent per year (1 Mtoe = 11.63 TWh) and how much of these is renewable energy. Non-energy products are not considered here. The data are of 2018.[^cite_note-balances-23][^cite_note-Data_tables_–_Data_&_Statistics-28] The world's renewable share of TFC was 18% in 2018: 7% traditional biomass, 3.6% hydropower and 7.4% other renewables.[^cite_note-29]

In the period 2005–2017 worldwide final consumption of coal increased by 23%, of oil and gas increased by 18%, and that of electricity increased by 41%.[^cite_note-balances-23]

Fuel comes in three types: Fossil fuel is natural gas, fuel derived from petroleum (LPG, gasoline, kerosene, gas/diesel, fuel oil), or from coal (anthracite, bituminous coal, coke, [blast furnace gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blast_furnace_gas)). Secondly, there is renewable fuel ([biofuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofuel) and fuel derived from waste). And lastly, the fuel used for [district heating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_heating).

The amounts of fuel in the tables are based on [lower heating value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_heating_value).

### Energy for energy

Main article: [Energy return on investment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_return_on_investment)

Some fuel and electricity is used to construct, maintain and demolish/recycle installations that produce fuel and electricity, such as [oil platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_platform), uranium [isotope separators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope_separator) and wind turbines. For these producers to be economical the *ratio of energy returned on energy invested* (EROEI) or *energy return on investment* (EROI) should be large enough.

If the final energy delivered for consumption is E and the EROI equals R, then the net energy available is E-E/R. The percentage available energy is 100-100/R. For R>10 more than 90% is available but for R=2 only 50% and for R=1 none. This steep decline is known as the *net energy cliff*.[^cite_note-31]

## Availability of data

Many countries publish statistics on the energy supply and consumption of either their own country, of other countries of interest, or of all countries combined in one chart. One of the largest organizations in this field, the [International Energy Agency](285292.md) (IEA), sells yearly comprehensive energy data which makes this data [paywalled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paywall) and [difficult to access for internet users](285292.md#Restricting_access_to_data).[^cite_note-balances-23] The organization Enerdata on the other hand publishes a free Yearbook, making the data more accessible.[^cite_note-Enerdata-5] Another trustworthy organization that provides accurate energy data, mainly referring to the USA, is the [U.S. Energy Information Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Energy_Information_Administration).

Due to the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md), there was a significant decline in energy usage worldwide in 2020, but total energy demand worldwide had recovered by 2021, and has hit a record high in 2022.[^cite_note-32]

In 2022, consumers worldwide spent nearly USD 10 trillion on energy, averaging more than USD 1,200 per person. This reflects a 20% increase over the previous five-year average, highlighting the significant economic impact and the increasing financial burden of energy consumption on a global scale.[^cite_note-33]<sup>: 13 </sup>

The IEA's "Electricity 2024" report details a 2.2% growth in global electricity demand for 2023, forecasting an annual increase of 3.4% through 2026, with notable contributions from emerging economies like [China](5405.md) and [India](14533.md), despite a slump in advanced economies due to economic and inflationary pressures.[^cite_note-IEA24-35] The report underscores the significant impact of [data centers](579730.md), [artificial intelligence](1164.md) and [cryptocurrency](36662188.md), projecting a potential doubling of electricity consumption to 1,000 TWh by 2026, which is on par with [Japan](15573.md)'s current usage.[^cite_note-IEA24-35] Notably, 85% of the additional demand is expected to originate from China and India, with India's demand alone predicted to grow over 6% annually until 2026, driven by economic expansion and increasing air conditioning use.[^cite_note-IEA24-35]

[Southeast Asia](28741.md)'s electricity demand is also forecasted to climb by 5% annually through 2026. In the [United States](3434750.md), a decrease was seen in 2023, but a moderate rise is anticipated in the coming years, largely fueled by data centers. The report also anticipates that a surge in electricity generation from low-emissions sources will meet the global demand growth over the next three years, with renewable energy sources predicted to surpass coal by early 2025.[^cite_note-IEA24-35]

### Alternative scenarios

The goal set in the [Paris Agreement](30242372.md) to [limit climate change](2119179.md) will be difficult to achieve.[^cite_note-36] Various scenarios for achieving the Paris Climate Agreement Goals have been developed, using IEA data but proposing transition to nearly 100% renewables by mid-century, along with steps such as reforestation. Nuclear power and carbon capture are excluded in these scenarios.[^cite_note-Teske-37] The researchers say the costs will be far less than the \$5 trillion per year governments currently spend subsidizing the fossil fuel industries responsible for climate change.[^cite_note-Teske-37]<sup>: ix </sup>

In the *+2.0 C (global warming) Scenario* total primary energy demand in 2040 can be 450 EJ = 10,755 Mtoe, or 400 EJ = 9560 Mtoe in the *+1.5 Scenario*, well below the current production. Renewable sources can increase their share to 300 EJ in the *+2.0 C Scenario* or 330 EJ in the *+1.5 Scenario* in 2040. In 2050 renewables can cover nearly all energy demand. Non-energy consumption will still include fossil fuels.[^cite_note-Teske-37]<sup>: xxvii Fig. 5 </sup>

Global electricity generation from renewable energy sources will reach 88% by 2040 and 100% by 2050 in the alternative scenarios. "New" renewables—mainly wind, solar and geothermal energy—will contribute 83% of the total electricity generated.[^cite_note-Teske-37]<sup>: xxiv </sup> The average annual investment required between 2015 and 2050, including costs for additional power plants to produce hydrogen and synthetic fuels and for plant replacement, will be around \$1.4 trillion.[^cite_note-Teske-37]<sup>: 182 </sup>

Shifts from domestic aviation to rail and from road to rail are needed. Passenger car use must decrease in the [OECD](33853117.md) countries (but increase in developing world regions) after 2020. The passenger car use decline will be partly compensated by strong increase in public transport rail and bus systems.[^cite_note-Teske-37]<sup>: xxii Fig.4 </sup>

CO<sub>2</sub> emission can reduce from 32 Gt in 2015 to 7 Gt (+2.0 Scenario) or 2.7 Gt (+1.5 Scenario) in 2040, and to zero in 2050.[^cite_note-Teske-37]<sup>: xxviii </sup>
