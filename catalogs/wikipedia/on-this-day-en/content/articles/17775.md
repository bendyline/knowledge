[Lebanon](17771.md) is a [parliamentary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_republic) [democratic republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_republic) within the overall framework of [confessionalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confessionalism_\(politics\)), a form of [consociationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consociationalism) in which the highest offices are proportionately reserved for representatives from certain religious communities. The [constitution of Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Lebanon) grants the people the right to change their government. However, from the mid-1970s until the parliamentary elections in 1992, the [Lebanese Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Civil_War) (1975–1990) precluded the exercise of political rights.

According to the constitution, direct elections must be held for the parliament every four years. However, after the parliamentary election in [2009](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Lebanese_general_election)[^cite_note-1] another election was not held until [2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Lebanese_general_election). The [Parliament](1618242.md) elects a [president](2032981.md) every six years to a single term. The president is not eligible for re-election. The last presidential election was in [2025](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022%E2%80%932025_Lebanese_presidential_election). The president and parliament choose the [prime minister](508949.md).

[Political parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_political_parties_in_Lebanon) may be formed, and many are organized along [sectarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sectarianism) lines. 2008 saw a new twist to Lebanese politics when the [Doha Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doha_Agreement_\(2008\)) set a new trend where the opposition is allowed a veto power in the [Council of Ministers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Ministers_of_Lebanon) and confirmed religious confessionalism in the distribution of political power.

&#x20;The [Maronite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronite) [Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic) and the [Druze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze) founded modern [Lebanon](17771.md) in the early eighteenth century, through the ruling and social system known as the "Maronite-Druze dualism" in [Mount Lebanon Mutasarrifate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Lebanon_Mutasarrifate).[^cite_note-2] Since the emergence of the post-1943 state and after the destruction of the Ottoman [Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate), national policy has been determined largely by a relatively restricted group of traditional regional and sectarian leaders.

The 1943 [National Pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Pact), an unwritten agreement that established the political foundations of modern Lebanon, [allocated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserved_political_positions) political power on an essentially confessional system based on the 1932 census. Seats in parliament were divided on a 6-to-5 ratio of [Christians](4796145.md) to [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Lebanon). In 1990, the ratio changed to half and half. Positions in the government bureaucracy are allocated on a similar basis.

The pact by custom allocated public offices along religious lines, with the top three positions in the ruling "troika" distributed as follows: the [president](2032981.md), a [Maronite Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronite_Christianity_in_Lebanon); the [speaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Speakers_of_the_Parliament_of_Lebanon) of the Parliament, a [Shi'a Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shi'a_Islam_in_Lebanon); and the [prime minister](508949.md), a [Sunni Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam_in_Lebanon).

Efforts to alter or abolish the confessional system of allocating power have been at the centre of Lebanese politics for decades. Those religious groups most favoured by the 1943 formula sought to preserve it, while those who saw themselves at a disadvantage sought either to revise it after updating key demographic data or to abolish it entirely. Many of the provisions of the national pact were codified in the 1989 [Taif Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taif_Agreement), perpetuating sectarianism as a key element of Lebanese political life.

Although moderated somewhat under Ta'if, the Constitution gives the president a strong and influential position. The president has the authority to promulgate laws passed by the Parliament, form the government to issue supplementary regulations to ensure the execution of laws, and to negotiate and ratify treaties.

The Parliament is elected by adult suffrage based on a system of majority or "winner-take-all" for the various confessional groups. The [majority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_majority) age for voting in elections is 21.[^cite_note-3] There has been a recent effort to switch to proportional representation which many argue will provide a more accurate assessment of the size of political groups and allow minorities to be heard. Most deputies do not represent political parties as they are known in the West, and rarely form Western-style groups in the assembly. Political blocs are usually based on confessional and local interests or on personal/family allegiance rather than on political affinities.

The parliament traditionally has played a significant role in financial affairs, since it has the responsibility for levying taxes and passing the budget. It exercises political control over the cabinet through formal questioning of ministers on policy issues and by requesting a confidence debate.

Lebanon's judicial system is based on the [Napoleonic Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Code). Juries are not used in trials. The Lebanese court system has three levels—courts of first instance, courts of appeal, and the court of cassation. There is a system of religious courts having jurisdiction over personal status matters within their own communities, e.g., rules on such matters as marriage, divorce, and inheritance. These courts operate alongside the civil legal system and apply sect-specific personal status laws to members of their respective religious communities. [^cite_note-4]

Lebanese political institutions often play a secondary role to highly confessionalized personality-based politics. Powerful families play an independent role in mobilizing votes for both local and parliamentary elections. A lively panoply [of domestic political parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_political_parties_in_Lebanon), some even predating independence, exists. The largest parties are all religiously aligned.

The [Free Patriotic Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Patriotic_Movement_\(Lebanon\)), The [Kataeb Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kataeb_Party_\(Lebanon\)), also known as the Phalange Party, the [National Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_National_Bloc), [National Liberal Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Liberal_Party_\(Lebanon\)), [Lebanese Forces](2113218.md) and the [Guardians of the Cedars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guardians_of_the_Cedars) (now outlawed) each have their own base among Christians.

[Amal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amal_Movement) and [Hezbollah](13919.md) are the main rivals for the organized [Shi'a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shi'a_Islam_in_Lebanon) vote. The PSP ([Progressive Socialist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_Socialist_Party)) is the leading [Druze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze_in_Lebanon) party. Shi'a and Druze parties command fierce loyalty to their respective leaderships. There is more factional infighting among many of the Christian parties.

Sunni parties have not been the standard vehicle for launching political candidates, and tend to focus across Lebanon's borders on issues that are important to the community at large. Lebanon's [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam_in_Lebanon) parties include [Hizb ut-Tahrir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hizb_ut-Tahrir), [Future Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_Movement), [Independent Nasserist Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Nasserist_Organization) (INO), the [Al-Tawhid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Unification_Movement), and [Ahbash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ahbash).

Besides the traditional confessional parties above, new secular parties have emerged, amongst which Sabaa and the Party of Lebanon[^cite_note-5] representing a new trend in Lebanese politics towards secularism and a truly democratic society. In addition to domestic parties, there are branches of pan-Arab secular parties ([Ba'ath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba'ath) parties, [socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist) and [communist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist) parties) that were active in the 1960s and throughout [the period of civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Civil_War).

There are differences both between and among Muslim and Christian parties regarding the role of religion in state affairs. There is a high degree of political activism among religious leaders across the sectarian spectrum. The interplay for position and power among the religious, political, and party leaders and groups produces a political tapestry of extraordinary complexity.

In the past, the system worked to produce a viable democracy. Events over the last decade and long-term demographic trends have upset the delicate [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Lebanon)–[Christian](4796145.md)–[Druze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze_in_Lebanon) balance and resulted in greater segregation across the social spectrum. Whether in political parties, places of residence, schools, media outlets, even workplaces, there is a lack of regular interaction across sectarian lines to facilitate the exchange of views and promote understanding. All factions have called for a reform of the political system.

Some Christians favor political and administrative decentralization of the government, with separate Muslim and Christian sectors operating within the framework of a confederation. Muslims, for the most part, prefer a unified, central government with an enhanced share of power commensurate with their larger share of the population. The reforms of the Ta'if agreement moved in this direction but have not been fully realized.

[Palestinian refugees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestinians_in_Lebanon), predominantly [Sunni Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Muslim), whose numbers are estimated at between 160,000 and 225,000, are not active on the domestic political scene.

In September 2004, the Lebanese Parliament voted 96–29 to amend the constitution to extend President [Émile Lahoud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89mile_Lahoud)'s six-year term, which was about to expire, by another three years. The move was supported by Syria, which maintained a large military presence in Lebanon.

Former prime minister [Rafic Hariri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafic_Hariri) was assassinated in February 2005.[^cite_note-6] Following the withdrawal of [Syrian troops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_of_Syria) in April 2005, Lebanon [held parliamentary elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_Lebanese_general_election) in four rounds, from 29 May to 19 June. The elections, the first for 33 years without the presence of Syrian military forces, were won by the Quadripartite alliance, which was part the [Rafik Hariri Martyr List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafik_Hariri_Martyr_List), a coalition of several parties and organizations newly opposed to Syrian domination of Lebanese politics.

In January 2015, the [Economist Intelligence Unit](15303116.md) released a report stating that Lebanon ranked second in the [Middle East](19323.md) and 98th out of 167 countries worldwide on the 2014 [Democracy Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy_Index). The index ranks countries according to election processes, pluralism, government functions, political participation, political cultures and fundamental freedoms.

From October 2019, there have been mass protests against the government, with participation ranging from hundreds of thousands to estimates as high as two million citizens, that mobilized to demand political and economic reform.[^cite_note-\:1-7] The October 2019 uprising in Lebanon reignited debate over the country’s political-sectarian system, institutionalized during the French Mandate period, such as through the 1943 National Pact.[^cite_note-\:0-8] The protests, marked by calls to dismantle this system, reflected decades of growing intersectional activism,[^cite_note-\:0-8] in which feminist groups played a key role.[^cite_note-\:1-7] Although protesters pursued multiple goals, one of the most unifying demands, that transcended sectarian and ideological divides, was the dismantling of entrenched corruption perpetrated by the political elite and their associates, which profit from cronyism prevalent in the country.[^cite_note-\:1-7] Demonstrators called for a more equitable and accountable government,[^cite_note-\:0-8] economic reforms to address the country’s deepening financial crisis, and debates on citizenship, census bureaucracy, and personal status laws.[^cite_note-\:1-7] However, the protests faced harsh repression from the Lebanese security forces, including mass arrests, the use of tear gas, and the shooting of demonstrators, along with other repressive measures.[^cite_note-\:1-7] In August 2020, [a large explosion in Beirut](64752725.md) killed at least 204 people and caused at least US\$3 billion in property damage. Following the explosion and protests against the government, the prime minister and his cabinet resigned.[^cite_note-9]

In May 2022, Lebanon [held its first election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Lebanese_general_election) since a painful economic crisis dragged it to the brink of becoming a [failed state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Failed_state). Lebanon's crisis has been so severe that more than 80 percent of the population is now considered poor by the [United Nations](31769.md). In the election, the Iran-backed Shia Muslim [Hezbollah](13919.md) movement and its allies lost their parliamentary majority. Hezbollah did not lose any of its seats, but its allies lost seats.\[^cite_note-[www.aljazeera.com-10](http://www.aljazeera.com-10/)][^cite_note-aljazeera.com-11][^cite_note-Hariri's_absence_leaves_Sunni_voter-12]

Hezbollah’s ally, President [Michel Aoun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Aoun)'s [Free Patriotic Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Patriotic_Movement), was no longer the biggest Christian party after the election. A rival Christian party, led by [Samir Geagea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samir_Geagea), with close ties to Saudi Arabia, the [Lebanese Forces](2113218.md) (LF), made gains. The Sunni [Future Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_Movement), led by former prime minister [Saad Hariri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saad_Hariri), did not participate in the election, leaving a political vacuum for other Sunni politicians to fill.\[^cite_note-[www.aljazeera.com-10](http://www.aljazeera.com-10/)][^cite_note-aljazeera.com-11][^cite_note-Hariri's_absence_leaves_Sunni_voter-12] In October 2024, leader of the Free Patriotic Movement, [Gebran Bassil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gebran_Bassil), announced that the party was no longer in alliance with [Hezbollah](13919.md).[^cite_note-13]

In November 2024, a [ceasefire deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Israel%E2%80%93Lebanon_ceasefire_agreement) was signed between Israel and the Lebanese armed group Hezbollah to end 13 months of conflict. According to the agreement, Hezbollah was given 60 days to end its armed presence in southern Lebanon and Israeli forces were obliged to withdraw from the area over the same period.[^cite_note-14] [The fall of Assad’s Baathist regime](78548066.md) in Syria was another blow to its Lebanese ally, Hezbollah, which was already weakened because of Israeli military actions.[^cite_note-15] The Syrian regime change in December 2024 was said to start a new chapter in Lebanese politics.[^cite_note-16] In January 2025, [Joseph Aoun](53467128.md), the Lebanese army commander, was elected Lebanese 14th [president](2032981.md) after a two-year vacancy.[^cite_note-17] In February 2025, Prime Minister [Nawaf Salam](21077090.md), former president of the [International Court of Justice](14918.md) (ICJ), formed a new government of 24 ministers after two-year caretaker cabinet.[^cite_note-18] On 26 February 2025, Lebanon's government of Nawaf Salam won a confidence vote in parliament.[^cite_note-19]

The [president](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Presidents_of_Lebanon) is elected by the [Parliament](1618242.md) for a six-year term. They cannot be reelected again until six years have passed from the end of their first term.[^cite_note-20] The [prime minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Prime_Ministers_of_Lebanon) and deputy prime minister are appointed by the president in consultation with the Parliament. The president is required to be [a Maronite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronite_Christianity_in_Lebanon), the prime minister [a Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam_in_Lebanon), and [the speaker](30862356.md) of the Parliament [a Shi'a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shi'a_Islam_in_Lebanon). See [list](78886120.md) of the ministers and their political affiliation for a list of ministers.

This confessional system is based on 1932 census data, which showed the Maronite Christians as having a substantial majority of the population. The Government of Lebanon continues to refuse to undertake a new census.

### President

Main article: [President of Lebanon](2032981.md)

Lebanon operates under a parliamentary system, where the president has a wide range of reserve powers but is largely symbolic. The president is not accountable to Parliament, unless for treason, and is elected by the Parliament with a two-thirds majority. The president has the sole power to appoint the prime minister, and may dismiss them at any point, without input from the Chamber of Deputies, which can force the president to resign.[^cite_note-Boell2014-21]

Following the end of the [Lebanese Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Civil_War), the president lost some powers to the [Council of Ministers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Ministers_of_Lebanon) through the [Taif Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taif_Agreement). Being the sole person who appoints it, however, they *de facto* still retain all, or most, of their pre-Taif powers.

## Legislative branch



The Lebanese parliament building at Place de l'Étoile in Beirut

Lebanon's [national legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_national_legislatures) is called the [Assembly of Representatives](1618242.md) (*Majlis al-Nuwab* in [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language)). Since the elections of 1992, the first since the reforms of the [Taif Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taif_Agreement) of 1989 removed the built-in majority previously enjoyed by Christians and distributed the seats equally between Christians and Muslims, the Parliament has had 128 seats. The term was four years, but has recently been extended to five.

Seats in the Parliament are confessionally distributed but elected by [universal suffrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_suffrage). Each religious community has an allotted number of seats in the Parliament. They do not represent only their co-religionists. All candidates in a particular constituency, regardless of religious affiliation, must receive a plurality of the total vote, which includes followers of all confessions. The system was designed to minimize inter-sectarian competition and maximize cross-confessional cooperation: candidates are opposed only by co-religionists, but must seek support from outside of their own faith in order to be elected.

The opposition [Qornet Shehwan Gathering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qornet_Shehwan_Gathering), a group opposed to the former pro-[Syrian](7515849.md) government, has claimed that constituency boundaries have been drawn to allow many [Shi'a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shi'a_Islam_in_Lebanon) Muslims to be elected from Shi'a-majority constituencies, where the [Hezbollah](13919.md) Party is strong, while allocating many Christian members to Muslim-majority constituencies, forcing Christian politicians to represent Muslim interests. Similar charges, but in reverse, were made against the [Chamoun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camille_Chamoun) administration in the 1950s.

## Judicial branch

Lebanon is a [civil law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_law_\(legal_system\)) country. Its judicial branch is composed of:

- Ordinary courts:&#x20;

  - One [Court of Cassation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Cassation) composed of nine chambers&#x20;

    [^cite_note-justice.gov.lb-22]

  - Courts of Appeal (in the centre of every [governorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governorates_of_Lebanon))&#x20;

    [^cite_note-justice.gov.lb-22]

  - Courts of First Instance&#x20;

    [^cite_note-justice.gov.lb-22]

- Special courts:&#x20;

  - The [Constitutional Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_Council_\(Lebanon\)) (called for in the [Taif Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taif_Agreement)) rules on constitutionality of laws

  - The [Supreme Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Council_\(Lebanon\)) hears charges against the president and prime minister as needed.

  - A system of military courts that also has jurisdiction over civilians for the crimes of espionage, treason, and other crimes that are considered to be security-related.

    [^cite_note-23]

## Political parties and elections

For other political parties, see [List of political parties in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_political_parties_in_Lebanon). An overview on elections and election results is included in [Elections in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_Lebanon).

Lebanon [has numerous political parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_political_parties_in_Lebanon), but they play a much less significant role in Lebanese politics than they do in most parliamentary democracies. Many of the "parties" are simply lists of candidates endorsed by a prominent national or local figure. Loose coalitions, usually organized locally, are formed for electoral purposes by negotiation among clan leaders and candidates representing various religious communities. Such coalitions usually exist only for the election, and rarely form a cohesive block in the [Parliament](1618242.md) after the election. No single party has ever won more than 12.5 percent of the seats in the Parliament. No coalition of parties has won more than 35 percent.

Especially outside of the major cities, elections tend to focus more on local than national issues. It is not unusual for a party to join an electoral ticket in one constituency while aligned with a rival party – even an ideologically opposite party – in another constituency.

It is not uncommon for election times to be accompanied by outbreaks of violence, especially in polling areas where there are people of conflicting political and religious backgrounds. Sectarianism is so ingrained into Lebanese politics and society that citizens supporting their political parties will kill or be killed defending them.

### Sustainable development goals

This section is an excerpt from [Sustainable Development Goals and Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_Development_Goals_and_Lebanon).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Sustainable_Development_Goals_and_Lebanon\&action=edit)]

[Sustainable Development Goals and Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_Development_Goals_and_Lebanon) explains major contributions launched in Lebanon towards the advancement of the [Sustainable Development Goals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_Development_Goals) SDGs and the 2030 agenda.

Lebanon adopted the Sustainable Development Goals in 2015. It presented its first Voluntary National Review (VNR) in 2018 at the [High Level Political Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-level_Political_Forum_on_Sustainable_Development) in New York. A national committee chaired by the Lebanese Prime Minister is leading the work on the SDGs in the country.[^cite_note-24] In 2019, Lebanon's overall performance in the SDG Index ranked 6th out of 21 countries in the Arab region.[^cite_note-25]

Multi-stakeholder forums were held by different UN agencies including the [UN](31769.md) Global Compact Network in Lebanon during the late 2010s for the advancement of Global Goals and their Impact on Businesses in Lebanon. The latest two were held in October 2018 and October 2019 under the title of connecting the global goals to Local Businesses.[^cite_note-Sustainable_Development_Goals_and_Lebanon_\:26-26]
