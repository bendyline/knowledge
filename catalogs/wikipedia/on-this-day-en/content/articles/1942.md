# Airline

For other uses, see [Airline (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_\(disambiguation\)).



A [Boeing 777](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_777) of [United Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Airlines), the largest airline in the world by fleet size.



Air trips per capita

An **airline** is a company that provides a regular service of [air transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_aviation) for [passengers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passenger) or [freight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freight) (cargo). Airlines use [aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft) to supply these services. Many passenger airlines also carry cargo in the belly of their aircraft, while dedicated [cargo airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cargo_airline) focus solely on freight transport. Generally, airlines are recognized with an [air operating certificate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_operating_certificate) or license issued by a governmental aviation body. These airlines may be scheduled or [charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_charter) operators.

Airline ownership has seen a shift from mostly personal ownership until the 1930s to government-ownership of major airlines from the 1940s to 1980s and back to large-scale [privatization](24661.md) following the mid-1980s.[^cite_note-\:5-1] Since the 1980s, there has been a trend of major airline [mergers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mergers) and the formation of [partnerships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partnership) or [alliances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_alliance) for [codeshare agreements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codeshare_agreement), in which they both offer and operate the same flight. The largest alliances are [Star Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Alliance), [SkyTeam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SkyTeam) and [Oneworld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oneworld). Airline alliances coordinate their passenger service programs (such as [lounges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airport_lounge) and [frequent-flyer programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequent-flyer_program)), offer special [interline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlining) tickets and often engage in extensive codesharing (sometimes systemwide).

### The first airlines

Further information: [Aviation in the pioneer era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_in_the_pioneer_era)

[DELAG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DELAG), *Deutsche Luftschiffahrts-Aktiengesellschaft*, was the world’s first commercial passenger airline.[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-AirshipsDELAG-3] It was founded on November 16, 1909,[^cite_note-AirshipsDELAG-3] and began services in June 1910,[^cite_note-4] providing a regular service until 1935.[^cite_note-AirshipsDELAG-3] DELAG operated airships manufactured by the [Zeppelin Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeppelin).[^cite_note-AirshipsDELAG-3]

The [Compagnie générale transaérienne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compagnie_g%C3%A9n%C3%A9rale_transa%C3%A9rienne) (CGT) was founded on October 10, 1909, making it the oldest airline company, which began cargo operations in April 1911 and began regular passenger operations in March 1913—weekly return [fixed-wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed-wing) flights.[^cite_note-5] CGT was the first of the companies that would eventually merge to become [Air France](14998012.md).[^cite_note-6]

A fixed-wing scheduled airline was started in the United States in January 1914. The [St. Petersburg–Tampa Airboat Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Petersburg%E2%80%93Tampa_Airboat_Line) was piloted by [Tony Jannus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Jannus) and flew from [St. Petersburg, Florida](84504.md), to [Tampa, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tampa,_Florida).[^cite_note-\:32-7][^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10]

### Europe

#### Beginnings

Further information: [Aviation in the interwar period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_in_the_interwar_period)



The [Handley Page W.8b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handley_Page_Type_W) was used by [Handley Page Transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handley_Page_Transport), an early British airline established in 1919.

The British airship, [HM Airship R34](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HM_Airship_R34) was the first aircraft of any type to carry passengers across the Atlantic Ocean when on 2 July 1919 it left [Scotland](26994.md), travelled to New York and returned.[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-Flight_10_jul-12]

[Aircraft Transport and Travel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_Transport_and_Travel) (AT\&T), formed by [George Holt Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Holt_Thomas) in 1916 as a fixed-wing airline; via a series of takeovers and mergers, this company is the earliest [predecessor company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Successor_company) of [British Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Airways).[^cite_note-Grant1996-13][^cite_note-Cook2023-14][^cite_note-15][^cite_note-\:02-16][^cite_note-\:4-17] Using a fleet of former military [Airco DH.4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airco_DH.4)A biplanes that had been modified to carry two passengers in the [fuselage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuselage), it operated relief flights between [Folkestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folkestone) and [Ghent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghent), Belgium. On July 15, 1919, the company flew a proving flight across the [English Channel](9230.md), despite a lack of support from the British government. Flown by Lt. H Shaw in an [Airco DH.9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airco_DH.9) between [RAF Hendon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAF_Hendon) and [Paris – Le Bourget Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_%E2%80%93_Le_Bourget_Airport), the flight took 2 hours and 30 minutes at £21 per passenger. On August 25, 1919, the company used [DH.16s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airco_DH.16) to pioneer a regular service from [Hounslow Heath Aerodrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hounslow_Heath_Aerodrome) to Paris's [Le Bourget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris%E2%80%93Le_Bourget_Airport), the first daily international service in the world.[^cite_note-\:52-18][^cite_note-Bluffield2014-19] The airline soon gained a reputation for reliability, despite problems with bad weather, and began to attract European competition. In November 1919, it won the first British civil [airmail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airmail) contract. Six [Royal Air Force](25679.md) [Airco DH.9A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airco_DH.9A) aircraft were lent to the company, to operate the airmail service between [Hawkinge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawkinge) and [Cologne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne). In 1920, they were returned to the Royal Air Force.[^cite_note-20]

Other British competitors were quick to follow – [Handley Page Transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handley_Page_Transport) was established in 1919 and used the company's converted [wartime](4764461.md) [Type O/400](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handley_Page_Type_O) [bombers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bomber) with a capacity for 12 passengers,[^cite_note-21] to run a [London](17867.md)-Paris passenger service.[^cite_note-22]

[Société des lignes Latécoère](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%C3%A9ropostale_\(aviation\)), a predecessor of Air France,[^cite_note-AAP1948-23] later known as Aéropostale, started its first [airmail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airmail) service in late 1924 to Spain.[^cite_note-Johnson2014-24][^cite_note-AFKLM-25] The [Société Générale des Transports Aériens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soci%C3%A9t%C3%A9_G%C3%A9n%C3%A9rale_des_Transports_A%C3%A9riens) was created in late 1919, by the [Farman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farman) brothers. It began a weekly service between Paris and Brussels on 22 March 1919, the world's first international commercial aviation service.[^cite_note-\:32-7][^cite_note-\:52-18] The [Farman F.60 Goliath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farman_F.60_Goliath) plane flew scheduled services from [Toussus-le-Noble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toussus-le-Noble) to [Kenley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenley), near [Croydon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croydon), England. Another early French airline was the [Compagnie des Messageries Aériennes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compagnie_des_Messageries_A%C3%A9riennes), established in 1919 by [Louis-Charles Breguet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis-Charles_Breguet), offering a mail and freight service between [Le Bourget Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Bourget_Airport), Paris and [Lesquin Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesquin_Airport), [Lille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lille).[^cite_note-26]



&#x20;[Junkers F.13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junkers_F.13) *D-190* of [Junkers Luftverkehr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junkers_Luftverkehr)

The first German airline to use heavier than air aircraft was [Deutsche Luft-Reederei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Luft-Reederei) (DLR) established in 1917 which started operating in February 1919. In its first year, the DLR operated regularly scheduled flights on routes with a combined length of nearly 1000 miles. By 1921 the DLR network was more than 3000 km (1865 miles) long, and included destinations in the Netherlands, Scandinavia and the Baltic Republics. Another important German airline was [Junkers Luftverkehr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junkers_Luftverkehr), which began operations in 1921. It was a division of the aircraft manufacturer [Junkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junkers), which became a separate company in 1924. It operated joint-venture airlines in Austria, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Hungary, Latvia, Norway, Poland, Sweden and Switzerland.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28]

The [Dutch](21148.md) airline [KLM](16882.md) made its first flight in 1920, and is the oldest airline still using the original name.[^cite_note-\:1-29][^cite_note-\:2-30] Established by aviator [Albert Plesman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Plesman),[^cite_note-31] it was immediately awarded a "Royal" predicate from [Queen Wilhelmina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Wilhelmina).[^cite_note-32] Its first flight was from [Croydon Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croydon_Airport), [London](17867.md) to [Amsterdam](844.md), using a leased [DH-16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airco_DH.16) from [Aircraft Transport and Travel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_Transport_and_Travel), carrying two British journalists and a number of newspapers. In 1921, KLM started scheduled services.[^cite_note-33]

In [Finland](10577.md), the charter establishing Aero O/Y (now [Finnair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnair)) was signed in the city of [Helsinki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsinki) on 12 September 1923. [Junkers F.13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junkers_F.13) D-335 became the first aircraft of the company, when Aero took delivery of it on 14 March 1924. The first flight was between Helsinki and [Tallinn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallinn), capital of [Estonia](28222445.md), and it took place on 20 March 1924, one week later.[^cite_note-34]

In the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union), the Chief Administration of the Civil Air Fleet was established in 1921. One of its first acts was to help found Deutsch-Russische Luftverkehrs A.G. (Deruluft), a German-Russian joint venture to provide air transport from Russia to the West. Domestic air service began around the same time, when Dobrolyot started operations on 15 July 1923 between Moscow and Nizhni Novgorod. Since 1932 all operations had been carried under the name [Aeroflot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeroflot).[^cite_note-35]

Early European airlines tended to favor comfort – the passenger cabins were often spacious with luxurious interiors – over speed and efficiency. The relatively basic navigational capabilities of pilots at the time also meant that delays due to the weather were commonplace.[^cite_note-36]

#### Rationalization



The [Imperial Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Airways) Empire Terminal, [Victoria, London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria,_London). Trains ran from here to [flying boats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_boats) in [Southampton](7920751.md), and to [Croydon Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croydon_Airport).

By the early 1920s, small airlines were struggling to compete, and there was a movement towards increased rationalization and consolidation. In 1924, [Imperial Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Airways) was formed from the merger of [Instone Air Line Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instone_Air_Line), [British Marine Air Navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Marine_Air_Navigation), [Daimler Airway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daimler_Airway) and [Handley Page Transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handley_Page_Transport), to allow British airlines to compete with stiff competition from French and German airlines that were enjoying heavy government subsidies. The airline was a pioneer in surveying and opening up air routes across the world to serve far-flung parts of the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire) and to enhance trade and integration.[^cite_note-37]

The first new airliner ordered by Imperial Airways, was the [Handley Page W8f](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handley_Page_W8f) *City of Washington*, delivered on 3 November 1924.[^cite_note-38] In the first year of operation the company carried 11,395 passengers and 212,380 letters. In April 1925, the film *[The Lost World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lost_World_\(1925_film\))* became the first film to be screened for passengers on a scheduled airliner flight when it was shown on the London-Paris route.

Two French airlines also merged to form [Air Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Union) on 1 January 1923. This later merged with four other French airlines to become [Air France](14998012.md), the country's flagship carrier to this day, on 17 May 1933.[^cite_note-39]

On 6 January 1926 two German airlines, Deutsche Aero Lloyd (DAL) and [Junkers Luftverkehr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junkers_Luftverkehr) merged to form Deutsche Luft Hansa Aktiengesellschaft. In 1933 The airline simplified its name to [Deutsche Lufthansa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Lufthansa).[^cite_note-40] Lufthansa, due to the [Junkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Junkers) heritage and unlike most other airlines at the time, became a major investor in airlines outside of Europe, providing capital to [Varig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varig) and Avianca. Post-war transport aircraft designed by Germans [Junkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junkers), [Dornier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dornier_GmbH), and Dutch [Anthony Fokker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Fokker) were among the most successful in the world, at the time, due to their cantilevered wings eliminating the need for external struts or braces.[^cite_note-41]

#### Expansion

In 1926, [Alan Cobham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Cobham) surveyed a flight route from the UK to [Cape Town](6653.md), [South Africa](17416221.md), following this up with another proving flight to [Melbourne](17306237.md), [Australia](4689264.md). Other routes to [British India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_India) and the [Far East](11672.md) were also charted and demonstrated at this time. Regular services to [Cairo](6293.md) and [Basra](57580.md) began in 1927 and were extended to [Karachi](17123.md) in 1929. The London-Australia service was inaugurated in 1932 with the [Handley Page HP 42](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handley_Page_HP_42) airliners. Further services were opened up to [Calcutta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcutta), [Rangoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rangoon), [Singapore](27318.md), [Brisbane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brisbane) and [Hong Kong](13404.md) passengers departed London on 14 March 1936 following the establishment of a branch from Penang to Hong Kong.[^cite_note-42]



April 1935 map showing [Imperial Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Airways)' routes from the [UK](31717.md) to [Australia](4689264.md) and [South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_of_South_Africa)

France began an air mail service to [Morocco](19291.md) in 1919 that was bought out in 1927, renamed [Aéropostale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%C3%A9ropostale_\(aviation\)), and injected with capital to become a major international carrier. In 1933, Aéropostale went [bankrupt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bankrupt), was nationalized and merged into [Air France](14998012.md).[^cite_note-43]

Although Germany lacked colonies, it also began expanding its services globally. In 1931, the airship [Graf Zeppelin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LZ_127_Graf_Zeppelin) began offering regular scheduled passenger service between Germany and South America, usually every two weeks, which continued until 1937.[^cite_note-44] In 1936, the airship [Hindenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LZ_129_Hindenburg) entered passenger service and successfully crossed the Atlantic 36 times before crashing at Lakehurst, New Jersey, on 6 May 1937.[^cite_note-45] In 1938, a weekly air service from Berlin to [Kabul](16826.md), [Afghanistan](737.md), started operating.[^cite_note-46]

From February 1934 until World War II began in 1939, [Deutsche Lufthansa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Luft_Hansa) operated an airmail service from [Stuttgart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuttgart), [Germany](11867.md) via [Spain](26667.md), the [Canary Islands](5717.md) and West Africa to [Natal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natal,_Rio_Grande_do_Norte) in [Brazil](3383.md). This was the first time an airline flew across an ocean.[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-Graue\&Duggan-48]

By the end of the 1930s [Aeroflot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeroflot) had become the world's largest airline, employing more than 4,000 pilots and 60,000 other service personnel and operating around 3,000 aircraft (of which 75% were considered obsolete by its own standards). During the Soviet era Aeroflot was synonymous with Russian civil aviation, as it was the only air carrier. It became the first airline in the world to operate sustained regular jet services on 15 September 1956 with the [Tupolev Tu-104](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupolev_Tu-104).[^cite_note-49]

#### Deregulation

[Deregulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deregulation) of the [European Union](9317.md) airspace in the early 1990s has had substantial effect on the structure of the industry there. The shift towards 'budget' airlines on shorter routes has been significant. Airlines such as [EasyJet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EasyJet) and [Ryanair](262878.md) have often grown at the expense of the traditional national airlines.

There has also been a trend for these national airlines themselves to be privatized such as has occurred for [Aer Lingus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aer_Lingus) and [British Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Airways). Other national airlines, including Italy's [Alitalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alitalia), suffered – particularly with the rapid increase of oil prices in early 2008.[^cite_note-50]

### United States

Main article: [Air transportation in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_transportation_in_the_United_States)

#### Early development



[TWA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TWA) [Douglas DC-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_DC-3) in 1940. The DC-3, often regarded as one of the most influential aircraft in the history of commercial aviation, revolutionized air travel.

[Tony Jannus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Jannus) conducted the United States' first scheduled commercial airline flight on January 1, 1914, for the [St. Petersburg-Tampa Airboat Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Petersburg-Tampa_Airboat_Line).[^cite_note-Society-51] The 23-minute flight traveled between [St. Petersburg, Florida](84504.md) and [Tampa, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tampa,_Florida), passing some 50 feet (15 m) above Tampa Bay in Jannus' [Benoist XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benoist_XIV) wood and muslin biplane flying boat. His passenger was a former mayor of St. Petersburg, who paid \$400 for the privilege of sitting on a wooden bench in the open cockpit. The Airboat line operated for about four months, carrying more than 1,200 passengers who paid \$5 each.[^cite_note-52] [Chalk's International Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalk's_International_Airlines) claimed to have begun a service between Miami and Bimini in the [Bahamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahamas) in February 1919. Based in [Ft. Lauderdale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ft._Lauderdale), Chalk's claimed to be the oldest continuously operating airline in the United States until its closure in 2008.[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55]

Following [World War I](4764461.md), the United States found itself swamped with aviators. Many decided to take their war-surplus aircraft on barnstorming campaigns, performing aerobatic maneuvers to woo crowds. In 1918, the [United States Post Office Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Post_Office_Department) won the financial backing of [Congress](31756.md) to begin experimenting with [air mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_mail) service, initially using [Curtiss Jenny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtiss_Jenny)[^cite_note-56] aircraft that had been procured by the [United States Army Air Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Air_Service). Private operators were the first to fly the mail but due to numerous accidents the US Army was tasked with mail delivery. During the Army's involvement they proved to be too unreliable and lost their air mail duties.[^cite_note-57] By the mid-1920s, the Post Office had developed its own air mail network, based on a transcontinental backbone between New York City and [San Francisco](49728.md).[^cite_note-58] To supplement this service, they offered twelve contracts for spur routes to independent bidders. Some of the carriers that won these routes would, through time and mergers, evolve into [Pan Am](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_Am), [Delta Air Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Air_Lines), [Braniff Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braniff_Airways), [American Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Airlines), [United Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Airlines) (originally a division of [Boeing](18933266.md)), [Trans World Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans_World_Airlines), [Northwest Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Airlines), and [Eastern Air Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Air_Lines).

Service during the early 1920s was sporadic: most airlines at the time were focused on [carrying bags of mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airmail#History). In 1925, however, the [Ford Motor Company](30433662.md) bought out the [Stout Aircraft Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stout_Aircraft_Company) and began construction of the all-metal [Ford Trimotor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Trimotor), which became the first successful American airliner. With a 12-passenger capacity, the Trimotor made passenger service potentially profitable.[^cite_note-59] Air service was seen as a supplement to [rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport) service in the American transportation network.

At the same time, [Juan Trippe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Trippe) began a crusade to create an air network that would link America to the world, and he achieved this goal through his airline, [Pan Am](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_Am), with a fleet of flying boats that linked [Los Angeles](18110.md) to [Shanghai](27643.md) and [Boston](24437894.md) to [London](17867.md). Pan Am and Northwest Airways (which began flights to Canada in the 1920s) were the only U.S. airlines to go international before the 1940s.

With the introduction of the [Boeing 247](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_247) and [Douglas DC-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_DC-3) in the 1930s, the U.S. airline industry was generally profitable, even during the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression). This trend continued until the beginning of [World War II](32927.md).[^cite_note-60]

#### Since 1945

Further information: [Post-war aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-war_aviation)



[Boeing 377](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_377) of [American Export Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Export_Airlines), the first airline to offer landplane flights across the North Atlantic in October 1945.

[^cite_note-61]

World War II, like World War I, brought new life to the airline industry. Many airlines in the Allied countries were flush from lease contracts to the military, and foresaw a future explosive demand for civil air transport, for both passengers and cargo. They were eager to invest in the newly emerging flagships of air travel such as the [Boeing Stratocruiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_Stratocruiser), [Lockheed Constellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Constellation), and [Douglas DC-6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_DC-6). Most of these new aircraft were based on American bombers such as the [B-29](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B-29), which had spearheaded research into new technologies such as [pressurization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressurization). Most offered increased efficiency from both added speed and greater payload.[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63]

In the 1950s, the [De Havilland Comet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Havilland_Comet), [Boeing 707](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_707), [Douglas DC-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_DC-8), and [Sud Aviation Caravelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sud_Aviation_Caravelle) became the first flagships of the [Jet Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_Age) in the West, while the Eastern bloc had [Tupolev Tu-104](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupolev_Tu-104) and [Tupolev Tu-124](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupolev_Tu-124) in the fleets of state-owned carriers such as Czechoslovak [ČSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%8CSA), Soviet [Aeroflot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeroflot) and East-German [Interflug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interflug). The [Vickers Viscount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vickers_Viscount) and [Lockheed L-188 Electra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_L-188_Electra) inaugurated turboprop transport.

On 4 October 1958, [British Overseas Airways Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Overseas_Airways_Corporation) started [transatlantic flights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transatlantic_flight) between [London Heathrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Heathrow) and [New York Idlewild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York-Idlewild_Airport) with a Comet 4, and [Pan Am](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_Am) followed on 26 October with a Boeing 707 service between New York and Paris.[^cite_note-64]

The next big boost for the airlines would come in the 1970s, when the [Boeing 747](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_747), [McDonnell Douglas DC-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonnell_Douglas_DC-10), and [Lockheed L-1011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_L-1011) inaugurated [widebody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Widebody) ("jumbo jet") service, which is still the standard in international travel.[^cite_note-65] The [Tupolev Tu-144](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupolev_Tu-144) and its Western counterpart, [Concorde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concorde), made supersonic travel a reality.[^cite_note-66] Concorde first flew in 1969 and operated through 2003. In 1972, [Airbus](26220236.md) began producing Europe's most commercially successful line of airliners to date. The added efficiencies for these aircraft were often not in speed, but in passenger capacity, payload, and range. Airbus also features modern electronic cockpits that were common across their aircraft to enable pilots to fly multiple models with minimal cross-training.[^cite_note-67]

#### Deregulation



[Pan Am](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_Am) [Boeing 747](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_747) *Clipper Neptune's Car* in 1985. The deregulation of the American airline industry increased the financial troubles of the airline which ultimately filed for bankruptcy in December 1991.

[^cite_note-68]

The 1978 U.S. [airline industry deregulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_deregulation_act) lowered federally controlled barriers for new airlines just as a downturn in the nation's economy occurred. New start-ups entered during the downturn, during which time they found aircraft and funding, contracted hangar and maintenance services, trained new employees, and recruited laid-off staff from other airlines.

Major airlines dominated their routes through aggressive pricing and additional capacity offerings, often swamping new start-ups. In the place of high barriers to entry imposed by regulation, the major airlines implemented an equally high barrier called [loss leader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loss_leader) pricing.[^cite_note-Andrew_R._Thomas-69] In this strategy an already established and dominant airline stomps out its competition by lowering airfares on specific routes, below the cost of operating on it, choking out any chance a start-up airline may have. The industry side effect is an overall drop in revenue and service quality.[^cite_note-70] Since deregulation in 1978 the average domestic ticket price has dropped by 40%.[^cite_note-71] So has airline employee pay. After incurring massive losses, the [airlines of the USA relied upon Chapter 11 bankruptcy proceedings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_airline_bankruptcies_in_the_United_States) to continue doing business.[^cite_note-72] [America West Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/America_West_Airlines) (which has since merged with US Airways) remained a significant survivor from this new entrant era, as dozens, even hundreds, have gone under.

In many ways, the biggest winner in the deregulated environment was the air passenger. Although not exclusively attributable to deregulation, indeed the U.S. witnessed an explosive growth in demand for air travel. Many millions who had never or rarely flown before became regular fliers, even joining [frequent flyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequent_flyer) loyalty programs and receiving free flights and other benefits from their flying. New services and higher frequencies meant that business fliers could fly to another city, do business, and return the same day, from almost any point in the country. Air travel's advantages put long-distance intercity [railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railroad) travel and bus lines under pressure, with most of the latter having withered away, whilst the former is still protected under [nationalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalization) through the continuing existence of [Amtrak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amtrak).

By the 1980s, almost half of the total flying in the world took place in the U.S., and today the domestic industry operates over 10,000 daily departures nationwide.

Toward the end of the century, a new style of [low cost airline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_cost_airline) emerged, offering a no-frills product at a lower price. [Southwest Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwest_Airlines), [JetBlue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JetBlue), [AirTran Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AirTran_Airways), [Skybus Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skybus_Airlines) and other low-cost carriers began to represent a serious challenge to the so-called "legacy airlines", as did their low-cost counterparts in many other countries.[^cite_note-73] Their commercial viability represented a serious competitive threat to the legacy carriers. However, of these, [ATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATA_Airlines) and Skybus have since ceased operations.

US airlines have been reincorporated and [spun off](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Off_spin) by newly created and internally led management companies, and becoming operating units and subsidiaries with limited financially decisive control. Among some of these [holding companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holding_companies) and [parent companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parent_companies) [include](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_airline_holding_companies) the [UAL Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UAL_Corporation), along with the [AMR Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AMR_Corporation). Less recognized are the [private-equity firms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private-equity_firm) which often seize managerial, financial, and [board of directors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_of_directors) control of distressed airline companies by temporarily investing large sums of [capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equity_\(finance\)) in air carriers, to rescheme an airlines assets into a profitable organization or [liquidating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquidating) an air carrier of their profitable and worthwhile routes and business operations.

Thus the last 50 years of the airline industry have varied from reasonably profitable, to devastatingly depressed. As the first major market to deregulate the industry in 1978, U.S. airlines have experienced more turbulence than almost any other country or region. In fact, no U.S. [legacy carrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legacy_carrier) survived bankruptcy-free. Among the outspoken critics of deregulation, former CEO of American Airlines, [Robert Crandall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Crandall) has publicly stated: "Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection filing shows airline industry deregulation was a mistake."[^cite_note-74]

#### Bailout

Congress passed the Air Transportation Safety and System Stabilization Act (P.L. 107–42) in response to a severe liquidity crisis facing the already-troubled airline industry in the aftermath of the [September 11 attacks](5058690.md). Through the [ATSB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Transportation_Stabilization_Board) Congress sought to provide cash infusions to carriers for both the cost of the four-day federal shutdown of the airlines and the incremental losses incurred through December 31, 2001, as a result of the terrorist attacks. This resulted in the first government bailout of the 21st century.[^cite_note-75] Between 2000 and 2005 US airlines lost \$30 billion with wage cuts of over \$15 billion and 100,000 employees laid off.[^cite_note-Bamber,_G.J.,_Gittell,_J.H.,_Kochan,_T.A._&_von_Nordenflytch,_A._2009-76]

In recognition of the essential national economic role of a healthy aviation system, Congress authorized partial compensation of up to \$5 billion in cash subject to review by the [U.S. Department of Transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Department_of_Transportation) and up to \$10 billion in loan guarantees subject to review by a newly created [Air Transportation Stabilization Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Transportation_Stabilization_Board) (ATSB). The applications to DOT for reimbursements were subjected to rigorous multi-year reviews not only by DOT program personnel but also by the [Government Accountability Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Accountability_Office)[^cite_note-77] and the DOT Inspector General.[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-79]

Ultimately, the federal government provided \$4.6 billion in one-time, subject-to-income-tax cash payments to 427 U.S. air carriers, with no provision for repayment, essentially a gift from the taxpayers. (Passenger carriers operating scheduled service received approximately \$4 billion, subject to tax.)[^cite_note-80] In addition, the ATSB approved loan guarantees to six airlines totaling approximately \$1.6 billion. Data from the [U.S. Treasury Department](53667.md) show that the government recouped the \$1.6 billion and a profit of \$339 million from the fees, interest and purchase of discounted airline stock associated with loan guarantees.[^cite_note-81]

As of May 2018 the four largest [major carriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_airlines_of_the_United_States) controlled 70% of the U.S. passenger market.[^cite_note-AvWeek14May2018-82]

### Asia



1935 Timetable of [Tata Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tata_Airlines), founded in 1932

Although [Philippine Airlines](296889.md) (PAL) was officially founded on February 26, 1941, its license to operate as an airliner was derived from merged Philippine Aerial Taxi Company (PATCO) established by mining magnate [Emmanuel N. Bachrach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmanuel_N._Bachrach?action=edit\&redlink=1) on 3 December 1930, making it Asia's oldest scheduled carrier still in operation.[^cite_note-83] Commercial air service commenced three weeks later from [Manila](184334.md) to [Baguio](162480.md), making it Asia's first airline route. Bachrach's death in 1937 paved the way for its eventual merger with Philippine Airlines in March 1941 and made it Asia's oldest airline. It is also the oldest airline in Asia still operating under its current name.[^cite_note-PAL-84] Bachrach's majority share in PATCO was bought by beer magnate Andres R. Soriano in 1939 upon the advice of General [Douglas MacArthur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_MacArthur) and later merged with newly formed Philippine Airlines with PAL as the surviving entity. Soriano has controlling interest in both airlines before the merger. PAL restarted service on 15 March 1941, with a single [Beech Model 18](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beech_Model_18) NPC-54 aircraft, which started its daily services between Manila (from [Nielson Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nielson_Field)) and Baguio, later to expand with larger aircraft such as the DC-3 and Vickers Viscount.



[Nakajima AT-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nakajima_Ki-34) of [Japan Air Transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Air_Transport), 1937

In Japan, [Japan Air Transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Air_Transport) was established in 1928 as the national flag carrier. Upon the completion of [Haneda Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haneda_Airport) in 1931, it became the airline's hub. The airline initially operated domestic routes such as [Tokyo](30057.md)–[Osaka](52230.md) and Osaka–[Fukuoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukuoka). In September 1929, it opened its first overseas route, which connected Fukuoka to [Dalian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalian) in the [Kwantung Leased Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwantung_Leased_Territory) via [Seoul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keij%C5%8D) and [Pyongyang](47948.md) in [Japanese Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Korea). After Japan established the puppet state of [Manchukuo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchukuo), the airline opened routes to major cities within this territory. The company was reorganized as [Japan Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Japanese_Airways) in 1938. During the Second World War, it operated routes to various Japanese-occupied territories and [Thailand](30128.md). The company was dissolved immediately after the war, as civil aviation was prohibited by the [Allied Occupation Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Japan). Civil aviation in Japan did not resume until the founding of [Japan Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Airlines) in 1951.[^cite_note-85]

[Cathay Pacific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathay_Pacific) was one of the first airlines to be launched among the other Asian countries in 1946.[^cite_note-86] The license to operate as an airliner was granted by the federal government body after reviewing the necessity at the national assembly. The [Hanjin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanjin) occupies the largest ownership of Korean Air as well as few low-budget airlines as of now. Korean Air is one of the four founders of [SkyTeam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SkyTeam), which was established in 2000. [Asiana Airlines](331239.md), launched in 1988, joined [Star Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Alliance) in 2003. Korean Air and Asiana Airlines comprise one of the largest combined airline miles and number of passenger served at the regional market of Asian airline industry

[India](14533.md) was also one of the first countries to embrace civil aviation.[^cite_note-87] One of the first Asian airline companies was [Air India](26457880.md), which was founded as [Tata Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tata_Airlines) in 1932, a division of Tata Sons Ltd. (now [Tata Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tata_Group)). The airline was founded by India's leading industrialist, [JRD Tata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JRD_Tata). On 15 October 1932, J. R. D. Tata himself flew a single engined [De Havilland Puss Moth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Havilland_Puss_Moth) carrying air mail (postal mail of [Imperial Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Airways)) from [Karachi](17123.md) to [Bombay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombay) via [Ahmedabad](206337.md). The aircraft continued to [Madras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madras) via Bellary piloted by [Royal Air Force](25679.md) pilot [Nevill Vintcent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevill_Vintcent). Tata Airlines was also one of the world's first major airlines which began its operations without any support from the Government.[^cite_note-88]

With the outbreak of World War II, the airline presence in Asia came to a relative halt, with many new flag carriers donating their aircraft for military aid and other uses. Following the end of the war in 1945, regular commercial service was restored in India and Tata Airlines became a public limited company on 29 July 1946, under the name Air India. After the [independence of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_of_India), 49% of the airline was acquired by the [Government of India](553883.md). In return, the airline was granted status to operate international services from India as the designated flag carrier under the name [Air India International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_India_International).[^cite_note-89]

On 31 July 1946, a chartered Philippine Airlines (PAL) [DC-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DC-4) ferried 40 American servicemen to [Oakland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oakland), [California](5407.md), from Nielson Airport in [Makati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makati) with stops in [Guam](11974.md), [Wake Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wake_Island), [Johnston Atoll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnston_Atoll) and [Honolulu](13887.md), [Hawaii](13270.md), making PAL the first Asian airline to cross the [Pacific Ocean](23070.md). A regular service between [Manila](184334.md) and [San Francisco](49728.md) was started in December. It was during this year that the airline was designated as the flag carrier of Philippines.[^cite_note-90]

During the era of [decolonization](302084.md), newly born Asian countries started to embrace air transport. Among the first Asian carriers during the era were [Cathay Pacific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathay_Pacific) of [Hong Kong](13404.md) (founded in September 1946), [Orient Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orient_Airways) (later [Pakistan International Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_International_Airlines); founded in October 1946), [Air Ceylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Ceylon) (later [SriLankan Airlines](483603.md); founded in 1947), [Malayan Airways Limited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malayan_Airways_Limited) in 1947 (later [Singapore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore_Airlines) and [Malaysia Airlines](153162.md)), [El Al](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Al) in [Israel](9282173.md) in 1948, [Garuda Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garuda_Indonesia) in 1949, [Thai Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thai_Airways) in 1960, and [Korean National Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_National_Airlines) in 1947.

### National



[Union Jack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Jack) tails of [British Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Airways), UK's [flag carrier](465816.md)

Many countries have [national airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_airline) that the government owns and operates. Fully private airlines are subject to much government regulation for economic, political, and safety concerns. For instance, governments often intervene to halt airline labor actions to protect the free flow of people, communications, and goods between different regions without compromising safety.

The United States, Australia, and to a lesser extent Brazil, Mexico, India, the United Kingdom, and Japan have "deregulated" their airlines. In the past, these governments dictated airfares, route networks, and other operational requirements for each airline. Since deregulation, airlines have been largely free to negotiate their own operating arrangements with different airports, enter and exit routes easily, and to levy airfares and supply flights according to market demand. The entry barriers for new airlines are lower in a deregulated market, and so the U.S. has seen hundreds of airlines start up (sometimes for only a brief operating period). This has produced far greater competition than before deregulation in most markets. The added competition, together with pricing freedom, means that new entrants often take market share with highly reduced rates that, to a limited degree, full service airlines must match. This is a major constraint on profitability for established carriers, which tend to have a higher cost base.

As a result, profitability in a deregulated market is uneven for most airlines. These forces have caused some major airlines to go out of business, in addition to most of the poorly established new entrants.

In the United States, the airline industry is dominated by four large firms. Because of industry consolidation, after fuel prices dropped considerably in 2015, very little of the savings were passed on to consumers.[^cite_note-94]

### International



[International Civil Aviation Organization](14985.md) headquarters in [Montreal](7954681.md)

Groups such as the [International Civil Aviation Organization](14985.md) establish worldwide standards for safety and other vital concerns. Most international air traffic is regulated by bilateral agreements between countries, which designate specific carriers to operate on specific routes. The model of such an agreement was the [Bermuda Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda_Agreement) between the US and UK following World War II, which designated airports to be used for transatlantic flights and gave each government the authority to nominate carriers to operate routes.

Bilateral agreements are based on the "[freedoms of the air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedoms_of_the_air)", a group of generalized traffic rights ranging from the freedom to overfly a country to the freedom to provide domestic flights within a country (a very rarely granted right known as [cabotage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabotage)). Most agreements permit airlines to fly from their home country to designated airports in the other country: some also extend the freedom to provide continuing service to a third country, or to another destination in the other country while carrying passengers from overseas.

In the 1990s, "[open skies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_skies)" agreements became more common. These agreements take many of these regulatory powers from state governments and open up international routes to further competition. Open skies agreements have met some criticism, particularly within the European Union, whose airlines would be at a comparative disadvantage with the United States' because of [cabotage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabotage) restrictions.

### Costs



An [Airbus A340](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A340)-600 of [Virgin Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_Atlantic). In October 2008, Virgin Atlantic offered to combine its operations with [BMI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Midland_International) in an effort to reduce operating costs.

[^cite_note-98]

Airlines have substantial fixed and operating costs to establish and maintain air services: labor, fuel, airplanes, engines, spares and parts, IT services and networks, airport equipment, airport handling services, booking commissions, advertising, catering, training, [aviation insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_insurance) and other costs. Thus all but a small percentage of the income from ticket sales is paid out to a wide variety of external providers or internal cost centers.

Moreover, the industry is structured so that airlines often act as tax collectors. Airline fuel is untaxed because of a series of treaties existing between countries. Ticket prices include a number of fees, taxes and surcharges beyond the control of airlines. Airlines are also responsible for enforcing government regulations. If airlines carry passengers without proper documentation on an international flight, they are responsible for returning them back to the original country.

Analysis of the 1992–1996 period shows that every player in the air transport chain is far more profitable than the airlines, who collect and pass through fees and revenues to them from ticket sales. While airlines as a whole earned 6% return on capital employed (2–3.5% less than the cost of capital), airports earned 10%, catering companies 10–13%, handling companies 11–14%, aircraft lessors 15%, aircraft manufacturers 16%, and global distribution companies more than 30%.[^cite_note-99]

There has been continuing cost competition from [low cost airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_cost_airline). Many companies emulate [Southwest Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwest_Airlines) in various respects.[^cite_note-100] The lines between full-service and low-cost airlines have become blurred – e.g., with most "full service" airlines introducing baggage check fees despite Southwest not doing so.

Many airlines in the U.S. and elsewhere have experienced business difficulty. U.S. airlines that have declared [Chapter 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapter_11) bankruptcy since 1990 have included [American Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Airlines), [Continental Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Airlines) (twice), [Delta Air Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Air_Lines), [Northwest Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Airlines), [Pan Am](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_Am), [United Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Airlines) and [US Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Airways) (twice).

Where an airline has established an engineering base at an airport, then there may be considerable economic advantages in using that same airport as a preferred focus (or "hub") for its scheduled flights.

[Fuel hedging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_hedging) is a [contractual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contractual) tool used by transportation companies like airlines to reduce their exposure to volatile and potentially rising fuel costs. Several low-cost carriers such as Southwest Airlines adopt this practice. Southwest is credited with maintaining strong business profits between 1999 and the early 2000s due to its fuel hedging policy. Many other airlines are replicating Southwest's hedging policy to control their fuel costs.[^cite_note-economist19jan2015-101]

Operating costs for US [major airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_airline) are primarily aircraft [operating expense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_expense) including [jet fuel](1205435.md), [aircraft maintenance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_maintenance), [depreciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depreciation) and [aircrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircrew) for 44%, servicing expense for 29% (traffic 11%, passenger 11% and aircraft 7%), 14% for reservations and sales and 13% for [overheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overheads) (administration 6% and advertising 2%). An average US major [Boeing 757](151995.md)-200 flies 1,252 miles (2,015 km) stages 11.3 block hours per day and costs \$2,550 per block hour: \$923 of ownership, \$590 of maintenance, \$548 of fuel and \$489 of crew; or \$13.34 per 186 seats per block hour. For a [Boeing 737-500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_737-500), a low-cost carrier like Southwest have lower operating costs at \$1,526 than a full service one like United at \$2,974, and higher [productivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Productivity) with 399,746 [ASM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Available_seat_miles) per day against 264,284, resulting in a unit cost of 0.38 \$cts/ASM against 1.13 \$cts/ASM.[^cite_note-ICAO20feb2017-102]

[McKinsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McKinsey) observes that "newer technology, larger aircraft, and increasingly efficient operations continually drive down the cost of running an airline", from nearly 40 US cents per [ASK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Available_seat_kilometer) at the beginning of the jet age, to just above 10 cents since 2000. Those improvements were passed onto the customer due to high competition: fares have been falling throughout the history of airlines.[^cite_note-103]

### Revenue



Diagram of an airline [Global Distribution System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Distribution_System)

Airlines assign prices to their services in an attempt to maximize profitability. The pricing of airline tickets has become increasingly complicated over the years and is now largely determined by computerized [yield management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yield_management) systems.

Because of the complications in scheduling flights and maintaining profitability, airlines have many loopholes that can be used by the knowledgeable traveler. Many of these airfare secrets are becoming more and more known to the general public, so airlines are forced to make constant adjustments.

Most airlines use differentiated pricing, a form of [price discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_discrimination), to sell air services at varying prices simultaneously to different segments. Factors influencing the price include the days remaining until departure, the booked load factor, the forecast of total demand by price point, competitive pricing in force, and variations by day of week of departure and by time of day. Carriers often accomplish this by dividing each cabin of the aircraft (first, business and economy) into a number of [travel classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel_class) for pricing purposes.

A complicating factor is that of origin-destination control ("O\&D control"). Someone purchasing a ticket from Melbourne to Sydney (as an example) for A\$200 is competing with someone else who wants to fly Melbourne to Los Angeles through Sydney on the same flight, and who is willing to pay A\$1400. Should the airline prefer the \$1400 passenger, or the \$200 passenger plus a possible Sydney-Los Angeles passenger willing to pay \$1300? Airlines have to make hundreds of thousands of similar pricing decisions daily.

The advent of advanced computerized reservations systems in the late 1970s, most notably [Sabre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabre_\(computer_system\)), allowed airlines to easily perform [cost-benefit analyses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost-benefit_analyses) on different pricing structures, leading to almost perfect price discrimination in some cases (that is, filling each seat on an aircraft at the highest price that can be charged without driving the consumer elsewhere).

The intense nature of airfare pricing has led to the term "[fare war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fare_war)" to describe efforts by airlines to undercut other airlines on competitive routes. Through computers, new airfares can be published quickly and efficiently to the airlines' sales channels. For this purpose the airlines use the [Airline Tariff Publishing Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_Tariff_Publishing_Company) (ATPCO), who distribute latest fares for more than 500 airlines to [Computer Reservation Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_Reservation_System) across the world.

The extent of these pricing phenomena is strongest in "legacy" carriers. In contrast, low fare carriers usually offer pre-announced and simplified price structure, and sometimes quote prices for each leg of a trip separately.

Computers also allow airlines to predict, with some accuracy, how many passengers will actually fly after making a reservation to fly. This allows airlines to overbook their flights enough to fill the aircraft while accounting for "no-shows", but not enough (in most cases) to force paying passengers off the aircraft for lack of seats, stimulative pricing for low demand flights coupled with [overbooking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overbooking) on high demand flights can help reduce this figure. This is especially crucial during tough economic times as airlines undertake massive cuts to ticket prices to retain demand.[^cite_note-104]

Over January/February 2018, the cheapest airline surveyed by price comparator [rome2rio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome2rio) was now-defunct [Tigerair Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigerair_Australia) with \$0.06/km followed by [AirAsia X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AirAsia_X) with \$0.07/km, while the most expensive was [Charterlines, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charterlines,_Inc.?action=edit\&redlink=1) with \$1.26/km followed by [Buddha Air](1597626.md) with \$1.18/km.[^cite_note-105]

For the [IATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IATA), the global airline industry [revenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revenue) was \$754 billion in 2017 for a \$38.4 billion collective [profit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profit_\(accounting\)), and should rise by 10.7% to \$834 billion in 2018 for a \$33.8 billion profit forecast, down by 12% due to rising [jet fuel](1205435.md) and [labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workforce) costs.[^cite_note-106]

The demand for air transport will be less [elastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_\(economics\)) for [longer flights](8758086.md) than for shorter flights, and more elastic for leisure travel than for [business travel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_travel).[^cite_note-107]

Airlines often have a strong [seasonality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seasonality), with traffic low in winter and peaking in summer. In Europe the most extreme market are the [Greek islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_islands) with July/August having more than ten times the winter traffic, as [Jet2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet2) is the most seasonal among [low-cost carriers](428422.md) with July having seven times the January traffic, whereas [legacy carriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legacy_carrier) are much less with only 85/115% variability.[^cite_note-108]

### Assets and financing



The 'Golden Lounge' of [Malaysia Airlines](153162.md) at [Kuala Lumpur International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuala_Lumpur_International_Airport) (KLIA).

Airline financing is quite complex, since airlines are highly leveraged operations. Not only must they purchase (or lease) new airliner bodies and engines regularly, they must make major long-term fleet decisions with the goal of meeting the demands of their markets while producing a fleet that is relatively economical to operate and maintain; comparably Southwest Airlines and their reliance on a single airplane type (the [Boeing 737](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_737) and derivatives), with the now defunct [Eastern Air Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Air_Lines) which operated 17 different aircraft types, each with varying pilot, engine, maintenance, and support needs.

A second financial issue is that of [hedging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedge_\(finance\)) [oil](23195.md) and [fuel](21188370.md) purchases, which are usually second only to [labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_\(economics\)) in its relative cost to the company. However, with the current high fuel prices it has become the largest cost to an airline. Legacy airlines, compared with new entrants, have been hit harder by rising fuel prices partly due to the running of older, less fuel efficient aircraft.[^cite_note-Bamber,_G.J.,_Gittell,_J.H.,_Kochan,_T.A._&_von_Nordenflytch,_A._2009-76] While hedging instruments can be expensive, they can easily pay for themselves many times over in periods of increasing fuel costs, such as in the 2000–2005 period.

In view of the congestion apparent at many international [airports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airport), the ownership of slots at certain airports (the right to take-off or land an aircraft at a particular time of day or night) has become a significant tradable asset for many airlines. Clearly take-off slots at popular times of the day can be critical in attracting the more profitable business traveler to a given airline's flight and in establishing a competitive advantage against a competing airline.

If a particular city has two or more airports, market forces will tend to attract the less profitable routes, or those on which competition is weakest, to the less congested airport, where slots are likely to be more available and therefore cheaper. For example, [Reagan National Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reagan_National_Airport) attracts profitable routes due partly to its congestion, leaving less-profitable routes to [Baltimore-Washington International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltimore-Washington_International_Airport) and [Dulles International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulles_International_Airport).[^cite_note-109]

Other factors, such as surface transport facilities and onward connections, will also affect the relative appeal of different airports and some long-distance flights may need to operate from the one with the longest runway. For example, [LaGuardia Airport](248547.md) is the preferred airport for most of [Manhattan](45470.md) due to its proximity, while long-distance routes must use [John F. Kennedy International Airport](97218.md)'s longer runways.

### Airline alliances

Main article: [Airline alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_alliance)

&#x20;The first [airline alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_alliance) was formed in the 1930s when Pan Am and its subsidiary, [Panair do Brasil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panair_do_Brasil), agreed to codeshare routes in Latin America when they overlapped with each other.[^cite_note-110]

&#x20; [Star Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Alliance)

&#x20; [SkyTeam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SkyTeam)

&#x20; [Oneworld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oneworld)

[Codesharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codesharing) involves one airline selling tickets for another airline's flights under its own airline code. An early example of this was [Japan Airlines' (JAL)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Airlines) codesharing partnership with [Aeroflot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeroflot) in the 1960s on [Tokyo](30057.md)–[Moscow](19004.md) flights; Aeroflot operated the flights using Aeroflot aircraft, but JAL sold tickets for the flights as if they were JAL flights.[^cite_note-111]Another example was the [Austrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_Airlines)–[Sabena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabena) partnership on the [Vienna](55866.md)–[Brussels](3708.md)–New York/JFK route during the late '60s, using a Sabena [Boeing 707](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_707) with Austrian [livery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_livery).

Since airline reservation requests are often made by city-pair (such as "show me flights from Chicago to Düsseldorf"), an airline that can codeshare with another airline for a variety of routes might be able to be listed as indeed offering a Chicago–[Düsseldorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%BCsseldorf) flight. The passenger is advised however, that airline no. 1 operates the flight from say Chicago to Amsterdam (for example), and airline no. 2 operates the continuing flight (on a different airplane, sometimes from another terminal) to Düsseldorf. Thus the primary rationale for code sharing is to expand one's service offerings in city-pair terms to increase sales.

A more recent development is the [airline alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_alliance), which became prevalent in the late 1990s. These alliances can act as virtual mergers to get around government restrictions. The largest are [Star Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Alliance), [SkyTeam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SkyTeam) and [Oneworld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oneworld), and these accounted for over 60% of global commercial air traffic as of 2015.[^cite_note-\:7-112] Alliances of airlines coordinate their passenger service programs (such as [lounges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airport_lounge) and [frequent-flyer programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequent-flyer_program)), offer special [interline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlining) tickets and often engage in extensive [codesharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codesharing) (sometimes systemwide). These are increasingly integrated business combinations—sometimes including cross-equity arrangements—in which products, service standards, schedules, and airport facilities are standardized and combined for higher efficiency. One of the first airlines to start an alliance with another airline was [KLM](16882.md), who partnered with [Northwest Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Airlines). Both airlines later entered the SkyTeam alliance after the [fusion of KLM and Air France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_France-KLM) in 2004.

Often the companies combine [IT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IT) operations, or purchase fuel and aircraft as a bloc to achieve higher bargaining power. However, the alliances have been most successful at purchasing invisible supplies and services, such as fuel. Airlines usually prefer to purchase items visible to their passengers to differentiate themselves from local competitors. If an airline's main domestic competitor flies Boeing airliners, then the airline may prefer to use Airbus aircraft regardless of what the rest of the alliance chooses.

### State support

Historically, air travel has survived largely through state support, whether in the form of equity or subsidies. The airline industry as a whole has made a cumulative loss during its 100-year history.[^cite_note-116][^cite_note-117]

One argument is that [positive externalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_externalities), such as higher growth due to global mobility, outweigh the microeconomic losses and justify continuing government intervention. A historically high level of government intervention in the airline industry can be seen as part of a wider political consensus on strategic forms of transport, such as [highways](48519.md) and [railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railways), both of which receive public funding in most parts of the world. Although many countries continue to operate state-owned or parastatal airlines, many large airlines today are privately owned and are therefore governed by microeconomic principles to maximize shareholder profit.[^cite_note-118]

In December 1991, the collapse of Pan Am, an airline often credited for shaping the international airline industry, highlighted the financial complexities faced by major airline companies.

Following the [1978 deregulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_Deregulation_Act), U.S. carriers did not manage to make an aggregate [profit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_profit) for 12 years in 31, including four years where combined [losses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_loss) amounted to \$10 billion, but rebounded with eight consecutive years of profits since 2010, including its four with over \$10 billion profits. They drop loss-making routes, avoid [fare wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fare_war) and [market share](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_share) battles, limit [capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seating_capacity) growth, add [hub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_hub) feed with [regional jets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_jet) to increase their [profitability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profitability). They change [schedules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schedule) to create more connections, buy used aircraft, reduce international frequencies and leverage [partnerships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partnership) to optimize capacities and benefit from overseas connectivity.[^cite_note-AvWeek14May2018-82]

## Call signs

Main article: [Aviation call signs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_call_signs)

Each operator of a scheduled or charter flight uses an [airline call sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_codes) when communicating with airports or air traffic control. Most of these call-signs are derived from the airline's trade name, but for reasons of history, marketing, or the need to reduce ambiguity in spoken English (so that pilots do not mistakenly make navigational decisions based on instructions issued to a different aircraft), some airlines and air forces use call-signs less obviously connected with their trading name. For example, British Airways uses a *Speedbird* call-sign, named after the logo of one of its predecessors, [BOAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BOAC), while [SkyEurope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SkyEurope) used *Relax*.

## Personnel



The [aircrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircrew) of a [Jetstar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jetstar) [Boeing 787](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_787)

The various types of airline personnel include [flight crew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_crew), responsible for the operation of the aircraft. Flight crew members include: [pilots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilots) ([captain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilot_in_command) and [first officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_officer_\(civil_aviation\)): some older aircraft also required a [flight engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_engineer) and/or a [navigator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigator)); [flight attendants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_attendant) (led by a [purser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purser) on larger aircraft); [In-flight security personnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_marshal) on some airlines (most notably [El Al](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Al))

[Groundcrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundcrew), responsible for operations at airports, include Aerospace and avionics engineers responsible for certifying the aircraft for flight and management of aircraft maintenance; [Aerospace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerospace) engineers, responsible for airframe, powerplant and electrical systems maintenance; [Avionics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avionics) engineers responsible for avionics and instruments maintenance; [Airframe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airframe) and [powerplant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_engine) technicians; Electric System technicians, responsible for maintenance of electrical systems; [Flight dispatchers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_dispatcher); [Baggage handlers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baggage_handler); Ramp Agents; Remote centralized weight and balancing;[^cite_note-130] [Gate agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gate_agent); Ticket agents; Passenger service agents (such as [airline lounge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_lounge) employees); Reservation agents, usually (but not always) at facilities outside the airport; Crew schedulers.

Airlines follow a [corporate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate) structure where each broad area of operations (such as maintenance, flight operations (including flight safety), and passenger service) is supervised by a vice president. Larger airlines often appoint vice presidents to oversee each of the airline's hubs as well. Airlines employ [lawyers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawyer) to deal with regulatory procedures and other administrative tasks.[^cite_note-131]

## Trends

Aircraft of various airlines parked side by side at [Tokyo Narita Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narita_International_Airport), [Japan](15573.md) in April 2012

The pattern of ownership has been privatized since the mid-1980s, that is, the ownership has gradually changed from governments to private and individual sectors or organizations. This occurs as regulators permit greater freedom and non-government ownership, in steps that are usually decades apart. This pattern is not seen for all airlines in all regions.[^cite_note-132] Many major airlines operating between the 1940s and 1980s were government-owned or government-established. However, most airlines from the earliest days of air travel in the 1920s and 1930s were personal businesses.[^cite_note-\:5-1]

Growth rates are not consistent in all regions, but countries with a deregulated airline industry have more competition and greater pricing freedom. This results in lower fares and sometimes dramatic spurts in traffic growth. The U.S., Australia, Canada, [Japan](15573.md), [Brazil](3383.md), [India](14533.md) and other markets exhibit this trend. The industry has been observed to be cyclical in its financial performance. Four or five years of poor earnings precede five or six years of improvement. But profitability even in the good years is generally low, in the range of 2–3% net profit after interest and tax. In times of profit, airlines lease new generations of airplanes and upgrade services in response to higher demand. Since 1980, the industry has not earned back the cost of capital during the best of times. Conversely, in bad times losses can be dramatically worse. [Warren Buffett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_Buffett) in 1999 said "the money that had been made since the dawn of aviation by all of this country's airline companies was zero. Absolutely zero."[^cite_note-133]

As in many mature industries, consolidation is a trend. Airline groupings may consist of limited bilateral partnerships, long-term, multi-faceted alliances between carriers, equity arrangements, [mergers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mergers), or [takeovers](56119.md).[^cite_note-\:6-134] Since governments often restrict ownership and merger between companies in different countries, most consolidation takes place within a country. By 1990, nearly 200 airlines in the U.S. had merged, been taken over, or gone out of business since the [Airline Deregulation Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_Deregulation_Act) in 1978.[^cite_note-135] Many international airline managers are lobbying their governments to permit greater consolidation to achieve higher economy and efficiency.

## Types

Passenger airlines can be grouped into various types, for example:

- [Mainline airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainline_\(air_travel\)) operate flights by the airline's main operating unit, rather than by regional affiliates or subsidiaries.

- [Regional airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_airline), non-"mainline" airlines that operate [regional aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_aircraft); regionals typically operate over shorter non-intercontinental distances, often as feeder services for legacy mainline networks.

- [Low-cost carriers](428422.md), giving a "basic", "no-frills" and perceived inexpensive service.

- [Business class airline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_class_airline), an airline aimed at the business traveler, featuring all [business class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_class) seating and amenities.

- [Charter airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charter_airline), operating outside regular schedule intervals.

- [Flag carriers](465816.md), the historically nationally owned airlines that were considered representative of the country overseas.

- [Legacy carriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legacy_carrier), US carriers that predate the [Airline Deregulation Act of 1978](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_Deregulation_Act_of_1978).

- [Major airlines of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_airlines_of_the_United_States), airlines with at least \$1 billion in revenues.

In addition, there are several [cargo-only airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cargo_airlines). This can involve specialized aircraft, like those with large cargo doors and reinforced floors, or modifying passenger planes for cargo-only flights. During the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md), some passenger airlines temporarily converted their planes for cargo-only flights to meet increased demand for freight transport.[^cite_note-136][^cite_note-137]
