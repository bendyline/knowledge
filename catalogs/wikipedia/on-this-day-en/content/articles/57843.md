**Trenton** is the [capital city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_capitals_in_the_United_States) of the [U.S. state](18618239.md) of [New Jersey](21648.md) and the [county seat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_seat) of [Mercer County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercer_County,_New_Jersey). It was the [capital of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_capital) from November 1 until December 24, 1784.[^cite_note-CountyMap-24][^cite_note-UScapital-25] Trenton and [Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey) are the two principal cities of the Trenton–Princeton [metropolitan statistical area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_statistical_area), which encompasses those cities and all of Mercer County for statistical purposes and constitutes part of the [New York combined statistical area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_metropolitan_area#Combined_statistical_area) by the [U.S. Census Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau).[^cite_note-auto-26] However, Trenton directly borders the [Philadelphia metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_metropolitan_area) to its west, and the city was part of the Philadelphia combined statistical area from 1990 until 2000.[^cite_note-FCC_DMA_Current-27]

In the [2020 United States census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_census), Trenton was the fifth largest city in [Central Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Jersey) and [the 10th-most-populous municipality statewide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_municipalities_in_New_Jersey),[^cite_note-Largest2020-28] with a population of 90,871,[^cite_note-Census2020-11][^cite_note-LWD2020-12] an increase of 5,958 (+7.0%) from the [2010 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_States_census) count of 84,913,[^cite_note-Census2010-29][^cite_note-LWD2010-30] which in turn had reflected a decline of 490 (−0.6%) from the 85,403 counted in the [2000 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_United_States_census).[^cite_note-31] The [Census Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau)'s [Population Estimates Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_Estimates_Program) calculated that the city's population was 91,506 in 2025,[^cite_note-Census2020-11][^cite_note-PopEst-14] ranking the city the [383rd-most-populous in the country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_United_States_cities_by_population).[^cite_note-ANNRNK-13] Trenton is the only city in New Jersey that is served by three [commuter rail](6286.md) transit systems ([Amtrak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amtrak), [NJ Transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NJ_Transit), and [SEPTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SEPTA)), and the city has encouraged a spate of [transit-oriented development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transit_village).[^cite_note-32]

Trenton dates back at least to June 3, 1719, when mention was made of a [constable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constable) being appointed for Trenton while the area was still part of [Hunterdon County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunterdon_County,_New_Jersey). Boundaries were recorded for Trenton Township as of March 2, 1720.[^cite_note-Story-33] A courthouse and jail were constructed in Trenton around 1720, and the Freeholders of Hunterdon County met annually in Trenton.[^cite_note-34]

On November 25, 1790, Trenton became New Jersey's capital, and by November 13, 1792, the City of Trenton was formed within Trenton Township. Trenton Township was incorporated as one of New Jersey's initial group of 104 townships by an act of the [New Jersey Legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_Legislature) on February 21, 1798.

The city historically had a major [manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing) industry, with factories producing iron, steel, rubber, pottery, and other products that served the nation. Today Trenton's economy is dominated by the [Government of New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_New_Jersey).[^cite_note-35]

## History



*[The Capture of the Hessians at Trenton, December 26, 1776](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Capture_of_the_Hessians_at_Trenton,_December_26,_1776)*, a painting by [John Trumbull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Trumbull)



The Old Barracks in Trenton

The earliest known inhabitants of the area that is today Trenton were the [Lenape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenape) [Native Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States),[^cite_note-36] specifically the [Axion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axion_people) band who were the largest tribe on the [Delaware River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware_River) in the mid-17th century.[^cite_note-37]

The first European settlement in what would become Trenton was established by [Quakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quakers) in 1679, in the region then called the Falls of the Delaware, led by [Mahlon Stacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahlon_Stacy) from [Handsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handsworth,_South_Yorkshire), [Sheffield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheffield), [England](9316.md). Quakers were being persecuted in England at this time, and North America provided an opportunity to exercise their religious freedom.[^cite_note-38]

By 1719, the town adopted the name "Trent-towne", after [William Trent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Trent_\(Trenton\)), one of its leading landholders who purchased much of the surrounding land from Stacy's family. This name was later shortened to "Trenton".[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41]

The first municipal boundaries were recorded on March 2, 1720, and a courthouse and jail were constructed around the same time.[^cite_note-\:0-42]

In 1758, the [Old Barracks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Barracks_Museum) were built to house British soldiers during the [French and Indian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_and_Indian_War). On January 19, 1764, [Benjamin Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin), Postmaster General of the colonies, appointed [Abraham Hunt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Hunt), a Lieutenant Colonel in the New Jersey [Hunterdon County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunterdon_County,_New_Jersey) militia and prominent merchant in Trenton, as the city's first postmaster. Hunt was again appointed Trenton's postmaster on October 13, 1775, shortly after the [American Revolutionary War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War) broke out.[^cite_note-napo-43][^cite_note-schuyler-44]

During the [American Revolutionary War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War), Trenton was the site of the [Battle of Trenton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Trenton). On December 25–26, 1776, [George Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington) and his army [crossed the icy Delaware River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington's_crossing_of_the_Delaware_River) to Trenton, where they defeated [Hessian troops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hessian_\(soldiers\)) garrisoned there.[^cite_note-45] The second battle of Trenton, [Battle of the Assunpink Creek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Assunpink_Creek), was fought here on January 2, 1777.[^cite_note-46] After the war, the [Congress of the Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_the_Confederation) met for two months at the [French Arms Tavern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Arms_Tavern) from November 1, 1784, to December 24, 1784.[^cite_note-UScapital-25] While the city was preferred by [New England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England) and other northern states as a permanent capital for the new country, the southern states ultimately prevailed in their choice of a location south of the [Mason–Dixon line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mason%E2%80%93Dixon_line).[^cite_note-47] On April 21, 1789, the city hosted a [reception for George Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington's_reception_at_Trenton) on his journey to New York City for [his first inauguration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_inauguration_of_George_Washington).[^cite_note-48] The [Trenton Battle Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trenton_Battle_Monument), a 150-foot (46 m) granite column topped with a statue of George Washington, was built in 1893 to commemorate the battle.[^cite_note-Britannica-49]

Trenton became the state capital in 1790, but prior to that year the [New Jersey Legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_Legislature) often met in the city.[^cite_note-50] The city was incorporated on November 13, 1792.[^cite_note-Story-33] In 1792, the [New Jersey State House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_State_House) was built, making it the third-oldest state house in the country.[^cite_note-Britannica-49] In 1799, the federal government [relocated its offices to Trenton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relocation_of_the_United_States_Government_to_Trenton) for a period of several months, following an outbreak of [yellow fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_fever) in the then-capital of Philadelphia.[^cite_note-51]

During the [War of 1812](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_1812), the [United States Army](32087.md)'s primary hospital was at a site on Broad Street.[^cite_note-52]

Trenton had maintained an iron industry since the 1730s and a pottery industry since at least 1723. The completion of both the Delaware and Raritan Canal and the Camden and Amboy Railroad in the 1830s spurred industrial development in Trenton. In 1845, industrialist [Peter Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Cooper) opened a rolling mill. In 1848, engineer [John Roebling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_A._Roebling) moved his [wire rope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wire_rope) mill to the city, where suspension cables for bridges were manufactured, including the [Brooklyn Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn_Bridge). Throughout the 19th century, Trenton grew steadily, as European immigrants came to work in its [pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pottery) and wire rope mills. Trenton became known as an industrial hub for [railroads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport), [trucking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trucking_industry_in_the_United_States), [rubber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubber), [plastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic), [metalworking](266443.md), [electrical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power_industry), [automobile](163778.md) parts, [glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass_production), and [textiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textile_industry) industries.[^cite_note-Britannica-49]

The city's largest and most long-standing industry was ceramics. From the 1850s to the 1950s, scores of pottery companies called the city home and Trenton became known as the "Staffordshire of America." In the late 19th century, Walter Scott Lenox founded the Ceramic Art Company, which eventually became [Lenox, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenox_\(company\)), a nationally known producer of fine china.[^cite_note-53] Lenox designed and manufactured [White House china](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_House_china) for multiple [Presidents of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidents_of_the_United_States).

On February 22, 1834, portions of Trenton Township were taken to form [Ewing Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ewing_Township,_New_Jersey). The remaining portion of Trenton Township was absorbed by the city on April 10, 1837. In 1837, with the population now too large for government by council, a new mayoral government was adopted, with by-laws that remain in operation to this day.[^cite_note-54] During the latter half of the century, Trenton annexed multiple municipalities: [South Trenton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Trenton,_New_Jersey) Borough on April 14, 1851, portions of [Nottingham Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nottingham_Township,_New_Jersey) on April 14, 1856, [Chambersburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chambersburg,_Trenton,_New_Jersey) and [Millham Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millham_Township,_New_Jersey) on March 30, 1888, and [Wilbur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilbur,_Trenton,_New_Jersey) borough on February 28, 1898.[^cite_note-\:0-42] Portions of Ewing Township and [Hamilton Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton_Township,_Mercer_County,_New_Jersey) were annexed to Trenton on March 23, 1900.[^cite_note-Story-33][^cite_note-55]

In 1855, the [College of New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_College_of_New_Jersey) was founded in Trenton. In 1865, [Rider University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rider_University) was also founded in Trenton. [Mercer Community College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercer_County_Community_College) began in Trenton in 1966.[^cite_note-Britannica-49]

The [Trenton Six](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trenton_Six) were a group of black men arrested for the alleged murder of an elderly white shopkeeper in January 1948 with a soda bottle. They were arrested without warrants, denied lawyers and sentenced to death based on what were described as coerced confessions. With the involvement of the Communist Party and the NAACP, there were several appeals, resulting in a total of four trials. Eventually the accused men (with the exception of one who died in prison) were released. The incident was the subject of the book *Jersey Justice: The Story of the Trenton Six*, written by Cathy Knepper.[^cite_note-56][^cite_note-57]

In the 1950s, the State of New Jersey purchased a large portion of what was then Stacy Park, a large riverfront park located next to downtown that contained large open lawns, landscaping, and [promenades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esplanade). Much of the park was demolished to make way for the construction of Route 29, despite the [protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highway_revolt) toward its construction. After it was built, the area was then mostly filled with parking lots and scattered state office buildings, disconnecting the city from the riverfront.[^cite_note-58]

### Riots of 1968

The Trenton Riots of 1968 were a major civil disturbance that took place during the week following the assassination of civil rights leader [Martin Luther King Jr.](20076.md) in [Memphis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memphis,_Tennessee) on April 4. [Race riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Race_riot) broke out nationwide following the murder of the civil rights activist. More than 200 Trenton businesses, mostly in Downtown, were ransacked and burned. More than 300 people, most of them young black men, were arrested on charges ranging from assault and arson to looting and violating the mayor's emergency curfew. In addition to 16 injured policemen, 15 firefighters were treated at city hospitals for injuries suffered while fighting raging blazes or inflicted by rioters. Area residents pulled false alarms and would then throw bricks at firefighters responding to the alarm boxes. This experience, along with similar experiences in other major cities, effectively ended the use of open-cab fire engines. As an interim measure, the Trenton Fire Department fabricated temporary cab enclosures from steel deck plating until new equipment could be obtained. The losses incurred by downtown businesses were initially estimated by the city to be \$7 million, but the total of insurance claims and settlements came to \$2.5 million.[^cite_note-59]

Trenton's [Battle Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_Monument,_Trenton,_New_Jersey) neighborhood was hardest hit. Since the 1950s, North Trenton had witnessed a steady exodus of middle-class residents, and the riots spelled the end for North Trenton. By the 1970s, the region had become one of the most blighted and crime-ridden in the city.[^cite_note-60]

## Geography



The "Falls of the Delaware," part of the [Delaware River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware_River) in Trenton

According to the [U.S. Census Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau), the city had a total area of 8.21 square miles (21.25 km<sup>2</sup>), including 7.58 square miles (19.63 km<sup>2</sup>) of land and 0.63 square miles (1.62 km<sup>2</sup>) of water (7.62%).[^cite_note-CensusArea-2][^cite_note-GR1-3] In terms of land area, Trenton is also the [second-smallest of the United States capital cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_capitals_in_the_United_States#State_capitals), behind [Annapolis, Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annapolis,_Maryland).[^cite_note-61]

Trenton is located near the [geographic center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographical_centre) of the state, which is located 5 miles (8.0 km) southeast of the city.[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63]

Mercer County constitutes its own [metropolitan statistical area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_statistical_area), the Trenton-[Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey) MSA, which is part of the [Tri-State Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tri-State_area_\(New_York-New_Jersey-Connecticut\)).[^cite_note-auto-26] Locals consider Trenton to be a part of [Central Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Jersey), and thus part of neither [North Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Jersey) nor [South Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Jersey) though sometimes included in both.[^cite_note-64] They are generally split as to whether they are within New York or Philadelphia's sphere of influence. While it is closer to Philadelphia, many people commute to New York City and have moved to Trenton to escape the New York region's high housing costs.

Trenton is one of two state capitals that border another state—the other being [Carson City, Nevada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carson_City,_Nevada).[^cite_note-65]

Trenton borders [Ewing Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ewing_Township,_New_Jersey), [Hamilton Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton_Township,_Mercer_County,_New_Jersey) and [Lawrence Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Township,_Mercer_County,_New_Jersey) in Mercer County; and [Falls Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falls_Township,_Bucks_County,_Pennsylvania), [Lower Makefield Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Makefield_Township,_Pennsylvania) and [Morrisville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morrisville,_Bucks_County,_Pennsylvania) in [Bucks County, Pennsylvania](91976.md), across the [Delaware River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware_River) in [Pennsylvania](23332.md).[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68]

The [Northeast Corridor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeast_Corridor) goes through Trenton. A straight line drawn between [Center City, Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_City,_Philadelphia) and [Downtown Manhattan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downtown_Manhattan) would pass within 2000 feet of the [New Jersey State House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_State_House).

Several bridges across the [Delaware River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware_River) connect Trenton to [Morrisville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morrisville,_Bucks_County,_Pennsylvania), [Pennsylvania](23332.md), all of which are operated by the [Delaware River Joint Toll Bridge Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware_River_Joint_Toll_Bridge_Commission).[^cite_note-69] The [Trenton–Morrisville Toll Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trenton%E2%80%93Morrisville_Toll_Bridge), originally constructed in 1952, stretches 1,324 feet (404 m), carrying [U.S. Route 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_1_in_New_Jersey).[^cite_note-70] The [Lower Trenton Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Trenton_Bridge), bearing the legend "TRENTON MAKES THE WORLD TAKES", is a 1,022-foot (312 m) span that was constructed in 1928 on the site of a bridge that dates back to 1804.[^cite_note-71] The [Calhoun Street Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calhoun_Street_Bridge), dating back to 1884, is 1,274 feet (388 m) long.[^cite_note-72]

### Neighborhoods



[Delaware and Raritan Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware_and_Raritan_Canal) flowing under Mulberry St.

Trenton is home to numerous neighborhoods and sub-neighborhoods. The main neighborhoods are taken from the four [cardinal directions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_direction). Trenton was once home to large Italian, Hungarian, and Jewish communities, but, since the 1950s, demographic shifts have changed the city into a relatively segregated urban enclave of middle and lower income African Americans and newer immigrants, many of whom arrive from Latin America. Italians are scattered throughout the city, but a distinct Italian community is centered in the [Chambersburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chambersburg,_New_Jersey) neighborhood, in South Trenton.[^cite_note-73] This community has been in decline since the 1970s, largely due to economic and social shifts to the suburbs surrounding the city. Today Chambersburg has a large Latino community. Many of the Latino immigrants are from Mexico, Guatemala and Nicaragua. There is also a significant and growing Asian community in the Chambersburg neighborhood primarily made up of Burmese and Bhutanese/Nepali refugees.

The [North Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Trenton), once a mecca for the city's middle class, is now one of the most economically distressed, torn apart by race riots following the [assassination of Martin Luther King Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination_of_Martin_Luther_King_Jr.) in 1968. Nonetheless, the area still retains many important architectural and historic sites. North Trenton still has a large [Polish-American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish-American) neighborhood that borders Lawrence Township, many of whom attend [St. Hedwig's Roman Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Hedwig's_Roman_Catholic_Church) on Brunswick Avenue. St. Hedwig's church was built in 1904 by Polish immigrants, many of whose families still attend the church. North Trenton is also home to the historic Shiloh Baptist Church—one of the largest houses of worship in Trenton and the oldest African American church in the city, founded in 1888.[^cite_note-74] The church is currently pastored by Rev. Darrell L. Armstrong, who carried the Olympic torch in 2002 for the Winter Olympics in Salt Lake City. Also located just at the southern tip of North Trenton is the city's Battle Monument, also known as "Five Points". It is a 150 ft (46 m) structure that marks the spot where [George Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington)'s [Continental Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Army) launched the [Battle of Trenton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Trenton) during the [American Revolutionary War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War). It faces downtown Trenton and is a symbol of the city's historic past.[^cite_note-BattleMonument-75]

[South Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Trenton) is a diverse neighborhood, home to many Latin American, Italian-American, and African American residents.[^cite_note-76] The Chambersburg neighborhood was once noted in the region as a destination for its many [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_cuisine) restaurants and pizzerias. With changing demographics, many of these businesses have either closed or relocated to suburban locations.

[East Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Trenton) is the smallest neighborhood in Trenton and is home to the [Trenton Transit Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trenton_Transit_Center) and [Trenton Central High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trenton_Central_High_School).

West Ward is the home of Trenton's more suburban neighborhoods.

## Climate

According to the [Köppen climate classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification), Trenton lies in the northern range of the [humid subtropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humid_subtropical) (*Cfa*) zone, and [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation_\(meteorology\)) fairly evenly distributed through the year. The *Cfa* climate is the result of [adiabatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adiabatic_process) warming of the [Appalachians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appalachian_Mountains), low altitude and proximity to the coast without being on the immediate edge for moderate temperatures.[^cite_note-78]

Summers are hot and humid, with a July daily average of 76.3 °F (24.6 °C); temperatures reaching or exceeding 90 °F (32 °C) occur on 21.8 days.[^cite_note-NCEI-79] Episodes of extreme heat and humidity can occur with [heat index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_index) values reaching 100 °F (38 °C). Extremes in air temperature have ranged from −14 °F (−26 °C) on February 9, 1934, up to 106 °F (41 °C) as recently as July 22, 2011.[^cite_note-80] However, air temperatures reaching 0 °F (−18 °C) or 100 °F (38 °C) are uncommon.

Winters are cold and damp: the daily average temperature in January is 32.0 °F (0.0 °C),[^cite_note-NCEI-79] and temperatures at or below 10 °F (−12 °C) occur on 3.9 nights annually, while there are 17 days where the temperature fails to rise above freezing.[^cite_note-NOAA-81] Episodes of extreme cold and wind can occur with [wind chill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_chill) values below 0 °F (−18 °C), every few years. The [plant hardiness zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_hardiness_zone) at the Trenton Municipal Court is 7a with an average annual extreme minimum air temperature of 1.2 °F (−17.1 °C).[^cite_note-USDA-82]

The average precipitation is 45.47 inches (115 cm) per year, which is fairly evenly distributed through the year.[^cite_note-NCEI-79][^cite_note-NOAA-81] The driest month on average is February, with 2.63 in (67 mm) of precipitation on average, while the wettest month is July with 4.39 in (11 cm) of rainfall on average which corresponds with the annual peak in [thunderstorm](70807.md) activity.[^cite_note-NCEI-79][^cite_note-NOAA-81] The all-time single-day rainfall record is 7.25 in (18.4 cm) on September 16, 1999, during the passage of [Hurricane Floyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Floyd).[^cite_note-NOAA-81] The all-time monthly rainfall record is 14.55 in (37.0 cm) in August 1955, due to the passage of [Hurricane Connie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Connie) and [Hurricane Diane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Diane). The wettest year on record was 1996, when 67.90 in (172 cm) of precipitation fell. On the flip side, the driest month on record was October 1963, when only 0.05 in (0.1 cm) of rain was recorded. The 28.79 in (73 cm) of precipitation recorded in 1957 were the lowest ever for the city.[^cite_note-83]

Snowfall can vary even more from year to year. The average seasonal (November–April) snowfall total is 24 to 30 inches (61 to 76 cm), but has ranged from as low as 2 in (5.1 cm) in the winter of 1918–1919 to as high as 76.5 in (194.3 cm) in 1995–1996, which included the greatest single-storm snowfall, the [Blizzard of January 7–8, 1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_blizzard_of_1996), when 24.2 inches (61.5 cm) of snow fell.[^cite_note-84] The average snowiest month is February which corresponds with the annual peak in [nor'easter](402179.md) activity.

### 2020 census

As of the [2020 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_census), Trenton had a population of 90,871. The median age was 33.3 years. 26.2% of residents were under the age of 18 and 9.8% of residents were 65 years of age or older. For every 100 females there were 104.9 males, and for every 100 females age 18 and over there were 105.6 males age 18 and over.[^cite_note-Census2020DP-103][^cite_note-Census2020PL-104]

100.0% of residents lived in urban areas, while 0.0% lived in rural areas.[^cite_note-Census2020DHC-105]

There were 34,322 housing units, of which 12.2% were vacant. The homeowner vacancy rate was 2.2% and the rental vacancy rate was 8.8%.[^cite_note-Census2020DP-103]

#### Hispanic or Latino population

The [Hispanic or Latino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic_and_Latino_Americans) population in Trenton has grown significantly over the past few decades and now represents a substantial portion of the city's residents. According to the [2020 U.S. census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_census), 45.01% of Trenton's population identified as Hispanic or Latino, up from 33.71% in 2010, 21.53% in 2000, and 14.13% in 1990. This demographic shift reflects a broader trend of increasing diversity in the city. The largest Hispanic or Latino ancestry group in Trenton is Guatemalan, accounting for 14.66% of the total Trenton population, followed by Puerto Rican (10.09%), Dominican (4.80%), and other Hispanic groups including Mexican, Honduran, and Ecuadorian communities.[^cite_note-\:1-106] This growing population has had a notable cultural, social, and economic impact on the city, contributing to its identity. In recent years, for example, the annual festival celebrating [Guatemala's independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Independence_of_Central_America) on September 15 has attracted crowds in the thousands.[^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108]

### 2010 census

The [2010 United States census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_States_census) counted 84,913 people, 28,578 households, and 17,747 families in the city. The [population density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_density) was 11,101.9 per square mile (4,286.5/km<sup>2</sup>). There were 33,035 housing units at an average density of 4,319.2 per square mile (1,667.7/km<sup>2</sup>). The racial makeup was 26.56% (22,549) [White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_\(U.S._census\)), 52.01% (44,160) [Black or African American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_\(U.S._census\)), 0.70% (598) [Native American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_\(U.S._census\)), 1.19% (1,013) [Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_\(U.S._census\)), 0.13% (110) [Pacific Islander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Islander_\(U.S._census\)), 15.31% (13,003) from [other races](273285.md), and 4.10% (3,480) from two or more races. [Hispanic or Latino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic_\(U.S._census\)) of any race were 33.71% (28,621) of the population.[^cite_note-Census2010-29]

Of the 28,578 households, 32.0% had children under the age of 18; 25.1% were married couples living together; 28.1% had a female householder with no husband present and 37.9% were non-families. Of all households, 30.8% were made up of individuals and 9.1% had someone living alone who was 65 years of age or older. The average household size was 2.79 and the average family size was 3.40.[^cite_note-Census2010-29]

25.1% of the population were under the age of 18, 11.0% from 18 to 24, 32.5% from 25 to 44, 22.6% from 45 to 64, and 8.8% who were 65 years of age or older. The median age was 32.6 years. For every 100 females, the population had 106.5 males. For every 100 females ages 18 and older there were 107.2 males.[^cite_note-Census2010-29]

The Census Bureau's 2006–2010 [American Community Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Community_Survey) showed that (in 2010 [inflation-adjusted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation_adjustment) dollars) [median household income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Median_household_income) was \$36,601 (with a margin of error of +/− \$1,485) and the median family income was \$41,491 (+/− \$2,778). Males had a median income of \$29,884 (+/− \$1,715) versus \$31,319 (+/− \$2,398) for females. The per capita income for the city was \$17,400 (+/− \$571). About 22.4% of families and 24.5% of the population were below the poverty line, including 36.3% of those under age 18 and 17.5% of those age 65 or over.[^cite_note-109]

## Economy



The [Lower Trenton Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Trenton_Bridge), often referred to as the "Trenton Makes Bridge" by locals

Trenton was a major manufacturing center in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. One relic of that era is the slogan "Trenton Makes, The World Takes", which is displayed on the [Lower Free Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Free_Bridge_\(Trenton\)) (just north of the [Trenton–Morrisville Toll Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trenton%E2%80%93Morrisville_Toll_Bridge)).[^cite_note-110] The city adopted the slogan in 1917 to represent Trenton's then-leading role as a major manufacturing center for rubber, wire rope, ceramics and cigars. It was home to [American Standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Standard_Brands)'s largest plumbing fixture manufacturing facility.[^cite_note-111]

Along with many other United States cities in the 1970s, Trenton fell on hard times when manufacturing and industrial jobs declined. Concurrently, state government agencies began leasing office space in the surrounding suburbs. State government leaders (particularly governors [William Cahill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_T._Cahill) and [Brendan Byrne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brendan_Byrne)) attempted to revitalize the downtown area by making it the center of state government. Between 1982 and 1992, more than a dozen office buildings were constructed primarily by the state to house state offices.[^cite_note-112] Today, Trenton's biggest employer is still the state of New Jersey. Each weekday, 20,000 state workers commute into the city from the surrounding suburbs.[^cite_note-113]

Notable businesses of the thousands based in Trenton include [Italian Peoples Bakery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Peoples_Bakery), a wholesale and retail bakery established in 1936.[^cite_note-114] [De Lorenzo's Tomato Pies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Lorenzo's_Tomato_Pies) and [Papa's Tomato Pies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papa's_Tomato_Pies) were also fixtures of the city for many years, though both have since relocated.

### Urban Enterprise Zone

Portions of Trenton are part of an [Urban Enterprise Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_Enterprise_Zone). The city was selected in 1983 as one of the initial group of 10 zones chosen to participate in the program.[^cite_note-115] In addition to other benefits to encourage employment within the Zone, shoppers can take advantage of a reduced 3.3125% [sales tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sales_tax) rate (half of the 6+5⁄8% rate charged statewide) at eligible merchants.[^cite_note-116] Established in January 1986, the city's Urban Enterprise Zone status expires in December 2023.[^cite_note-117]

The UEZ program in Trenton and four other original UEZ cities had been allowed to lapse as of January 1, 2017, after Governor [Chris Christie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Christie), who called the program an "abject failure", vetoed a compromise bill that would have extended the status for two years.[^cite_note-118] In May 2018, Governor [Phil Murphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phil_Murphy) signed a law that reinstated the program in these five cities and extended the expiration date in other zones.[^cite_note-119]

In 2018, the city had an average property tax bill of \$3,274, the lowest in the county, compared to an average bill of \$8,292 in Mercer County and \$8,767 statewide.[^cite_note-120][^cite_note-121] The city had the sixth-highest property tax rate in New Jersey, with an equalized rate of 5.264% in 2020, compared to 2.760% in the county as a whole and a statewide average of 2.279%.[^cite_note-122]

## Sports

Because of Trenton's nearly equal distance between New York City and [Philadelphia](50585.md), two of the nation's largest cities, and because most homes in Mercer County receive network broadcasts from both cities, locals are sharply divided in fan loyalty between both cities. It is common to find fans of the Philadelphia Phillies, Eagles, 76ers, Union, and/or Flyers cheering (and arguing) alongside fans of the New York Yankees, Mets, Nets, Knicks, Rangers, Islanders, Jets, Red Bulls, Giants, and/or the New Jersey Devils.[^cite_note-136]

Between 1948 and 1979, [Trenton Speedway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trenton_Speedway), located in adjacent Hamilton Township, hosted world class auto racing. Drivers such as [Jim Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Clark), [A. J. Foyt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._J._Foyt), [Mario Andretti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Andretti), [Al Unser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Unser), [Bobby Unser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Unser), [Richard Petty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Petty) and [Bobby Allison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Allison) raced on the 1-mile (1.6 km) asphalt oval and then reconfigured 1+1⁄2-mile (2.4 km) race track.[^cite_note-137] The speedway, which closed in 1980, was part of the larger New Jersey State Fairgrounds complex, which also closed in 1983. The former site of the speedway and fairgrounds is now the [Grounds for Sculpture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grounds_for_Sculpture).[^cite_note-138]

### Local government

See also: [Mayor of Trenton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_Trenton,_New_Jersey)

Trenton is governed within the [Faulkner Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faulkner_Act), formally known as the Optional Municipal Charter Law, under the [Mayor-Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faulkner_Act_\(mayor%E2%80%93council\)) system of municipal government, one of 79 municipalities (of the 564) statewide that use this form of government.[^cite_note-152] The governing body is comprised of a mayor and a seven-member city council. Three city council members are elected [at-large](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/At-large), and four come from each of four [wards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ward_\(United_States\)). The mayor and council members are elected concurrently on a [non-partisan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-partisan) basis to four-year terms of office as part of the November general election.[^cite_note-DataBook-4][^cite_note-153][^cite_note-154]

In April 1887, *[The New York Times](30680.md)* identified alderman-elect Alexander Weber as the first [person of color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Person_of_color) to hold public office in the city of Trenton.[^cite_note-155]

In October 2020, the city council overrode a mayoral veto and shifted municipal elections from May to November, with proponents citing the increased turnout and savings to the city of \$180,000 in each election cycle. The mayor and members of council all had their term-end dates extended by six months and moved to December 31 from June 30, 2022.[^cite_note-156] The city retained a provision that would have a December runoff in the event that the candidate with the most votes does not obtain a majority.[^cite_note-157]

As of 2023, the mayor of Trenton is [Reed Gusciora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reed_Gusciora), whose term of office ends December 31, 2026.[^cite_note-158] Members of the city council are Jasi Edwards (at-large), Crystal Feliciano (at-large), Teska Frisby (West Ward), Yazminelly Gonzalez (at-large), Joseph A. Harrison (East Ward), Jenna Figueroa Kettenburg (South Ward) and [Jennifer Williams (North Ward)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jennifer_Williams_\(politician\)).[^cite_note-Council-5][^cite_note-159][^cite_note-MercerOfficials-160][^cite_note-Mercer2022-161][^cite_note-Mercer2022RunoffWards-162][^cite_note-Mercer2022RunoffAtLarge-163]

As they had not exceeded the minimum of 50 percent in the November 2022 general election, a runoff was held in December for the seats in the North and South Wards. Jennifer Williams won the North seat by a single vote against Algernon Ward,[^cite_note-164] which made Williams the first transgender individual to be elected to a city council position in New Jersey history as well as being the first LGBTQ+ city council member in Trenton history.[^cite_note-165] Jenna Figueroa Kettenburg won the South ward seat, defeating Damian G. Malave who had been ahead on Election Day but short of the cutoff, while a January 2023 runoff had Jasi Edwards, Crystal Feliciano and Yazminelly Gonzalez winning the three at-large seats.[^cite_note-Mercer2022-161][^cite_note-Mercer2022RunoffWards-162][^cite_note-Mercer2022RunoffAtLarge<ref-166]

In February 2023, Judge William Anklowitz of the New Jersey Superior Court heard a case for election challenges in the North Ward runoff election for both candidates Algernon Ward and Jennifer Williams. Three of the ballots Ward contested were all rejected because they were mail-in ballots that were returned without the required inner envelope. The other rejection Ward challenged was a case involving a cure letter that a voter sent to the wrong place, leading to it being not counted. Williams contested one ballot that was not counted due to it having both a vote for Ward and for Williams. Judge Anklowitz determined that the slash through Ward's vote signaled the voter's intention to vote for Williams and thus determined the vote should have been counted. These election challenges were heard following a recount that was held that did not change the outcome of the vote. Jennifer Williams thus remained to hold her seat on Trenton City Council for the North Ward seat.[^cite_note-167]

#### Mayor's conviction and removal from office

On February 7, 2014, [Tony F. Mack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_F._Mack) and his brother, Raphiel, were convicted by a federal jury of bribery, fraud and extortion, based on the details of their participation in a scheme to take money in exchange for helping get approvals to develop a downtown parking garage as part of a sting operation by law enforcement.[^cite_note-168] Days after the conviction, the office of the [New Jersey Attorney General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_Attorney_General) filed motions to have Mack removed from office, as state law requires the removal of elected officials after convictions for corruption.[^cite_note-169] Initially, Mack fought the removal of him from the office but on February 26, a superior court judge ordered his removal and any actions taken by Mack between February 7 and February 26 could have been reversed by Muschal.[^cite_note-MuschalJuly-170] Previously, Mack's housing director quit after it was learned he had a theft conviction. His chief of staff was arrested trying to buy heroin. His half-brother, whose authority he elevated at the city water plant, was arrested on charges of stealing. His law director resigned after arguing with Mack over complying with open-records laws and potential violations of laws prohibiting city contracts to big campaign donors.[^cite_note-171]

From February 7 to July 1, 2014, the acting mayor was George Muschal who retroactively assumed the office on that date due to Mack's felony conviction, who had taken office on July 1, 2010.[^cite_note-MuschalSwornIn-172] Muschal, who was council president, was selected by the city council to serve as the interim mayor to finish the term.[^cite_note-MuschalJuly-170]

## Fire department

The city of Trenton is protected on a full-time basis by the city of Trenton Fire and Emergency Services Department (TFD), which has been a paid department since 1892 after having been originally established in 1747 as a volunteer fire department.[^cite_note-208] The TFD operates out of seven fire stations and operates a fire apparatus fleet of 7 engine companies, 3 ladder companies and one rescue company, along with one HAZMAT unit, an air cascade unit, a mobile command unit, a foam unit, one fireboat, and numerous special, support and reserve units, under the command of two battalion chiefs and a deputy chief/tour commander each shift.[^cite_note-209][^cite_note-210]

## Education

### Colleges and universities

Trenton is the home of two post-secondary institutions: [Thomas Edison State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Edison_State_University), serving adult students around the nation and worldwide[^cite_note-211] and [Mercer County Community College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercer_County_Community_College)'s James Kerney Campus.[^cite_note-212]

[The College of New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_College_of_New_Jersey), formerly named Trenton State College, was founded in Trenton in 1855 and is now located in nearby [Ewing Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ewing_Township,_New_Jersey). [Rider University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rider_University) was founded in Trenton in 1865 as The Trenton Business College. In 1959, Rider moved to its current location in nearby [Lawrence Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Township,_Mercer_County,_New_Jersey).[^cite_note-213]

### Public schools

The [Trenton Public Schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trenton_Public_Schools) serve students in [pre-kindergarten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-kindergarten) through [twelfth grade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelfth_grade).[^cite_note-214] The district is one of 31 former [Abbott districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbott_district) statewide that were established pursuant to the decision by the [New Jersey Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_Supreme_Court) in *Abbott v. Burke*[^cite_note-215] which are now referred to as "SDA Districts" based on the requirement for the state to cover all costs for school building and renovation projects in these districts under the supervision of the [New Jersey Schools Development Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_Schools_Development_Authority).[^cite_note-216][^cite_note-217] The district's [board of education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_of_education), comprised of seven members, sets policy and oversees the fiscal and educational operation of the district through its superintendent administration. As a Type I school district, the board's trustees are appointed by the mayor to serve three-year terms of office on a staggered basis, with either two or three seats up for re-appointment each year. The board appoints a superintendent to oversee the district's day-to-day operations and a business administrator to supervise the business functions of the district.[^cite_note-218][^cite_note-219] The school district has undergone a 'construction' renaissance throughout the district.

As of the 2022–23 school year, the district, comprised of 25 schools, had an enrollment of 14,852 students and 966.4 classroom teachers (on an [FTE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full-time_equivalent) basis), for a [student–teacher ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student%E2%80%93teacher_ratio) of 15.4\:1.[^cite_note-NCES-220] The district includes 13 elementary schools,[^cite_note-221] six intermediate schools,[^cite_note-TrentonIntermediate2024-222] three middle schools,[^cite_note-TrentonMiddle2024-223] and three high schools.[^cite_note-TrentonHigh2024-224] They are as follows:

#### Charter schools

Trenton is home to several [charter schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charter_school), including [Capital Preparatory Charter High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_Preparatory_Charter_High_School), Emily Fisher Charter School, [Foundation Academy Charter School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundation_Academy_Charter_School), International Charter School, Paul Robeson Charter School and Village Charter School.[^cite_note-229]

The International Academy of Trenton, owned and monitored by the [SABIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SABIS) school network, became a charter school in 2014. On February 22, 2017, Trenton's mayor, Eric Jackson, visited the school when it opened its doors in the former *[Trenton Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times_\(Trenton\))* building on 500 Perry Street, after completion of a \$17 million renovation project. After receiving notice from the [New Jersey Department of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_Department_of_Education) that the school's charter would not be renewed due to issues with academic performance and school management, the school closed its doors on June 30, 2018.[^cite_note-230]

### Private schools

[Trenton Catholic Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trenton_Catholic_Academy) high school serves students in grades 9–12, while Trenton Catholic Academy grammar school serves students in Pre-K through 8th grade; both schools operate under the auspices of the [Roman Catholic Diocese of Trenton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_Trenton).[^cite_note-231]

Trenton is home to [Al-Bayaan Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Bayaan_Academy), which opened for preschool students in September 2001 and added grades in subsequent years.[^cite_note-232]

Trenton Community Music School is a not-for-profit community school of the arts. The school was founded by executive director Marcia Wood in 1997. The school operates at Blessed Sacrament Catholic Church and the Copeland Center for the Performing Arts.

## Crime

The Trenton Police Department was founded in 1792, when the city was incorporated. It works in conjunction with the [Mercer County Sheriff's Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercer_County_Sheriff's_Office).[^cite_note-233]

In 2005, there were 31 homicides in Trenton, which at that time was the largest number in a single year in the city's history.[^cite_note-234] The city was named the 4th "Most Dangerous" in 2005 out of 129 cities with a population of 75,000 to 99,999 ranked nationwide in the 12th annual [Morgan Quitno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morgan_Quitno) survey.[^cite_note-235] In the 2006 survey, Trenton was ranked as the 14th most dangerous city overall out of 371 cities included nationwide in the Morgan Quitno survey, and was again named as the fourth most dangerous municipality of 126 cities in the 75,000–99,999 population range.[^cite_note-236]

In September 2011, the city laid off 108 police officers due to budget cuts; this constituted almost one-third of the Trenton Police Department and required 30 senior officers to be sent out on patrols in lieu of supervisory duties.[^cite_note-237]

In 2013, the city set a new record with 37 homicides.[^cite_note-238] In 2014, there were 23 murders through the end of July and the city's homicide rate was on track to break the record set the previous year until an 81-day period when there were no murders in Trenton; the city ended the year with 34 murders.[^cite_note-239][^cite_note-240] In 2020, the city surpassed the 2013 homicide number with a record 40 homicides.[^cite_note-241]

The [New Jersey State Prison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_State_Prison) (formerly Trenton State Prison) has two maximum security units. It houses some of the state's most dangerous individuals, which included New Jersey's [death row](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_row) population until the state banned capital punishment in 2007.[^cite_note-242]

In 2024, the City of Trenton and the Trenton Police Department were investigated by the United States Department of Justice and the NJ State Attorney’s Office. These agencies found that both entities “engage in a pattern or practice of misconduct that deprives people of their rights under the fourth amendment.”[^cite_note-243]

## Transportation

### Roads and highways



[U.S. Route 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_1) through downtown Trenton, looking north from the East State Street overpass

As of May 2010, the city had a total of 168.80 miles (271.66 km) of roadways, of which 145.57 miles (234.27 km) were maintained by the municipality, 11.33 miles (18.23 km) by Mercer County, 10.92 miles (17.57 km) by the [New Jersey Department of Transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_Department_of_Transportation) and 0.99 miles (1.59 km) by the [Delaware River Joint Toll Bridge Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware_River_Joint_Toll_Bridge_Commission).[^cite_note-244]

Several highways pass through the city.[^cite_note-245] These include the Trenton Freeway (part of [U.S. Route 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_1_in_New_Jersey))[^cite_note-246] and the John Fitch Parkway, which is part of [Route 29](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_Route_29).[^cite_note-247] Canal Boulevard, more commonly known as [Route 129](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_Route_129), connects U.S. Route 1 and Route 29 in South Trenton.[^cite_note-248] [U.S. Route 206](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_206),[^cite_note-249] [Route 31](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_Route_31)[^cite_note-250] and [Route 33](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_Route_33)[^cite_note-251] also pass through the city via regular city streets (Broad Street/Brunswick Avenue/Princeton Avenue, Pennington Avenue, and Greenwood Avenue, respectively).

Route 29 connects the city to [Interstate 295](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_295_\(Delaware%E2%80%93Pennsylvania\)) and [Interstate 195](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_195_\(New_Jersey\)), the latter providing a connection to the [New Jersey Turnpike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_Turnpike) ([Interstate 95](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_95_in_New_Jersey)) at Exit 7A in [Robbinsville Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robbinsville_Township,_New_Jersey), although the section near downtown is planned to be converted to an urban boulevard.[^cite_note-252]

### Public transportation



The Trenton Transit Center, which serves [Amtrak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amtrak), [NJ Transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NJ_Transit), and [SEPTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SEPTA)

Public transportation within the city and its nearby suburbs is provided in the form of local bus routes run by [NJ Transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NJ_Transit). [SEPTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SEPTA) provides bus service to adjacent [Bucks County, Pennsylvania](91976.md).

The [Trenton Transit Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trenton_Transit_Center), located on the heavily traveled [Northeast Corridor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeast_Corridor), serves as the northbound terminus for [SEPTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SEPTA)'s [Trenton Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trenton_Line) (local train service to Philadelphia) and southbound terminus for [NJ Transit Rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NJ_Transit_Rail_Operations)'s [Northeast Corridor Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeast_Corridor_Line) (local train service to [New York Penn Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Penn_Station)). The train station also serves as the northbound terminus for the [River Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Line_\(NJ_Transit\)), a diesel [light rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_rail) line that runs to [Camden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camden,_New_Jersey).[^cite_note-253] Two additional River Line stops, [Cass Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cass_Street_\(River_Line_station\)) and [Hamilton Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton_Avenue_\(River_Line_station\)), are located within the city.[^cite_note-254] Long-distance transportation is provided by [Amtrak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amtrak) train service along the Northeast Corridor.[^cite_note-255]

The closest commercial airport is [Trenton–Mercer Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trenton%E2%80%93Mercer_Airport) in [Ewing Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ewing_Township,_New_Jersey), about 8 miles (13 km) from the center of Trenton, which is served by [Frontier Airlines](163354.md).[^cite_note-256] Nearby major airports are [Newark Liberty International Airport](248492.md) and [Philadelphia International Airport](262559.md), located 55.2 miles (88.8 km) and 43.4 miles (69.8 km) away, respectively, and reachable by direct New Jersey Transit or Amtrak rail (to Newark) and by [SEPTA Regional Rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SEPTA_Regional_Rail) (to Philadelphia).

[NJ Transit Bus Operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NJ_Transit_Bus_Operations) provides bus service between Trenton and [Philadelphia](50585.md) on the [409](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/409_\(New_Jersey_bus\)) route, with service to surrounding communities on the [600](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/600_\(New_Jersey_bus\)), [601](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/601_\(New_Jersey_bus\)), [603](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/603_\(New_Jersey_bus\)), [606](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/606_\(New_Jersey_bus\)), [607](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/607_\(New_Jersey_bus\)), [608](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/608_\(New_Jersey_bus\)), [609](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/609_\(New_Jersey_bus\)), [611](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/611_\(New_Jersey_bus\)) and [624](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/624_\(New_Jersey_bus\)) routes.[^cite_note-257][^cite_note-258]

The Greater Mercer Transportation Management Association offers service on the Route 130 Connection between the Trenton Transit Center and the [South Brunswick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Brunswick,_New_Jersey) warehouse district with stops along the route including [Hamilton train station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton_station_\(NJ_Transit\)), Hamilton Marketplace, [Hightstown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hightstown,_New_Jersey) and East Windsor Town Center Plaza.[^cite_note-259]

## Media

Trenton is served by two daily newspapers, *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times_\(Trenton\))* and *[The Trentonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Trentonian)*, and a monthly advertising magazine, *"The City" Trenton N.E.W\.S.*. Radio station [WKXW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WKXW) and Top 40 [WPST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WPST) are also licensed to Trenton. Defunct periodicals include the *Trenton True American*. A local television station, [WPHY-CD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WPHY-CD) TV-25, serves the Trenton area.[^cite_note-260]

Trenton is part of the [Philadelphia](50585.md) television market but some local TV operators also carry stations serving the [New York City](645042.md) market. While it is its own radio market, many Philadelphia and New York stations are easily receivable.

Trenton was the site of the studios of the former public television station [New Jersey Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_Network).
