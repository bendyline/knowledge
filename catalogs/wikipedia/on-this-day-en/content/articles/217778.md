The **House of Representatives** ([Japanese](15606.md): 衆議院, [Hepburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepburn_romanization): *Shūgiin*; Japanese pronunciation: [\[ɕɯː.ɡʲiꜜ.iɴ, -ŋʲiꜜ-\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Japanese)[^cite_note-5]) is the [lower house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_house) of the [National Diet](163847.md) of [Japan](15573.md). The [Senate](217779.md) is the [upper house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_house). The composition of the House is established by [Article 41](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_41_of_the_Constitution_of_Japan?action=edit\&redlink=1) and [Article 42](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_42_of_the_Constitution_of_Japan?action=edit\&redlink=1) of the [Constitution of Japan](62699.md). The House of Representatives has 465 members, elected for a four-year term. Of these, 176 members are elected from 11 multi-member constituencies by a [party-list system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party-list_proportional_representation) of [proportional representation](23298.md), and 289 are elected from single-member constituencies.

The overall voting system used to elect the House of Representatives is a [parallel system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_voting), a form of [semi-proportional representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-proportional_representation). Under a parallel system, the allocation of list seats does not take into account the outcome in the single seat constituencies. Therefore, the overall allocation of seats in the House of Representatives is not fully proportional, to the advantage of larger parties.

The House of Representatives is the more powerful of the two houses, able to override vetoes on bills imposed by the Senate with a two-thirds majority. In the case of [treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty), the [budget](4360909.md), and the selection of the [prime minister](24833.md), the Senate can only delay passage, but not block the legislation. The [most recent election](77996830.md) for the House of Representatives was held on February 8, 2026, in which the [Liberal Democratic Party](164231.md) won a super-majority of seats, over 316; in the [previous election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Japanese_general_election), they had failed to reach a majority of 233 seats, instead winning 191.

## Right to vote and candidature

The composition of the House is established by [Article 41](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_41_of_the_Constitution_of_Japan?action=edit\&redlink=1) and [Article 42](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_42_of_the_Constitution_of_Japan?action=edit\&redlink=1) of the [Constitution of Japan](62699.md).[^cite_note-6]

- Japanese nationals aged 18 years and older may vote (prior to 2016, the voting age was 20).

  [^cite_note-7]

- Japanese nationals aged 25 years and older may run for office in the lower house.

## Differences between the Upper and Lower Houses

The House of Representatives is the more powerful of the two houses. The House of Representatives has several powers not given to the Senate. If a bill is passed by the [lower house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_house) (the House of Representatives) but is voted down by the [upper house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_house) (the [Senate](217779.md)) the House of Representatives can override the decision of the Senate by a two-thirds vote in the affirmative. However, in the case of [treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty), the [budget](4360909.md), and the selection of the prime minister, the Senate can only delay passage, but not block the legislation. As a result, the House of Representatives is considered the more powerful house.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10]

Members of the House of Representatives, who are elected to a maximum of four years, sit for a shorter term than senators, who are elected to full six-year terms. The lower house can also be dissolved by the Prime Minister or the passage of a nonconfidence motion, while the Senate cannot be dissolved. Thus the House of Representatives is considered to be more sensitive to public opinion, and is termed the "lower house". While the legislative term is nominally 4 years, early elections for the lower house are very common, and the median lifespan of postwar legislatures has in practice been around 3 years.

## History

| \[icon] | This section **needs expansion** with: history after 1947. You can help by [adding missing information](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=House_of_Representatives_\(Japan\)\&action=edit\&section=). *(August 2020)* |

### Meiji period (1890–1912)



[Kuroda Kiyotaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuroda_Kiyotaka), [Satsuma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satsuma_Domain) [samurai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samurai) and prime minister in the late 1880s, coined the term "transcendentalism" (超然主義, chōzen shugi) on the occasion of the promulgation of the Meiji Constitution in 1889. The oligarchs should try to "transcend" electoral politics and govern without partisan majorities the House of Representatives.



[Itō Hirobumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It%C5%8D_Hirobumi), a [Chōshū](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C5%8Dsh%C5%AB_Domain) samurai, member of the [House of Peers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Peers_\(Japan\)) and [prime minister of Japan](24833.md) on three non-consecutive occasions between 1885 and 1901. He was a main architect of the Imperial Constitution which created the Imperial Diet. When the oligarchs attempts to govern "transcendentally" mostly failed in the 1890s, he saw the necessity for permanent allies among elected political parties.



[Hara Takashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hara_Takashi), although born a [Morioka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morioka_Domain) noble, made his career as commoner-politician and became the first and one of only three prime ministers from the House of Representatives in the Empire.

See also: [Meiji Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration)

The Japanese parliament, then known as the Imperial Diet, was established in 1890 as a result of the 1889 [Meiji Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Constitution). It was modeled on the parliaments of several Western countries, particularly the [German Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire) and the United Kingdom, because of the [Emperor Meiji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Meiji)'s westernizing reforms. The Imperial Diet consisted of [two chambers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicameralism), the elected House of Representatives which was the lower house, and the [House of Peers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Peers_\(Japan\)) which was the upper house. This format was similar to the [House of Lords](13658.md) in the [Westminster system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_system), or the [Herrenhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_House_of_Lords) in [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussia), where the upper house represented the [aristocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristocracy_\(government\)).

Both houses, and also the Emperor, had to agree on legislation, and even at the height of party-based constitutional government, the House of Peers could simply vote down bills deemed too liberal by the [Meiji oligarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_oligarchy), such as the introduction of [women's suffrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_suffrage), increases in local autonomy, or trade union rights. The [prime minister](24833.md) and his government served [at the Emperor's pleasure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/At_Her_Majesty's_pleasure), and could not be removed by the Imperial Diet. However, the right to vote on, and if necessary to block, legislation including the budget, gave the House of Representatives leverage to force the government into negotiations. After an early period of frequent confrontation and temporary alliances between the cabinet and political parties in the lower house, parts of the Meiji oligarchy more sympathetic to political parties around [Itō Hirobumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It%C5%8D_Hirobumi) and parts of the liberal parties eventually formed a more permanent alliance, in the form of the [Rikken Seiyūkai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rikken_Seiy%C5%ABkai) in 1900. The confidence of the House of Representatives was never a formal requirement to govern, but between 1905 and 1918, only one cabinet took office that did not enjoy majority support in the House of Representatives.[^cite_note-22]

### Taisho and early Showa periods (1912–1937)

See also: [Politics of the Empire of Japan (1914–1944)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_the_Empire_of_Japan_\(1914%E2%80%931944\))

During the [Taishō political crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taish%C5%8D_political_crisis) in 1913, a [no-confidence vote](208101.md)[^cite_note-23] against the [third Katsura government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katsura_Tar%C5%8D#Third_administration), accompanied by major demonstrations outside the Diet, was followed shortly by resignation. Subsequently, in the period often referred to as [Taishō democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taish%C5%8D_democracy), it became increasingly customary to appoint many ministers, including several prime ministers, from the House of Representatives – [Hara Takashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hara_Takashi) was the first commoner to become prime minister in 1918.

In the same year, the [Rice Riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice_Riots_of_1918) had confronted the government with an unprecedented scale of domestic unrest, and a [German Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Revolution_of_1918%E2%80%931919) brought the Prusso-German monarchy to an end, the very system Meiji oligarchs had used as the main model for the Meiji constitution to consolidate and preserve Imperial power. Even [Yamagata Aritomo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamagata_Aritomo) and other oligarchs that had been fundamentally opposed to political parties, became more inclined to cooperate with the still mainly [bourgeoisie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourgeoisie) parties, to prevent a rise of socialism or other movements that might threaten Imperial rule. Socialist parties would not be represented in significant numbers in the lower house until the 1930s.

The initially very high [census suffrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffrage#Census_suffrage) requirement was reduced several times, until the introduction of [universal male suffrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_manhood_suffrage) in 1925. The electoral system to the House of Representatives was also fundamentally changed several times: between systems of "small" mostly single- and few multi-member electoral districts (1890s, 1920, 1924), "medium" mostly multi-member districts (1928–1942) and "large" electoral districts (usually only one, rarely two city and one counties district per prefecture; 1900s and 1910s), using [first-past-the-post](261709.md) in single-member districts, [plurality-at-large voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plurality-at-large_voting) (1890s) or [single non-transferable vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_non-transferable_vote) in the multi-member districts.

Influence of the House of Representatives on the government increased, and the party cabinets of the 1920s brought Japan apparently closer to a [parliamentary system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_system) of government, and there were several reforms to the upper house in 1925. However, the balance of powers between the two houses and the influential role of extra-constitutional actors such as the [Genrō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genr%C5%8D) (who still selected the prime minister) or the military (that had brought down several cabinets) remained in essence untouched. Within a year of the [Japanese invasion of Manchuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_invasion_of_Manchuria) in September 1931, a series of assassinations and coup attempts followed. Party governments were replaced by [governments of "national unity"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_unity_government) (*kyokoku itchi*) which were dominated by nobles, bureaucrats and increasingly the military.

### World War II and aftermath (1937–1947)

See also: [Politics of the Empire of Japan (1914–1944)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_the_Empire_of_Japan_\(1914%E2%80%931944\))

After the [Marco Polo Bridge Incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marco_Polo_Bridge_Incident) and the start of war in 1937, the influence of the Imperial Diet was further diminished, though never eliminated, by special laws such as the [National Mobilization Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Mobilization_Law) and expanded powers for cabinet agencies such as the Planning Board.[^cite_note-24] The House of Representatives in the Empire had a four-year term and could be dissolved by the [Emperor](10110.md). In contrast, members of the House of Peers had either [life tenure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_tenure) (subject to revocation by the Emperor) or a seven-year term in the case of members elected in mutual peerage elections among the three lower peerage ranks, top taxpayer and academic peerage elections. During the war, the term of the members of the House of Representatives elected in the last pre-war [election of 1937](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1937_Japanese_general_election) was extended by one year.

In the [1946 election to the House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1946_Japanese_general_election), held under the U.S.-led [Allied occupation of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Japan), [women's suffrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_suffrage) was introduced, and a system of "large" electoral districts (one or two per prefecture) with [limited voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited_voting) was used. A change in the electoral law in April 1945 had for the first time allocated 30 seats to the established colonies of the Empire: Karafuto ([Sakhalin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakhalin)), [Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_under_Japanese_rule), and Chōsen ([Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea_under_Japanese_rule)); but this change was never implemented. Similarly, Korea and Taiwan were granted several appointed members of the [House of Peers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Peers_\(Japan\)) in 1945.

In 1946, both houses of the Imperial Diet (together with the Emperor) passed the [postwar constitutional amendment](62699.md) which took effect in 1947. The Imperial Diet was renamed the National Diet, the House of Peers was replaced by an elected upper house called the [House of Councillors](217779.md), and the House of Representatives would now be able to override the upper house in important matters. The constitution also gave the Diet exclusive legislative authority, without involvement of the Emperor, and explicitly made the cabinet responsible to the Diet and requires that the [prime minister](24833.md) has the support of a majority in the House of Representatives.

### Late Showa period (1947–1989)



[Shigeru Yoshida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shigeru_Yoshida), prime minister 1946–1947 as a member of the House of Peers and 1948–1954 as a member of the House of Representatives, oversaw the end of the [American-led occupation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Japan) and the beginning of the [Japanese economic miracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_economic_miracle).

See also: [Post-occupation Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-occupation_Japan)

The Diet first met under the new constitution on May 20, 1947.[^cite_note-25] Four days later, [Tetsu Katayama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetsu_Katayama) of the [Democratic Socialist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Socialist_Party_\(Japan\)) became Japan's first socialist prime minister and the first since the introduction of parliamentarianism.

Since the end of US rule in 1952, it has been the norm that the prime minister dissolves the House of Representatives before its 4-year term expires. Only once, in 1976, did the House last a full 4 years. It has become tradition to give nicknames to each dissolution, usually referencing a major political issue or controversy. One infamous example was on March 14, 1953, when [Shigeru Yoshida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shigeru_Yoshida) dissolved the House and called for new election, after he name called people during a meeting of the budget committee. This came to be known as the *["you idiot" dissolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%22you_idiot%22_dissolution?action=edit\&redlink=1)*.[^cite_note-26]

In 1955, prime minister [Ichirō Hatoyama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ichir%C5%8D_Hatoyama) oversaw the creation of the [Liberal Democratic Party](164231.md) (LDP), which since [his third government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Ichir%C5%8D_Hatoyama_Cabinet) has dominated Japanese politics under the [1955 System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1955_System). The LDP would govern without interruption for nearly 40 years until the [1993 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1993_Japanese_general_election), alone save for a three-year coalition government with the [New Liberal Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Liberal_Club) after the [1983 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1983_Japanese_general_election).

Hatoyama planned to change the electoral system to [first past the post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_past_the_post), introducing a bill to that effect in March 1956. This was met with opposition from the [Socialist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Socialist_Party), who criticized Hatoyama's plan as a "[Hatomander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatomander)". The bill passed the House of Representatives in May 1956, but was never voted on by the House of Councillors. Electoral reform came into vogue again in the 1970s, but [Kakuei Tanaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kakuei_Tanaka)'s plan met opposition internally in the LDP and never came to a vote in either chamber of the Diet.

### Heisei and Reiwa periods (since 1989)



[Shinzo Abe](2634434.md), prime minister 2006–2007 and again 2012–2020, was the longest-serving PM in Japanese history.

Japan entered a lengthy recession in the 1990s (see [Lost Decades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Decades)), which many people blamed on the LDP. In the [1993 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1993_Japanese_general_election), the party lost power for the first time under the 1955 System, when an eight-party coalition led by [Morihiro Hosokawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morihiro_Hosokawa) of the [Japan New Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_New_Party) were able to form [a government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hosokawa_Cabinet). This government fell apart after nine months, and was succeeded by the [Hata Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hata_Cabinet), another short-lived non-LDP government. The LDP returned to power in 1994 with the [Murayama Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murayama_Cabinet), this time in a coalition with their old rivals the Socialists, whose leader [Tomiichi Murayama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomiichi_Murayama) became prime minister.

As with party colleagues [Ichirō Hatoyama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ichir%C5%8D_Hatoyama) and [Kakuei Tanaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kakuei_Tanaka) before him, prime minister [Toshiki Kaifu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toshiki_Kaifu) of the LDP unsuccessfully tried to reform the electoral system in 1991. However, the Morihiro Hosokawa government got the [1994 Japanese electoral reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1994_Japanese_electoral_reform) through the Diet, introducing a [parallel voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_voting) system which went into effect at the [next election in 1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Japanese_general_election). Under this system, which remains in effect as of 2022, 300 (since reduced to 289) members of the House of Representatives are elected using [first past the post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_past_the_post) in single-member constituencies, while 200 (since reduced to 176) members are elected in regional blocs using [party-list proportional representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party-list_proportional_representation).

Prime minister [Junichiro Koizumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junichiro_Koizumi) introduced a bill to the House of Representatives in 2006 on changing the [Imperial Household Law](6300387.md) to allow a woman to ascend the [Chrysanthemum Throne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrysanthemum_Throne) (see [Japanese imperial succession debate](3167373.md)), but he withdrew the bill after the birth of [Prince Hisahito of Akishino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Hisahito_of_Akishino) the same year. The LDP once again lost power at the [2009 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Japanese_general_election), when the [Democratic Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Party_of_Japan)-led [Hatoyama Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukio_Hatoyama_Cabinet) took over, followed in rapid succession by the [Kan Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kan_Cabinet) and [Noda Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noda_Cabinet). The LDP and [Komeito](163859.md), who had formed a two-party government between 2003 and 2009, came to power again after the [2012 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Japanese_general_election). [Shinzo Abe](2634434.md), who had previously led the [First Abe Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Abe_Cabinet), was prime minister for another stint lasting eight years, stepping down for health reasons in 2020. He was succeeded by [Yoshihide Suga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshihide_Suga).

When the Emperor [Akihito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akihito) expressed interest in abdicating, the Diet passed the [Emperor Abdication Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Abdication_Law) in 2017, allowing for the [2019 Japanese imperial transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Japanese_imperial_transition) and the succession to the throne of [Naruhito](266108.md). In December 2022, in light of the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine) and increased [military cooperation between China and Russia](30964882.md#Military_relations), prime minister [Fumio Kishida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fumio_Kishida) announced plans to significantly increase funding for the [Japan Self-Defense Forces](2785204.md); this was continued under his successor as prime minister, [Shigeru Ishiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shigeru_Ishiba).
