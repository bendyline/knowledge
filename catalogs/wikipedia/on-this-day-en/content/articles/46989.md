The **Grand Canyon**[^cite_note-6] is a steep-sided [canyon](78478.md) carved by the [Colorado River](180968.md) in [Arizona](21883824.md), United States. The Grand Canyon is 277 miles (446 km) long, up to 18 miles (29 km) wide and attains a depth of over a mile (6,093 feet or 1,857 meters).[^cite_note-Kiver-7]<sup>: 902 </sup>

The canyon and adjacent rim are contained within [Grand Canyon National Park](46986.md), the [Kaibab National Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaibab_National_Forest), [Grand Canyon–Parashant National Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canyon%E2%80%93Parashant_National_Monument), the [Hualapai Indian Reservation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hualapai), the [Havasupai Indian Reservation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havasupai_Indian_Reservation) and the [Navajo Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navajo_Nation). President [Theodore Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Roosevelt) was a major proponent of the preservation of the Grand Canyon area and visited it on numerous occasions to hunt and enjoy the scenery.

Nearly two billion years of Earth's geological history have been exposed as the Colorado River and its tributaries cut their [channels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream_channel) through layer after layer of rock while the [Colorado Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_Plateau) was [uplifted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tectonic_uplift).[^cite_note-Witze-8][^cite_note-npsgeol-9] While some aspects about the history of incision of the canyon are debated by geologists,[^cite_note-Witze-8][^cite_note-carvingGC-10] several recent studies support the hypothesis that the Colorado River established its course through the area about 5 to 6 million years ago.[^cite_note-Karlstrom-1][^cite_note-Witze-8][^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12] Since that time, the Colorado River has driven the down-cutting of the tributaries and retreat of the cliffs, simultaneously deepening and widening the canyon.

For thousands of years, the area has been continuously inhabited by Native Americans, who built settlements within the canyon and its many caves. The [Pueblo people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puebloan_peoples) considered the Grand Canyon a holy site, and made [pilgrimages](23763.md) to it.[^cite_note-13] The first European known to have viewed the Grand Canyon was [García López de Cárdenas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garc%C3%ADa_L%C3%B3pez_de_C%C3%A1rdenas) from Spain, who arrived in 1540.[^cite_note-14]

## Geography



Image of the Grand Canyon and surrounding area taken from the [International Space Station](15043.md)



The Grand Canyon from an airplane, with the Colorado River visible



Grand Canyon, Arizona, Nevada, [Lake Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Powell) to [Lake Mead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Mead), June 27, 2017, [Sentinel-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentinel-2) true-color satellite image. Scale 1\:450,000.

The Grand Canyon is a [river valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_valley) in the [Colorado Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_Plateau) that exposes uplifted [Proterozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proterozoic) and [Paleozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleozoic) [strata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratum),[^cite_note-Ranney-15] and it is also one of the six distinct physiographic sections of the Colorado Plateau province.[^cite_note-Leydsman_McGinty-16] Even though it is not the deepest canyon on land in the world ([Kali Gandaki Gorge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kali_Gandaki_Gorge) in Nepal is much deeper[^cite_note-17]), the Grand Canyon is known for its visually overwhelming size and its intricate and colorful landscape. Geologically, it is significant because of the thick sequence of ancient rocks that are well preserved and exposed in the walls of the canyon. These rock layers record much of the early geologic history of the North American continent.[^cite_note-Witze-8]

Uplift associated with mountain formation later moved these sediments thousands of feet upward and created the Colorado Plateau. The higher elevation has also resulted in greater precipitation in the Colorado River drainage area, but not enough to change the Grand Canyon area from being semi-arid.[^cite_note-18] The uplift of the Colorado Plateau is uneven, and the [Kaibab Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaibab_Plateau) that the Grand Canyon bisects is over one thousand feet (300 m) higher at the North Rim than at the South Rim. Almost all runoff from the North Rim (which also gets more rain and snow) flows toward the Grand Canyon, while much of the runoff on the plateau behind the South Rim flows away from the canyon (following the general tilt).[^cite_note-Witze-8] The result is deeper and longer tributary washes and canyons on the north side and shorter and steeper side canyons on the south side.[^cite_note-Kiver-7]<sup>: 406 </sup>

Temperatures on the North Rim are generally lower than those on the South Rim because of the greater elevation (averaging 8,000 feet or 2,400 meters above sea level).[^cite_note-NPSnatsci-19] Heavy rains are common on both rims during the summer months. Access to the North Rim via the primary route leading to the canyon ([State Route 67](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_State_Route_67)) is limited during the winter season due to road closures.[^cite_note-off-season(jump-link)-20]

## Geology

Main article: [Geology of the Grand Canyon area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology_of_the_Grand_Canyon_area)



Diagram showing the placement, age and thickness of the rock units exposed in the Grand Canyon



Rockfalls in recent times, along with other [mass wasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_wasting), have further widened the canyon

The Grand Canyon is part of the Colorado River basin, which has developed over the past 70 million years.[^cite_note-Witze-8][^cite_note-21] For more than 150 years, scientists have gathered data, proposed new ideas, and debated sometimes contentious theories about the geologic origins of the Grand Canyon and the Colorado River. Formation of the Grand Canyon and the Colorado River may involve a complex history in which multiple factors and geologic processes have interacted over time and in different locations.[^cite_note-Ranney-15]

In the most recent round of "old river" vs. "young river" controversy, researchers have challenged estimates that had placed the age of the canyon at 5–6 million years. The research has aroused considerable controversy because it suggests a substantial departure from prior widely supported scientific consensus.[^cite_note-Witze-8][^cite_note-Johnson1-22][^cite_note-Johnson2-23]

In a 2008 study, Victor Polyak examined caves near the Grand Canyon and placed their origins about 17 million years ago. The study, which was published in the journal *[Science](193513.md)* in 2008, used [uranium-lead dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-lead_dating) to analyze [calcite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcite) deposits found on the walls of nine caves throughout the canyon.[^cite_note-UNM_Study-24][^cite_note-NPR2008-25][^cite_note-Polyak-26]

In another 2008 study, Rebecca Flowers reported on [apatite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apatite) (U-Th)/He [thermochronometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermochronology) results suggesting that parts of the Grand Canyon had reached a depth near to the modern depth around 20 million years ago.[^cite_note-NYT-29-Nov-2012-27][^cite_note-Flowers-28] In a subsequent study published in the journal *Science* in 2012, she suggested that the western part of the Grand Canyon could be as old as 70 million years.[^cite_note-Achenbach-29][^cite_note-NYT-29-Nov-2012-27][^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31]

The emerging scientific consensus is that the canyon is made up of multiple segments which formed at different times and eventually connected to become the waterway now traversed by the Colorado River. Of the three central segments, the "Hurricane" was formed 50–70 million years ago, and the "Eastern Grand Canyon" was cut 15–25 million years ago. In contrast, the "Marble Canyon" and "Westernmost Grand Canyon" segments at the ends of the canyon were carved in the last five to six million years.[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33]

The major geologic exposures in the Grand Canyon range in age from the two-billion-year-old [Vishnu Schist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu_Schist) at the bottom of the Inner Gorge to the 270-million-year-old [Kaibab Limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaibab_Limestone) on the Rim. Within that there is a gap, the [Great Unconformity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Unconformity), between 1.75 billion and 1.25 billion years ago for which no deposits are present.[^cite_note-Witze-8]

Then, between 1.25 billion and 730 million years ago, intermittent sediments began to form the [Grand Canyon Supergroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canyon_Supergroup).[^cite_note-Witze-8] Many of the [formations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formation_\(geology\)) were deposited in warm shallow seas, near-shore environments (such as beaches), and swamps as the seashore repeatedly advanced and retreated over the edge of a proto-North America. Major exceptions include the Permian [Coconino Sandstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coconino_Sandstone), which contains abundant geological evidence of [aeolian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeolian_processes) sand dune deposition. Several parts of the [Supai Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology_of_the_Grand_Canyon_area#Supai_Group) also were deposited in non-marine environments.

The great depth of the Grand Canyon and especially the height of its strata (most of which formed below sea level) can be attributed to 5,000–10,000 feet (1,500–3,000 m) of uplift of the Colorado Plateau, starting about 65 million years ago (during the [Laramide orogeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laramide_orogeny)). This uplift has steepened the [stream gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream_gradient) of the Colorado River and its tributaries, which in turn has increased their speed and thus their ability to cut through rock (see the [elevation summary](180968.md#Elevation_summary) of the Colorado River for present conditions).

Weather conditions during the [ice ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternary_glaciation) also increased the amount of water in the Colorado River drainage system. The ancestral Colorado River responded by cutting its channel faster and deeper.

The base level and course of the Colorado River (or its ancestral equivalent) changed 5.3 million years ago when the [Gulf of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_California) opened and [lowered the river's base level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_rejuvenation) (its lowest point). This increased the rate of erosion and cut nearly all of the Grand Canyon's current depth by 1.2 million years ago. The [terraced walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluvial_terrace) of the canyon were created by [differential erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_erosion).[^cite_note-34]

Between 100,000 and 3 million years ago, [volcanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology_of_the_Grand_Canyon_area#Volcanic_activity_in_the_western_canyon) activity deposited ash and lava over the area, which at times completely obstructed the river. These volcanic rocks are the youngest in the canyon.

## Hydrology



&#x20;Ribbon Falls, near the [North Kaibab Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Kaibab_Trail), is produced by ground water reaching the surface

Groundwater flow in the Grand Canyon region is an active area of study. [Groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater) forms when rain soaks down into the earth and reaches the [water table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_table). The composition of the earth in a given area determines its [permeability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permeability_\(earth_sciences\)), the ease with which water flows through it. Sand is more permeable than clay. Less permeable rock layers composed of clay can block the passage of water and are known as [aquitards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquitard). More permeable areas of rock that hold and transport groundwater underground are known as [aquifers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquifers). An area of water bounded by two aquitards is called a confined aquifer, while water below the surface and above an aquitard is called an unconfined aquifer.[^cite_note-Wesoloski-35]

The different geologic levels of the Grand Canyon have created two major aquifers where groundwater collects. The higher C-aquifer is an unconfined aquifer. It collects groundwater that seeps through the Kaibab and Toroweap Formations into the Coconino Sandstone. Below it, the Permian Hermit Formation and Supai Group provide a dense barrier. Groundwater from the C-aquifer can flow laterally, appearing as seeps along the canyon walls at the base of the Coconino Sandstone but can also descend vertically through fault zones to recharge the underlying confined R-aquifer. The R-aquifer, also known as the Red Wall Muav aquifer, is a [karst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karst) aquifer. It involves an area of substantial fracturing through the Redwall Limestone, Temple Butte Formation and Cambrian Muav Limestone of the Tonto Group. Five individual systems flow through the R-aquifer and compose the regional groundwater-flow system which drains into the Grand Canyon: Kaibab, Uinkaret-Kanab, Marble-Shinumo, Cataract, and Blue Spring.[^cite_note-Knight-36]

The flow of groundwater in the Grand Canyon region is influenced in multiple ways by [geologic faults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_\(geology\)) and [folds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fold_\(geology\)). Discharge from the R-aquifer appears as springs and seeps in both the Grand Canyon and tributary canyons. Springs discharge to the Grand Canyon in areas of lower Paleozoic carbonates, and are associated with geologic faults and fractures. Fractures are believed to provide dominant pathways both for vertical circulation in the Paleozoic section, and for lateral collection and transport of water to springs deep in the canyons. The largest springs discharge from the R-aquifer. A smaller number of springs discharge at lower rates from the C-aquifer. Much of the water that could potentially recharge the aquifers is likely released as springs rather than reaching the aquifers.[^cite_note-Knight-36]

Studies of the chemical composition of groundwater at sites across the Grand Canyon region indicate that groundwater contains a fraction of modern water (post-1950), and that many springs have a mix of modern water and older groundwater. Estimated mean ages for South Rim groundwater range from 6 years old (San Francisco Peaks) to nearly 20,000 years old (Bar Four well, Blue Spring). Groundwater age in the South Rim groundwater system also correlates to longitude, with age increasing from east to west from Red Canyon to Boucher springs. Surprisingly, the Canyon Mine Observation well is more similar to Redwall-Muav aquifer wells (R-aquifer) than to the Coconino C-aquifer. This suggests the possibility of a hydrologic connection or similar recharge sources for that hydrologic position. Old groundwater from Havasupai well may have a similar source to the Havasu Spring upwelling from the Redwall-Muav aquifer. Sites with younger estimated mean ages tend to be associated with the unconfined Coconino aquifer. They may recharge quickly as a result of snowmelt, run-off and local precipitation. It is likely that the deeper confined R-aquifer relies primarily on snowmelt from the San Francisco Peaks to recharge.[^cite_note-Solder-37]

Other research has tried to relate groundwater flow paths to possible levels of risk for contamination and identify vulnerability regions for the underlying aquifers. Almost half of the Kaibab plateau's surface was associated with high to very high vulnerability of the unconfined Coconino aquifer (C-aquifer), while about a fifth of the Kaibab Plateau was estimated to be an area of high vulnerability for the Redwall-Muav aquifer (R-aquifer).[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-Jones-39]

## History

Main article: [History of the Grand Canyon area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Grand_Canyon_area)



[Ancestral Puebloan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancestral_Puebloans) granaries at Nankoweap Creek

### Native Americans

The [Ancestral Puebloans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancestral_Puebloans) were a Native American culture centered on the present-day [Four Corners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Corners) area of the United States. They were the first people known to live in the Grand Canyon area. The cultural group has often been referred to in archaeology as the Anasazi, although the term is not preferred by the modern [Puebloan peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puebloan_peoples).[^cite_note-40] The word "Anasazi" is [Navajo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navajo_language) for "enemy ancestors" or "alien ancestors".[^cite_note-41]

Archaeologists still debate when this distinct culture emerged. The current consensus, based on terminology defined by the [Pecos Classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pecos_Classification), suggests their emergence was around 1200 [BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Before_Common_Era) during the [Basketmaker II Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pecos_Classification#Early_Basketmaker_II_Era). Beginning with the earliest explorations and excavations, researchers have believed that the Ancestral Puebloans are ancestors of the modern [Pueblo peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pueblo_people).[^cite_note-UNCO-42]

In addition to the Ancestral Puebloans, a number of distinct cultures have inhabited the Grand Canyon area. The [Cohonina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohonina) lived to the west of the Grand Canyon, between 500 and 1200 CE.[^cite_note-Tufts-43][^cite_note-44] The Cohonina were ancestors of the [Yuman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuman), [Havasupai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havasupai), and [Hualapai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hualapai) peoples who inhabit the area today.[^cite_note-McGregor1951-45]

The [Sinagua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinagua) were a cultural group occupying an area to the southeast of the Grand Canyon, between the [Little Colorado River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Colorado_River) and the [Salt River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_River_\(Arizona\)), between approximately 500 and 1425 CE. The Sinagua may have been ancestors of several [Hopi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopi) clans.

By the time of the arrival of Europeans in the 16th century, newer cultures had evolved. The Hualapai inhabit a 100-mile (160 km) stretch along the pine-clad southern side of the Grand Canyon. The Havasupai have been living in the area near Cataract Canyon since the beginning of the 13th century, occupying an area the size of [Delaware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware).[^cite_note-Whiting-46] The Southern [Paiutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Paiute) live in what is now southern [Utah](31716.md) and northern Arizona. The [Navajo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navajo_people), or Diné, live in a wide area stretching from the [San Francisco Peaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Peaks) eastwards towards the Four Corners. Archaeological and linguistic evidence suggests the Navajo descended from the [Athabaskan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athabaskan_language) people near [Great Slave Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Slave_Lake), Canada, who migrated after the 11th century.[^cite_note-47] In the mythology of some [Third Mesa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopi_Reservation#Third_Mesa) Hopi communities, the Grand Canyon was the location humankind arose out of the [Third World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopi_mythology#Four_Worlds) from a [sipapu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sipapu).[^cite_note-48]

### European arrival and settlement

#### Spanish explorers



*La conquista del Colorado* (2017), by [Augusto Ferrer-Dalmau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augusto_Ferrer-Dalmau), depicts [Francisco Vázquez de Coronado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_V%C3%A1zquez_de_Coronado)'s 1540–1542 expedition. [García López de Cárdenas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garc%C3%ADa_L%C3%B3pez_de_C%C3%A1rdenas) can be seen overlooking the Grand Canyon.

In September 1540, under orders from the [conquistador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conquistador) [Francisco Vázquez de Coronado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_V%C3%A1zquez_de_Coronado) to search for the fabled [Seven Cities of Cibola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Cities_of_Cibola), Captain [García López de Cárdenas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garc%C3%ADa_L%C3%B3pez_de_C%C3%A1rdenas), along with Hopi guides and a small group of Spanish soldiers, traveled to the south rim of the Grand Canyon between Desert View and Moran Point. Pablo de Melgrossa, Juan Galeras, and a third soldier descended some one third of the way into the canyon until they were forced to return because of lack of water. In their report, they noted that some of the rocks in the canyon were "bigger than the great tower of Seville, [Giralda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giralda)".[^cite_note-greatabyss-49] It is speculated that their Hopi guides likely knew routes to the canyon floor, but may have been reluctant to lead the Spanish to the river. No Europeans visited the canyon again for more than two hundred years.

Fathers [Francisco Atanasio Domínguez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_Atanasio_Dom%C3%ADnguez) and [Silvestre Vélez de Escalante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silvestre_V%C3%A9lez_de_Escalante) were two Spanish priests who, with a group of Spanish soldiers, [explored southern Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dom%C3%ADnguez%E2%80%93Escalante_expedition) and traveled along the north rim of the canyon in Glen and Marble Canyons in search of a route from [Santa Fe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Fe,_New_Mexico) to California in 1776. They eventually found a crossing, formerly known as the "Crossing of the Fathers", that today lies under [Lake Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Powell).

Also in 1776, Fray Francisco Garces, a Franciscan missionary, spent a week near Havasupai unsuccessfully attempting to convert a band of Native Americans to Christianity. He described the canyon as "profound".[^cite_note-greatabyss-49]

#### American exploration

James Ohio Pattie, along with a group of American trappers and mountain men, may have been the next European to reach the canyon, in 1826.[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-51]

[Jacob Hamblin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Hamblin), a [Mormon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mormon) missionary, was sent by [Brigham Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigham_Young) in the 1850s to locate suitable river crossing sites in the canyon. Building good relations with local Hualapai and white settlers, he reached the [Crossing of the Fathers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossing_of_the_Fathers),[^cite_note-52] crossed the location that would become [Lees Ferry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lees_Ferry) on a raft in 1858[^cite_note-Lee-53] and [Pearce Ferry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearce_Ferry,_Lake_Mead) (later operated by, and named for, [Harrison Pearce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Pearce)).[^cite_note-54] He also acted as an advisor to [John Wesley Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wesley_Powell), before his second expedition to the Grand Canyon, serving as a diplomat between Powell and the local native tribes to ensure the safety of his party.[^cite_note-Lee-53]



[William Bell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Bell_\(photographer\))'s photograph of the Grand Canyon, taken in 1872 as part of the [Wheeler expedition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheeler_Survey)

In 1857, [Edward Fitzgerald Beale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Fitzgerald_Beale) was superintendent of an expedition to survey a wagon road along the 35th parallel from [Fort Defiance, Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Defiance,_Arizona) to the Colorado River. He led a small party of men in search of water on the [Coconino Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coconino_Plateau) near the canyon's south rim. On September 19, near present-day National Canyon, they came upon what May Humphreys Stacey described in his journal as "a wonderful canyon; four thousand feet deep. Everybody in the party admitted that he never before saw anything to match or equal this astonishing natural curiosity."[^cite_note-55]

Also in 1857, the U.S. [War Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_War) asked Lieutenant Joseph Ives to lead an expedition to assess the feasibility of an up-river navigation from the Gulf of California. On December 31, 1857, Ives embarked from the mouth of the Colorado in the [stern wheeler steamboat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paddle_steamer) [*Explorer*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explorer_\(sternwheeler\)). His party reached the lower end of Black Canyon on March 8, 1858, then continued on by rowboat past the mouth of the Virgin River after the *Explorer* struck a rock.[^cite_note-56][^cite_note-57] Ives led his party east into the canyon – they may have been the first Europeans to travel the Diamond Creek drainage.[^cite_note-58] In his "Report Upon the Colorado River of the West" to the Senate in 1861 Ives states that "The marvellous story of [Cardinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garcia_Lopez_de_Cardenas), that had formed for so long a time the only record concerning this rather mythical locality, was rather magnified than detracted from by the accounts of one or two trappers, who professed to have seen the cañon".[^cite_note-59]



Noon rest in [Marble Canyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble_Canyon), second Powell Expedition, 1872

According to the *[San Francisco Herald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Herald)*, in a series of articles run in 1853, Captain Joseph R. Walker in January 1851 with his nephew James T. Walker and six men, traveled up the Colorado River to a point where it joined the Virgin River and continued east into Arizona, traveling along the Grand Canyon and making short exploratory side trips along the way. Walker is reported to have said he wanted to visit the "Moqui" (Hopi) Indians. who he had met briefly before and found exceptionally interesting.[^cite_note-Gilbert-60]

In 1858, [John Strong Newberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Strong_Newberry) became probably the first geologist to visit the Grand Canyon.[^cite_note-61]

In 1869, Major [John Wesley Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wesley_Powell) set out to explore the Colorado River and the Grand Canyon in the first expedition down the canyon. Powell ordered a shipwright to build four reinforced Whitewall rowboats from Chicago and had them shipped west on the newly completed Continental railroad.[^cite_note-Stojka-62] He hired nine men, including his brother Walter, and collected provisions for ten months. They set out from [Green River, Wyoming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_River,_Wyoming), on May 24.[^cite_note-Utah-63] On June 7, they lost one of their boats, 1/3 of their food, and other badly needed supplies: as a result the team eventually had to subsist on starvation rations.[^cite_note-Howland-64] Passing through (or portaging around) a series of dangerous rapids, the group passed down the [Green River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_River_\(Colorado_River_tributary\)), reaching its [confluence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confluence) with the Colorado River, near present-day [Moab, Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moab,_Utah), on July 17. Continuing on down the Colorado River, the party encountered more rapids and falls.[^cite_note-Ribokas-65]

On August 28, 1869, faced with what some felt to be impassable rapids, three men left the expedition on foot in an attempt to reach a settlement 75 miles (121 km) away. Ironically, the remaining members went safely through the rapids on August 29, 1869, while Seneca Howland, Oramel Howland, and William H. Dunn were murdered.[^cite_note-Howland-64] The area through which the three men traveled was marked by tensions between farming and hunting [Shivwits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shivwits) and incoming [Mormon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mormon) settlers. Which group was responsible for killing the three men has been hotly debated.[^cite_note-Waterman-66] Powell himself visited the area the following year, and was told (through a Mormon interpreter) that the Shivwits had mistakenly killed the men, believing them to be prospectors who had murdered an Indian woman. He chose to smoke a peace pipe with them.[^cite_note-Howland-64][^cite_note-67] Powell went on to become the first Director of the U.S. Bureau of Ethnology of the Smithsonian Institution (1879–1902) and the second Director of the US Geological Survey (1881–1894).[^cite_note-68] He was the first to use the term "Grand Canyon", in 1871; previously it had been called the "Big Canyon".[^cite_note-69]

In 1889, Frank M. Brown wanted to build a railroad along the Colorado River to carry coal. He, his chief engineer [Robert Brewster Stanton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Brewster_Stanton), and 14 others started to explore the Grand Canyon in poorly designed cedar wood boats, with no life preservers. Brown drowned in an accident near [Marble Canyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble_Canyon): Stanton made new boats and proceeded to explore the Colorado all of the way to the [Gulf of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_California).[^cite_note-70]

The Grand Canyon became an official national monument in 1908 and a national park in 1919.[^cite_note-71]

#### Federal protection: National monument and park



Railway Station and [El Tovar Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Tovar_Hotel), Facing WNW, [Grand Canyon Village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canyon_Village). 1994 photo, [HAER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HAER)&#x20;



[Fred Harvey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Harvey_Company) postcard, The Towering Cliffs above [Hermit Camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermit_Canyon)&#x20;

U.S. President Theodore Roosevelt visited the Grand Canyon in 1903. An avid outdoorsman and staunch conservationist, Roosevelt established the Grand Canyon Game Preserve on November 28, 1906. Livestock grazing was reduced, but predators such as mountain lions, eagles, and wolves were eradicated. Roosevelt along with other members of his conservation group, the [Boone and Crockett Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boone_and_Crockett_Club) helped form the [National Parks Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Parks_Conservation_Association), which in turn lobbied for the [Antiquities Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiquities_Act) of 1906 which gave Roosevelt the power to create national monuments. Once the act was passed, Roosevelt immediately added adjacent [national forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Forest) lands and redesignated the preserve a [U.S. National Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._National_Monument) on January 11, 1908.[^cite_note-85] Opponents such as land and mining claim holders blocked efforts to reclassify the monument as a [U.S. National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._National_Park) for 11 years. Grand Canyon National Park was finally established as the 17th U.S. National Park by an Act of Congress signed into law by President [Woodrow Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodrow_Wilson) on February 26, 1919.[^cite_note-Tufts-43]

The federal government administrators who manage park resources face many challenges. These include issues related to the recent reintroduction into the wild of the highly endangered [California condor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_condor), air tour overflight noise levels, water rights[^cite_note-Denetclaw-86] and management disputes, and forest fire management.

The canyon's ecosystem was permanently changed after the construction of the [Glen Canyon Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glen_Canyon_Dam) in 1963. Average flood levels dropped from 85,000 to 8,000 cubic ft/sec. In the absence of natural flooding, sandbars and beaches eroded and invasive species began to displace native species. Federal officials started releasing floods in the Grand Canyon in hopes of restoring its [ecosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem) beginning with 1996, 2004, and 2008.[^cite_note-Witze-8][^cite_note-87] In 2018, the Department of Interior started experimenting with "adaptive management" of the Glen Canyon Dam, using a High-Flow Experiment (HFE) water release to shift volumes of sand and monitoring effects such as the dispersal of invasive [tamarisk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamarisk) seeds.[^cite_note-88] In 2022, extreme drought dropped Lake Powell's water levels, delaying a planned water release to maintain Glen Canyon Dam's [hydropower](14073.md) generation.[^cite_note-Hager-89]

## Weather

&#x20;Weather in the Grand Canyon varies according to elevation. The forested rims are high enough to receive winter snowfall, but along the Colorado River in the Inner Gorge, temperatures are similar to those found in Tucson and other low elevation desert locations in Arizona. Conditions in the Grand Canyon region are generally dry, but substantial [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation_\(meteorology\)) occurs twice annually, during seasonal pattern shifts in winter (when Pacific storms usually deliver widespread, moderate rain and high-elevation snow to the region from the west) and in late summer (due to the [North American Monsoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Monsoon), which delivers waves of moisture from the southeast, causing dramatic, localized thunderstorms fueled by the heat of the day).[^cite_note-gcweather-90] Average annual precipitation on the South Rim is less than 16 inches (41 cm), with 60 inches (150 cm) of snow; the higher North Rim usually receives 27 inches (69 cm) of moisture, with a typical snowfall of 144 inches (370 cm); and Phantom Ranch, far below the canyon's rims along the Colorado River at 2,500 feet (760 m) gets just 8 inches (200 mm) of rain, and snow is a rarity.



Grand Canyon covered with snow

Temperatures vary wildly throughout the year, with summer highs within the Inner Gorge commonly exceeding 100 °F (38 °C) and winter minimum temperatures sometimes falling below zero degrees Fahrenheit (−18 °C) along the canyon's rims.[^cite_note-gcweather-90] Visitors are often surprised by these potentially extreme conditions, and this, along with the high altitude of the canyon's rims, can lead to unpleasant side effects such as [dehydration](155056.md), [sunburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunburn), and [hypothermia](146879.md).

Weather conditions can greatly affect hiking and canyon exploration, and visitors should obtain accurate forecasts because of hazards posed by exposure to extreme temperatures, winter storms and late summer monsoons. While the park service posts weather information at gates and visitor centers, this is a rough approximation only, and should not be relied upon for trip planning. For accurate weather in the canyon, hikers should consult the [National Weather Service](286989.md)'s [NOAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NOAA) weather radio or the official National Weather Service website.[^cite_note-91]

The National Weather Service has had a cooperative station on the South Rim since 1903. The record high temperature on the South Rim was 105 °F (41 °C) on June 26, 1974, and the record low temperature was −20 °F (−29 °C) on January 1, 1919, February 1, 1985, and December 23, 1990.[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94]

### Air quality



Smoke from prescribed fires on the South Rim, as seen from Yavapai Point, April 2007.

[^cite_note-95]

As of 1999 the Grand Canyon area had some of the cleanest air in the United States.[^cite_note-Project_MOHAVE_Final_Report-96]<sup>: p.5-2 </sup>[^cite_note-IMPROVE_viewer-97] However, air quality in the area can be affected by air pollution from coal-fired power plants, mining, oil and gas, vehicles, and urban and industrial pollution from nearby states, California and Mexico.[^cite_note-98][^cite_note-99][^cite_note-Nicla-100] Events such as forest fires and [dust storms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_storm) in the [Southwest](179571.md) can have a considerable impact.[^cite_note-Brewer-101]

Differences in visibility tend to be seasonal: best during the winter and poorest during the summer.[^cite_note-AQ2002-102] Winter cold fronts tend to carry clean, crisp air from the northwest, a less populated area. However they can also carry pollution from nearby mines and power plants. The average visibility during the winter is 160 miles, with the potential to reach 210 miles under ideal conditions. In the summer, prevailing winds from the southwest carry pollution from major urban and industrial centers, and visibility on average is only 100 miles. Within the canyon itself, smog can be trapped.[^cite_note-103]

Air quality and visibility in the canyon are affected mainly by sulfates, soils, and organics. The sulfates largely result from urban emissions in southern California, borne on the prevailing [westerly winds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westerlies) throughout much of the year, and emissions from Arizona's copper smelter region, borne on southerly or southeasterly winds during the [monsoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Monsoon). Airborne soils originate with windy conditions and [road dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust#Roads). Organic particles result from vehicle emissions, long-range transport from urban areas, and forest fires, as well as from [VOCs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volatile_organic_compound) emitted by vegetation in the surrounding forests. Nitrates, carried in from urban areas, stationary sources, and vehicle emissions; as well as [black carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_carbon) from forest fires and vehicle emissions, also contribute to a lesser extent.[^cite_note-IMPROVE_viewer-97][^cite_note-intro_vis-104]<sup>: 26, 49–51 </sup>

A number of actions have been taken to preserve and further improve air quality and visibility at the canyon. In 1990, amendments to the [Clean Air Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_Air_Act_\(United_States\)) established the Grand Canyon Visibility Transport Commission (GCVTC) to advise the [US EPA](58666.md) on strategies for protecting visual air quality on the [Colorado Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_Plateau). The GCVTC released its final report in 1996 and initiated the Western Regional Air Partnership (WRAP), a partnership of state, tribal and federal agencies to help coordinate the implementation of the commission's recommendations.[^cite_note-105][^cite_note-106]

In 1999, the [Regional Haze Rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Best_Available_Retrofit_Technology) established a goal of restoring visibility in national parks and wilderness areas (Class 1 areas), such as the Grand Canyon, to natural background levels by 2064. Subsequent revisions to the rule provide specific requirements for making reasonable progress toward that goal.[^cite_note-107]



Natural fog sometimes fills the canyon, during [temperature inversions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature_inversion)

In the early 1990s, studies indicated that emissions of SO<sub>2</sub>, a sulfate precursor, from the [Navajo Generating Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navajo_Generating_Station) affected visibility in the canyon, mainly in the winter.[^cite_note-NGS_SO2_visibility_history-108]<sup>: p.C-2, C-6 </sup> As a result, scrubbers were added to the plant's three units in 1997 through 1999 to reduce SO2 emissions by 90% on an annual average.[^cite_note-Reitze-109] Before and after observations showed a wintertime decrease of 33% for particulate sulfur (Sp), improving visibility.[^cite_note-Green-110] The plant also installed low-NO<sub>x</sub> SOFA burners in 2009–2011, reducing emissions of NO<sub>x</sub>, a nitrate precursor, by 40%[^cite_note-Hurlbut-111] The plant shut down completely in 2019.[^cite_note-Nicla-100] Emissions from the [Mohave Generating Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohave_Power_Station) to the west were similarly found to affect visibility in the canyon. The plant was required to have installed SO<sub>2</sub> scrubbers, but was instead shut down in 2005, eliminating its emissions.[^cite_note-112]

[Prescribed fires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_burn) are typically conducted in the spring and fall in the forests adjacent to the canyon to reduce the potential for severe forest fires and resulting smoke conditions. Although prescribed fires also affect air quality, the controlled conditions allow the use of management techniques to minimize their impact.[^cite_note-113][^cite_note-114]<sup>: p.86, 93 </sup>

## Climate change



Grand Canyon Climate Summary Chart (NPS)

Due to the increase of greenhouse gases, temperatures have steadily risen making recent years the warmest in the century-long record.[^cite_note-\:0-115] Temperatures have increased in Arizona by 2.1 degrees Fahrenheit since 1895.[^cite_note-116] From 1916 to 2014, naturalized streamflow in the Upper Colorado River Basin decreased by 16.5%, in spite of a slight increase of +1.4% in annual precipitation over the same period.[^cite_note-Xiao-117] Increases in temperature correlate to increased evaporation, loss of reflective snow, and decreases in snowpack, reducing available water in the Colorado River by an estimated 9.3% per degree Celsius of warming.[^cite_note-Childs-118][^cite_note-Milly-119]

Over the last 30 years, the South Rim of the Grand Canyon has averaged 13.4 inches of rainfall per year, while the North Rim has averaged 24 inches.[^cite_note-120] The first dual distribution water system in the United States was built on the South Rim, treating and reclaiming wastewater for nonpotable reuse as early as 1926. Early on, water for human use was brought in by tankers, and pumped up from a spring deeper in the canyon.[^cite_note-Okun-121] In the 1960s, the Transcanyon Pipeline was built to carry water from the North Rim down into the canyon and back up to the South Rim.[^cite_note-Repanshek-122] Every day 500,000 gallons of water are pumped from underground springs on the North Rim through the pipeline to supply the more heavily developed South Rim with water.[^cite_note-Witze-8][^cite_note-O'Sullivan-123]

As water consumption continues to soar due to increasing visitation in the park, both the sustainability of the water supply and the condition of the pipeline are in question.[^cite_note-Witze-8][^cite_note-O'Sullivan-123] As of 2019, plans to replace the aging 16-mile aluminum pipeline were proposed.[^cite_note-Repanshek-122] Another proposal, from the Bureau of Reclamation, recommended that the park drill a well into the Redwall-Muav aquifer to meet the increasing water consumption. However, the aquifer's recharge rate stands to decline over time due to decreases in precipitation and snowpack.[^cite_note-124] Some effects of over pumping aquifers include land subsidence, reduction in rivers and lakes, disrupted riparian systems, and poor water quality.[^cite_note-125]

In the [National Park Service](161535.md)'s "Climate Action Plan," goals were set to reduce greenhouse gases 30 percent below 2008 levels by 2020 and plan and implement measures that best allow the park to adapt to the impacts of [climate change](5042951.md). Strategies to accomplish these goals included: "reduce \[greenhouse gases] emissions resulting from activities within and by the park, developing and implementing a plan to adapt to current and future impacts of climate change, increase climate change education and outreach, and monitor progress and identify areas for improvement".[^cite_note-\:0-115]

## Biology and ecology

### Plants

There are approximately 1,737 known species of [vascular plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vascular_plant), 167 species of [fungi](19178965.md), 64 species of [moss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moss), and 195 species of [lichen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lichen) found in Grand Canyon National Park.[^cite_note-NPSplants-126] This variety is largely due to the 8,000 foot (2,400 m) elevation change from the Colorado River up to the highest point on the North Rim.[^cite_note-NPSplants-126] Grand Canyon boasts a dozen endemic plants (known only within the Park's boundaries) while only ten percent of the Park's flora is exotic.[^cite_note-NPSplants-126] Sixty-three plants found here have been given special status by the [U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service](75289.md).[^cite_note-NPSplants-126]

Grand Canyon Clouds time lapse VP8

The [Mojave Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mojave_Desert) influences the western sections of the canyon, [Sonoran Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonoran_Desert) vegetation covers the eastern sections, and [ponderosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinus_ponderosa) and [pinyon pine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyon_pine) forests grow on both rims.[^cite_note-NPSanimals-127]

Natural seeps and springs percolating out of the canyon walls are home to 11% of all the plant species found in the Grand Canyon.[^cite_note-NPSanimals-127] The canyon itself can act as a connection between the east and the west by providing corridors of appropriate habitat along its length.[^cite_note-NPSanimals-127] The canyon can also be a genetic barrier to some species, like the [tassel-eared squirrel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abert's_squirrel).[^cite_note-NPSanimals-127]

The aspect, or direction a slope faces, also plays a major role in adding diversity to the Grand Canyon. North-facing slopes receive about one-third the normal amount of sunlight, so plants growing there are similar to plants found at higher elevations, or in more northern latitudes.[^cite_note-NPSanimals-127] The south-facing slopes receive the full amount of sunlight and are covered in vegetation typical of the Sonoran Desert.[^cite_note-NPSanimals-127]

### Animals



A [bighorn ram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bighorn_sheep) perched on a cliff in the Grand Canyon

Of the 90 [mammal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammal) species found along the Colorado River corridor, 18 are [rodents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodent) and 22 are bats.[^cite_note-NPSmammals-128]

### Life zones and communities

The Park contains several major ecosystems.[^cite_note-NPSnatsci-19] Its great biological diversity can be attributed to the presence of five of the seven [life zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_zone) and three of the four desert types in North America.[^cite_note-NPSnatsci-19] The five life zones represented are the Lower Sonoran, Upper Sonoran, Transition, Canadian, and Hudsonian.[^cite_note-NPSnatsci-19] This is equivalent to traveling from Mexico to Canada. Differences in elevation and the resulting variations in climate are the major factors that form the various life zones and communities in and around the canyon. Grand Canyon National Park contains 129 vegetation communities, and the composition and distribution of plant species are influenced by climate, geomorphology and geology.[^cite_note-NPSplants-126]

#### Lower Sonoran

The Lower Sonoran life zone spans from the Colorado River up to 3,500 feet (1,100 m). Along the Colorado River and its perennial tributaries, a riparian community exists.[^cite_note-NPSplants-126] [Coyote willow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salix_exigua), [arrowweed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluchea_sericea), [seep-willow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baccharis_salicifolia), [western honey mesquite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosopis_glandulosa), [catclaw acacia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senegalia_greggii), and exotic [tamarisk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamarix) (saltcedar) are the predominant species.[^cite_note-NPSplants-126] Hanging gardens, seeps and springs often contain rare plants such as the white-flowering western [redbud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cercis_occidentalis), [stream orchid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epipactis_gigantea), and *[Flaveria mcdougallii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flaveria_mcdougallii)*.[^cite_note-NPSplants-126] Endangered fish in the river include the [humpback chub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humpback_chub) and the [razorback sucker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Razorback_sucker).[^cite_note-NPSfish-129]



A [bighorn ewe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bighorn_sheep) at the Grand Canyon, 2008

The three most common amphibians in these riparian communities are the [canyon tree frog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canyon_tree_frog), [red-spotted toad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red-spotted_toad), and Woodhouse's [Rocky Mountain toad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_Mountain_toad).[^cite_note-NPSamphib-130] [Leopard frogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopard_frog) are very rare in the Colorado River corridor; they have undergone major declines and have not been seen in the Canyon in several years.[^cite_note-NPSamphib-130] There are 33 crustacean species found in the Colorado River and its tributaries within Grand Canyon National Park. Of these 33, 16 are considered true zooplankton organisms.[^cite_note-NPScrustaceans-131]

Only 48 bird species regularly nest along the river, while others use the river as a migration corridor or as an overwintering habitat. The [bald eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bald_eagle) is one species that uses the river corridor as a winter habitat.[^cite_note-NPSbirds-132]

[River otters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_river_otter) may have disappeared from the park in the late 20th century, and [muskrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muskrat) are extremely rare.[^cite_note-NPSmammals-128] [Beavers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaver) cut willows, cottonwoods, and shrubs for food, and can significantly affect the riparian vegetation.[^cite_note-NPSmammals-128] Other rodents, such as [antelope squirrels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antelope_squirrel) and [pocket mice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perognathinae), are mostly omnivorous, using many different vegetation types.[^cite_note-NPSmammals-128] Grand Canyon bats typically roost in desert uplands but forage on the abundance of insects along the river and its tributaries.[^cite_note-NPSmammals-128] In addition to bats, [coyotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coyote), [ringtails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ringtail), and [spotted skunks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spotted_skunk) are the most numerous riparian predators and prey on invertebrates, rodents, and reptiles.[^cite_note-NPSmammals-128]

[Raccoons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raccoon), [weasels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weasel), [bobcats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobcat), [gray foxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gray_fox), and [mountain lions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cougar) are also present but are much rarer.[^cite_note-NPSmammals-128] Ungulate species such as [mule deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mule_deer) and [desert bighorn sheep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert_bighorn_sheep) frequent the river corridor. Since the removal of 500 [feral burros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donkey) in the early 1980s, bighorn sheep numbers have rebounded.[^cite_note-NPSmammals-128] Mule deer are generally not permanent residents along the river but travel down from the rim when food and water resources there become scarce.[^cite_note-NPSmammals-128]

The insect species commonly found in the river corridor and tributaries are [midges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midge), [caddisflies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caddisfly), [mayflies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayfly), [stoneflies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plecoptera), [black flies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_fly), [mites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mite), [beetles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beetle), [butterflies](48338.md), [moths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moth), and [fire ants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_ant).[^cite_note-NPSbugs-133] Numerous species of spiders and several species of [scorpions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scorpion), including the [bark scorpion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_bark_scorpion) and the [giant desert hairy scorpion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadrurus_arizonensis), inhabit the riparian zone.[^cite_note-NPSbugs-133]

Eleven aquatic and 26 terrestrial species of [mollusks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mollusca) have been identified in and around Grand Canyon National Park.[^cite_note-NPSmollusks-134] Of the aquatic species, two are [bivalves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bivalvia) (clams) and nine are [gastropods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastropoda) (snails).[^cite_note-NPSmollusks-134] Twenty-six species of terrestrial gastropods have been identified, primarily [land snails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_snail) and [slugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slug).[^cite_note-NPSmollusks-134]

There are approximately 41 reptile species in Grand Canyon National Park. Ten are considered common along the river corridor and include lizards and snakes.[^cite_note-NPSreptiles-135] Lizard density tends to be highest along the stretch of land between the water's edge and the beginning of the upland desert community.[^cite_note-NPSreptiles-135] The two largest lizards in the canyon are [gila monsters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gila_monster) and [chuckwallas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuckwalla).[^cite_note-NPSreptiles-135] Many snake species, which are not directly dependent on surface water, may be found both within the inner gorge and the Colorado River corridor. Six [rattlesnake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rattlesnake) species have been recorded in the park.[^cite_note-NPSreptiles-135]

Above the river corridor a desert scrub community, composed of North American desert flora, thrives. Typical warm desert species such as [creosote bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larrea_tridentata), [white bursage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambrosia_dumosa), [brittlebush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encelia_farinosa), catclaw acacia, [ocotillo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fouquieria_splendens), [mariola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenium_incanum), western honey mesquite, [four-winged saltbush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atriplex_canescens), [big sagebrush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemisia_tridentata), [blackbrush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coleogyne), and [rubber rabbitbrush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ericameria_nauseosa) grow in this community.[^cite_note-NPSplants-126] The mammalian fauna in the woodland scrub community consists of 50 species, mostly rodents and bats.[^cite_note-NPSmammals-128] Three of the five Park [woodrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pack_rat) species live in the desert scrub community.[^cite_note-NPSmammals-128]

Except for the [western banded gecko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_banded_gecko), which seems to be distributed only near water along the Colorado River, all of the reptiles found near the river also appear in the uplands, but in lower densities.[^cite_note-NPSreptiles-135] The [desert gopher tortoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gopherus), a threatened species, inhabits the desert scrublands in the western end of the park.[^cite_note-NPSreptiles-135]

Some of the common insects found at elevations above 2,000 feet (610 m) are [orange paper wasps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vespidae), [honey bees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honey_bee), black flies, [tarantula hawks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarantula_hawk), [stink bugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentatomidae), beetles, [black ants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_garden_ant), and [monarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarch_butterfly) and [swallowtail butterflies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swallowtail_butterfly).[^cite_note-NPSbugs-133] [Solifugids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solifugae), [wood spiders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huntsman_spider), [garden spiders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argiope_aurantia), [black widow spiders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latrodectus), and [tarantulas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarantula) can be found in the desert scrub and higher elevations.[^cite_note-NPSbugs-133]

#### Upper Sonoran and Transition



A [California condor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_condor) in flight, photographed from [Navajo Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navajo_Bridge) at [Marble Canyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble_Canyon), 2008. Wild condors are numbered to aid wildlife researchers. As of April 2009, there were 172 wild California condors known.

The Upper Sonoran Life Zone includes most of the inner canyon and South Rim at elevations from 3,500 to 7,000 feet (1,100 to 2,100 m).[^cite_note-NPSanimals-127] This zone is generally dominated by blackbrush, [sagebrush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sagebrush), and pinyon-juniper woodlands. Elevations of 3,500 to 4,000 feet (1,100 to 1,200 m) are in the Mojave Desert Scrub community of the Upper Sonoran. This community is dominated by the four-winged saltbush and creosote bush; other important plants include [Utah agave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agave_utahensis), [narrowleaf mesquite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salix_exigua), [ratany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krameria), catclaw acacia, and various [cacti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cactus) species.[^cite_note-NPSanimals-127]

Approximately 30 bird species breed primarily in the desert uplands and cliffs of the inner canyon.[^cite_note-NPSbirds-132] Virtually all bird species present breed in other suitable habitats throughout the Sonoran and Mohave deserts.[^cite_note-NPSbirds-132] The abundance of bats, swifts, and riparian birds provides ample food for [peregrines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peregrine_falcon), and suitable [eyrie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_nest) sites are plentiful along the steep canyon walls. Also, several critically endangered [California condors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_condor) have made the eastern part of the Park their home, having been re-introduced to the Colorado Plateau on the Arizona Strip earlier.[^cite_note-NPSbirds-132]



Red-tailed Hawk flying at the south rim of Grand Canyon



An [elk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elk) searching for water at Grand Canyon National Park in 2018

The conifer forests provide habitat for 52 mammal species.[^cite_note-NPSmammals-128] [Porcupines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porcupine), [shrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrew), [red squirrels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_squirrel), [tassel-eared Kaibab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaibab_squirrel) and [Abert's squirrels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abert's_squirrel), [black bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_black_bear), mule deer, and [elk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elk) are found at the park's higher elevations on the Kaibab Plateau.[^cite_note-NPSmammals-128]

Above the desert scrub and up to 6,200 feet (1,900 m) is a pinyon pine forest and one seed juniper woodland.[^cite_note-NPSplants-126] Within this woodland one can find big sagebrush, [snakeweed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gutierrezia), [Mormon tea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephedra_\(plant\)), Utah agave, banana and narrowleaf [Yucca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yucca), [winterfat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winterfat), [Indian ricegrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_ricegrass), [dropseed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dropseed), and [needlegrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Needlegrass_\(disambiguation\)).[^cite_note-NPSplants-126] There are a variety of snakes and lizards here, but one species of reptile, the [mountain short-horned lizard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_short-horned_lizard), is a particularly abundant inhabitant of the piñon-juniper and ponderosa pine forests.[^cite_note-NPSreptiles-135]

Ponderosa pine forests grow at elevations between 6,500 and 8,200 feet (2,000 and 2,500 m), on both North and South rims in the Transition life zone.[^cite_note-NPSplants-126] The South Rim includes species such as gray fox, mule deer, bighorn sheep, [rock squirrels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_squirrel), pinyon pine, and [Utah juniper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utah_juniper).[^cite_note-NPSanimals-127] Additional species such as [Gambel oak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gambel_oak), [New Mexico locust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Mexico_locust), [mountain mahogany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_mahogany), [elderberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elderberry), [creeping mahonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creeping_mahonia), and [fescue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fescue) have been identified in these forests.[^cite_note-NPSplants-126] The [Utah tiger salamander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiger_salamander) and the [Great Basin spadefoot toad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Basin_spadefoot) are two amphibians that are common in the rim forests.[^cite_note-NPSamphib-130] Of the approximately 90 bird species that breed in the coniferous forests, 51 are summer residents and at least 15 of these are known to be neotropical migrants.[^cite_note-NPSbirds-132]

#### Canadian and Hudsonian

Elevations of 8,200 to 9,000 feet (2,500 to 2,700 m) are in the Canadian Life Zone, which includes the North Rim and the [Kaibab Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaibab_Plateau).[^cite_note-NPSanimals-127] Spruce-fir forests characterized by [Engelmann spruce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picea_engelmannii), [blue spruce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_spruce), [Douglas fir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_fir), [white fir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abies_concolor), [aspen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspen), and [mountain ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rowan), along with several species of perennial grasses, [groundsels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundsel), [yarrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achillea_millefolium), [cinquefoil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potentilla), [lupines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lupinus), [sedges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyperaceae), and [asters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aster_\(genus\)), grow in this sub-alpine climate.[^cite_note-NPSplants-126] Mountain lions, Kaibab squirrels, and [American goshawks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_goshawk) are found here.[^cite_note-NPSanimals-127]

Montane meadows and subalpine grassland communities of the Hudsonian life zone are rare and located only on the North Rim.[^cite_note-NPSplants-126] Both are typified by many grass species. Some of these grasses include [blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouteloua_gracilis) and [black grama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouteloua_eriopoda), [big galleta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilaria_rigida), [Indian ricegrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oryzopsis_hymenoides), and [three-awns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristida).[^cite_note-NPSplants-126] The wettest areas support sedges and [forbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forb).[^cite_note-NPSplants-126]



View from the South Rim

## Tourism

Grand Canyon National Park is one of the world's premier natural attractions, attracting about five million visitors per year. According to a survey conducted of visitors to the canyon between September 2003 through August 2004, 83% were from the United States: California (12%), Arizona (9%), Texas (5%), Florida (3%) and New York (4%) represented the top domestic visitors. Seventeen percent of visitors were from outside the United States; the most prominently represented nations were the United Kingdom (3%), Canada (4%), Japan (2%), Germany (2%) and the Netherlands (1%).[^cite_note-136] The South Rim is open all year, weather permitting. The North Rim is generally open mid-May to mid-October.[^cite_note-137]

### Activities



Grand Canyon regional map



Rafters in the Grand Canyon pass one of the rapids of the Colorado River

There are many viewing points on all sides of the canyon, with the most accessible locations inside Grand Canyon National Park and the [Hualapai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hualapai) Indian Reservation. About 90% of tourists visit the South Rim from [Arizona State Route 64](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_State_Route_64), which averages 7,000 feet (2,100 m) above sea level. The South Rim houses two visitor centers, a geological museum, art gallery/bookstore, and other attractions near [Grand Canyon Village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canyon_Village,_Arizona). Further east along the South Rim is the [Desert View Watchtower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert_View_Watchtower). Grand Canyon National Park also has a visitor center in an easterly area known as the North Rim, via [Arizona State Route 67](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_State_Route_67); the center and the road are closed in winter months.[^cite_note-138] On the Hualapai reservation, [Grand Canyon West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canyon_West) is the third major area with tourist services, about 125 mi (201 km) driving from Las Vegas and about 250 miles (400 km) by road from Grand Canyon Village at the South Rim.[^cite_note-Skywalk-139] The tribe opened the glass-bottomed [Grand Canyon Skywalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canyon_Skywalk) in 2007, which has since attracted "thousands of visitors a year, most from Las Vegas".[^cite_note-nyt14-140] Also at Grand Canyon West is [Guano Point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guano_Point), which features a narrow peninsula surrounded on all sides by steep dropoffs. On the north side of the canyon to the west, the [Grand Canyon–Parashant National Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canyon%E2%80%93Parashant_National_Monument) has no paved roads and no visitor services.

Rafting, hiking, running, and helicopter tours are also popular in the canyon area. The floor of the valley is accessible by foot, muleback, or by boat or raft from upriver. Hiking down to the river and back up to the rim in one day is discouraged by park officials because of the distance, steep and rocky trails, change in elevation, and danger of [heat exhaustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_exhaustion) from the much higher temperatures at the bottom. Rescues are required annually of unsuccessful rim-to-river-to-rim travelers. Nevertheless, hundreds of fit and experienced hikers complete the trip every year.

Camping on the North and South rims is generally restricted to established campgrounds and reservations are highly recommended, especially at the busier South Rim. There is at large camping available along many parts of the North Rim managed by [Kaibab National Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaibab_National_Forest). North Rim campsites are only open seasonally due to road closures from weather and winter snowpack. All overnight camping below the rim requires a backcountry permit from the Backcountry Office (BCO).[^cite_note-backcountry-141] Each year Grand Canyon National Park receives approximately 30,000 requests for backcountry permits. The park issues 13,000 permits, and close to 40,000 people camp overnight.[^cite_note-backcountry-141] The earliest a permit application is accepted is the first of the month, four months before the proposed start month.

A 6-minute video of a flight over the Grand Canyon (view in high quality)

Tourists wishing for a more vertical perspective can go skydiving, board helicopters and small airplanes in Boulder, Las Vegas, Phoenix, [Grand Canyon West Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canyon_West_Airport), and [Grand Canyon National Park Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canyon_National_Park_Airport) (seven miles from the South Rim) for canyon flyovers. Scenic flights are no longer allowed to fly within 1,500 feet (460 m) of the rim within the national park because of a late 1990s crash.[^cite_note-142] The last aerial video footage from below the rim was filmed in 1984. However, some helicopter flights land on the Havasupai and Hualapai Indian Reservations within the Grand Canyon (outside of the park boundaries).

In 2016, skydiving at the Grand Canyon become possible with the first Grand Canyon Skydiving operation opening up at the Grand Canyon National Park Airport, on the South Rim.

In 2014, a developer announced plans to build a multimedia complex on the canyon's rim called the [Grand Canyon Escalade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canyon_Escalade). On 420 acres (170 ha) there would be shops, an IMAX theater, hotels and an RV park. A gondola would enable easy visits to the canyon floor where a "riverwalk" of "connected walkways, an eatery, a tramway station, a seating area and a wastewater package plant" would be situated. On October 31, 2017, the Navajo Nation Council voted against the project.[^cite_note-escaladecancelled-143]

## Fatalities



Grand Canyon rescue helicopter, 1978

About 770 deaths have occurred between the mid-1800s and 2015.[^cite_note-Deathsactivetimes-144][^cite_note-Deathscitylab-145] Of the fatalities that occurred from 1869 to 2001, the most common cause was airplane and helicopter crashes, with 242 deaths (128 of them in the 1956 disaster mentioned below), along with the following: 53 resulted from falls; 65 were attributable to environmental causes, including heat stroke, cardiac arrest, dehydration, and hypothermia; 7 were caught in flash floods; 79 were drowned in the Colorado River; 25 died in freak errors and accidents, including lightning strikes and rock falls; and 23 were the victims of homicides.[^cite_note-146] Nearly all deaths that occur are preventable.[^cite_note-147]

In 1956, the Grand Canyon was the site of the deadliest commercial aviation disaster in history at the time when [two commercial planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1956_Grand_Canyon_mid-air_collision) en route from Los Angeles [collided](7281229.md) at 21,000 feet above the canyon. The disaster killed all 128 passengers and crew members aboard both planes.

On October 3, 2020, former [Major League Baseball](38776.md) player [Charlie Haeger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Haeger) was found dead from a self-inflicted gunshot wound on a canyon trail. He was under investigation for the murder of his ex-girlfriend which had taken place the day before in [Scottsdale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottsdale,_Arizona).[^cite_note-148]

In June 2026, three hikers died from suspected heat-related illnesses within the Inner Canyon region of Grand Canyon National Park in Arizona. The fatalities occurred across two separate incidents amidst a severe summer heatwave, where midday temperatures in shaded sections of the canyon floor regularly exceeded 109 °F (43 °C).[^cite_note-149]
