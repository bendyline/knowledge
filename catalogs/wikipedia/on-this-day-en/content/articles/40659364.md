# Arctic sea ice decline



Decline in arctic sea ice *extent* (area) since 1979



Decline in arctic sea ice *volume* since 1979

[Sea ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_ice) in the [Arctic](36971.md) region has declined in recent decades in area and volume due to [climate change](5042951.md). It has been melting more in summer than it refreezes in winter. [Global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming), caused by [greenhouse gas forcing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiative_forcing#Forcing_due_to_changes_in_atmospheric_gases) is responsible for the decline in Arctic sea ice. The decline of sea ice in the Arctic has been accelerating during the early twenty-first century, with a decline rate of 4.7% per decade (it has declined over 50% since the first satellite records).[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-\:22-2][^cite_note-\:32-3] Summertime sea ice will likely cease to exist sometime during the 21st century.[^cite_note-4]

The region is at its warmest in at least 4,000 years.[^cite_note-5] Furthermore, the Arctic-wide melt season has lengthened at a rate of five days per decade (from 1979 to 2013), dominated by a later autumn freeze-up.[^cite_note-6] The [IPCC Sixth Assessment Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPCC_Sixth_Assessment_Report) (2021) stated that Arctic sea ice area will likely drop below 1 million km<sup>2</sup> in at least some Septembers before 2050.[^cite_note-\:2-7]<sup>: 1249 </sup> In September 2020, the US [National Snow and Ice Data Center](901329.md) reported that the Arctic sea ice in 2020 had melted to an extent of 3.74 million km<sup>2</sup>, its second-smallest extent since records began in 1979.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9] Earth lost 28 trillion tonnes of ice between 1994 and 2017, with Arctic sea ice accounting for 7.6 trillion tonnes of this loss. The rate of ice loss has risen by 57% since the 1990s.[^cite_note-10]

Sea ice loss is one of the main drivers of [Arctic amplification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_amplification), the phenomenon that the Arctic warms faster than the rest of the world under climate change. It is plausible that sea ice decline also makes the [jet stream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_stream) weaker, which would cause more persistent and [extreme weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_weather) in [mid-latitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-latitudes).[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12] Shipping is more often possible in the Arctic now, and will likely increase further. Both the disappearance of sea ice and the resulting possibility of more human activity in the [Arctic Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_Ocean) pose a risk to local wildlife such as [polar bears](24408.md).

One important aspect in understanding sea ice decline is the [Arctic dipole anomaly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_dipole_anomaly). This phenomenon appears to have slowed down the overall loss of sea ice between 2007 and 2021, but such a trend will probably not continue.[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15] In March 2026, the US National Snow and Ice Data Center reported that Arctic sea ice reached about 14.22 million km<sup>2</sup>, which was among the lowest yearly high points recorded in more than four decades of [satellite monitoring](46597.md).[^cite_note-16]

## Definitions

September 2, 2012, the record lowest minimum ever observed in the satellite record

On September 2, 2012, a record lowest minimum occurred: 3,410,000 square kilometres (1,320,000 sq mi).

January 1, 2013, to September 10, 2016, when the sea ice reached its annual minimum extent

The [Arctic Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_Ocean) is the mass of water positioned approximately above latitude 65° N. [Arctic Sea Ice](42282764.md) refers to the area of the Arctic Ocean covered by ice. The *Arctic sea ice minimum* is the day in a given year when Arctic sea ice reaches its smallest extent, occurring at the end of the summer melting season, normally during September. *Arctic Sea ice maximum* is the day of a year when Arctic sea ice reaches its largest extent near the end of the Arctic cold season, normally during March.[^cite_note-auto-18][^cite_note-19] Typical data visualizations for Arctic sea ice include average monthly measurements or graphs for the annual minimum or maximum extent, as shown in the adjacent images.

Sea ice *extent* is defined as the area with at least 15% of sea ice cover; it is more often used as a metric than simple total sea ice area. This metric is used to address uncertainty in distinguishing open sea water from melted water on top of solid ice, which satellite detection methods have difficulty differentiating. This is primarily an issue in summer months.

### Satellite era

An animation of the annual Arctic sea ice minimum with a graph overlay showing the area of the minimum sea ice in millions of square kilometres



2018 sea ice extent visualization

Observation with satellites shows that Arctic sea ice area, extent, and volume have been in decline for a few decades.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26] The amount of multi-year sea ice in the Arctic has declined considerably in recent decades. In 1988, ice that was at least 4 years old accounted for 26% of the Arctic's sea ice. By 2013, ice that age was only 7% of all Arctic sea ice.[^cite_note-27]

Scientists recently measured sixteen-foot (five-meter) [wave heights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_heights) during a storm in the [Beaufort Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaufort_Sea) in mid-August until late October 2012. This is a new phenomenon for the region, since a permanent sea ice cover normally prevents wave formation. Wave action breaks up sea ice, and thus could become a feedback mechanism, driving sea ice decline.[^cite_note-28]

For January 2016, the satellite-based data showed the lowest overall Arctic sea ice extent of any January since records began in 1979. Bob Henson from Wunderground noted:

> Hand in hand with the skimpy ice cover, temperatures across the Arctic have been extraordinarily warm for midwinter. Just before New Year's, a slug of mild air pushed temperatures above freezing to within 200 miles of the North Pole. That warm pulse quickly dissipated, but it was followed by a series of intense North Atlantic cyclones that sent very mild air poleward, in tandem with a strongly negative [Arctic oscillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_oscillation) during the first three weeks of the month.[^cite_note-29]

January 2016's remarkable phase transition of Arctic oscillation was driven by a [rapid tropospheric warming in the Arctic](http://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/2017GL073012/abstract), a pattern that appears to have increased surpassing the so-called [stratospheric sudden warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudden_stratospheric_warming).[^cite_note-30] The previous record of the lowest *extent* of the Arctic Ocean covered by ice in 2012 saw a low of 1.31 million square miles (3.387 million square kilometers). This replaced the previous record set on September 18, 2007, at 1.61 million square miles (4.16 million square kilometers). The minimum extent on 18th Sept 2019 was 1.60 million square miles (4.153 million square kilometers). [^cite_note-31]

A 2018 study of the thickness of sea ice found a decrease of 66% or 2.0 m over the last six decades and a shift from permanent ice to largely seasonal ice cover.[^cite_note-32]

#### Earlier data

The overall trend indicated in the passive microwave record from 1978 through mid-1995 shows that the extent of [Arctic sea ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_sea_ice) is decreasing 2.7% per decade.[^cite_note-johannessen-33] Subsequent work with the satellite passive-microwave data indicates that from late October 1978 through the end of 1996 the extent of [Arctic](36971.md) sea ice decreased by 2.9% per decade.[^cite_note-cavalieri-34] Sea ice extent for the Northern Hemisphere showed a decrease of 3.8% ± 0.3% per decade from November 1978 to December 2012.[^cite_note-35]

## Future ice loss



Arctic sea ice grows and extends through the winter. On March 7, 2017, Arctic sea ice reached its record lowest maximum.

Older and thicker ice changes from 1984 to 2016 (video)

An "ice-free" Arctic Ocean, sometimes referred to as a "blue ocean event" (BOE),[^cite_note-36] is often defined as "having less than 1 million square kilometers of sea ice", because it is very difficult to melt the thick ice around the [Canadian Arctic Archipelago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Arctic_Archipelago).[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39] The IPCC AR5 defines "nearly ice-free conditions" as a sea ice extent of less than 10<sup>6</sup> km<sup>2</sup> for at least five consecutive years.[^cite_note-AR5-40][^cite_note-41]

Estimating the exact year when the Arctic Ocean will become "ice-free" is very difficult, due to the large role of interannual variability in sea ice trends. In Overland and Wang (2013), the authors investigated three different ways of predicting future sea ice levels. They noted that the average of all models used in 2013 was decades behind the observations, and only the subset of models with the most aggressive ice loss was able to match the observations. However, the authors cautioned that there is no guarantee those models would continue to match the observations, and hence that their estimate of ice-free conditions first appearing in 2040s may still be flawed. Thus, they advocated for the use of expert judgement in addition to models to help predict ice-free Arctic events, but they noted that expert judgement could also be done in two different ways: directly extrapolating ice loss trends (which would suggest an ice-free Arctic in 2020) or assuming a slower decline trend punctuated by the occasional "big melt" seasons (such as those of 2007 and 2012) which pushes back the date to 2028 or further into 2030s, depending on the starting assumptions about the timing and the extent of the next "big melt".[^cite_note-\:1-42][^cite_note-43] Consequently, there has been a recent history of competing projections from climate models and from individual experts.

### Climate models



Some of the key feedbacks related to Arctic sea ice loss.

[^cite_note-44]

A 2006 paper examined projections from the [Community Climate System Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_Climate_System_Model) and predicted "near ice-free September conditions by 2040".[^cite_note-45]

A 2009 paper from Muyin Wang and James E. Overland applied observational constraints to the projections from six CMIP3 climate models and estimated nearly ice-free Arctic Ocean around September 2037, with a chance it could happen as early as 2028.[^cite_note-46] In 2012, this pair of researchers repeated the exercise with [CMIP5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CMIP5) models and found that under the highest-emission scenario in CMIP5, [Representative Concentration Pathway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_Concentration_Pathway) 8.5, ice-free September first occurs between 14 and 36 years after the baseline year of 2007, with the median of 28 years (i.e. around 2035).[^cite_note-47]

In 2009, a study using 18 CMIP3 climate models found that they project ice-free Arctic a little before 2100 under a scenario of medium future greenhouse gas emissions.[^cite_note-48] In 2012, a different team used CMIP5 models and their moderate emission scenario, RCP 4.5 (which represents somewhat lower emissions than the scenario in CMIP3), and found that while their mean estimate avoids ice-free Arctic before the end of the century, ice-free conditions in 2045 were within one standard deviation of the mean.[^cite_note-agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com-49]

In 2013, a study compared projections from the best-performing subset of CMIP5 models with the output from all 30 models after it was constrained by the historical ice conditions, and found good agreement between these approaches. Altogether, it projected ice-free September between 2054 and 2058 under RCP 8.5, while under RCP 4.5, Arctic ice gets very close to the ice-free threshold in 2060s, but does not cross it by the end of the century, and stays at an extent of 1.7 million km<sup>2</sup>.[^cite_note-agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com-49]

In 2014, [IPCC Fifth Assessment Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPCC_Fifth_Assessment_Report) indicated a risk of ice-free summer around 2050 under the scenario of highest possible emissions.[^cite_note-AR5-40]

The Third U.S. [National Climate Assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Climate_Assessment) (NCA), released May 6, 2014, reported that the [Arctic Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_Ocean) is expected to be ice free in summer before mid-century. Models that best match historical trends project a nearly ice-free Arctic in the summer by the 2030s.[^cite_note-Melting_Ice-50][^cite_note-51]

In 2021, the [IPCC Sixth Assessment Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPCC_Sixth_Assessment_Report) assessed that there is "high confidence" that the Arctic Ocean will likely become practically ice-free in September before the year 2050 under all [SSP scenarios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shared_Socioeconomic_Pathways).[^cite_note-\:2-7]<sup>: 1247–1251 </sup>

A paper published in 2021 shows that the [CMIP6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CMIP6) models which perform the best at simulating Arctic sea ice trends project the first ice-free conditions around 2035 under SSP5-8.5, which is the scenario of continually accelerating greenhouse gas emissions.[^cite_note-52]

By weighting multiple [CMIP6 projections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupled_Model_Intercomparison_Project#CMIP_Phase_6), the first year of an ice-free Arctic is likely to occur during 2040–2072 under the [SSP3-7.0 scenario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shared_Socioeconomic_Pathways#SSP_temperature_projections_from_the_IPCC_Sixth_Assessment_Report).[^cite_note-53]

## Impacts on the physical environment

### Global climate change

Main article: [Ice–albedo feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice%E2%80%93albedo_feedback)

See also: [Climate change feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_feedback)



The dark ocean surface reflects only 6 percent of incoming solar radiation; instead sea ice reflects 50 to 70 percent.

[^cite_note-54]

Arctic sea ice maintains the cool temperature of the polar regions and it has an important [albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albedo) effect on the climate. Its bright shiny surface reflects sunlight during the Arctic summer; dark ocean surface exposed by the melting ice absorbs more sunlight and becomes warmer, which increases the total [ocean heat content](13165796.md) and helps to drive further sea ice loss during the melting season, as well as potentially delaying its recovery during the [polar night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_night). Arctic ice decline between 1979 and 2011 is estimated to have been responsible for as much [radiative forcing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiative_forcing) as a quarter of [CO<sub>2</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) emissions the same period,[^cite_note-Pistone2019-55] which is equivalent to around 10% of the cumulative CO<sub>2</sub> increase since the start of the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution). When compared to the other [greenhouse gases](21350772.md), it has had the same impact as the cumulative increase in [nitrous oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrous_oxide), and nearly half of the cumulative increase in [methane](18582230.md) concentrations.[^cite_note-56]

The effect of Arctic sea ice decline on global warming will intensify in the future as more and more ice is lost. This feedback has been accounted for by all [CMIP5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CMIP5) and CMIP6 models,[^cite_note-57] and it is included in all warming projections they make, such as the estimated warming by 2100 under each [Representative Concentration Pathway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_Concentration_Pathway) and [Shared Socioeconomic Pathway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shared_Socioeconomic_Pathways). They are also capable of resolving the second-order effects of sea ice loss, such as the effect on [lapse rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapse_rate) feedback, the changes in [water vapor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor) concentrations and regional cloud feedbacks.[^cite_note-58]

### Ice-free summer vs. ice-free winter



As ice melts, the liquid water collects in depressions on the surface and deepens them, forming these melt ponds in the Arctic. These fresh water ponds are separated from the salty sea below and around it, until breaks in the ice merge the two.

In 2021, the [IPCC Sixth Assessment Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPCC_Sixth_Assessment_Report) said with high confidence that there is no [hysteresis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hysteresis) and no [tipping point](16817594.md) in the loss of Arctic summer sea ice.[^cite_note-\:2-7]<sup>: 1247–1251 </sup> This can be explained by the increased influence of stabilizing feedback compared to the ice albedo feedback. Specifically, thinner sea ice leads to increased heat loss in the winter, creating a negative feedback loop. This counteracts the positive [ice albedo feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice%E2%80%93albedo_feedback). As such, sea ice would recover even from a true ice-free summer during the winter, and if the next Arctic summer is less warm, it may avoid another ice-free episode until another similarly warm year down the line. However, higher levels of global warming would delay the recovery from ice-free episodes and make them occur more often and earlier in the summer. A 2018 paper estimated that an ice-free September would occur once in every 40 years under a global warming of 1.5 degrees Celsius, but once in every 8 years under 2 degrees and once in every 1.5 years under 3 degrees.[^cite_note-59]

Very high levels of global warming could eventually prevent Arctic sea ice from reforming during the Arctic winter. This is known as an ice-free winter, and it ultimately amounts to a total of loss of Arctic ice throughout the year. A 2022 assessment found that unlike an ice-free summer, it may represent an irreversible tipping point. It estimated that it is most likely to occur at around 6.3 degrees Celsius, though it could potentially occur as early as 4.5 °C or as late as 8.7 °C. Relative to today's climate, an ice-free winter would add 0.6 degrees, with a regional warming between 0.6 and 1.2 degrees.[^cite_note-McKay2022-60][^cite_note-Explainer-61]

### Amplified Arctic warming

Arctic amplification and its acceleration is strongly tied to declining Arctic sea ice: modelling studies show that strong Arctic amplification only occurs during the months when significant sea ice loss occurs, and that it largely disappears when the simulated ice cover is held fixed.[^cite_note-62] Conversely, the high stability of ice cover in Antarctica, where the thickness of the [East Antarctic ice sheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Antarctic_ice_sheet) allows it to rise nearly 4 km (2.5 mi) above the sea level, means that this continent has not experienced any net warming over the past seven decades:[^cite_note-63] ice loss in the Antarctic and its contribution to [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise) is instead driven entirely by the warming of the [Southern Ocean](20611428.md), which had absorbed 35–43% of the total heat taken up by all oceans between 1970 and 2017.[^cite_note-64]

### Impacts on extreme weather

This section is an excerpt from [Jet stream § Longer-term climatic changes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_stream#Longer-term_climatic_changes).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Jet_stream\&action=edit)]



Meanders ([Rossby waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rossby_wave)) of the northern hemisphere's polar jet stream developing (a), (b); then finally detaching a "drop" of cold air (c). Orange: warmer masses of air; pink: jet stream.

Since the early 2000s, climate models have consistently identified that [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming) will gradually push jet streams poleward. In 2008, this was confirmed by observational evidence, which proved that from 1979 to 2001, the northern jet stream moved northward at an average rate of 2.01 kilometres (1.25 mi) per year, with a similar trend in the southern hemisphere jet stream.[^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66] Climate scientists have hypothesized that the jet stream will also gradually weaken as a result of global warming. Trends such as Arctic sea ice decline, reduced snow cover, [evapotranspiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evapotranspiration) patterns, and other weather anomalies have caused the Arctic to heat up faster than other parts of the globe, in what is known as the [Arctic amplification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_amplification). In 2021–2022, it was found that since 1979, the warming within the [Arctic Circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_Circle) has been nearly four times faster than the global average,[^cite_note-Jet_stream_Rantanen2022-67][^cite_note-Jet_stream_4X2021-68] and some hotspots in the [Barents Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barents_Sea) area warmed up to seven times faster than the global average.[^cite_note-Jet_stream_Isaksen2022-69][^cite_note-Jet_stream_Carrington22-70] While the Arctic remains one of the coldest places on Earth today, the temperature gradient between it and the warmer parts of the globe will continue to diminish with every decade of global warming as the result of this amplification. If this gradient has a strong influence on the jet stream, then it will eventually become weaker and more variable in its course, which would allow more cold air from the [polar vortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_vortex) to leak [mid-latitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-latitudes) and slow the progression of [Rossby waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rossby_wave), leading to more persistent and more [extreme weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_weather).[^cite_note-Jet_stream_Francis_2012-71]

#### Barents Sea ice



Fragmented sea ice within the Barents Sea marginal ice zone southeast of Svalbard, 5 May 2016

Barents Sea is the fastest-warming part of the Arctic, and some assessments now treat Barents sea ice as a separate tipping point from the rest of the Arctic sea ice, suggesting that it could permanently disappear once the global warming exceeds 1.5 degrees.[^cite_note-Explainer-61] This rapid warming also makes it easier to detect any potential connections between the state of sea ice and weather conditions elsewhere than in any other area. The first study proposing a connection between floating ice decline in the Barents Sea and the neighbouring [Kara Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kara_Sea) and more intense winters in Europe was published in 2010,[^cite_note-Petoukhov_2010-72] and there has been extensive research into this subject since then. For instance, a 2019 paper holds BKS ice decline responsible for 44% of the 1995–2014 central Eurasian cooling trend, far more than indicated by the models,[^cite_note-73] while another study from that year suggests that the decline in BKS ice reduces snow cover in the North Eurasia but increases it in central Europe.[^cite_note-74] There are also potential links to summer precipitation:[^cite_note-75] a connection has been proposed between the reduced BKS ice extent in November–December and greater June rainfall over [South China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_China).[^cite_note-76] One paper even identified a connection between Kara Sea ice extent and the ice cover of [Lake Qinghai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Qinghai) on the [Tibetan Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_Plateau).[^cite_note-77]

However, BKS ice research is often subject to the same uncertainty as the broader research into Arctic amplification/whole-Arctic sea ice loss and the jet stream, and is often challenged by the same data.[^cite_note-Song2021-78] Nevertheless, the most recent research still finds connections which are statistically robust,[^cite_note-79] yet non-linear in nature: two separate studies published in 2021 indicate that while autumn BKS ice loss results in cooler Eurasian winters, ice loss during winter makes Eurasian winters warmer:[^cite_note-80] as BKS ice loss accelerates, the risk of more severe Eurasian winter extremes diminishes while heatwave risk in the spring and summer is magnified.[^cite_note-Song2021-78][^cite_note-81]

#### Other possible impacts on weather

In 2019, it was proposed that the reduced sea ice around [Greenland](12118.md) in autumn affects snow cover during the Eurasian winter, and this intensifies Korean summer [monsoon](57630.md), and indirectly affects the Indian summer monsoon.[^cite_note-82]

2021 research suggested that autumn ice loss in the [East Siberian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Siberian_Sea), [Chukchi Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chukchi_Sea) and [Beaufort Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaufort_Sea) can affect spring Eurasian temperature. Autumn sea ice decline of one standard deviation in that region would reduce mean spring temperature over central Russia by nearly 0.8 °C, while increasing the probability of cold anomalies by nearly a third.[^cite_note-83]

### Atmospheric chemistry

See also: [Arctic methane release](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_methane_release)

A 2015 study concluded that Arctic sea ice decline accelerates methane emissions from the Arctic [tundra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tundra), with the emissions for 2005–2010 being around 1.7 million tonnes higher than they would have been with the sea ice at 1981–1990 levels.[^cite_note-84] One of the researchers noted, "The expectation is that with further sea ice decline, temperatures in the Arctic will continue to rise, and so will [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_methane) emissions from northern wetlands."[^cite_note-85]

Cracks in Arctic sea ice expose the seawater to the air, causing mercury in the air to be absorbed into the water. This absorption leads to more mercury, a toxin, entering the [food chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_chain) where it can negatively affect fish and the animals and people who consume them.[^cite_note-86][^cite_note-87] Mercury is part of [Earth's atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth) due to natural causes (see [mercury cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_cycle)) and due to human emissions.[^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89]

### Shipping



Map illustrating various Arctic shipping routes

Economic implications of ice-free summers and the decline in Arctic ice volumes include a greater number of journeys across the Arctic Ocean Shipping lanes during the year. This number has grown from 0 in 1979 to 400–500 along the [Bering strait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bering_strait) and >40 along the [Northern Sea Route](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Sea_Route) in 2013.[^cite_note-90] Traffic through the Arctic Ocean is likely to increase further.[^cite_note-91][^cite_note-92] An early study by [James Hansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hansen) and colleagues suggested in 1981 that a warming of 5 to 10 °C, which they expected as the range of Arctic temperature change corresponding to doubled CO<sub>2</sub> concentrations, could open the [Northwest Passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Passage).[^cite_note-93] A 2016 study concludes that Arctic warming and sea ice decline will lead to "remarkable shifts in trade flows between Asia and Europe, diversion of trade within Europe, heavy shipping traffic in the Arctic and a substantial drop in Suez traffic. Projected shifts in trade also imply substantial pressure on an already threatened Arctic ecosystem."[^cite_note-94]

In August 2017, the first ship traversed the Northern Sea Route without the use of ice-breakers.[^cite_note-\:0-95] Also in 2017, the Finnish icebreaker [MSV *Nordica*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MSV_Nordica) set a record for the earliest crossing of the Northwest Passage.[^cite_note-96] According to the *New York Times*, this forebodes more shipping through the Arctic, as the sea ice melts and makes shipping easier.[^cite_note-\:0-95] A 2016 report by the Copenhagen Business School found that large-scale trans-Arctic shipping will become economically viable by 2040.[^cite_note-97][^cite_note-\:0-95]

## Impacts on wildlife

See also: [Polar bear conservation § Climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_bear_conservation#Climate_change)

The decline of Arctic sea ice will provide humans with access to previously remote coastal zones. As a result, this will lead to an undesirable effect on terrestrial ecosystems and put marine species at risk.[^cite_note-98]

Sea ice decline has been linked to [boreal forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boreal_forest) decline in North America and is assumed to culminate with an intensifying wildfire regime in this region.[^cite_note-99] The annual net primary production of the Eastern Bering Sea was enhanced by 40–50% through phytoplankton blooms during warm years of early sea ice retreat.[^cite_note-100]

Polar bears are turning to alternative food sources because Arctic sea ice melts earlier and freezes later each year. As a result, they have less time to hunt their historically preferred prey of seal pups, and must spend more time on land and hunt other animals.[^cite_note-101] As a result, the diet is less nutritional, which leads to reduced body size and reproduction, thus indicating population decline in polar bears.[^cite_note-102] The Arctic refuge is where polar bears main habitat is to den and the melting arctic sea ice is causing a loss of species. There are only about 900 bears in the Arctic refuge national conservation area.[^cite_note-103]

As arctic ice decays, microorganisms produce substances with various effects on melting and stability. Certain types of bacteria in [rotten ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotten_ice) pores produce polymer-like substances, which may influence the physical properties of the ice. A team from the [University of Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Washington) studying this phenomenon hypothesizes that the polymers may provide a stabilizing effect to the ice.[^cite_note-104] However, other scientists have found algae and other microorganisms help create a substance, [cryoconite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryoconite), or create other pigments that increase rotting and increase the growth of the microorganisms.[^cite_note-\:6-105][^cite_note-106]
