A **blockade** is the use of [military force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_force) to prevent [food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food), [supplies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contraband), [weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materiel), or [communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication), and sometimes people, entering or leaving a country or region. Unlike [sanctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_sanctions) or an [embargo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embargo), which are legal barriers to trade, a blockade is physical. Blockades are distinct from [sieges](26905.md) in that a blockade is usually directed at an entire country or region, rather than a fortress or city, and the objective of a blockade is not necessarily to conquer the area. A blockade is directed at interdicting the movement of shipping and goods, and is therefore distinguished from a siege, which aims at the reduction of a defended place.

A blockading power can seek to cut off all maritime transport from and to the blockaded country, although stopping all land transport to and from an area may also be considered a blockade. Blockades restrict the trading rights of neutral parties, who must submit for inspection for [contraband](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contraband), which the blockading power may define narrowly or broadly, sometimes including food and medicine. In the 20th century, [air power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_power) has also been used to enhance the effectiveness of blockades by halting air traffic within the blockaded airspace.

Close patrol of hostile ports, in order to prevent naval forces from putting to sea, is also referred to as a blockade. When coastal cities or fortresses were besieged from the landward side, the besiegers would often blockade the seaward side as well. Modern blockades have included cutting off electronic communications by [jamming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_jamming) radio signals and severing [undersea cables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_communications_cable). Blockades often result in the starvation of the civilian population, notably during the [blockade of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockade_of_Germany_\(1914%E2%80%931919\)) during [World War I](4764461.md) and the [blockade of Biafra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockade_of_Biafra) during the [Nigerian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigerian_Civil_War).[^cite_note-1]

## History

Although primitive naval blockades have been in use for millennia, early attempts were limited by the time ships were able to stay at sea uninterruptedly.[^cite_note-2] The first successful attempts at establishing a full naval blockade were made by the British [Royal Navy](26061.md) during the [Seven Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Years'_War) (1754–1763) against [France](5843419.md).[^cite_note-3] Following [the 1759 British naval victory at Quiberon Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Quiberon_Bay), which ended any immediate threat of a major [invasion of Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planned_French_invasion_of_Britain_\(1759\)),[^cite_note-4] the British Royal Navy established a [close blockade on the French coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain_in_the_Seven_Years'_War). This starved French ports of commerce, weakening France's economy. [Admiral Edward Hawke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Hawke,_1st_Baron_Hawke) took command of the blockading fleet off [Brest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brest,_France) and extended the blockade to cover the entire French Atlantic coast from [Dunkirk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunkirk) to [Bordeaux](4097.md), and to [Marseille](40888948.md) on France's Mediterranean coast.[^cite_note-5]

During the [North American operations of the Seven Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_and_Indian_War), the British Royal Navy blockaded the French on the other side of the Atlantic, specifically impeding access and supply to the colonies of [New France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_France) on the [St. Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Lawrence_River).[^cite_note-6] Blockades contributed to the [French loss of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conquest_of_New_France) in 1763.[^cite_note-7]

The strategic importance of blockade became increasingly apparent during the [French Revolutionary Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolutionary_Wars) of 1792 to 1802 and in the [Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars) of 1803 to 1815, when the Royal Navy successfully blockaded France, leading to major economic disruptions. The [Union blockade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_blockade) of southern ports was a major factor in the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War) of 1861 to 1865.&#x20;

During [World War I](4764461.md) (1914–1918), [the Allies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_I)[ blockaded the Central Powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockade_of_Germany_\(1914%E2%80%931919\)), depriving them of food-supplies and strategic materials. [Germany's attempted U-boat blockade of Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-boat_Campaign_\(World_War_I\)) caused some shortages, but ultimately failed. A similar outcome followed in [World War II](32927.md) (1939–1945).

Naval strategic thinkers, such as Sir [Julian Corbett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Corbett) (1854–1922) and [Alfred Thayer Mahan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Thayer_Mahan) (1840–1914), wrote that naval conflicts were won primarily by decisive battles, but also by blockade.[^cite_note-8]

## Types of blockade

### Close, distant, and loose blockades

See also: [Maritime Exclusion Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_Exclusion_Zone)

A *close* blockade entails placing warships within sight of the blockaded coast or port, to ensure the immediate interception of any ship entering or leaving. It is both the most effective and the most difficult form of blockade to implement. Difficulties arise because the blockading ships must remain continuously at sea, exposed to storms and hardship, usually far from any support, and vulnerable to sudden attack from the blockaded side, whose ships may stay safe in harbor until they choose to come out.



In World War II, [German](21212.md) [U-boats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-boat) attempted to stop ships carrying food, supplies and [*matériel*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materiel) from reaching the United Kingdom, an example of a *distant blockade*.

In a *distant* blockade, the blockaders stay well away from the blockaded coast and try to intercept any ships going in or out. This may require more ships on station, but they can usually operate closer to their bases, and are at much less risk from enemy raids. This was almost impossible prior to the 16th century due to the nature of the ships used.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmer200522-9]

A *loose* blockade is a close blockade where the blockading ships are withdrawn out of sight from the coast, behind the horizon, but no farther. The object of loose blockade is to lure the enemy into venturing out but to stay close enough to strike.

British [admiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiral) [Horatio Nelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horatio_Nelson) applied a loose blockade at [Cádiz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C3%A1diz) in 1805. The Franco-Spanish fleet under [Pierre-Charles Villeneuve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Charles_Villeneuve) then came out, resulting in the [Battle of Trafalgar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Trafalgar).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEReynolds199898-10]

### Pacific blockade

Until 1827, blockades, as part of [economic warfare](4757142.md), were always a part of a war. This changed when France, [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire) and Britain came to the aid of the [Greek rebels against Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Hellenic_Republic). They blockaded the Turkish-occupied coast, which led to the [battle of Navarino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Navarino). War was never declared, so it is considered the first *pacific* – i.e., peaceful – blockade.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOppenheimRoxburgh20055-11] The first truly [pacific blockade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_blockade), involving no shooting at all, was the British blockade of the [Republic of New Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_New_Granada) in 1837, established to compel New Granada to release an imprisoned British consul.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPacific_Blockade1911433-434-12]

## Legal status



[President Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy) and his advisors discuss the [Cuban Missile Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Missile_Crisis). Part of the US response to [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) missiles being placed in [Cuba](5042481.md) was a naval blockade of the island.

Since 1945, the [United Nations Security Council](31956.md) determines the legal status of blockades. By [article 42](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapter_VII_of_the_United_Nations_Charter) of the [UN Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Charter), the council can also apply blockades.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTED'Amato1995138-13] The UN Charter allows for the right of self-defense but requires that this must be immediately reported to the Security Council to ensure the maintenance of international peace.

According to the not ratified document [San Remo Manual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Remo_Manual) on International Law Applicable to Armed Conflicts at Sea, June 1994,[^cite_note-14] a blockade is a legal method of warfare at sea, but is governed by rules. The manual describes what can never be contraband. The blockading nation is free to select anything else as contraband in a list, which it must publish.

The blockading nation typically establishes a blockaded area of water, and any ship can be inspected as soon as it is established that it is attempting to break the blockade. This inspection can occur inside the blockaded area or in international waters, but never inside the territorial waters of a [neutral nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrality_\(international_relations\)).

A neutral ship must obey a request to stop for inspection from the blockading nation. If the situation so demands, the blockading nation can request that the ship divert to a known place or harbour for inspection. If the ship does not stop, then the ship is subject to capture. If people aboard the ship resist capture, they can be lawfully attacked.

### Act of war

Main article: [Casus belli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casus_belli)

According to modern international law, blockades are an act of war.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERussell2023-15] A blockade is therefore only legal if applied in [self-defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-defense), and not as part of a [war of aggression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_aggression). Blockades are illegal when used as to starve or to collectively punish a civilian population.[^cite_note-russia-16][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDannenbaum2021–2022368-17][^cite_note-unlawful-18][^cite_note-starvation-19][^cite_note-palmer-20] Such a blockade is a [war crime](48626.md) and potentially a [crime against humanity](61099.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDannenbaum2021–2022368-17][^cite_note-unlawful-18]

## Blockade planning

Blockades depend on four general factors:



A Northern cartoonist ridicules the [Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_\(American_Civil_War\))'s initial attempts to blockade ports of the [Confederacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederate_States_of_America) in the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War)

- The **value** of the item being blockaded must warrant the need to blockade. For example, during the 1962 [Cuban Missile Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Missile_Crisis), the items to be blockaded (or "[quarantined](25237.md)" to use the more neutral term selected by President [John F. Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy)) were [Medium-range ballistic missiles](478272.md), capable of delivering [nuclear weaponry](21785.md), bound for Cuba. Their value was high, as a military threat against the United States.

- The **strength** of the blockading force must be equal to or greater in strength than the opposition. The blockade is only successful if the 'thing' in question is prevented from reaching its receiver. For example, the overwhelming power of the [Royal Navy](26061.md) allowed a successful [blockade of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockade_of_Germany_\(1914%E2%80%931919\)) during and after World War I.

- **Geography**. Knowing the routes of the enemy will help the blockader choose where to blockade: for example, a high [mountain pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_pass) or a [strait](58646.md) is a natural [choke point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choke_point) and a candidate for [fortification](204118.md).

- A blockade tends to be a long [campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_campaign) requiring a **long-term commitment** by the blockading power. The [Atlantic U-boat campaign of World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_U-boat_campaign_of_World_War_I) and [Battle of the Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Atlantic) were essentially about German blockades, and lasted nearly as long as their respective wars. The [Imperial Japanese Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Japanese_Navy), however, made only sporadic efforts at blockade during the [Pacific War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_War), preferring to seek victory by [fleet action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleet_action).

## Blockade running

Main article: [Blockade runner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockade_runner)

Blockade running is the practice of delivering cargo, food, for example, to a blockaded area. It has mainly been done by ships called [blockade runners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockade_runner) to ports under naval blockade. Blockade runners were typically the fastest ships available and often lightly armed and armored. It is now also done by aircraft, forming [airbridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbridge_\(logistics\)), such as over the [Berlin Blockade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Blockade) after [World War II](32927.md).
