The **Hwasong-11A** ([Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language): 《화성포-11가》형; lit. 'Mars Artillery Type 11A'), also known as **KN-23** under the [United States](3434750.md) [naming convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KN_number) and other names,[^cite_note-1] is a [North Korean](21255.md) single-stage, solid-fueled [short-range ballistic missile](1320069.md) (SRBM). Unveiled in February 2018 and first test-fired in May 2019, Hwasong-11A is the most notable variant of [Hwasong-11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-11) series of solid-fueled SRBMs. Analysts believe the missile, with its quasi-ballistic trajectory and a range of up to 900 km (560 mi), may be able to penetrate [South Korean](27019.md) [missile defenses](1099196.md). It can be launched from road-mobile platforms, railway car and silo. Variants of the Hwasong-11A include a [larger warhead version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-11C), a [smaller version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-11D) and an [underwater-launched version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-11S). The missile has been reported to be used by [Russian](25391.md) forces during the [Russo-Ukrainian war](70149799.md).

## Description

The Hwasong-11A bears an external resemblance to the Russian [Iskander-M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iskander-M) and South Korean [Hyunmoo-2B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyunmoo-2) [short-range ballistic missiles](1320069.md) (SRBMs), being distinguished by its elongated cable raceway, different jet vane actuators and smooth base. Like the Iskander-M, it flies in a quasi-[ballistic trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistic_trajectory), flattening out below an altitude of about 50 km (31 mi) where the atmosphere is dense enough so the missile's fins can change course along its flight path.[^cite_note-north-2][^cite_note-missilethreat-3][^cite_note-MDA-4] However, it is significantly larger than the Iskander, with estimated length and diameter to be 8.77 m (28.8 ft) and 1.1 m (3 ft 7 in), respectively.[^cite_note-38north-20260303-5] The mass estimation of Hwasong-11A is also different, with the [Center for Strategic and International Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_Strategic_and_International_Studies) giving a mass of 3,415 kg (7,529 lb),[^cite_note-missilethreat-3] while a 2026 assessment published by *[38 North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/38_North)* estimates it to be 8,729 kg (19,244 lb) for flight simulations.[^cite_note-38north-20260303-5] The missile is painted army green,[^cite_note-datayo-6] as well as likely uses the same 1.1 m (3 ft 7 in) diameter motor as the [Pukguksong-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pukguksong-1). The motor is somewhat lengthened, having only one stage, compared to the Pukguksong-1. The motor has a very different structure, compared to the Iskander. Its TEL has more space for the missile, as it lacks the structure immediately after the cab.[^cite_note-\:0-7][^cite_note-CAR-8]

Data from test-fires shows a minimum range of 190 km (120 mi) using depressed trajectory,[^cite_note-38north-20260303-5] with the maximum range being assessed as 900 km (560 mi),[^cite_note-antd-9] putting all of South Korea within range.[^cite_note-MDA-4] It is believed that Hwasong-11A's maximum flight altitude is 50–60 km (31–37 mi).[^cite_note-10] With a 500 kg (1,100 lb) warhead, the missile has an estimated range of 450 km (280 mi).[^cite_note-missilethreat-3][^cite_note-11] The missile's warhead section has enough space for up to 1,500 kg (3,300 lb) of high explosives compared to 700 kg (1,500 lb) for [Hwasong-5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-5) (Scud-B) and 800 kg (1,800 lb) of 9K720 Iskander.[^cite_note-Delory-12] It can be conventional, likely unitary,[^cite_note-missilethreat-3] [nuclear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_warhead), or [submunition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submunition),[^cite_note-vnn-13] which can devastate large areas with high firepower.[^cite_note-14] The Hwasong-11A is also capable of carrying the [Hwasan-31](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasan-31) tactical nuclear warhead.[^cite_note-15] Its active steering capability could make it accurate to within 35–200 m (115–656 ft) [CEP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_error_probability), depending whether the missile uses satellite guidance or INS alone.[^cite_note-north-2][^cite_note-Delory-12]

The Hwasong-11A's launch platform is diverse. It can be launched from a wheeled, four-axle [transporter erector launcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transporter_erector_launcher) (TEL), or a tracked one. Both are capable of carrying two missiles.[^cite_note-onn3-16][^cite_note-17] However, the four-axle truck used in test-fires is different from the truck used to carrying the missile during 8 February 2018 military parade.[^cite_note-18] Hwasong-11A also has railway-based mobile launch platform,[^cite_note-38northsep21-19][^cite_note-onnsep21-20] as well as silo-based launcher.[^cite_note-twz-21]

The missile is likely to replace older [liquid-fueled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid-propellant_rocket) North Korean SRBMs, like the [Hwasong-5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-5) and [Hwasong-6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-6).[^cite_note-north-2] Being solid-fueled and capable of rapid firing from mobile launchers, Hwasong-11A is difficult for an opposing force to locate and target, meaning it is ideal for fast and precise tactical attacks. Once launched, it is very hard to detect and be intercepted by traditional [missile defense systems](1099196.md), as the missile can fly at low altitude, conduct a terminal "pull-up" maneuver,[^cite_note-missilethreat-3][^cite_note-vnn-13] and have a short overall flight time.[^cite_note-MDA-4] For example, Hwasong-11A's trajectory is too low or too high for [THAAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_High_Altitude_Area_Defense) and [MIM-104 Patriot](52024.md) to intercept reliably, respectively.[^cite_note-22] Its increased accuracy also reduces the number of missiles that would be needed to destroy a single target.[^cite_note-north-2] It is capable of following non-ballistic trajectories, including depressed ballistic trajectories, pitch down to approach target from directly above, and range-extension using aerodynamic lift.[^cite_note-38north-20260303-5]

The Hwasong-11A is likely to feature some form of foreign involvement such as parts, as when compared to the later developed [Hwasong-11B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-11B) (KN-24), the [Korean Central News Agency](632399.md) focuses mainly on the deployment of the missile, with little coverage on its research. While the Hwasong-11B are called "[Juche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juche) projectiles", it is never mentioned for the Hwasong-11A. The focus on the combat-readiness of the system suggests that it had been deployed for a while but not tested, like the [Hwasong-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-10).[^cite_note-datayo-6] It still bears significant differences from the [9K720 Iskander](5607520.md).[^cite_note-jmcns-23]

### Naming

As of 2026, North Korean state media refers to Hwasong-11A as *Hwasongpho-11 Ka* (《화성포-11가》형) and classified it as a [tactical ballistic missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tactical_ballistic_missile).[^cite_note-kcnaapr26-24] It was first mentioned directly during a test in May 2025.[^cite_note-kcna2025-25] Formerly, when the official designation of Hwasong-11A was revealed, its Korean name was "《화성-11가》형" (lit. 'Mars Type 11A'),[^cite_note-jsfjuly23-26] sometimes romanized as *Hwasong-11Ga*, with the letter "Ga" being the first letter of the [Korean alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangul).[^cite_note-38north-20260303-5] The official designation can also be alternatively romanized as *Hwasongpho-11Ga* or *Hwasong-11Ka*.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-qngtvapr26-28] However, as the missile and [KN-02 Toksa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KN-02_Toksa), whose official designation is "Hwasong-11", are totally different missiles, the Hwasong-11A (and its subsequent variants) can not be called *Hwasong-11*.[^cite_note-29]

In its earlier launches, occurred between 2019 and 2023, North Korea called Hwasong-11A by generic descriptive terms instead of the official designation, such as "tactical guided weapon", "tactical guided missile" and "tactical ballistic missile".[^cite_note-MDA-4][^cite_note-38north-20220131-30][^cite_note-kcna3-31]

External designations and nicknames of Hwasong-11A include the alphanumeric codes of *[KN-23](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KN_number)* and *19-1 SRBM*, used by the United States and South Korea respectively.[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33] Analysts also call the missile *Kimskander* (a portmanteau of [Kim Jong Un](5962511.md) and [Iskander](5607520.md)), given its similarities to the Russian counterpart.[^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35]

## History

In mid-October 2017, North Korea apparently tested a solid-fueled engine in [Hamhung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamhung), possibly a static test of Hwasong-11A's engine.[^cite_note-jmcns-23]

North Korea first displayed the Hwasong-11A publicly in a military parade on 8 February 2018,[^cite_note-MDA-4] when it was mounted on Taebaeksan wheeled launchers.[^cite_note-36] More than a year later, on 4 May 2019, North Korea publicly tested Hwasong-11A for the first time. Five days later, North Korea test-fired another two missiles. On 17 May 2019, the missile was designated by the [United States Forces Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Forces_Korea) as *KN-23*.[^cite_note-missilethreat-3] Further test-fires were carried out on 25 July and 6 August 2019, as well as in 2021, 2022 and 2023.[^cite_note-nti-37]

The official name of KN-23 was first suggested as Hwasong-11A in October 2021, after the confirmation of KN-24's official name.[^cite_note-38] During the "Weaponry Exhibition-2023" military exhibition held in July 2023, North Korea confirmed this designation.[^cite_note-jsfjuly23-26]

According to the [International Institute for Strategic Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Institute_for_Strategic_Studies) (IISS), as of 2025, North Korea is possessing more than 17 Hwasong-11A launchers, in road- and rail-mobile variants.[^cite_note-IISS2025-39]<sup>: 268 </sup>

Another launch of Hwasong-11A occurred on 8 May 2025, during a military drill that also involved [KN-25](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KN-25).[^cite_note-hbtv-40][^cite_note-41] It was the first known Hwasong-11A launch since 2023.[^cite_note-38northjul25-42] Further launches of this missile occurred in April and September 2026.[^cite_note-38northapr26-43][^cite_note-44]

### Mass production

In November 2024, satellite imagery revealed that the buildings at the February 11 Plant ([Hamhung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamhung)), used to manufacture ballistic missiles (including Hwasong-11A), were expanded, likely related to increased production.[^cite_note-45]

Images published by North Korean state media during [Kim Jong Un](5962511.md) inspection to missile factories in December 2025 and June 2026 suggested Hwasong-11A had been entered mass production for conventional warfare, with approximately 90 non-warhead Hwasong-11A missiles showed in December 2025 visit and around 80 missile bodies in June 2026 visit.[^cite_note-46][^cite_note-47]

### Russian use in Ukraine

The Hwasong-11A has been identified to be used since at least December 2023 by Russian forces and possibly, one of North Korean missile units for a time, during the [Russo-Ukrainian war](70149799.md).[^cite_note-38northaug26-48] An unknown number of North Korean ballistic missiles were transferred to Russia in October 2023, according to declassified US intelligence informations. Based on debris left by Russian attacks on Ukrainian targets on 30 December 2023, the ring housing the control vanes have been identified as the characteristics of Hwasong-11A and [Hwasong-11B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-11B) missiles.[^cite_note-49] The barometer on the missile was found to have a [Hangul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangul) character, and various parts of the missile were stamped with the possible factory name.[^cite_note-CAR-8]

According to the Conflict Armament Research (CAR), 75% of components in the guidance control system for the Hwasong-11A fired at Kharkiv are based on American parts.[^cite_note-50] They also indicated that some of the missiles were newly produced.[^cite_note-51]

During the [Kharkiv strikes](74401306.md) of 2 January 2024, Ukrainian authorities noted that the missiles used were of North Korean origin, noting that the shape and design were different than that of [Iskander missiles](5607520.md). On 29 April 2024, the [UNSC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNSC) confirmed that the munitions used during the strike were that of Hwasong-11A missiles.[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53]

On 7 May 2024, Ukrainian sources reported that the missiles have a high failure rate. According to these officials, half of the North Korean missiles lost their programmed trajectories and exploded in the air.[^cite_note-54] In August 2024, the CAR documented the debris of four additional North Korean ballistic missiles that landed in Ukraine. These debris, retrieved in [Bila Tserkva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bila_Tserkva), Vitrova Balka and Rozhivka, were identified as debris from North Korean missiles, likely from Hwasong-11A.[^cite_note-msmt-55] In December 2024, during an interview, Ukrainian military official Andrii Cherniak claimed that Russia had used around 60 Hwasong-11A missiles in their attacks against Ukraine.[^cite_note-56]

According to Ukraine, at the start of 2025, North Korea had supplied Russia 148 Hwasong-11A and 11B missiles.[^cite_note-reuters24apr25-57] In February 2025, it was also reported the Hwasong-11A missiles used by Russia in Ukraine had an improved CEP of 50–100 m (160–330 ft).[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59] Ukraine also claimed that Russia launched Hwasong-11A in the 23 April 2025 Russian attack on Kyiv.[^cite_note-reuters24apr25-57] Information from a Ukrainian source obtained by [Reuters](18998750.md) indicated that the Hwasong-11A missiles were armed with a 1,000 kg (2,200 lb) warhead.[^cite_note-60]

In May 2025, the Multilateral Sanctions Monitoring Team (MSMT) published images of Hwasong-11A's debris collected in Ukraine.[^cite_note-msmt-55]

In April 2026, a Ukrainian report revealed that North Korean ballistic missiles (including Hwasong-11A) used by Russia in 2024 were made using commercial electronics with outdated manufacturing methods (up to 50 years old) and used less energy-efficient fuel, requiring North Korean missiles to use larger engines to achieve the same range as Russian counterpart.[^cite_note-61] Ukraine also confirmed that Hwasong-11A is not a direct copy or licensed production of Russia's Iskander.[^cite_note-62] Four months later, Ukraine claimed that North Korea had supplied another batch of 40 Hwasong-11A and 11B missiles to Russia and would deploy a missile unit that could be equipped with six launchers and additional 120 missiles (including Hwasong-11A).[^cite_note-63]

### Alleged tests

Member states of the [United Nations Security Council](31956.md) alleged that there were test-fires of Hwasong-11A on 5 June 2022 and 9 November 2022.[^cite_note-79][^cite_note-s2023171-80] It is also possible that a test of Hwasong-11A took place in June 2023, with two missiles being launched and one of which travelled 900 km (560 mi).[^cite_note-antd-9]

An alleged test-fire of Hwasong-11A took place on 4 January 2026,[^cite_note-81] but it is highly likely that it involved [Hwasong-11E](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-11E), based on the description of "hypersonic missile" by North Korean state media.[^cite_note-bacninhtv-82]

According to German analyst Norbert Brügge, North Korea might launch Hwasong-11A during an undated test, with an image showing a TEL of [Hwasong-7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-7).[^cite_note-\:0-7]

## Variants

Along with the base version, Hwasong-11A has some variants:

### Railway-borne missile

#### Design and development

North Korea has expressed interest in rail-mobile ballistic missile launchers for a long time, likely started in late-1980s and early-1990s. However, due to international sanctions, this interest accelerated in early 2000s.[^cite_note-83] In early 2021, during the [8th Congress of the Workers' Party of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8th_Congress_of_the_Workers'_Party_of_Korea), a railway-borne missile regiment was founded.[^cite_note-kcnasep21-84][^cite_note-85]

On 15 September 2021, North Korea test-fired two missiles from a modified [railway car](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_car), rather than the typical road-mobile launcher.[^cite_note-86] These missiles appeared to be baseline Hwasong-11A versions, although North Korean state media did not mention it explicitly. They flew much further than any previous flight of the weapon, and went an even greater distance than the [larger variant tested earlier in 2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-11C). Such excess range could indicate it hadn't previously been tested out to its maximum range, or that the design underwent modifications such as a reduced payload or flight profile improvements. The launch railcar uses two side-by-side erector/launcher mechanisms like the side-by-side arrangement used in the TEL. The use of a rail-mobile system is unusual for an SRBM, as road-mobile launchers are easier for deploying and hiding relatively small missiles than the railway-borne. However, its enhanced mobility enables North Korea to launch missiles from anywhere in its [rail network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_network), and the train launchers can be disguised as passenger trains to avoid external surveillance, making it more difficult to be detected pre-launch. Adding railway launchers may also be an effort to further increase and diversify the country's SRBM missile force, since modifying existing railcars to fire missiles could be a way to supplement a limited number of launch trucks, or possibly to test the concept before applying it to [intercontinental ballistic missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercontinental_ballistic_missile) (ICBMs). A rail-borne ICBM would have advantages over one carried by a wheeled TEL, as such large liquid-fueled missiles carried in railway cars would be able to move more places and be kept in a higher readiness state.[^cite_note-38northsep21-19][^cite_note-onnsep21-20][^cite_note-nbtv-87]

On 14 January 2022, North Korea again tested railway-launched Hwasong-11A. The next day, the [Korean Central News Agency](632399.md) released photos of the test.[^cite_note-reutersjan22-88]

### Silo-based missile

#### Design and development

On 19 March 2023, North Korea test-fired a ballistic missile during a drill claimed by North Korean state media as nuclear counterattack simulation.[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93] The missile appeared to be Hwasong-11A, based on photos released by the Korean Central News Agency.[^cite_note-94] The missile was fired from an underground silo, located within the [Sohae Satellite Launching Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sohae_Satellite_Launching_Station) and constructed for less than two months. It is the first known missile launch from silo of North Korea.[^cite_note-nti-37][^cite_note-nknmarch23-95] Images showed the rising, V-shaped exhaust flame, consistent with the launch from an underground silo.[^cite_note-38north29mar23-96]

The use of silo can help North Korea fire missiles with little warning while avoiding outside surveillance.[^cite_note-97] However, given its vulnerability and operational unfavorables, the deployment of silo-based missiles may be limited, despite several economic advantages, including effective operation and maintenance cost, as well as low construction expense.[^cite_note-38north29mar23-96]

### Larger variant

Main article: [Hwasong-11C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-11C)

Hwasong-11C is the larger version of Hwasong-11A, claimed to have a 2.5 tons warhead.[^cite_note-onn8aug23-99] It was first tested on 25 March 2021, with North Korean state media calling it "new-type tactical guided projectile".[^cite_note-100]

A variant with 4.5 tons warhead, called Hwasong-11C-4.5, was tested on 1 July 2024 and 18 September 2024.[^cite_note-btv-101][^cite_note-nknews-102]

### Smaller variant

Main article: [Hwasong-11D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-11D)

Hwasong-11D is the smaller variant of Hwasong-11A, with reduced range.[^cite_note-103] Its first test occurred on 16 April 2022 when North Korea called it "tactical guided weapon",[^cite_note-104] and a test launch occurred on 19 April 2026 resulted in the publication of the official designation by KCNA.[^cite_note-105]

### Hypersonic glide vehicle variant

Main article: [Hwasong-11E](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-11E)

The Hwasong-11E is a Hwasong-11C-based variant of Hwasong-11A with [hypersonic glide vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersonic_glide_vehicle), first displayed in 2025 during a military exhibition.[^cite_note-106] It highly likely underwent a test launch on 4 January 2026.[^cite_note-bacninhtv-82][^cite_note-107]

### SLBM variant

Main article: [Hwasong-11S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hwasong-11S)

Hwasong-11S is the underwater-launched version of Hwasong-11A.[^cite_note-onn8aug23-99] It was tested on 19 October 2021 and 25 September 2022.[^cite_note-nti-37]
