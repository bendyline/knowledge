**Restitution and unjust enrichment** is the field of law relating to gains-based recovery. In contrast with [damages](8134.md) (the law of compensation), restitution is a claim or remedy requiring a defendant to give up benefits wrongfully obtained. Liability for restitution is primarily governed by the "principle of unjust enrichment": A person who has been unjustly enriched at the expense of another is required to make restitution.[^cite_note-1]

Where an individual is unjustly enriched, modern common law imposes an obligation upon the recipient to make restitution, subject to defences such as [change of position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Change_of_position) and the protection of [bona fide purchasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bona_fide_purchaser) from contrary equitable title. Liability for an unjust enrichment arises irrespective of wrongdoing on the part of the recipient, though it may affect available remedies. Restitution can also be ordered [for wrongs](#Restitution_for_wrongs) (also called "waiver of tort" because [election of remedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_of_remedies) historically occurred when first filing a suit). This may be treated as a distinct basis for restitution, or it may be treated as a subset of unjust enrichment.

Unjust enrichment is not to be confused with [illicit enrichment](69979637.md), which is a legal concept referring to the enjoyment of an amount of wealth by a person that is not justified by reference to their lawful income.

## History

### Roman law

This principle derives from late Roman law, as stated in the Latin maxim attributed to [Sextus Pomponius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sextus_Pomponius), *Jure naturae aequum est neminem cum alterius detrimentum et injuria fieri locupletiorem* ("By natural law it is just that no one should be enriched by another's loss or injury").[^cite_note-2] In [civil law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_law_\(legal_system\)) systems, it is also referred to as enrichment without cause or unjustified enrichment.

Its history can be traced back to the [Corpus Iuris Civilis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_Juris_Civilis).[^cite_note-Davrados2018-3] While the concept was unknown in classical Roman law,[^cite_note-4] Roman legal compilers eventually enunciated the principle of unjustified enrichment based on two actions of the classical Roman period—the *condictio* and the *actio de in rem verso*.[^cite_note-Davrados2018-3]

The *condictio* authorized recovery by the plaintiff of a certain object or money in the hands of the defendant, where the property had been given mistakenly, [illegally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ex_turpi_causa_non_oritur_actio), or for a [frustrated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frustration_of_purpose) or otherwise invalid reason. Under the condictio, the plaintiff was regarded as a borrower charged with returning the object or money.[^cite_note-5] For the *actio de in rem verso*, the plaintiff bore the burden of specifying the cause for his demand, namely, demanding the restitution of assets that had exited the plaintiff's patrimony and entered the defendant’s patrimony through the acts of the defendant’s servants.[^cite_note-6]

The *actio de in rem verso* gradually expanded to cover instances in which third parties were enriched at the expense of the impoverished obligee, and *unjustified enrichment* was recognized as a source of obligations under the heading of "[quasi-contract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-contract)".[^cite_note-Davrados2018-3]

### Civil law

The modern French and German law on unjustified enrichment is based on the Roman law principles, particularly their interpretation by the French jurist [Jean Domat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Domat) and the German jurist [Friedrich Carl von Savigny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Carl_von_Savigny).[^cite_note-7] Domat developed the French unjustified enrichment principles based on the *actio de in rem verso* and the Roman concept of *causa* (cause), such that quasi-contracts became actionable even when they are not normally recognized under Roman law.[^cite_note-Davrados2018-3] In German civil law, the concept of unjustified enrichment is considerably broader and addresses issues of restitution and restoration for failed juridical acts.[^cite_note-8]

#### Spain

For the [School of Salamanca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_of_Salamanca) members, like [Tomás de Mercado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom%C3%A1s_de_Mercado), the prohibition of unjustified enrichment finds directly its source in [natural law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_law)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDecock2013512_and_570-571-9] and the principle of [commutative justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_justice?action=edit\&redlink=1).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDecock2013507-508-10] As such, it applied to the entire law of property and contract.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDecock2013502-505-11]

### Common law

The medieval predecessors to the contemporary common law concept of unjust and enrichment were the claims brought in *[assumpsit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assumpsit)* and for [money had and received](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_had_and_received). The seminal medieval case was [Lord Mansfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Mansfield)'s decision in *[Moses v Macferlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses_v_Macferlan)* (1760), which imported into the common law notions of [equity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equity_\(law\)) or conscience from English [chancery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Chancery). Blackstone's Commentaries also endorsed this approach, citing *Moses*.[^cite_note-12]

In the late 19th-century, a group of scholars at [Harvard Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_Law_School) began to revise and critique the common law of unjust enrichment, believing the doctrine to be inconsistent and incoherent. The word 'restitution', as used to describe the concept, was popularised by the [American Law Institute's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Law_Institute) [Restatement of Restitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restatement_of_Restitution?action=edit\&redlink=1) (1931), and by the 1970s, United States law had developed a doctrine of restitution separate from contract and tort. In the [United Kingdom](31717.md), however, academic and judicial treatment of restitution did not become popular until the mid-1980s. The publication of [Peter Birks'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Birks) *[An Introduction to the Law of Restitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Introduction_to_the_Law_of_Restitution?action=edit\&redlink=1)* (1985), and his related seminar in the [Bachelor of Civil Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Civil_Law) degree at the [University of Oxford](31797.md), were hugely influential in the development of [English unjust enrichment law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_unjust_enrichment_law). By that time, however, interest in restitution within the United States had largely died out.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESaiman200899–104-13]

## Common law

### England and Commonwealth

Main article: [Unjust enrichment in English law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unjust_enrichment_in_English_law)

In Australian law, actions derived from the common money counts continue to generate only personal remedies. The doctrinal basis of subrogation is not unsettled: it has nothing to do with unjust enrichment.[^cite_note-17]

In *[Attorney General v Blake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attorney_General_v_Blake)*,[^cite_note-18] an English court found itself faced with the following claim. The defendant had made a profit somewhere in the region of £60,000 as a direct result of breaching his contract with the claimant. The claimant was undoubtedly entitled to claim compensatory damages but had suffered little or no identifiable loss. It therefore decided to seek restitution for the wrong of breach of contract. The claimant won the case and the defendant was ordered to pay over his profits to the claimant. However, the court was careful to point out that the normal legal response to a breach of contract is to award compensation. An order to make restitution was said to be available only in exceptional circumstances.

#### Australia

Whether there is a distinct body of law in Australia known as the law of unjust enrichment is a highly controversial question. In *[Pavey & Mathews v Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavey_&_Matthews_Pty_Ltd_v_Paul)* [(1987) 162 CLR 221](http://www.austlii.edu.au/cgi-bin/viewdoc/au/cases/cth/HCA/1987/5.html) the concept of unjust enrichment was expressly endorsed by the High Court of Australia. This was subsequently followed in numerous first instance and appellate decisions, as well as by the High Court itself.

Considerable skepticism about the utility of the concept of unjust enrichment has been expressed in recent years. The [equitable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equity_\(law\)) basis for the action for money had and received has instead been emphasised and in [*Australian Financial v Hills* \[2014\] HCA 14](http://www.austlii.edu.au/cgi-bin/viewdoc/au/cases/cth/HCA/2014/14.html) the plurality held that the concept of unjust enrichment was effectively 'inconsistent' with the law of restitution as it had developed in Australia. It is worth noting that the analytic framework had been expressly endorsed by the High Court just two years before in [*Equuscorp v Haxton* \[2012\] HCA 7](http://www.austlii.edu.au/cgi-bin/viewdoc/au/cases/cth/HCA/2012/7.html). For the moment, the concept of unjust enrichment appears to serve only a taxonomical function.[^cite_note-19]

#### Canada

The doctrine of unjust enrichment was definitively established as a fully fledged course of action in Canada in [*Pettkus v. Becker*, 1980 CanLII 22 (SCC), \[1980\] 2 SCR 834](https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/1980/1980canlii22/1980canlii22.html?searchUrlHash=AAAAAQAHcGV0dGt1cwAAAAAB\&resultIndex=1)[^cite_note-Pettkus-20]

To establish unjust enrichment, the Plaintiff needs to show: (i) enrichment; (ii) deprivation; (iii) causal connection between enrichment and deprivation; and (iv) absence of juristic justification for the enrichment.[^cite_note-Pettkus-20]

The concept of deprivation and enrichment are extremely broad. Deprivation refers to any loss of money or money's worth in the form of contribution while A is enriched if B contributes to the acquisition of assets in A's name.[^cite_note-Pettkus-20] The causal connection between enrichment and deprivation must be "substantial and direct".[^cite_note-Pettkus-20] The absence of juristic reason is satisfied if a Plaintiff establishes a reason why the benefit ought not be retained, or if the Defendant demonstrates a convincing argument in favour of retention of the property.[^cite_note-Pettkus-20] Remedy for unjust enrichment is frequently an imposition of constructive trust over the property unjustly retained.[^cite_note-Pettkus-20]

### United States

For restitution sought by U.S. regulatory agencies, see [disgorgement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disgorgement).

The *[Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restatements_of_the_Law)* (2011) (“R3RUE”) states that unjust enrichment is a body of legal obligations under the [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law) and [equity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equity_\(law\)) – but separate from [tort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tort) and [contract law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contract_law) – that is available to take away an enrichment that lacks an adequate legal basis. A claim of restitution for unjust enrichment “results from a transaction that the law treats as ineffective to work a conclusive alteration in ownership rights.” The restatements of restitution advocate for treating restitution as a unified and cohesive body of law, rather than comprising a variety of miscellaneous legal and equitable claims, remedies, and doctrines such as [quantum meruit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_meruit), [quantum valebant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_valebant), [account of profits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Account_of_profits), [quasi-contract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-contract), [constructive trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_trust), and [money had and received](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_had_and_received).[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22]

The enactment of the [Federal Rules of Civil Procedure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Rules_of_Civil_Procedure) in 1938 merged procedures for law and equity and replaced the common-law [forms of action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Form_of_action) with a single civil action. This has, to some extent, blurred differences between legal and equitable restitution, and obscured awareness of legal restitution's origin in the action of [assumpsit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assumpsit).[^cite_note-23]

### Comparative law

The common law of restitution and unjust enrichment in the American and Anglo-Commonwealth traditions differ significantly for historical, political, and jurisprudential reasons. Whereas the leading source of law on restitution in the United States is the [American Law Institute's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Law_Institute) *Restatement*, the leading texts in [England and Wales](275187.md) are the textbooks of analytical legal scholars from the [University of Oxford](31797.md), including [Peter Birks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Birks), [Gareth Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gareth_Jones_\(legal_scholar\)), [Robert Goff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Goff,_Baron_Goff_of_Chieveley), [Ewan McKendrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ewan_McKendrick), [Andrew Burrows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Burrows,_Lord_Burrows), and [Robert Stevens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Stevens_\(legal_scholar\)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESaiman200899–104-13]

In Birks's conceptual analysis of the English [law of obligations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_obligations), bases of liability and remedy can be distinguished based on "causative events": the basis of contract is the consent of parties; tort and other legal wrongs (e.g. in some [constructive trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_trusts_in_English_law) and [disgorgement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disgorgement) cases), breaches of duty; and restitution, unjust enrichment. Birks also argues that restitutionary remedies can be logically divided into proprietary (*in rem*) and personal (*in personam*) categories, with the former being governed by more rigid rules than the latter. In contrast, American jurisprudence in the late 20th century, dominated by [legal realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_realism), abandoned the private-public law distinction, eroded the relevance of proprietary and personal rights in adjudication, and distrusted the use of [analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_philosophy) legal reasoning and doctrinal categorisation. Rather, the paradigm of judicial reasoning in US academic scholarship centred around extra-legal factors, such as [economic efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_efficiency), [morality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morality), and [political theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_philosophy).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESaiman2008104–109-24]

## Civil law

### Belgium

The reception of unjust enrichment into Belgian law has been upheld multiple times by the [Court of Cassation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Cassation_\(Belgium\)), which has ruled that unjust enrichment is a general principle of law.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26][^cite_note-27] The Court has stated that the legal basis for unjust enrichment is [equity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equity_\(law\)) (*ius aequum*).

According to the Court, five elements constitute unjust enrichment:

1. an enrichment;

2. an impoverishment;

3. a connection between the enrichment and the impoverishment;

4. an absence of a basis (*sine causa*) of the enrichment;

5. a person alleging unjust enrichment may not simultaneously do so for benevolent intervention (*[negotiorum gestio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negotiorum_gestio)*) or undue payment (*solutio indebiti*).

### Scotland

The [law of unjust enrichment in England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_unjust_enrichment_law) rapidly developed during the second half of the 20th century. It has been heavily influenced by the writings of legal scholars from the universities of [Oxford](31797.md) and [Cambridge](25978572.md).[^cite_note-28]

In Scotland, the law developed in a piecemeal fashion through the twentieth century, culminating in three pivotal cases in the late 1990s. The most crucial of these was *Shilliday v Smith*, in which Lord Roger essentially laid the bedrock for what is now considered modern Scots unjustified enrichment law, bringing together the fragmented law into one framework, drawing from the principles of Roman Law upon which Scots Law as a whole is based (note the term "unjustified" is preferred to "unjust" in Scotland). Unjustified enrichment is more established as a fundamental part of the Scots law of obligations than unjust enrichment is in English law.[^cite_note-29]

It lies for money paid by [mistake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mistake_\(contract_law\)), or on a [consideration which happens to fail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Failure_of_consideration), or through imposition, extortion, or oppression, or where [undue advantage](2055881.md) is taken of the plaintiff's situation. \[Footnote:] [4 Burr. 1012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses_v_Macferlan)").
