Presidential elections are scheduled to be held in [France](5843419.md) on 18 April 2027, with a second round on 2 May if no candidate secures a majority vote. The election may be held earlier under exceptional circumstances if the presidency falls vacant before then.&#x20;

Incumbent president [Emmanuel Macron](43671127.md) is ineligible to stand, as the [French constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_France) limits presidents to two consecutive terms.

## Background

See also: [2024–2025 French political crisis](77741666.md)

The dissolution of the [National Assembly](299616.md) announced by [Emmanuel Macron](43671127.md) following the [2024 European election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_European_Parliament_election_in_France) triggered [a period of political instability](77741666.md).[^cite_note-1] The subsequent [snap legislative election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_French_legislative_election) resulted in a [hung parliament](652214.md). After weeks of negotiations, [Michel Barnier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Barnier) was appointed Prime Minister on 5 September 2024, leading a government based on cooperation between [Ensemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ensemble_\(political_coalition\)) and [The Republicans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Republicans_\(France\)).[^cite_note-2] He was later overthrown by a [vote of no confidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_of_no_confidence) in December.[^cite_note-3]

[François Bayrou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Bayrou) succeeded him and managed to pass the 2025 budget, but failed to stabilise a governing majority. He was in turn ousted on 9 September 2025,[^cite_note-4] and replaced by [Sébastien Lecornu](54364868.md).[^cite_note-5] This sequence of unstable governments has shaped the political context leading up to the 2027 presidential election.

In March 2025, [Marine Le Pen](572897.md), a declared candidate for the [National Rally](67119.md), was found guilty of [embezzlement](209411.md) of [European Parliament](9581.md) funds.[^cite_note-6] She was sentenced at first instance to five years of ineligibility with immediate effect, preventing her from running in upcoming elections.[^cite_note-7] On 7 July 2026, the [Court of Appeal of Paris](37196535.md) upheld her conviction but reduced her ineligibility sentence to 45 months, with 30 months suspended, potentially reopening a path for her to stand in the 2027 presidential election. The court also sentenced her to three years' imprisonment, two of them suspended, and ordered her to serve one year under electronic monitoring, leaving initial uncertainty over whether she would pursue a campaign under those conditions.[^cite_note-REP-8][^cite_note-APR-9] Following the ruling, Le Pen said she would run for president and appeal the conviction again.[^cite_note-LPR-10]

The left-wing [New Popular Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Popular_Front) alliance formed in 2024 has weakened due to strategic disagreements, particularly between the [Socialist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Party_\(France\)) and [La France Insoumise](52520313.md).[^cite_note-11] Several figures have expressed support for a joint candidacy under project "Building 2027",[^cite_note-12] which was rejected by [Jean-Luc Mélenchon](20298042.md),[^cite_note-13] [Raphaël Glucksmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapha%C3%ABl_Glucksmann), [Fabien Roussel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabien_Roussel), and others.[^cite_note-14] The [United Left primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_Left_primary), organised by [Les Écologistes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ecologists) and smaller left-wing parties, is scheduled for October 11, 2026.[^cite_note-15] The Socialist Party has decided to hold [its own internal primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_French_Socialist_Party_presidential_primary), while LFI and the [French Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Communist_Party) chose their own separate candidates, Mélenchon and Roussel, respectively.[^cite_note-\:6-16][^cite_note-\:7-17]

On the political right, divisions centered on the method of selecting the candidate.[^cite_note-\:0-18] [Michel Barnier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Barnier) proposed a coalition project, "Building Together," to unite the right and centre.[^cite_note-19] [Bruno Retailleau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruno_Retailleau) was nominated as [The Republicans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Republicans_\(France\))' candidate on 19 April 2026 following a member vote. [Gérald Darmanin](54070416.md), [Laurent Wauquiez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurent_Wauquiez), and [David Lisnard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lisnard) favoured a primary election—the latter two seeking to include [Reconquête](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconqu%C3%AAte)—[^cite_note-\:0-18][^cite_note-20]while [Xavier Bertrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xavier_Bertrand) and [Édouard Philippe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89douard_Philippe) rejected the idea.[^cite_note-\:0-18] Following the nomination, Wauquiez withdrew his candidacy, while Lisnard left the party.[^cite_note-\:5-21]

The [centrist presidential majority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ensemble_\(political_coalition\)) faces mounting internal rivalries as candidates attempt to succeed Macron, who is ineligible to seek a third term. Former Prime Ministers [Édouard Philippe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89douard_Philippe) ([Horizons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizons_\(France\))) and [Gabriel Attal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Attal) ([Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_\(French_political_party\))) are locked in an internecine rivalry, sparking fears among supporters that a fractured [center](39619035.md) could hand second-round runoff spots to far-right Le Pen and far-left candidate Mélenchon.[^cite_note-22]

The number of declared or presumed candidates is unprecedented in the Fifth Republic.[^cite_note-\:1-23][^cite_note-\:2-24][^cite_note-25] In mid-February 2026, eleven prominent figures had already declared their candidacy, and several others were expected to do so;[^cite_note-\:2-24] *[Le HuffPost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HuffPost)* and the *[Revue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revue_Politique_et_Parlementaire)* *[Politique et Parlementaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revue_Politique_et_Parlementaire)* counted around 30 potential candidates in April.[^cite_note-\:3-26][^cite_note-\:4-27] Journalist Marie-Ève Malouines notes that most of these contenders are likely to be eliminated before they can even officially declare their candidacy, due to a lack of sufficient funding, endorsements, or organization.[^cite_note-\:4-27] *[20 Minutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20_minutes_\(France\))* and *[Le Figaro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Figaro)* project a potential number of 17 candidates or even more, while the previous record was set in [2002](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_French_presidential_election), with 16 candidates.[^cite_note-\:2-24][^cite_note-28]

According to *Le HuffPost*, Emmanuel Macron's constitutional inability to run for re-election and the fragmentation of the political class are fueling this frenzy.[^cite_note-\:3-26] According to Malouines, this proliferation of candidates stems less from realistic electoral prospects than from a dynamic unique to French politics, where refusing to declare oneself a potential candidate would be tantamount to losing all standing.[^cite_note-\:4-27] For political scientist Bruno Jeanbart, it reflects “the fragmentation of the political landscape and the decline of the party system” that began in 2017.[^cite_note-\:1-23] Political scientists Antoine Bristielle and Élie Michel see it as a symptom of a deep [crisis of representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crisis_of_representation?action=edit\&redlink=1), with parties struggling to fulfill their role in selecting candidates and personal ambitions breaking free from the party framework.[^cite_note-29]

## Electoral system

The [president of the French Republic](24899.md) is elected to a five-year term under the [two-round system](25932.md) as stipulated in Article 7 of the [French constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_France). If no candidate secures an absolute majority (50% + 1) of votes in the first round, a second round is held two weeks later between the two candidates who received the most votes.[^cite_note-30] Per the constitution, the first round of the presidential election must be held between 20 and 35 days before the conclusion of the president's current five-year term. [Emmanuel Macron](43671127.md)'s second term, which began on 14 May 2022, is scheduled to end on 13 May 2027, meaning that the first round of the presidential election is scheduled to be held between 8 and 23 April 2027.[^cite_note-31]

To be listed on the first-round ballot, candidates must secure 500 signatures (often referred to as *parrainages*) from national or local elected officials from at least 30 different [departments](38523.md) or [overseas collectivities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_collectivity), with no more than a tenth of these signatories from any single department.[^cite_note-32] According to the Article 6 of the [French constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_constitution), the president cannot "exercise more than two consecutive periods in office".[^cite_note-33]
