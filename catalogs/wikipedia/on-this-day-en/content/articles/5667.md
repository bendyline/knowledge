**Chlorine** is a [chemical element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element); it has [symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbol_\(chemistry\)) **Cl** and [atomic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_number) 17. The second-lightest of the [halogens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halogen), it appears between [fluorine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorine) and [bromine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bromine) in the periodic table and its properties are mostly intermediate between them. Chlorine is a yellow-green gas at room temperature. It is an extremely reactive element and a strong [oxidising agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidizing_agent): among the elements, it has the highest [electron affinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_affinity) and the third-highest [electronegativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronegativity) on the revised [Pauling scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronegativity#Pauling_electronegativity), behind only [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) and fluorine.

Chlorine played an important role in the experiments conducted by medieval [alchemists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy), which commonly involved the heating of chloride [salts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_\(chemistry\)) like [ammonium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium_chloride) ([sal ammoniac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sal_ammoniac)) and [sodium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chloride) ([table salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_salt)), producing various chemical substances containing chlorine such as [hydrogen chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_chloride), [mercury(II) chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury\(II\)_chloride) (corrosive sublimate), and *[aqua regia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqua_regia)*. However, the nature of free chlorine gas as a separate substance was only recognised around 1630 by [Jan Baptist van Helmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Baptist_van_Helmont). [Carl Wilhelm Scheele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Wilhelm_Scheele) wrote a description of chlorine gas in 1774, supposing it to be an [oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxide) of a new element. In 1809, chemists suggested that the gas might be a pure element, and this was confirmed by [Sir Humphry Davy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Humphry_Davy) in 1810, who named it after the [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language) χλωρός (*khlōrós*, "pale green") because of its colour.

Because of its great reactivity, all chlorine in the Earth's crust is in the form of [ionic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion) [chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloride) compounds, which includes table salt. It is the [second-most abundant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abundance_of_elements_in_Earth's_crust) halogen (after fluorine) and 20th most abundant element in Earth's crust. These crystal deposits are nevertheless dwarfed by the huge reserves of chloride in seawater.

Elemental chlorine is commercially produced from [brine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brine) by [electrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolysis), predominantly in the [chloralkali process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloralkali_process). The high oxidising potential of elemental chlorine led to the development of commercial [bleaches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bleach_\(chemical\)) and [disinfectants](478185.md), and a [reagent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reagent) for many processes in the chemical industry. Chlorine is used in the manufacture of a wide range of consumer products, about two-thirds of them organic chemicals such as [polyvinyl chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyvinyl_chloride) (PVC), many intermediates for the production of [plastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastics), and other end products which do not contain the element. As a common disinfectant, elemental chlorine and chlorine-generating compounds are used more directly in [swimming pools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swimming_pool) to keep them [sanitary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swimming_pool_sanitation). Elemental chlorine at high [concentration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration) is extremely dangerous, and [poisonous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisonous) to most living organisms. As a [chemical warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_warfare) agent, chlorine was first used in [World War I](4764461.md) as a [poison gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poison_gas) weapon.

In the form of chloride [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion), chlorine is necessary to all known species of life. Other types of chlorine compounds are rare in living organisms, and artificially produced chlorinated organics range from inert to toxic. In the [upper atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_atmosphere), chlorine-containing organic molecules such as [chlorofluorocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorofluorocarbon) have been implicated in [ozone depletion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_depletion). Small quantities of elemental chlorine are generated by oxidation of chloride ions in [neutrophils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrophil) as part of an [immune system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immune_system) response against bacteria.

## History

The most common compound of chlorine, sodium chloride, has been known since ancient times; archaeologists have found evidence that [rock salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_salt) was used as early as 3000 BC and [brine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brine) as early as 6000 BC.[^cite_note-11]

### Early discoveries

Around 900, the authors of the Arabic writings attributed to [Jabir ibn Hayyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabir_ibn_Hayyan) (Latin: Geber) and the Persian physician and alchemist [Abu Bakr al-Razi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Bakr_al-Razi) (c. 865–925, Latin: Rhazes) were experimenting with [sal ammoniac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sal_ammoniac) ([ammonium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium_chloride)), which when it was distilled together with [vitriol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitriol) (hydrated [sulfates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfates) of various metals) produced [hydrogen chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_chloride).[^cite_note-12] However, it appears that in these early experiments with chloride [salts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_\(chemistry\)), the gaseous products were discarded, and hydrogen chloride may have been produced many times before it was discovered that it can be put to chemical use.[^cite_note-13] One of the first such uses was the synthesis of [mercury(II) chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury\(II\)_chloride) (corrosive sublimate), whose production from the heating of [mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_\(element\)) either with [alum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alum) and ammonium chloride or with vitriol and sodium chloride was first described in the *De aluminibus et salibus* ("On Alums and Salts", an eleventh- or twelfth century Arabic text falsely attributed to Abu Bakr al-Razi and [translated into Latin in the second half of the twelfth century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_translations_of_the_12th_century) by [Gerard of Cremona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerard_of_Cremona), 1144–1187).[^cite_note-14] Another important development was the discovery by [pseudo-Geber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Geber) (in the *De inventione veritatis*, "On the Discovery of Truth", after c. 1300) that by adding ammonium chloride to [nitric acid](21655.md), a strong solvent capable of dissolving gold (i.e., *[aqua regia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqua_regia)*) could be produced.[^cite_note-15] Although *aqua regia* is an unstable mixture that continually gives off fumes containing free chlorine gas, this chlorine gas appears to have been ignored until c. 1630, when its nature as a separate gaseous substance was recognised by the [Brabantian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Brabant) chemist and physician [Jan Baptist van Helmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Baptist_van_Helmont).[^cite_note-Greenwood789-16][^cite_note-17]

### Isolation



[Carl Wilhelm Scheele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Wilhelm_Scheele), discoverer of chlorine

The element was first studied in detail in 1774 by Swedish chemist [Carl Wilhelm Scheele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Wilhelm_Scheele), and he is credited with the discovery.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-krogt-19] Scheele produced chlorine by reacting [MnO<sub>2</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese_dioxide) (as the mineral [pyrolusite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrolusite)) with HCl:[^cite_note-Greenwood789-16]

4 HCl + MnO<sub>2</sub> → MnCl<sub>2</sub> + 2 H<sub>2</sub>O + Cl<sub>2</sub>

Scheele observed several of the properties of chlorine: the bleaching effect on [litmus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litmus_test_\(chemistry\)), the deadly effect on insects, the yellow-green colour, and the smell similar to [aqua regia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqua_regia).[^cite_note-Greenwood792-20] He called it "*dephlogisticated muriatic acid air*" since it is a gas (then called "airs") and it came from [hydrochloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid) (then known as "muriatic acid").[^cite_note-krogt-19] He failed to establish chlorine as an element.[^cite_note-krogt-19]

Common chemical theory at that time held that an acid is a compound that contains oxygen (the element's name is from the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) roots *oxys* ("acid") and *-genēs* ("producer", literally 'begetter')[^cite_note-mellor-21], so a number of chemists, including [Claude Berthollet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Berthollet), suggested that Scheele's *dephlogisticated muriatic acid air* must be a combination of oxygen and the yet undiscovered element, *muriaticum*.[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-Weeks-23]

In 1809, [Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Louis_Gay-Lussac) and [Louis-Jacques Thénard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis-Jacques_Th%C3%A9nard) tried to decompose *dephlogisticated muriatic acid air* by reacting it with charcoal to release the free element *muriaticum* (and carbon dioxide).[^cite_note-krogt-19] They did not succeed and published a report in which they considered the possibility that *dephlogisticated muriatic acid air* is an element, but were not convinced.[^cite_note-24]

In 1810, [Sir Humphry Davy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Humphry_Davy) tried the same experiment again, and concluded that the substance was an element, and not a compound.[^cite_note-krogt-19] He announced his results to the Royal Society on 15 November that year.[^cite_note-Greenwood789-16] At that time, he named this new element "chlorine", from the Greek word χλωρος (*chlōros*, "green-yellow"), in reference to its colour.[^cite_note-25] The name "[halogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halogen)", meaning "salt producer", was originally used for chlorine in 1811 by [Johann Salomo Christoph Schweigger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Salomo_Christoph_Schweigger).[^cite_note-26] This term was later used as a generic term to describe all the elements in the chlorine family (fluorine, bromine, iodine), after a suggestion by [Jöns Jakob Berzelius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%B6ns_Jakob_Berzelius) in 1826.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28] In 1823, [Michael Faraday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Faraday) liquefied chlorine for the first time,[^cite_note-29][^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31] and demonstrated that what was then known as "solid chlorine" had a structure of [chlorine hydrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clathrate_hydrate) (Cl<sub>2</sub>·H<sub>2</sub>O).[^cite_note-Greenwood789-16]

### Later uses

Chlorine gas was first used by French chemist [Claude Berthollet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Louis_Berthollet) to bleach textiles in 1785.[^cite_note-encyc-32][^cite_note-Cotton-33] Modern bleaches resulted from further work by Berthollet, who first produced [sodium hypochlorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_hypochlorite) in 1789 in his laboratory in the town of [Javel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Javel%E2%80%93Andr%C3%A9_Citro%C3%ABn_station) (now part of Paris, France), by passing chlorine gas through a solution of sodium carbonate. The resulting liquid, known as "*Eau de Javel*" ("[Javel water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Javel_water)"), was a weak solution of sodium hypochlorite. This process was not very efficient, and alternative production methods were sought. Scottish chemist and industrialist [Charles Tennant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Tennant) first produced a solution of [calcium hypochlorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_hypochlorite) ("chlorinated lime"), then solid calcium hypochlorite (bleaching powder).[^cite_note-encyc-32] These compounds produced low levels of elemental chlorine and could be more efficiently transported than sodium hypochlorite, which remained as dilute solutions because when purified to eliminate water, it became a dangerously powerful and unstable oxidiser. Near the end of the nineteenth century, E. S. Smith patented a method of sodium hypochlorite production involving electrolysis of [brine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brine) to produce [sodium hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_hydroxide) and chlorine gas, which then mixed to form sodium hypochlorite.[^cite_note-34] This is known as the [chloralkali process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloralkali_process), first introduced on an industrial scale in 1892, and now the source of most elemental chlorine and sodium hydroxide.[^cite_note-Greenwood798-35] In 1884, the [Chemische Fabrik Griesheim](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemische_Fabrik_Griesheim-Elektron) in Germany developed another chloralkali process which entered commercial production in 1888.[^cite_note-36]

Elemental chlorine solutions dissolved in [chemically basic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_\(chemistry\)) water (sodium and [calcium hypochlorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_hypochlorite)) were first used as anti-[putrefaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Putrefaction) agents and [disinfectants](478185.md) in the 1820s, in France, long before the establishment of the [germ theory of disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germ_theory_of_disease). This practice was pioneered by [Antoine-Germain Labarraque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine-Germain_Labarraque), who adapted Berthollet's "Javel water" bleach and other chlorine preparations.[^cite_note-bouvet-37] Elemental chlorine has since served a continuous function in topical [antisepsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiseptic) (wound irrigation solutions and the like) and public sanitation, particularly in swimming and drinking water.[^cite_note-Greenwood792-20]

Chlorine gas was first used as a weapon on 22 April 1915 at the [Second Battle of Ypres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Battle_of_Ypres) by the [German Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Army_\(German_Empire\)).[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39] The effect on the Allied forces was devastating because the [existing gas masks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WWI_gas_mask) were difficult to deploy and had not been broadly distributed.[^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41]

## Properties



Solid chlorine at −150 °C



Chlorine, liquefied under a pressure of 7.4 bar at room temperature, displayed in a quartz ampule embedded in [acrylic glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poly\(methyl_methacrylate\))



Gaseous chlorine stored inside a spherical container.

Chlorine is the second [halogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halogen), being a [nonmetal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonmetal_\(chemistry\)) in group 17 of the periodic table. Its properties are thus similar to [fluorine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorine), [bromine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bromine), and [iodine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iodine), and are largely intermediate between those of the first two. Chlorine has the electron configuration \[Ne]3s<sup>2</sup>3p<sup>5</sup>, with the seven electrons in the third and outermost shell acting as its [valence electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_electron). Like all halogens, it is thus one electron short of a full octet, and is hence a strong oxidising agent, reacting with many elements in order to complete its outer shell.[^cite_note-Greenwood800-42] Corresponding to [periodic trends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_trend), it is intermediate in [electronegativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronegativity) between fluorine and bromine (F: 3.98, Cl: 3.16, Br: 2.96, I: 2.66), and is less reactive than fluorine and more reactive than bromine. It is also a weaker oxidising agent than fluorine, but a stronger one than bromine. Conversely, the [chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloride) ion is a weaker reducing agent than bromide, but a stronger one than fluoride.[^cite_note-Greenwood800-42] It is intermediate in [atomic radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_radius) between fluorine and bromine, and this leads to many of its atomic properties similarly continuing the trend from iodine to bromine upward, such as first [ionisation energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionisation_energy), [electron affinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_affinity), enthalpy of dissociation of the X<sub>2</sub> molecule (X = Cl, Br, I), ionic radius, and X–X bond length. (Fluorine is anomalous due to its small size.)[^cite_note-Greenwood800-42]

All four stable halogens experience intermolecular [van der Waals forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_der_Waals_force) of attraction, and their strength increases together with the number of electrons among all homonuclear diatomic halogen molecules. Thus, the melting and boiling points of chlorine are intermediate between those of fluorine and bromine: chlorine melts at −101.0 °C and boils at −34.0 °C. As a result of the increasing molecular weight of the halogens down the group, the density and heats of fusion and vaporisation of chlorine are again intermediate between those of bromine and fluorine, although all their heats of vaporisation are fairly low (leading to high volatility) thanks to their diatomic molecular structure.[^cite_note-Greenwood800-42] The halogens darken in colour as the group is descended: thus, while fluorine is a pale yellow gas, chlorine is distinctly yellow-green. This trend occurs because the wavelengths of visible light absorbed by the halogens increase down the group.[^cite_note-Greenwood800-42] Specifically, the colour of a halogen, such as chlorine, results from the [electron transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_electron_transition) between the [highest occupied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HOMO/LUMO) antibonding *π<sub>g</sub>* molecular orbital and the lowest vacant antibonding *σ<sub>u</sub>* molecular orbital.[^cite_note-Greenwood804-43] The colour fades at low temperatures, so that solid chlorine at −195 °C is almost colourless.[^cite_note-Greenwood800-42]

Like solid bromine and iodine, solid chlorine crystallises in the [orthorhombic crystal system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthorhombic_crystal_system), in a layered lattice of Cl<sub>2</sub> molecules. The Cl–Cl distance is 198 pm (close to the gaseous Cl–Cl distance of 199 pm) and the Cl···Cl distance between molecules is 332 pm within a layer and 382 pm between layers (compare the van der Waals radius of chlorine, 180 pm). This structure means that chlorine is a very poor conductor of electricity, and indeed its conductivity is so low as to be practically unmeasurable.[^cite_note-Greenwood800-42]

### Isotopes

Main article: [Isotopes of chlorine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopes_of_chlorine)

Chlorine has two stable isotopes, <sup>35</sup>Cl and <sup>37</sup>Cl. These are its only two natural isotopes occurring in quantity, with <sup>35</sup>Cl making up 76% of natural chlorine and <sup>37</sup>Cl making up the remaining 24%. Both are synthesised in stars in the [oxygen-burning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen-burning_process) and [silicon-burning processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon-burning_process).[^cite_note-Cameron-44] Both have nuclear spin 3/2+ and thus may be used for [nuclear magnetic resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_resonance), although the spin magnitude being greater than 1/2 results in non-spherical nuclear charge distribution and thus resonance broadening as a result of a nonzero [nuclear quadrupole moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_quadrupole_moment) and resultant quadrupolar relaxation. The other chlorine isotopes are all radioactive, with [half-lives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-life) too short to occur in nature [primordially](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primordial_element). Of these, the most commonly used in the laboratory are <sup>36</sup>Cl (*t*<sub>1/2</sub> = 3.0×10<sup>5</sup> y) and <sup>38</sup>Cl (*t*<sub>1/2</sub> = 37.2 min), which may be produced from the [neutron activation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_activation) of natural chlorine.[^cite_note-Greenwood800-42]

The most stable chlorine radioisotope is <sup>36</sup>Cl. The primary decay mode of isotopes lighter than <sup>35</sup>Cl is [electron capture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_capture) to isotopes of [sulfur](27127.md); that of isotopes heavier than <sup>37</sup>Cl is [beta decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_decay) to isotopes of [argon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argon); and <sup>36</sup>Cl may decay by either mode to stable <sup>36</sup>S or <sup>36</sup>Ar.[^cite_note-NUBASE-45] [<sup>36</sup>Cl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine-36) occurs in trace quantities in nature as a [cosmogenic nuclide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmogenic_nuclide) in a ratio of about (7–10)×10<sup>−13</sup> to 1 with stable chlorine isotopes: it is produced in the atmosphere by [spallation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spallation) of <sup>36</sup>Ar by interactions with [cosmic ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_ray) [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton). In the top meter of the [lithosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithosphere), <sup>36</sup>Cl is generated primarily by [thermal neutron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_neutron) activation of <sup>35</sup>Cl and spallation of <sup>39</sup>[K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium) and <sup>40</sup>[Ca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium). In the subsurface environment, [muon capture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muon_capture) by <sup>40</sup>Ca becomes more important as a way to generate <sup>36</sup>Cl.[^cite_note-Zreda-46][^cite_note-47]

Chlorine is intermediate in reactivity between fluorine and bromine, and is one of the most reactive elements. Chlorine is a weaker oxidising agent than fluorine but a stronger one than bromine or iodine. This can be seen from the [standard electrode potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_electrode_potential) of the X/X<sup>−</sup> couples (F, +2.866 V; Cl, +1.395 V; Br, +1.087 V; I, +0.615 V; [At](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astatine), approximately +0.3 V). However, this trend is not shown in the bond energies because fluorine is singular due to its small size, low polarisability, and inability to show [hypervalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypervalence). As another difference, chlorine has a significant chemistry in positive oxidation states while fluorine does not. Chlorination often leads to higher oxidation states than bromination or iodination but lower oxidation states than fluorination. Chlorine tends to react with compounds including M–M, M–H, or M–C bonds to form M–Cl bonds.[^cite_note-Greenwood804-43]

Given that *E*<sup>⦵</sup> (O<sub>2</sub>/<sub>2</sub> H<sub>2</sub>O) = +1.229 V, which is less than +1.395 V, it would be expected that chlorine should be able to oxidise water to oxygen and hydrochloric acid. However, the kinetics of this reaction are unfavourable, and there is also a bubble [overpotential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overpotential) effect to consider, so that electrolysis of aqueous chloride solutions evolves chlorine gas and not oxygen gas, a fact that is very useful for the industrial production of chlorine.[^cite_note-Greenwood853-48]

### Other binary chlorides



Hydrated [nickel(II) chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel\(II\)_chloride), NiCl<sub>2</sub>·6H<sub>2</sub>O

Nearly all elements in the periodic table form binary chlorides. The exceptions are decidedly in the minority and stem in each case from one of three causes: extreme inertness and reluctance to participate in chemical reactions (the [noble gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_gas), with the exception of [xenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenon) in the highly unstable [XeCl<sub>2</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenon_dichloride) and XeCl<sub>4</sub>); extreme nuclear instability hampering chemical investigation before decay and transmutation (many of the heaviest elements beyond [bismuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bismuth)); and having an electronegativity higher than chlorine's ([oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) and [fluorine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorine)) so that the resultant binary compounds are formally not chlorides but rather oxides or fluorides of chlorine.[^cite_note-Greenwood821-52] Even though [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen) in NCl<sub>3</sub> is bearing a negative charge, the compound is usually called [nitrogen trichloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_trichloride).

Chlorination of metals with Cl<sub>2</sub> usually leads to a higher oxidation state than bromination with Br<sub>2</sub> when multiple oxidation states are available, such as in [MoCl<sub>5</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molybdenum\(V\)_chloride) and [MoBr<sub>3</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molybdenum\(III\)_bromide). Chlorides can be made by reaction of an element or its oxide, hydroxide, or carbonate with hydrochloric acid, and then dehydrated by mildly high temperatures combined with either low pressure or anhydrous hydrogen chloride gas. These methods work best when the chloride product is stable to hydrolysis; otherwise, the possibilities include high-temperature oxidative chlorination of the element with chlorine or hydrogen chloride, high-temperature chlorination of a metal oxide or other halide by chlorine, a volatile metal chloride, [carbon tetrachloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_tetrachloride), or an organic chloride. For instance, [zirconium dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zirconium_dioxide) reacts with chlorine at standard conditions to produce [zirconium tetrachloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zirconium_tetrachloride), and [uranium trioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_trioxide) reacts with [hexachloropropene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexachloropropene) when heated under [reflux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflux) to give [uranium tetrachloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_tetrachloride). The second example also involves a reduction in [oxidation state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation_state), which can also be achieved by reducing a higher chloride using hydrogen or a metal as a reducing agent. ### Chlorine fluorides

The three fluorides of chlorine form a subset of the [interhalogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interhalogen) compounds, all of which are [diamagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamagnetic).[^cite_note-Greenwood824-54] Some cationic and anionic derivatives are known, such as ClF<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup>, ClF<sub>4</sub><sup>−</sup>, ClF<sub>2</sub><sup>+</sup>, and Cl<sub>2</sub>F<sup>+</sup>.[^cite_note-Greenwood835-56] Some [pseudohalides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudohalogen) of chlorine are also known, such as [cyanogen chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanogen_chloride) (ClCN, linear), chlorine [cyanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanate) (ClNCO), chlorine [thiocyanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thiocyanate) (ClSCN, unlike its oxygen counterpart), and chlorine [azide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azide) (ClN<sub>3</sub>).[^cite_note-Greenwood824-54]

[Chlorine monofluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine_monofluoride) (ClF) is extremely thermally stable, and is sold commercially in 500-gram steel lecture bottles. It is a colourless gas that melts at −155.6 °C and boils at −100.1 °C. It may be produced by the reaction of its elements at 225 °C, though it must then be separated and purified from [chlorine trifluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine_trifluoride) and its reactants. Its properties are mostly intermediate between those of chlorine and fluorine. It will react with many metals and nonmetals from room temperature and above, fluorinating them and liberating chlorine. It will also act as a chlorofluorinating agent, adding chlorine and fluorine across a multiple bond or by oxidation: for example, it will attack [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide) to form carbonyl chlorofluoride, COFCl. It will react analogously with [hexafluoroacetone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexafluoroacetone), (CF<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>CO, with a [potassium fluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_fluoride) catalyst to produce heptafluoroisopropyl hypochlorite, (CF<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>CFOCl; with [nitriles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrile) RCN to produce RCF<sub>2</sub>NCl<sub>2</sub>; and with the sulfur oxides SO<sub>2</sub> and SO<sub>3</sub> to produce ClSO<sub>2</sub>F and ClOSO<sub>2</sub>F respectively. It will also react exothermically with compounds containing –OH and –NH groups, such as water:[^cite_note-Greenwood824-54]

H<sub>2</sub>O + 2 ClF → 2 HF + Cl<sub>2</sub>O

[Chlorine trifluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine_trifluoride) (ClF<sub>3</sub>) is a volatile colourless molecular liquid which melts at −76.3 °C (196.8 K) and boils at 11.8 °C. It may be formed by directly fluorinating gaseous chlorine or chlorine monofluoride at 200–300 °C. One of the most reactive chemical compounds known, the list of elements it sets on fire is diverse, containing [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen), [potassium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium), [phosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus), [arsenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsenic), [antimony](898.md), [sulfur](27127.md), [selenium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selenium), [tellurium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tellurium), [bromine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bromine), [iodine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iodine), and powdered [molybdenum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molybdenum), [tungsten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten), [rhodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodium), [iridium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iridium), and [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron). It will also ignite water, along with many substances which in ordinary circumstances would be considered chemically inert such as [asbestos](21492663.md), concrete, glass, and sand. When heated, it will even corrode [noble metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_metal) as [palladium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palladium), [platinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platinum), and [gold](12240.md), and even the [noble gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_gas) [xenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenon) and [radon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon) do not escape fluorination. An impermeable fluoride layer is formed by [sodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium), [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium), [aluminium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium), [zinc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc), [tin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin), and [silver](27119.md), which may be removed by heating. [Nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel), copper, and steel containers are usually used due to their great resistance to attack by chlorine trifluoride, stemming from the formation of an unreactive layer of metal fluoride. Its reaction with [hydrazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrazine) to form hydrogen fluoride, nitrogen, and chlorine gases was used in experimental rocket engine, but has problems largely stemming from its extreme [hypergolicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypergolic_propellant) resulting in ignition without any measurable delay. Today, it is mostly used in nuclear fuel processing, to oxidise [uranium](31743.md) to [uranium hexafluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_hexafluoride) for its enriching and to separate it from [plutonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium), as well as in the semiconductor industry, where it is used to clean [chemical vapour deposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_vapor_deposition) chambers.[^cite_note-SIcvd-57] It can act as a fluoride ion donor or acceptor (Lewis base or acid), although it does not dissociate appreciably into ClF<sub>2</sub><sup>+</sup> and ClF<sub>4</sub><sup>−</sup> ions.[^cite_note-Greenwood828-58]

[Chlorine pentafluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine_pentafluoride) (ClF<sub>5</sub>) is made on a large scale by direct fluorination of chlorine with excess [fluorine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorine) gas at 350 °C and 250 atm, and on a small scale by reacting metal chlorides with fluorine gas at 100–300 °C. It melts at −103 °C and boils at −13.1 °C. It is a very strong fluorinating agent, although it is still not as effective as chlorine trifluoride. Only a few specific stoichiometric reactions have been characterised. [Arsenic pentafluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsenic_pentafluoride) and [antimony pentafluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimony_pentafluoride) form ionic adducts of the form \[ClF<sub>4</sub>]<sup>+</sup>\[MF<sub>6</sub>]<sup>−</sup> (M = As, Sb) and water reacts vigorously as follows:[^cite_note-Greenwood832-59]

2 H<sub>2</sub>O + ClF<sub>5</sub> → 4 HF + FClO<sub>2</sub>

The product, [chloryl fluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloryl_fluoride), is one of the five known chlorine oxide fluorides. These range from the thermally unstable FClO to the chemically unreactive [perchloryl fluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perchloryl_fluoride) (FClO<sub>3</sub>), the other three being FClO<sub>2</sub>, F<sub>3</sub>ClO, and F<sub>3</sub>ClO<sub>2</sub>. All five behave similarly to the chlorine fluorides, both structurally and chemically, and may act as Lewis acids or bases by gaining or losing fluoride ions respectively or as very strong oxidising and fluorinating agents.[^cite_note-Greenwood875-60]

### Chlorine oxides



Yellow [chlorine dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine_dioxide) (ClO<sub>2</sub>) gas above a solution of hydrochloric acid and sodium chlorite in water, also containing dissolved chlorine dioxide



Structure of [dichlorine heptoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dichlorine_heptoxide), Cl<sub>2</sub>O<sub>7</sub>, the most stable of the chlorine oxides

The [chlorine oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine_oxide) are well-studied in spite of their instability (all of them are endothermic compounds). They are important because they are produced when [chlorofluorocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorofluorocarbon) undergo photolysis in the upper atmosphere and cause the destruction of the ozone layer. None of them can be made from directly reacting the elements.[^cite_note-Greenwood844-61]

[Dichlorine monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dichlorine_monoxide) (Cl<sub>2</sub>O) is a brownish-yellow gas (red-brown when solid or liquid) which may be obtained by reacting chlorine gas with yellow [mercury(II) oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury\(II\)_oxide). It is very soluble in water, in which it is in equilibrium with [hypochlorous acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypochlorous_acid) (HOCl), of which it is the anhydride. It is thus an effective bleach and is mostly used to make [hypochlorites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypochlorite). It explodes on heating or sparking or in the presence of ammonia gas.[^cite_note-Greenwood844-61]

[Chlorine dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine_dioxide) (ClO<sub>2</sub>) was the first chlorine oxide to be discovered in 1811 by [Humphry Davy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humphry_Davy). It is a yellow paramagnetic gas (deep-red as a solid or liquid), as expected from its having an odd number of electrons: it is stable towards dimerisation due to the delocalisation of the unpaired electron. It explodes above −40 °C as a liquid and under pressure as a gas and therefore must be made at low concentrations for wood-pulp bleaching and water treatment. It is usually prepared by reducing a [chlorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorate) as follows:[^cite_note-Greenwood844-61]

2 ClO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup> + 2 Cl<sup>−</sup> + 4 H<sup>+</sup> → 2 ClO<sub>2</sub> + Cl<sub>2</sub> + 2 H<sub>2</sub>O

Its production is thus intimately linked to the redox reactions of the chlorine oxoacids. It is a strong oxidising agent, reacting with [sulfur](27127.md), [phosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus), phosphorus halides, and [potassium borohydride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_borohydride). It dissolves exothermically in water to form dark-green solutions that very slowly decompose in the dark. Crystalline clathrate hydrates ClO<sub>2</sub>·*n*H<sub>2</sub>O (*n* ≈ 6–10) separate at low temperatures. However, in the presence of light, these solutions rapidly photodecompose to form a mixture of chloric and hydrochloric acids. Photolysis of individual ClO<sub>2</sub> molecules result in the radicals ClO and ClOO, while at room temperature mostly chlorine, oxygen, and some ClO<sub>3</sub> and Cl<sub>2</sub>O<sub>6</sub> are produced. Cl<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub> is also produced when photolysing the solid at −78 °C: it is a dark brown solid that explodes below 0 °C. The ClO radical leads to the depletion of atmospheric ozone and is thus environmentally important as follows:[^cite_note-Greenwood844-61]

Cl• + O<sub>3</sub> → ClO• + O<sub>2</sub>

ClO• + O• → Cl• + O<sub>2</sub>

[Chlorine perchlorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine_perchlorate) (ClOClO<sub>3</sub>) is a pale yellow liquid that is less stable than ClO<sub>2</sub> and decomposes at room temperature to form chlorine, oxygen, and [dichlorine hexoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dichlorine_hexoxide) (Cl<sub>2</sub>O<sub>6</sub>).[^cite_note-Greenwood844-61] Chlorine perchlorate may also be considered a chlorine derivative of [perchloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perchloric_acid) (HOClO<sub>3</sub>), similar to the thermally unstable chlorine derivatives of other oxoacids: examples include [chlorine nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine_nitrate) (ClONO<sub>2</sub>, vigorously reactive and explosive), and chlorine fluorosulfate (ClOSO<sub>2</sub>F, more stable but still moisture-sensitive and highly reactive).[^cite_note-Greenwood883-62] Dichlorine hexoxide is a dark-red liquid that freezes to form a solid which turns yellow at −180 °C: it is usually made by reaction of chlorine dioxide with oxygen. Despite attempts to rationalise it as the dimer of ClO<sub>3</sub>, it reacts more as though it were chloryl perchlorate, \[ClO<sub>2</sub>]<sup>+</sup>\[ClO<sub>4</sub>]<sup>−</sup>, which has been confirmed to be the correct structure of the solid. It hydrolyses in water to give a mixture of chloric and perchloric acids: the analogous reaction with anhydrous [hydrogen fluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_fluoride) does not proceed to completion.[^cite_note-Greenwood844-61]

[Dichlorine heptoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dichlorine_heptoxide) (Cl<sub>2</sub>O<sub>7</sub>) is the anhydride of [perchloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perchloric_acid) (HClO<sub>4</sub>) and can readily be obtained from it by dehydrating it with [phosphoric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphoric_acid) at −10 °C and then distilling the product at −35 °C and 1 mmHg. It is a shock-sensitive, colourless oily liquid. It is the least reactive of the chlorine oxides, being the only one to not set organic materials on fire at room temperature. It may be dissolved in water to regenerate perchloric acid or in aqueous alkalis to regenerate perchlorates. However, it thermally decomposes explosively by breaking one of the central Cl–O bonds, producing the radicals ClO<sub>3</sub> and ClO<sub>4</sub> which immediately decompose to the elements through intermediate oxides.[^cite_note-Greenwood844-61]

The hypochlorite ions also disproportionate further to produce chloride and chlorate (3 ClO<sup>−</sup> ⇌ 2 Cl<sup>−</sup> + ClO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup>) but this reaction is quite slow at temperatures below 70 °C in spite of the very favourable equilibrium constant of 10<sup>27</sup>. The chlorate ions may themselves disproportionate to form chloride and perchlorate (4 ClO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup> ⇌ Cl<sup>−</sup> + 3 ClO<sub>4</sub><sup>−</sup>) but this is still very slow even at 100 °C despite the very favourable equilibrium constant of 10<sup>20</sup>. The rates of reaction for the chlorine oxyanions increases as the oxidation state of chlorine decreases. The strengths of the chlorine oxyacids increase very quickly as the oxidation state of chlorine increases due to the increasing delocalisation of charge over more and more oxygen atoms in their conjugate bases.[^cite_note-Greenwood853-48]

Most of the chlorine oxoacids may be produced by exploiting these disproportionation reactions. Hypochlorous acid (HOCl) is highly reactive and quite unstable; its salts are mostly used for their bleaching and sterilising abilities. They are very strong oxidising agents, transferring an oxygen atom to most inorganic species. Chlorous acid (HOClO) is even more unstable and cannot be isolated or concentrated without decomposition: it is known from the decomposition of aqueous chlorine dioxide. However, [sodium chlorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chlorite) is a stable salt and is useful for bleaching and stripping textiles, as an oxidising agent, and as a source of chlorine dioxide. Chloric acid (HOClO<sub>2</sub>) is a strong acid that is quite stable in cold water up to 30% concentration, but on warming gives chlorine and chlorine dioxide. Evaporation under reduced pressure allows it to be concentrated further to about 40%, but then it decomposes to perchloric acid, chlorine, oxygen, water, and chlorine dioxide. Its most important salt is [sodium chlorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chlorate), mostly used to make chlorine dioxide to bleach paper pulp. The decomposition of chlorate to chloride and oxygen is a common way to produce oxygen in the laboratory on a small scale. Chloride and chlorate may comproportionate to form chlorine as follows:[^cite_note-Greenwood856-63]

ClO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup> + 5 Cl<sup>−</sup> + 6 H<sup>+</sup> → 3 Cl<sub>2</sub> + 3 H<sub>2</sub>O

Perchlorates and perchloric acid (HOClO<sub>3</sub>) are the most stable oxo-compounds of chlorine, in keeping with the fact that chlorine compounds are most stable when the chlorine atom is in its lowest (−1) or highest (+7) possible oxidation states. Perchloric acid and aqueous perchlorates are vigorous and sometimes violent oxidising agents when heated, in stark contrast to their mostly inactive nature at room temperature due to the high activation energies for these reactions for kinetic reasons. Perchlorates are made by electrolytically oxidising sodium chlorate, and perchloric acid is made by reacting anhydrous [sodium perchlorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_perchlorate) or [barium perchlorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barium_perchlorate) with concentrated hydrochloric acid, filtering away the chloride precipitated and distilling the filtrate to concentrate it. Anhydrous perchloric acid is a colourless mobile liquid that is sensitive to shock that explodes on contact with most organic compounds, sets [hydrogen iodide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_iodide) and [thionyl chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thionyl_chloride) on fire and even oxidises silver and gold. ### Organochlorine compounds

Main article: [Organochlorine compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organochlorine_compound)



Suggested mechanism for the chlorination of a carboxylic acid by phosphorus pentachloride to form an [acyl chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acyl_chloride)

Like the other carbon–halogen bonds, the C–Cl bond is a common functional group that forms part of core [organic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_chemistry). Formally, compounds with this functional group may be considered organic derivatives of the chloride anion. Due to the difference of electronegativity between chlorine (3.16) and carbon (2.55), the carbon in a C–Cl bond is electron-deficient and thus [electrophilic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrophilic). [Chlorination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorination_reaction) modifies the physical properties of hydrocarbons in several ways: chlorocarbons are typically denser than [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) due to the higher atomic weight of chlorine versus hydrogen, and aliphatic organochlorides are [alkylating agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkylating_agent) because chloride is a [leaving group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaving_group).[^cite_note-Ullmann-67]

[Alkanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkanes) and [aryl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryl) alkanes may be chlorinated under [free-radical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free-radical) conditions, with UV light. However, the extent of chlorination is difficult to control: the reaction is not [regioselective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regioselectivity) and often results in a mixture of various isomers with different degrees of chlorination, though this may be permissible if the products are easily separated. Aryl chlorides may be prepared by the [Friedel-Crafts halogenation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedel-Crafts_halogenation), using chlorine and a [Lewis acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_acid) catalyst.[^cite_note-Ullmann-67] The [haloform reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haloform_reaction), using chlorine and [sodium hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_hydroxide), is also able to generate alkyl halides from methyl ketones, and related compounds. Chlorine adds to the multiple bonds on [alkenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkene) and [alkynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkyne) as well, giving di- or tetrachloro compounds. However, due to the expense and reactivity of chlorine, organochlorine compounds are more commonly produced by using hydrogen chloride, or with chlorinating agents such as [phosphorus pentachloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus_pentachloride) (PCl<sub>5</sub>) or [thionyl chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thionyl_chloride) (SOCl<sub>2</sub>). The last is very convenient in the laboratory because all side products are gaseous and do not have to be distilled out.[^cite_note-Ullmann-67]

Many organochlorine compounds have been isolated from natural sources ranging from bacteria to humans.[^cite_note-Gribble-68][^cite_note-Gribble99-69] Chlorinated organic compounds are found in nearly every class of biomolecules including [alkaloids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkaloid), [terpenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terpene), [amino acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid), [flavonoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flavonoid), [steroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steroid), and [fatty acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatty_acid).[^cite_note-Gribble-68][^cite_note-70] Organochlorides, including [dioxins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polychlorinated_dibenzodioxins), are produced in the high temperature environment of forest fires, and dioxins have been found in the preserved ashes of lightning-ignited fires that predate synthetic dioxins.[^cite_note-71] In addition, a variety of simple chlorinated hydrocarbons including dichloromethane, chloroform, and [carbon tetrachloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_tetrachloride) have been isolated from marine algae.[^cite_note-72] A majority of the [chloromethane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloromethane) in the environment is produced naturally by biological decomposition, forest fires, and volcanoes.[^cite_note-73]

Some types of organochlorides, though not all, have significant toxicity to plants or animals, including humans. Dioxins, produced when organic matter is burned in the presence of chlorine, and some [insecticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insecticide), such as [DDT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DDT), are [persistent organic pollutants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistent_organic_pollutant) which pose dangers when they are released into the environment. For example, DDT, which was widely used to control insects in the mid 20th century, also accumulates in food chains, and causes reproductive problems (e.g., eggshell thinning) in certain bird species.[^cite_note-74] Due to the ready homolytic fission of the C–Cl bond to create chlorine radicals in the upper atmosphere, [chlorofluorocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorofluorocarbon) have been discontinued due to the harm they do to the ozone layer.[^cite_note-Greenwood844-61]

## Occurrence



Liquid chlorine analysis

Chlorine is too reactive to occur as the free element in nature but is very abundant in the form of its chloride salts. It is the 20th most abundant element[^cite_note-75] in Earth's crust and makes up 126 [parts per million](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts_per_million) of it, through the large deposits of chloride minerals, especially [sodium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chloride), that have been evaporated from water bodies. All of these pale in comparison to the reserves of chloride ions in seawater: smaller amounts at higher concentrations occur in some inland seas and underground [brine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brine) wells, such as the [Great Salt Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Salt_Lake) in Utah and the [Dead Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Sea) in Israel.[^cite_note-Greenwood795-76]

Small batches of chlorine gas are prepared in the laboratory by combining hydrochloric acid and [manganese dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese_dioxide), but the need rarely arises due to its ready availability. In industry, elemental chlorine is usually produced by the electrolysis of sodium chloride dissolved in water. This method, the [chloralkali process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloralkali_process) industrialised in 1892, now provides most industrial chlorine gas.[^cite_note-Greenwood798-35] Along with chlorine, the method yields [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) gas and [sodium hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_hydroxide), which is the most valuable product. The process proceeds according to the following [chemical equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_equation):[^cite_note-77]

2 NaCl + 2 H<sub>2</sub>O → Cl<sub>2</sub> + H<sub>2</sub> + 2 NaOH

## Production

Main article: [Chlorine production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine_production)

Chlorine is primarily produced by the [chloralkali process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloralkali_process), although non-chloralkali processes exist. Global 2022 production was estimated to be 97 million tonnes.[^cite_note-78] The most visible use of chlorine is in [water disinfection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_disinfection). 35–40 % of chlorine produced is used to make [poly(vinyl chloride)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poly\(vinyl_chloride\)) through [ethylene dichloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethylene_dichloride) and [vinyl chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinyl_chloride).[^cite_note-79] The chlorine produced is available in cylinders from sizes ranging from 450 g to 70 kg, as well as drums (865 kg), tank wagons (15 tonnes on roads; 27–90 tonnes by rail), and barges (600–1200 tonnes).[^cite_note-Greenwood796-80] Due to the difficulty and hazards in transporting elemental chlorine, production is typically located near where it is consumed. As examples, vinyl chloride producers such as [Westlake Chemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westlake_Chemical)[^cite_note-81] and [Formosa Plastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formosa_Plastics)[^cite_note-82] have integrated chloralkali assets.

### Chloralkali processes

Main article: [Chloralkali process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloralkali_process)

The electrolysis of chloride solutions all proceed according to the following equations:

Cathode: 2 H<sub>2</sub>O + 2 e<sup>−</sup> → H<sub>2</sub> + 2 OH<sup>−</sup>

Anode: 2 Cl<sup>−</sup> → Cl<sub>2</sub> + 2 e<sup>−</sup>

In the conventional case where sodium chloride is electrolysed, [sodium hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_hydroxide) and chlorine are coproducts.

Industrially, there are three chloralkali processes:

- The [Castner–Kellner process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castner%E2%80%93Kellner_process) that utilises a mercury electrode

- The diaphragm cell process that utilises an asbestos diaphragm that separates the cathode and anode

- The membrane cell process that uses an [ion exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion_exchange) membrane in place of the diaphragm

The Castner–Kellner process was the first method used at the end of the nineteenth century to produce chlorine on an industrial scale.[^cite_note-pauling-83][^cite_note-lenn2-84] Mercury (that is toxic) was used as an electrode to [amalgamate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_amalgam) the sodium product, preventing undesirable side reactions.

In diaphragm cell electrolysis, an [asbestos](21492663.md) (or polymer-fibre) diaphragm separates a cathode and an [anode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anode), preventing the chlorine forming at the anode from re-mixing with the sodium hydroxide and the hydrogen formed at the cathode.[^cite_note-Euro_Chlor3-85] The salt solution (brine) is continuously fed to the anode compartment and flows through the diaphragm to the cathode compartment, where the [caustic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causticity) [alkali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali) is produced and the brine is partially depleted. Diaphragm methods produce dilute and slightly impure alkali, but they are not burdened with the problem of [mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_\(element\)) disposal and they are more energy efficient.[^cite_note-Greenwood798-35]

Membrane cell electrolysis employs [permeable membrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semipermeable_membrane) as an [ion exchanger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion_exchange). Saturated sodium (or potassium) chloride solution is passed through the anode compartment, leaving at a lower [concentration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration). This method also produces very pure sodium (or potassium) hydroxide but has the disadvantage of requiring very pure brine at high concentrations.[^cite_note-Euro_Chlor4-86] However, due to the lower energy requirements of the membrane process, new chlor-alkali installations are now almost exclusively employing the membrane process. Next to this, the use of large volumes of mercury is considered undesirable. Also, older plants are converted into the membrane process.



Membrane cell process for chloralkali production

### Non-chloralkali processes

In the [Deacon process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deacon_process), hydrogen chloride recovered from the production of [organochlorine compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organochlorine_compound) is recovered as chlorine. The process relies on oxidation using oxygen:

4 HCl + O<sub>2</sub> → 2 Cl<sub>2</sub> + 2 H<sub>2</sub>O

The reaction requires a catalyst. As introduced by Deacon, early catalysts were based on copper. Commercial processes, such as the Mitsui MT-Chlorine Process, have switched to chromium and ruthenium-based catalysts.[^cite_note-chlorine-87]

## Applications



A railway [tank car](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tank_car) carrying chlorine, displaying [hazardous materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazardous_materials) information including a diamond-shaped [U.S. DOT placard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placard#United_States_Department_of_Transportation) showing a [UN number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UN_number)

Sodium chloride is the most common chlorine compound, and is the main source of chlorine for the demand by the chemical industry. About 15000 chlorine-containing compounds are commercially traded, including such diverse compounds as chlorinated [methane](18582230.md), [ethanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethane), [vinyl chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinyl_chloride), [polyvinyl chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyvinyl_chloride) (PVC), [aluminium trichloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_trichloride) for [catalysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalysis), the chlorides of [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium), [titanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanium), [zirconium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zirconium), and [hafnium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafnium) which are the precursors for producing the pure form of those elements.[^cite_note-Greenwood792-20]

Other particularly important organochlorines are [methyl chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methyl_chloride), [methylene chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methylene_chloride), [chloroform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroform), [vinylidene chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinylidene_chloride), [trichloroethylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichloroethylene), [perchloroethylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perchloroethylene), [allyl chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allyl_chloride), [epichlorohydrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epichlorohydrin), [chlorobenzene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorobenzene), [dichlorobenzenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dichlorobenzene), and [trichlorobenzenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichlorobenzene). The major inorganic compounds include HCl, Cl<sub>2</sub>O, HOCl, NaClO<sub>3</sub>, [AlCl<sub>3</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_chloride), [SiCl<sub>4</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_tetrachloride), [SnCl<sub>4</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin\(IV\)_chloride), [PCl<sub>3</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus_trichloride), [PCl<sub>5</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus_pentachloride), [POCl<sub>3</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphoryl_chloride), [AsCl<sub>3</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsenic_trichloride), [SbCl<sub>3</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimony_trichloride), [SbCl<sub>5</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimony_pentachloride), [BiCl<sub>3</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bismuth_chloride), and [ZnCl<sub>2</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc_chloride).[^cite_note-Greenwood796-80]

### Sanitation, disinfection, and antisepsis

Main articles: [Water chlorination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_chlorination) and [Bleach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bleach)

#### Combating putrefaction

In France (as elsewhere), [animal intestines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catgut) were processed to make musical instrument strings, [Goldbeater's skin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldbeater's_skin) and other products. This was done in "gut factories" (*boyauderies*), and it was an odiferous and unhealthy process. In or about 1820, the [Société d'encouragement pour l'industrie nationale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soci%C3%A9t%C3%A9_d'encouragement_pour_l'industrie_nationale) offered a prize for the discovery of a method, chemical or mechanical, for separating the [peritoneal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peritoneum) membrane of animal intestines without [putrefaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Putrefaction).[^cite_note-nbu-91][^cite_note-knight-92] The prize was won by [Antoine-Germain Labarraque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine-Germain_Labarraque), a 44-year-old French chemist and pharmacist who had discovered that Berthollet's chlorinated bleaching solutions ("*[Eau de Javel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Javel_water)*") not only destroyed the smell of putrefaction of animal tissue decomposition, but also actually retarded the decomposition.[^cite_note-knight-92][^cite_note-bouvet-37]

Labarraque's research resulted in the use of chlorides and hypochlorites of lime ([calcium hypochlorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_hypochlorite)) and of sodium ([sodium hypochlorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_hypochlorite)) in the *boyauderies.* The same chemicals were found to be useful in the routine [disinfection](478185.md) and deodorisation of [latrines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latrine), [sewers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewerage), markets, [abattoirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abattoir), [anatomical theatres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatomical_theatre), and morgues.[^cite_note-gedeon-93] They were successful in [hospitals](18994221.md), [lazarets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lazaretto), [prisons](19008450.md), infirmaries (both on land and at sea), [magnaneries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnanery), [stables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable), cattle-sheds, etc.; and they were beneficial during [exhumations](177497.md),[^cite_note-94] [embalming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embalming), outbreaks of epidemic disease, fever, and [blackleg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackleg_\(disease\)) in cattle.[^cite_note-nbu-91]

#### Disinfection

Labarraque's chlorinated lime and soda solutions have been advocated since 1828 to prevent infection (called "contagious infection", presumed to be transmitted by "[miasmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miasma_theory_of_disease)"), and to treat [putrefaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Putrefaction) of existing wounds, including septic wounds.[^cite_note-95] In his 1828 work, Labarraque recommended that doctors breathe chlorine, wash their hands in chlorinated lime, and even sprinkle chlorinated lime about the patients' beds in cases of "contagious infection". In 1828, the contagion of infections was well known, even though the agency of the [microbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microorganism) was not discovered until more than half a century later.

During the [Paris cholera outbreak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1832_cholera_epidemic) of 1832, large quantities of so-called *chloride of lime* were used to disinfect the capital. This was not simply modern [calcium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_chloride), but chlorine gas dissolved in lime-water (dilute [calcium hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_hydroxide)) to form [calcium hypochlorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_hypochlorite) (chlorinated lime). Labarraque's discovery helped to remove the terrible stench of decay from hospitals and dissecting rooms, and by doing so, effectively deodorised the [Latin Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Quarter,_Paris) of Paris.[^cite_note-corbin-96] These "putrid miasmas" were thought by many to cause the spread of "contagion" and "infection" – both words used before the germ theory of infection. Chloride of lime was used for destroying odors and "putrid matter". One source claims chloride of lime was used by Dr. John Snow to disinfect water from the cholera-contaminated well that was feeding the Broad Street pump in 1854 London,[^cite_note-97] though three other reputable sources that describe that famous cholera epidemic do not mention the incident.[^cite_note-Vinten-98][^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100] One reference makes it clear that chloride of lime was used to disinfect the [offal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offal) and filth in the streets surrounding the Broad Street pump – a common practice in mid-nineteenth century England.[^cite_note-Vinten-98]<sup>: 296 </sup>

#### Semmelweis and experiments with antisepsis



[Ignaz Semmelweis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Semmelweis)

Perhaps the most famous application of Labarraque's chlorine and [chemical base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_\(chemistry\)) solutions was in 1847, when [Ignaz Semmelweis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Semmelweis) used chlorine-water (chlorine dissolved in pure water, which was cheaper than chlorinated lime solutions) to disinfect the hands of Austrian doctors, which Semmelweis noticed still carried the stench of decomposition from the dissection rooms to the patient examination rooms. Long before the germ theory of disease, Semmelweis theorised that "cadaveric particles" were transmitting decay from fresh medical cadavers to living patients, and he used the well-known "Labarraque's solutions" as the only known method to remove the smell of decay and tissue decomposition (which he found that soap did not). The solutions proved to be far more effective antiseptics than soap (Semmelweis was also aware of their greater efficacy, but not the reason), and this resulted in Semmelweis's celebrated success in stopping the transmission of [childbed fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Childbed_fever) ("puerperal fever") in the maternity wards of [Vienna General Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_General_Hospital) in [Austria](26964606.md) in 1847.[^cite_note-101]

Much later, during World War I in 1916, a standardised and diluted modification of Labarraque's solution containing hypochlorite (0.5%) and boric acid as an acidic stabiliser was developed by [Henry Drysdale Dakin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Drysdale_Dakin) (who gave full credit to Labarraque's prior work in this area). Called [Dakin's solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dakin's_solution), the method of wound irrigation with chlorinated solutions allowed antiseptic treatment of a wide variety of open wounds, long before the modern antibiotic era. A modified version of this solution continues to be employed in wound irrigation in modern times, where it remains effective against bacteria that are resistant to multiple antibiotics.[^cite_note-102]

#### Public sanitation



Chlorinated water is used in [swimming pools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swimming_pool) to disinfect water from microbial contaminants.



Liquid pool chlorine

The first continuous application of chlorination to drinking U.S. water was installed in [Jersey City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jersey_City), New Jersey, in 1908.[^cite_note-103] By 1918, the [US Department of Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Department_of_Treasury) called for all drinking water to be disinfected with chlorine. Chlorine is presently an important chemical for [water purification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_purification) (such as in water treatment plants), in [disinfectants](478185.md), and in [bleach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bleach). Even small water supplies are now routinely chlorinated.[^cite_note-CRC-104]

Chlorine is usually used (in the form of [hypochlorous acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypochlorous_acid)) to kill [bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteria) and other microbes in [drinking water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drinking_water) supplies and public swimming pools. In most private swimming pools, chlorine itself is not used, but rather [sodium hypochlorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_hypochlorite), formed from chlorine and [sodium hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_hydroxide), or solid tablets of chlorinated isocyanurates. The drawback of using chlorine in swimming pools is that the chlorine reacts with the [amino acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid) in proteins in human hair and skin. Contrary to popular belief, the distinctive "chlorine aroma" associated with swimming pools is not the result of elemental chlorine itself, but of [chloramine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monochloramine), a chemical compound produced by the reaction of free dissolved chlorine with amines in organic substances including those in urine and sweat.[^cite_note-105] As a disinfectant in water, chlorine is more than three times as effective against *[Escherichia coli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escherichia_coli)* as [bromine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bromine), and more than six times as effective as [iodine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iodine).[^cite_note-106] Increasingly, monochloramine itself is being directly added to drinking water for purposes of disinfection, a process known as [chloramination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloramination).[^cite_note-CDC-Chloramination-107]

It is often impractical to store and use poisonous chlorine gas for water treatment, so alternative methods of adding chlorine are used. These include [hypochlorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypochlorite) solutions, which gradually release chlorine into the water, and compounds like [sodium dichloro-s-triazinetrione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_dichloro-s-triazinetrione) (dihydrate or anhydrous), sometimes referred to as "dichlor", and [trichloro-s-triazinetrione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichloro-s-triazinetrione), sometimes referred to as "trichlor". These compounds are stable while solid and may be used in powdered, granular, or tablet form. When added in small amounts to pool water or industrial water systems, the chlorine atoms hydrolyse from the rest of the molecule, forming hypochlorous acid (HOCl), which acts as a general [biocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biocide), killing germs, microorganisms, algae, and so on.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1997860-108][^cite_note-109]

### Use as a weapon

#### World War I

Main article: [Chemical weapons in World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_weapons_in_World_War_I)

Chlorine gas, also known as bertholite, was first [used as a weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_warfare) in [World War I](4764461.md) by Germany on 22 April 1915, in the [Second Battle of Ypres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Battle_of_Ypres).[^cite_note-110][^cite_note-CEN2015-111] As described by the soldiers, it had the distinctive smell of a mixture of pepper and pineapple.[^cite_note-112] It also tasted metallic and stung the back of the throat and chest. Chlorine reacts with water in the [mucosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mucous_membrane) of the lungs to form [hydrochloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid), destructive to living tissue and potentially lethal. Human respiratory systems can be protected from chlorine gas by [gas masks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_mask) with [activated charcoal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activated_charcoal) or other filters, which makes chlorine gas much less lethal than other chemical weapons. It was pioneered by a German scientist later to be a Nobel laureate, [Fritz Haber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Haber) of the [Kaiser Wilhelm Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiser_Wilhelm_Institute_for_Chemistry) in Berlin, in collaboration with the German chemical conglomerate [IG Farben](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IG_Farben), which developed methods for discharging chlorine gas against an [entrenched](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trench) enemy.[^cite_note-113] After its first use, both sides in the conflict used chlorine as a chemical weapon, but it was soon replaced by the more deadly [phosgene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosgene) and more debilitating [mustard gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustard_gas).[^cite_note-First_World_War-114]

#### Middle East

Main articles: [Chlorine bombings in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine_bombings_in_Iraq) and [Use of chemical weapons in the Syrian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Use_of_chemical_weapons_in_the_Syrian_Civil_War)

Chlorine gas was also used during the [Iraq War in Anbar Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_War_in_Anbar_Province) in 2007, with insurgents packing [truck bombs](155282.md) with [mortar](324499.md) shells and chlorine tanks. The attacks killed two people from the explosives and sickened more than 350. Most of the deaths were caused by the force of the explosions rather than the effects of chlorine since the toxic gas is readily dispersed and diluted in the atmosphere by the blast. In some bombings, over a hundred civilians were hospitalised due to breathing difficulties. The Iraqi authorities tightened security for elemental chlorine, which is essential for providing safe drinking water to the population.[^cite_note-cnnchlorine-115][^cite_note-Chlorine_bomb_BBC-116]

On 23 October 2014, it was reported that the [Islamic State of Iraq and the Levant](9087364.md) had used chlorine gas in the town of Duluiyah, [Iraq](7515928.md).[^cite_note-117] Laboratory analysis of clothing and soil samples confirmed the use of chlorine gas against Kurdish [Peshmerga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peshmerga) Forces in a vehicle-borne improvised explosive device attack on 23 January 2015 at the Highway 47 Kiske Junction near Mosul.[^cite_note-118]

Another country in the Middle East, [Syria](7515849.md), has used chlorine as a [chemical weapon](27179600.md)[^cite_note-119] delivered from [barrel bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrel_bomb) and rockets.[^cite_note-120][^cite_note-121] In 2016, the [OPCW-UN Joint Investigative Mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OPCW-UN_Joint_Investigative_Mechanism) concluded that the Syrian government used chlorine as a chemical weapon in three separate attacks.[^cite_note-122] Later investigations from the OPCW's Investigation and Identification Team concluded that the [Syrian Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Air_Force) was responsible for chlorine attacks in 2017 and 2018.[^cite_note-123]

## Biological role

The [chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloride) anion is an [essential nutrient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral_\(nutrient\)) for metabolism. Chlorine is needed for the production of [hydrochloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid) in the stomach and in cellular pump functions.[^cite_note-124] The main dietary source is table salt, or sodium chloride. Overly low or high concentrations of chloride in the blood are examples of [electrolyte disturbances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolyte_disturbance). [Hypochloremia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypochloremia) (having too little chloride) rarely occurs in the absence of other abnormalities. It is sometimes associated with [hypoventilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypoventilation).[^cite_note-pmid3764530-125] It can be associated with chronic [respiratory acidosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Respiratory_acidosis).[^cite_note-pmid13611033-126] [Hyperchloremia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperchloremia) (having too much chloride) usually does not produce symptoms. When symptoms do occur, they tend to resemble those of [hypernatremia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypernatremia) (having too much [sodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium)). Reduction in blood chloride leads to cerebral dehydration; symptoms are most often caused by rapid rehydration which results in [cerebral edema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerebral_edema). Hyperchloremia can affect oxygen transport.[^cite_note-pmid9760315-127]

Chlorine is a toxic gas that attacks the respiratory system, eyes, and skin.[^cite_note-132] Because it is denser than air, it tends to accumulate at the bottom of poorly ventilated spaces. Chlorine gas is a strong oxidiser, which may react with flammable materials.[^cite_note-msds-cl-133][^cite_note-134]

Chlorine is detectable with measuring devices in concentrations as low as 0.2 parts per million (ppm), and by smell at 3 ppm. Coughing and vomiting may occur at 30 ppm and lung damage at 60 ppm. About 1000 ppm can be fatal after a few deep breaths of the gas.[^cite_note-Greenwood792-20] The [IDLH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IDLH) (immediately dangerous to life and health) concentration is 10 ppm.[^cite_note-NIOSH-135] Breathing lower concentrations can aggravate the respiratory system and exposure to the gas can irritate the eyes.[^cite_note-tox-136] When chlorine is inhaled at concentrations greater than 30 ppm, it reacts with water within the lungs, producing [hydrochloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid) (HCl) and [hypochlorous acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypochlorous_acid) (HOCl).

When used at specified levels for water disinfection, the reaction of chlorine with water is not a major concern for human health. Other materials present in the water may generate [disinfection by-products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disinfection_by-products) that are associated with negative effects on human health.[^cite_note-137][^cite_note-138]

In the United States, the [Occupational Safety and Health Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_Safety_and_Health_Administration) (OSHA) has set the [permissible exposure limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permissible_exposure_limit) for elemental chlorine at 1 ppm, or 3 mg/m<sup>3</sup>. The [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_for_Occupational_Safety_and_Health) has designated a [recommended exposure limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recommended_exposure_limit) of 0.5 ppm over 15 minutes.[^cite_note-NIOSH-135]

In the home, accidents occur when hypochlorite bleach solutions come into contact with certain acidic drain-cleaners to produce chlorine gas.[^cite_note-139] Hypochlorite bleach (a popular [laundry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laundry) additive) combined with [ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia) (another popular laundry additive) produces [chloramines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloramines), another toxic group of chemicals.[^cite_note-140]

### Chlorine-induced cracking in structural materials



Chlorine "attack" on an acetal resin plumbing joint resulting from a fractured acetal joint in a water supply system which started at an [injection moulding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injection_molding) defect in the joint and slowly grew until the part failed. The fracture surface shows iron and calcium salts that were deposited in the leaking joint from the water supply before failure and are the indirect result of the chlorine attack.

Chlorine is widely used for purifying water, especially potable water supplies and water used in swimming pools. Several catastrophic collapses of swimming pool ceilings have occurred from chlorine-induced [stress corrosion cracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_corrosion_cracking) of [stainless steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel) suspension rods.[^cite_note-141] Some [polymers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer) are also sensitive to attack, including [acetal resin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetal_resin) and [polybutene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polybutene). Both materials were used in hot and cold water domestic plumbing, and stress corrosion cracking caused widespread failures in the US in the 1980s and 1990s.[^cite_note-142]

### Chlorine–iron fire

The element [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron) can combine with chlorine at high temperatures in a strong exothermic reaction, creating a *chlorine–iron fire*. Chlorine–iron fires are a risk in chemical process plants, where much of the pipework that carries chlorine gas is made of steel.[^cite_note-bayerdatasheet2008-143][^cite_note-sanders2004-144]
