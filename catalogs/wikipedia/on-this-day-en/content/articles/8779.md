In [naval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navy) terminology, a **destroyer** is a fast, manoeuvrable, long-endurance [warship](263960.md) intended to escort larger vessels in a [fleet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_fleet), [convoy](42990.md), or [carrier battle group](7772.md) and defend them against a wide range of general threats. They were conceived in the late 19th century as a defence against [torpedo boats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torpedo_boat), and by the time of the [Russo-Japanese War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Japanese_War) in 1904, these "**torpedo boat destroyers**" (**TBDs**) were "large, swift, and powerfully armed torpedo boats designed to destroy other torpedo boats".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGove20022412-1] Although the term "destroyer" had been used interchangeably with "TBD" and "torpedo boat destroyer" by navies since 1892, the term "torpedo boat destroyer" was generally shortened to simply "destroyer" by nearly all navies by the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELyon19968,_9-2]

Before [World War II](32927.md), destroyers were light vessels with little endurance for unattended ocean operations; typically, a number of destroyers and a single [destroyer tender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destroyer_tender) operated together. After the war, destroyers grew in size. The American [*Allen M. Sumner*-class destroyers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_M._Sumner-class_destroyer) (from 1943) had a displacement of 2,200 tons, while the [*Arleigh Burke* class](197341.md) (from 1991) has a displacement of up to 9,600 tons, a difference of nearly 340%. Moreover, the advent of guided missiles allowed destroyers to take on the surface-combatant roles previously filled by [battleships](4054.md) and [cruisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruiser). This resulted in larger and more powerful [guided-missile destroyers](232005.md) more capable of independent operation.

At the start of the 21st century, destroyers are the global standard for [surface-combatant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_combatant) ships, with only two nations (the [United States](20518076.md) and [Russia](383258.md)) officially operating the heavier cruisers, with no battleships or true [battlecruisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battlecruiser) remaining.[^cite_note-3] Modern guided-missile destroyers are equivalent in [tonnage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonnage) but vastly superior in firepower to cruisers of the World War II era, and are capable of carrying [nuclear-tipped](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_missile) [cruise missiles](6590.md). At 510 feet (160 m) long, a displacement of 9,200 tons, and with an armament of more than 90 missiles,[^cite_note-4] guided-missile destroyers such as the *Arleigh Burke* class are actually larger and more heavily armed than most previous ships classified as guided-missile cruisers. The Chinese [Type 055 destroyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_055_destroyer) has been described as a cruiser in some US Navy reports due to its size and armament.[^cite_note-5]

Many [NATO](21133.md) navies, such as the [French](383256.md), [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Navy), [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Navy), [Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Danish_Navy), and [German](12960.md), use the term "[frigate](11464.md)" for their destroyers, which leads to some confusion.

## Origins



[Fernando Villaamil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernando_Villaamil), credited as the inventor of the destroyer concept with his specifications for the *Destructor*.

The emergence and development of the destroyer was related to the invention of the [self-propelled torpedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-propelled_torpedo) in the 1860s. A navy now had the potential to destroy a superior enemy battle fleet using steam [launches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launch_\(boat\)) to fire torpedoes. Cheap, fast boats armed with torpedoes, called [torpedo boats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torpedo_boat), were built and became a threat to large capital ships near enemy coasts. The first seagoing vessel designed to launch the self-propelled [Whitehead torpedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitehead_torpedo) was the 33-ton [HMS *Lightning*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Lightning_\(1876\)) in 1876.[^cite_note-ast-6] She was armed with two [drop collars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drop_collar) to launch these weapons; these were replaced in 1879 by a single [torpedo tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torpedo_tube) in the bow. By the 1880s, the type had evolved into small ships of 50–100 tons, fast enough to evade enemy picket boats.

At first, the threat of a torpedo-boat attack to a battle fleet was considered to exist only when at anchor, but as faster and longer-range torpedo boats and torpedoes were developed, the threat extended to cruising at sea. In response to this new threat, more heavily gunned picket boats called "catchers" were built, which were used to escort the battle fleet at sea. They needed significant seaworthiness and endurance to operate with the battle fleet, and as they inherently became larger, they became officially designated "torpedo-boat destroyers", and by the First World War were largely known as "destroyers" in English. The antitorpedo boat origin of this type of ship is retained in its name in other languages, including [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) (*contre-torpilleur*), [Italian](14708.md) (*cacciatorpediniere*), [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language) (*contratorpedeiro*), [Czech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_language) (*torpédoborec*), [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) (*antitorpiliko*, αντιτορπιλικό), [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language) (*torpedobootjager*) and, up until the Second World War, [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_language) (*kontrtorpedowiec*, now obsolete).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELyon19968-7]

Once destroyers became more than just catchers guarding an anchorage, they were recognized to be also ideal to take over the offensive role of torpedo boats themselves, so they were also fitted with torpedo tubes in addition to their antitorpedo-boat guns. At that time, and even into World War I, the only function of destroyers was to protect their own battle fleet from enemy torpedo attacks and to make such attacks on the battleships of the enemy. The task of escorting merchant convoys was still in the future.

### Early designs



The [Imperial Japanese Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Japanese_Navy)'s *Kotaka* (1887)

An important development came with the construction of [HMS *Swift*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_TB_81_\(1885\)) in 1884, later redesignated TB 81.[^cite_note-8] This was a large (137 ton) torpedo boat with four [47 mm quick-firing guns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QF_3-pounder_Hotchkiss) and three torpedo tubes. At 23.75 [knots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_\(unit\)) (43.99 km/h; 27.33 mph), while still not fast enough to engage enemy torpedo boats reliably, the ship at least had the armament to deal with them.

Another forerunner of the torpedo-boat destroyer (TBD) was the Japanese torpedo boat[^cite_note-9] [*Kotaka*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_torpedo_boat_Kotaka) (*Falcon*), built in 1885.[^cite_note-10] Designed to Japanese specifications and ordered from the Isle of Dogs, London [Yarrow shipyard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yarrow_shipyards) in 1885, she was transported in parts to Japan, where she was assembled and launched in 1887. The 165-foot (50 m) long vessel was armed with four 1-pounder (37 mm) quick-firing guns and six [torpedo](31424.md) tubes, reached 19 knots (35 km/h), and at 203 tons, was the largest torpedo boat built to date. In her trials in 1889, *Kotaka* demonstrated that she could exceed the role of coastal defence, and was capable of accompanying larger [warships](263960.md) on the high seas. The Yarrow shipyards, builder of the parts for *Kotaka*, "considered Japan to have effectively invented the destroyer".[^cite_note-11]

The German [aviso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviso) [*Greif*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Greif_\(1886\)), launched in 1886, was designed as a "*Torpedojäger*" (torpedo hunter), intended to screen the fleet against attacks by torpedo boats. The ship was significantly larger than torpedo boats of the period, displacing some 2,266 t (2,230 long tons), with an armament of 10.5 cm (4.1 in) guns and 3.7 cm (1.5 in) [Hotchkiss revolver cannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotchkiss_revolver_cannon).[^cite_note-12]

### Torpedo gunboat

Main article: [Torpedo gunboat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torpedo_gunboat)



HMS *Spider* was an early model of torpedo gunboat

The first vessel designed for the explicit purpose of hunting and destroying torpedo boats was the [torpedo gunboat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torpedo_gunboat). Essentially very small cruisers, torpedo gunboats were equipped with torpedo tubes and an adequate gun armament, intended for hunting down smaller enemy boats. By the end of the 1890s, torpedo gunboats were made obsolete by their more successful contemporaries, the TBDs.

[Fernando Villaamil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernando_Villaamil), second officer of the Ministry of the [Navy of Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Navy), in his study of the situation for the minister of the navy, came to the conclusion that what was needed was a new type of enlarged torpedo gunboat, with the ability to escort capital ships on long voyages, to combat the threat of the torpedo boats.[^cite_note-13] He asked several British shipyards to submit proposals capable of fulfilling these specifications. In 1885, the Spanish Navy chose the design submitted by the shipyard of James and [George Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Thomson_\(shipbuilder\)) of [Clydebank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clydebank). [*Destructor*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_warship_Destructor) (*Destroyer* in Spanish) was laid down at the end of the year, launched in 1886, and commissioned in 1887. Some authors consider *Destructor* to be the first destroyer ever built.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15]



Spanish warship [*Destructor*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_warship_Destructor) in 1890

She displaced 348 tons, and was the first warship[^cite_note-16] equipped with twin [triple-expansion engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triple-expansion_engine) generating 3,784 ihp (2,822 kW), for a maximum speed of 22.6 knots (41.9 km/h),[^cite_note-dest-17] which made her one of the faster ships in the world in 1888.[^cite_note-18] She was armed with one 90 mm (3.5 in) Spanish-designed [Hontoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hontoria) [breech-loading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breech-loading_weapon) gun,[^cite_note-Bernie2-19] four 57 mm (2.2 in) ([6-pounder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QF_6_pounder_Nordenfelt)) [Nordenfelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorsten_Nordenfelt) guns, two 37 mm (1.5 in) (3-pdr) [Hotchkiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotchkiss_et_Cie) cannons and two 15-inch (38 cm) [Schwartzkopff torpedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwartzkopff_torpedo) tubes.[^cite_note-dest-17] The ship carried three torpedoes per tube.[^cite_note-Bernie2-19] She carried a crew of 60.[^cite_note-dest-17] In terms of gunnery, dimensions, the specialised design and speed to chase torpedo boats, and her high-seas capabilities, *Destructor* was a highly influential precursor to the TBD.[^cite_note-20][^cite_note-Bernie2-19]

Built at the same time, [HMS *Rattlesnake*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Rattlesnake_\(1886\)), designed by [Nathaniel Barnaby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathaniel_Barnaby) in 1885, was commissioned in 1887 in response to the [Russian War scare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panjdeh_Incident).[^cite_note-L\&W-21] The gunboat was armed with torpedoes and designed for hunting and destroying smaller [torpedo boats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torpedo_boat). Exactly 200 feet (61 m) long and 23 feet (7.0 m) in beam, she displaced 550 tons. Built of steel, *Rattlesnake* was unarmoured with the exception of a 3⁄4-inch protective deck. She was armed with a single [4-inch/25-pounder breech-loading gun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BL_4_inch_naval_gun_Mk_I_%E2%80%93_VI), six [3-pounder QF guns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QF_3_pounder_Hotchkiss) and four 14-inch (360 mm) torpedo tubes, arranged with two fixed tubes at the bow and a set of torpedo-dropping carriages on either side. Four torpedo reloads were carried.[^cite_note-L\&W-21]

A number of torpedo gunboat classes followed, including the *Grasshopper* class, the [*Sharpshooter* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharpshooter-class_torpedo_gunboat), the [*Alarm* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alarm-class_torpedo_gunboat), and the [*Dryad* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dryad-class_torpedo_gunboat) – all built for the Royal Navy during the 1880s and the 1890s. In the 1880s, the [Chilean Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilean_Navy) ordered the construction of two [*Almirante Lynch* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almirante_Lynch-class_torpedo_gunboat) torpedo gunboats from the British shipyard Laird Brothers, which specialized in the construction of this type of vessel. The novelty is that one of these *Almirante Lynch*-class torpedo boats managed to sink the ironclad [*Blanco Encalada*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilean_ironclad_Blanco_Encalada) with self-propelled torpedoes in the [Battle of Caldera Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Caldera_Bay) in 1891, thus surpassing its main function of hunting torpedo boats.[^cite_note-22]

## Development of the destroyer class



[HMS *Havock*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Havock_\(1893\)), the first ship formally designated as destroyer class, 1894

The first classes of ships to bear the formal designation TBD were the [*Daring* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daring-class_destroyer_\(1893\)) of two ships and [*Havock* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havock-class_destroyer) of two ships of the Royal Navy.[^cite_note-23]

Early torpedo gunboat designs lacked the range and speed to keep up with the fleet they were supposed to protect. In 1892, the [Third Sea Lord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Sea_Lord), [Rear Admiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rear_Admiral) [John "Jacky" Fisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Fisher,_1st_Baron_Fisher) ordered the development of a new type of ships equipped with the then-novel [water-tube boilers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water-tube_boiler) and [quick-firing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_ordnance_terms#QF) small-calibre guns. Six ships to the specifications circulated by the admiralty were ordered initially, comprising three different designs each produced by a different shipbuilder: [HMS *Daring*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Daring_\(1893\)) and [HMS *Decoy*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Decoy_\(1894\)) from [John I. Thornycroft & Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_I._Thornycroft_&_Company), [HMS *Havock*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Havock_\(1893\)) and [HMS *Hornet*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Hornet_\(1893\)) from [Yarrows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yarrow_Shipbuilders), and [HMS *Ferret*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Ferret_\(1893\)) and [HMS *Lynx*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Lynx_\(1894\)) from [Laird, Son & Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cammell_Laird).[^cite_note-24]

These ships all featured a turtleback (i.e. rounded) [forecastle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forecastle) that was characteristic of early British TBDs. [HMS *Daring*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Daring_\(1893\)) and [HMS *Decoy*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Decoy_\(1894\)) were both built by [Thornycroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thornycroft), displaced 260 tons (287.8 tons full load), and were 185 feet in length. They were armed with one [12-pounder gun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QF_12_pounder_12_cwt_naval_gun) and three 6-pounder guns, with one fixed 18-in torpedo tube in the bow plus two more torpedo tubes on a revolving mount abaft the two funnels. Later, the bow torpedo tube was removed and two more 6-pounder guns added, instead. They produced 4,200 hp from a pair of Thornycroft water-tube boilers, giving them a top speed of 27 knots, giving the range and speed to travel effectively with a battle fleet. In common with subsequent early Thornycroft boats, they had sloping sterns and double rudders.[^cite_note-25]

The French navy, an extensive user of torpedo boats, built its first TBD in 1899, with the [*Durandal*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durandal-class_destroyer) *torpilleur d'escadre*. The United States commissioned its first TBD, [USS *Bainbridge*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Bainbridge_\(DD-1\)), Destroyer No. 1, in 1902, and by 1906, 16 destroyers were in service with the US Navy.[^cite_note-26]

### Subsequent improvements



Builders' plans for the British [*Charger* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charger-class_destroyer), built 1894–95

Torpedo boat destroyer designs continued to evolve around the turn of the 20th century in several key ways. The first was the introduction of the [steam turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_turbine). The spectacular unauthorized demonstration of the turbine-powered [*Turbinia*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbinia) at the 1897 Spithead Navy Review, which, significantly, was of torpedo-boat size, prompted the Royal Navy to order a prototype turbine-powered destroyer, [HMS *Viper*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Viper_\(1899\)) of 1899. This was the first turbine warship of any kind, and achieved a remarkable 34 knots (63 km/h; 39 mph) on sea trials. By 1910, the turbine had been widely adopted by all navies for their faster ships.[^cite_note-ast-6]

The second development was the replacement of the torpedo boat-style turtleback foredeck by a raised forecastle for the new [River-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River-class_destroyer_\(1903\)) destroyers built in 1903, which provided better sea-keeping and more space below deck.

The first warship to use only [fuel oil](392547.md) propulsion was the Royal Navy's TBD [HMS *Spiteful*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Spiteful_\(1899\)), after experiments in 1904, although the obsolescence of coal as a fuel in British warships was delayed by oil's availability.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28] Other navies also adopted oil, for instance the USN with the [*Paulding* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paulding-class_destroyer) of 1909. In spite of all this variety, destroyers adopted a largely similar pattern. The hull was long and narrow, with a relatively shallow draft. The bow was either raised in a forecastle or covered under a turtleback; underneath this were the crew spaces, extending 1⁄4 to 1⁄3 the way along the hull. Aft of the crew spaces was as much engine space as the technology of the time would allow - several boilers and engines or turbines. Above deck, one or more quick-firing guns were mounted in the bows, in front of the bridge; several more were mounted amidships and astern. Two tube mountings (later on, multiple mountings) were generally found amidships.

Between 1892 and 1914, destroyers became markedly larger; initially 275 tons with a length of 165 feet (50 m) for the Royal Navy's first [*Havock* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havock-class_destroyer) of TBDs,[^cite_note-29] up to the First World War with 300-foot (91 m) long destroyers displacing 1,000 tons was not unusual. Construction remained focused on putting the biggest possible engines into a small hull, though, resulting in a somewhat flimsy construction. Often, hulls were built of [high-tensile steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-tensile_steel)[^cite_note-ast-6] only 1⁄8 in (3.2 mm) thick.

By 1910, the steam-driven displacement (that is, not [hydroplaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planing_\(sailing\))) torpedo boat had become redundant as a separate type. Germany, nevertheless, continued to build such boats until the end of World War I, although these were effectively small coastal destroyers. In fact, Germany never distinguished between the two types, giving them pennant numbers in the same series and never giving names to destroyers. Ultimately, the term "torpedo boat" came to be attached to a quite different vessel – the very fast-hydroplaning, motor-driven [motor torpedo boat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_torpedo_boat).

## Early use and World War I

Navies originally built TBDrs to protect against torpedo boats, but admirals soon appreciated the flexibility of the fast, multipurpose vessels that resulted. Vice-Admiral Sir [Baldwin Walker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldwin_Walker) laid down destroyer duties for the Royal Navy:[^cite_note-30]

- Screening the advance of a fleet when hostile torpedo craft are about

- Searching a hostile coast along which a fleet might pass

- Watching an enemy's port for the purpose of harassing his torpedo craft and preventing their return

- Attacking an enemy fleet

Early destroyers were extremely cramped places to live, being "without a doubt magnificent fighting vessels ... but unable to stand bad weather".[^cite_note-31] During the [Russo-Japanese War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Japanese_War) in 1904, the commander of the Imperial Japanese Navy TBD *Akatsuki*[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34] described "being in command of a destroyer for a long period, especially in wartime ... is not very good for the health". Stating that he had originally been strong and healthy, he continued, "life on a destroyer in winter, with bad food, no comforts, would sap the powers of the strongest men in the long run. A *destroyer* is always more uncomfortable than the others, and rain, snow, and *sea-water* combine to make them damp; in fact, in bad weather, there is not a dry spot where one can rest for a moment."[^cite_note-Grant-35]

The Japanese destroyer-commander finished with, "Yesterday, I looked at myself in a mirror for a long time; I was disagreeably surprised to see my face thin, full of wrinkles, and as old as though I were 50. My clothes (uniform) cover nothing but a skeleton, and my bones are full of [rheumatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rheumatism)."[^cite_note-Grant-35]

In 1898, the US Navy officially classified [USS *Porter*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Porter_\(TB-6\)), a 175-foot (53 m) long all steel vessel displacing 165 tons, as a torpedo boat, but her commander, LT. John C. Fremont, described her as "a compact mass of machinery not meant to keep the sea nor to live in ... as five-sevenths of the ship are taken up by machinery and fuel, whilst the remaining two-sevenths, fore and aft, are the crew's quarters; officers forward and the men placed aft. And even in those spaces are placed anchor engines, steering engines, steam pipes, etc. rendering them unbearably hot in tropical regions."[^cite_note-36]

### Early combat



[HMS *Loyal*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Loyal_\(1913\)), of the [*Laforey* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laforey-class_destroyer_\(1913\))

The TBD's first major use in combat came during the [Japanese surprise attack on the Russian fleet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Port_Arthur) anchored in [Port Arthur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%BCshunkou_District) at the opening of the [Russo-Japanese War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Japanese_War) on 8 February 1904.

Three destroyer divisions attacked the Russian fleet in port, firing a total of 18 torpedoes, but only two Russian battleships, [*Tsesarevich*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_battleship_Tsesarevich) and [*Retvizan*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_battleship_Retvizan), and a [protected cruiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protected_cruiser), [*Pallada*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_cruiser_Pallada_\(1899\)), were seriously damaged due to the proper deployment of [torpedo nets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torpedo_net). *Tsesarevich*, the Russian flagship, had her nets deployed, with at least four enemy torpedoes "hung up" in them,[^cite_note-37] and other warships were similarly saved from further damage by their nets.[^cite_note-38]

While capital-ship engagements were scarce in World War I, destroyer units engaged almost continually in raiding and patrol actions. The first shot of the war at sea was fired on 5 August 1914 by [HMS *Lance*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Lance_\(1914\)), one of the [3rd Destroyer Flotilla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3rd_Destroyer_Flotilla), in an engagement with the German auxiliary [minelayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minelayer) [*Königin Luise*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_K%C3%B6nigin_Luise_\(1913\)).[^cite_note-39]

Destroyers were involved in the skirmishes that prompted the [Battle of Heligoland Bight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Heligoland_\(1914\)), and filled a range of roles in the [Battle of Gallipoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Gallipoli), acting as troop transports and as fire-support vessels, as well as their fleet-screening role. Over 80 British destroyers and 60 German torpedo boats took part in the [Battle of Jutland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Jutland), which involved pitched small-boat actions between the main fleets, and several foolhardy attacks by unsupported destroyers on capital ships. Jutland also concluded with a messy night action between the German [High Seas Fleet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Seas_Fleet) and part of the British destroyer screen.



[USS *Wickes*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Wickes_\(DD-75\)), a [*Wickes*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wickes-class_destroyer) destroyer

The threat evolved by World War I with the development of the [submarine](28825.md), or [U-boat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-boat). The submarine had the potential to hide from gunfire and close underwater to fire torpedoes. Early-war destroyers had the speed and armament to intercept submarines before they submerged, either by gunfire or by ramming. Destroyers also had a shallow enough draft that they were difficult to hit with torpedoes.



[HMS *Badger*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Badger_\(1911\)) was the first destroyer to successfully ram a submarine.

The desire to attack submarines under water led to rapid destroyer evolution during the war. They were quickly equipped with strengthened bows for ramming, and [depth charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depth_charge) and [hydrophones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrophone) for identifying submarine targets. The first submarine casualty credited to a destroyer was the German [*U-19*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SM_U-19_\(Germany\)), rammed by [HMS *Badger*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Badger_\(1911\)) on 29 October 1914. While *U-19* was only damaged, the next month, [HMS *Garry*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Garry) successfully sank [*U-18*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SM_U-18). The first depth-charge sinking was on 4 December 1916, when [*UC-19*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SM_UC-19)[^cite_note-40] was sunk by HMS *Llewellyn*.

The submarine threat meant that many destroyers spent their time on antisubmarine patrol. Once Germany adopted [unrestricted submarine warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unrestricted_submarine_warfare) in January 1917, destroyers were called on to escort merchant [convoys](42990.md). US Navy destroyers were among the first American units to be dispatched upon the American entry to the war, and a squadron of Japanese destroyers even joined Allied patrols in the Mediterranean. Patrol duty was far from safe; of the 67 British destroyers lost in the war, collisions accounted for 18, while 12 were wrecked.

At the end of the war, the state-of-the-art was represented by the British [W class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V_and_W-class_destroyer).

## 1918–1945

See also: [List of destroyers of the Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_destroyers_of_the_Second_World_War)



V-class destroyer, [HMS *Velox*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Velox_\(D34\))

The trend during World War I had been towards larger destroyers with heavier armaments. A number of opportunities to fire at capital ships had been missed during the war, because destroyers had expended all their torpedoes in an initial salvo. The British [V and W classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V_and_W-class_destroyer) of the late war had sought to address this by mounting six torpedo tubes in two triple mounts, instead of the four or two on earlier models. The V and W classes set the standard of destroyer building well into the 1920s.[^cite_note-41]

Two Romanian destroyers [*Mărăști*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NMS_M%C4%83r%C4%83%C8%99ti) and [*Mărășești*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NMS_M%C4%83r%C4%83%C8%99e%C8%99ti), though, had the greatest firepower of all destroyers in the world throughout the first half of the 1920s. This was largely because, between their commissioning in 1920 and 1926, they retained the armament that they had while serving in the Italian Navy as [scout cruisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scout_cruiser) (*[esploratori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esploratori)*). When initially ordered by Romania in 1913, the Romanian specifications envisioned three 120 mm guns, a calibre which would eventually be adopted as the standard for future Italian destroyers. Armed with three 152 mm and four 76 mm guns after being completed as scout cruisers, the two warships were officially re-rated as destroyers by the [Romanian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_Navy). The two Romanian warships were thus the destroyers with the greatest firepower in the world throughout much of the interwar period. As of 1939, when the Second World War started, their artillery, although changed, was still close to cruiser standards, amounting to nine heavy naval guns (five of 120 mm and four of 76 mm). In addition, they retained their two twin 457 mm torpedo tubes and two machine guns, plus the capacity to carry up to 50 mines.[^cite_note-42]



*Fubuki*-class destroyer, [*Uranami*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_destroyer_Uranami_\(1928\))

The next major innovation came with the Japanese [*Fubuki* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fubuki-class_destroyer) or "special type", designed in 1923 and delivered in 1928. The design was initially noted for its powerful armament of six 5-inch (127 mm) guns and three triple torpedo mounts. The second batch of the class gave the guns high-angle turrets for antiaircraft warfare, and the 24-inch (61 cm), oxygen-fueled Long Lance [Type 93 torpedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_93_torpedo). The later [*Hatsuharu* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatsuharu-class_destroyer) of 1931 further improved the torpedo armament by storing its reload torpedoes close at hand in the superstructure, allowing reloading within 15 minutes.[^cite_note-43]

Most other nations replied with similar larger ships. The US [*Porter* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter-class_destroyer) adopted twin 5-inch (127 mm) guns, and the subsequent [*Mahan* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahan-class_destroyer) and [*Gridley* classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gridley-class_destroyer) (the latter of 1934) increased the number of torpedo tubes to 12 and 16, respectively.



France's [*Le Fantasque* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Fantasque-class_destroyer) is the fastest destroyer class ever built.

In the Mediterranean, the Italian Navy's building of very fast light cruisers of the [*Condottieri* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condottieri-class_cruiser) prompted the French to produce exceptional destroyer designs. The French had long been keen on large destroyers, with their [*Chacal* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chacal-class_destroyer) of 1922 displacing over 2,000 tons and carrying 130 mm guns; a further three similar classes were produced around 1930. The [*Le Fantasque* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Fantasque-class_destroyer) of 1935 carried five 138 millimetres (5.4 in) guns and nine torpedo tubes, but could achieve speeds of 45 knots (83 km/h), which remains the record speed for a steamship and for any destroyer.[^cite_note-44] The Italians' own destroyers were almost as swift; most Italian designs of the 1930s were rated at over 38 knots (70 km/h), while carrying torpedoes and either four or six 120 mm guns.

Germany started to build destroyers again during the 1930s as part of Hitler's rearmament program. The Germans were also fond of large destroyers, but while the initial [Type 1934](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_World_War_II_destroyers) displaced over 3,000 tons, their armament was equal to smaller vessels. This changed from the Type 1936 onwards, which mounted heavy 150 millimetres (5.9 in) guns. German destroyers also used innovative high-pressure steam machinery; while this should have helped their efficiency, it more often resulted in mechanical problems.

HNLMS Tjerk Hiddes, a British built N-class destroyer, laid down on 22 May 1940 and transferred to the Royal Netherlands Navy

HNLMS *[Tjerk Hiddes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HNLMS_Tjerk_Hiddes_\(G16\))*, a British built N-class destroyer, laid down on 22 May 1940 and transferred to the Royal Netherlands Navy&#x20;

Once German and Japanese rearmament became clear, the British and American navies consciously focused on building destroyers that were smaller, but more numerous than those used by other nations. The British built a series of destroyers (the [A class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A-class_destroyer_\(1929\)) to [I class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I-class_destroyer)), which were about 1,400 tons standard displacement, and had four 4.7-inch (119 mm) guns and eight torpedo tubes; the American [*Benson* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benson-class_destroyer) of 1938 was similar in size, but carried five 5-inch (127 mm) guns and ten torpedo tubes. Realizing the need for heavier gun armament, the British built the [Tribal class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribal-class_destroyer_\(1936\)) of 1936 (sometimes called *Afridi* after one of two lead ships). These ships displaced 1,850 tons and were armed with eight 4.7-inch (119 mm) guns in four twin turrets and four torpedo tubes. These were followed by the [J-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J-,_K-_and_N-class_destroyer) and L-class destroyers, with six 4.7-inch (119 mm) guns in twin turrets and eight torpedo tubes.

Antisubmarine sensors included [sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar) (or ASDIC), although training in their use was indifferent. Antisubmarine weapons changed little, and ahead-throwing weapons, a need recognized in World War I, had made no progress.

### Later combat

Further information: [American World War II destroyers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_World_War_II_destroyers), [British World War II destroyers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_World_War_II_destroyers), [French World War II destroyers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_World_War_II_destroyers), [German World War II destroyers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_World_War_II_destroyers), [Italian World War II destroyers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_World_War_II_destroyers), [Japanese World War II destroyers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_World_War_II_destroyers), [Soviet World War II destroyers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_World_War_II_destroyers), and [Romanian World War II destroyers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_World_War_II_destroyers)



[USS *McGowan*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_McGowan), a [*Fletcher*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fletcher-class_destroyer) destroyer during World War II

During the 1920s and 1930s, destroyers were often deployed to areas of diplomatic tension or humanitarian disaster. British and American destroyers were common on the Chinese coast and rivers, even supplying landing parties to protect colonial interests. By World War II, the threat had evolved once again. Submarines were more effective, and [aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft) had become important weapons of naval warfare; once again the early-war fleet destroyers were ill-equipped for combating these new targets. They were fitted with new light [antiaircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiaircraft) guns, [radar](25676.md), and [forward-launched ASW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedgehog_\(weapon\)) weapons, in addition to their existing [dual-purpose guns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual-purpose_gun), [depth charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depth_charge), and torpedoes. Increasing size allowed improved internal arrangement of propulsion machinery with [compartmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compartmentation), so ships were less likely to be sunk by a single hit.[^cite_note-ast-6] In most cases torpedo and/or dual-purpose gun armament was reduced to accommodate new [anti-air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-air_warfare) and [anti-submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-submarine_warfare) weapons. By this time the destroyers had become large, multi-purpose vessels, expensive targets in their own right. As a result, casualties on destroyers were among the highest. In the US Navy, particularly in World War II, destroyers became known as tin cans due to their light armour compared to battleships and cruisers.

The need for large numbers of antisubmarine ships led to the introduction of smaller and cheaper specialized antisubmarine warships called [corvettes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corvette) and [frigates](11464.md) by the Royal Navy and [destroyer escorts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destroyer_escort) by the USN. A similar programme was belatedly started by the Japanese (see [*Matsu*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matsu-class_destroyer) destroyer). These ships had the size and displacement of the original TBDs from which the contemporary destroyer had evolved.

## Post-World War II



The [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Navy) destroyer [ORP *Błyskawica*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ORP_B%C5%82yskawica) is preserved as a [museum ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_ship) in [Gdynia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gdynia).

Some conventional destroyers completed in the late 1940s and 1950s were built on wartime experience. These vessels were significantly larger than wartime ships and had fully automatic main guns, unit machinery, radar, sonar, and antisubmarine weapons, such as the [squid mortar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squid_\(weapon\)). Examples include the British [*Daring*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daring-class_destroyer_\(1949\)), US [*Forrest Sherman*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forrest_Sherman-class_destroyer), and the Soviet [Kotlin-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kotlin-class_destroyer) destroyers.

Some World War II–vintage ships were modernized for antisubmarine warfare, and to extend their service lives, to avoid having to build (expensive) brand-new ships. Examples include the US [FRAM I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FRAM_I) programme and the British [Type 15 frigates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_15_frigate) converted from fleet destroyers.

The advent of [surface-to-air missiles](182664.md) and [surface-to-surface missiles](1483499.md), such as the [Exocet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exocet), in the early 1960s changed naval warfare. [Guided-missile destroyers](232005.md) (DDG in the US Navy) were developed to carry these weapons and protect the fleet from air, submarine, and surface threats. Examples include the Soviet [Kashin class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashin-class_destroyer), the British [County class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County-class_destroyer), and the US [*Charles F. Adams* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_F._Adams-class_destroyer).[^cite_note-\:0-45][^cite_note-\:0-45]

The 21st century destroyers tend to display features such as large, slab sides without complicated corners and crevices to keep the [radar cross-section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_cross-section) small, [vertical launch systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_launch_system) to carry a large number of missiles at high readiness to fire, and [helicopter](8286923.md) flight decks and [hangars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangars).

  [^cite_note-54]

  &#x20;The *Hyūga*-class ships, despite officially being classified as helicopter destroyers, incorporate a level of armament more akin to [helicopter cruisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helicopter_cruiser) than [helicopter carriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helicopter_carrier). The even larger [Izumo-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izumo-class_destroyer) vessels were classed as destroyers as well until being reclassified as "aircraft-carrying multi-role cruisers" (CVM) in 2025.

  [^cite_note-izumo_cruiser-55]

-  [Republic of Korea Navy](2284275.md): 4 [*Sejong the Great*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sejong_the_Great-class_destroyer) destroyers. Despite their classification as a destroyer, many naval analysts feel they are in fact cruisers due to their size and armament, which are both greater than most of the world's destroyer classes.

  [^cite_note-56]

  [^cite_note-57]

-  [United States Navy](20518076.md): 2 [*Zumwalt*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zumwalt-class_destroyer) destroyers (plus one on sea trials as of 2026). Even if considered a destroyer, they remain significantly larger and more capable than the only definitive cruisers in USN service, the *Ticonderoga*-class.

  [^cite_note-58]

## Future development

- The  [Brazilian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazilian_Navy) plans to build 7,000-ton destroyers after the delivery of the new frigates, and TKMS presented to the Navy its most modern 7,200-ton MEKO A-400 air defence destroyer, an updated version of the German [F-125](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baden-W%C3%BCrttemberg-class_frigate)-class frigates. The similarities between the projects and the high rate of commonality between requirements were also crucial for the consortium's victory.

  [^cite_note-59]

- The  [Royal Canadian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Canadian_Navy) is building up to 15 [River-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River-class_destroyer_\(2030s\)) destroyers based on the Royal Navy's [Type 26 Global Combat Ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_26_Global_Combat_Ship). They will be more powerful than the Type 26, being fitted with the [Aegis Combat System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegis_Combat_System) and long range surface-to-air and surface-to-surface missiles.

- The  [People's Liberation Army Navy](24918.md) is adding six more [Type 052D destroyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_052D_destroyer) and sixteen more [Type 055 destroyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_055_destroyer) class ships to its navy.

- The  [French Navy](383256.md) is building five new [*Amiral Ronarc'h*-class destroyers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fr%C3%A9gate_de_d%C3%A9fense_et_d'intervention) (classed as "first rank frigates" in the French Navy).

  [^cite_note-frenchnavy-49]

- The  [Hellenic Navy](425896.md) has ordered three [Frégate de défense et d'intervention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fr%C3%A9gate_de_d%C3%A9fense_et_d'intervention) (with an option on a fourth) from France.

- The  [Indian Navy](604522.md) has begun development of its [Project-18 Next Generation Destroyers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_18-class_destroyer).

  [^cite_note-60]

- The  [Islamic Republic of Iran Navy](2160969.md) is building 1-2 [*Khalije Fars*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khalije_Fars-class_destroyer) destroyers.

- The  [Italian Navy](1126939.md) is researching development into their new DDX project to replace their *Durand da le Penne*-class destroyers.

  [^cite_note-61]

- The  [Japan Maritime Self-Defense Force](2370744.md) is developing plans for its [DDR Destroyer Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DDR_Destroyer_Revolution) Project.

- The  [Philippine Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine_Navy) will receive 5 [*Abukuma*-class destroyer escorts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abukuma-class_destroyer_escort) from Japan starting in 2027.

- The  [Republic of Korea Navy](2284275.md) has begun development of its [KDX-IIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KDX-IIA) destroyers. These ships are to be a subclass of South Korea's [*Chungmugong Yi Sun-shin*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chungmugong_Yi_Sun-shin-class_destroyer) destroyers. The first unit is expected to enter service in 2019. Additionally, [*Sejong the Great*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sejong_the_Great-class_destroyer) destroyers are being built.

- The  [Russian Navy](383258.md) has begun development of its [Lider-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lider-class_destroyer) destroyer. Design work was ongoing as of 2020.

- The  [Turkish Naval Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_Naval_Forces) is currently developing its [TF2000-class destroyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TF2000-class_frigate) as the largest part of the [MILGEM project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MILGEM_project). A total of seven ships will be constructed and will specialise in [anti-air warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-air_warfare).

- The United Kingdom's  [Royal Navy](26061.md) had started planning for a new class of destroyer, the [Type 83 destroyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_83_destroyer) after the unveiling of these plans in the [2021 defence white paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defence_in_a_Competitive_Age). The class had been projected to replace the [Type 45 destroyer](466618.md) fleet beginning in the latter 2030s.

  [^cite_note-62]

  &#x20;However, the project was cancelled in 2026 in favor of a cheaper "Common Combat Vessel".

- The  [United States Navy](20518076.md) has 19 additional *Arleigh Burke* destroyers planned or under construction. The new ships will be the upgraded "flight III" version.

  [^cite_note-CRS_summary-63]

  &#x20;The United States has also started development of its [DDG(X)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DDG\(X\)) next-generation destroyer project.

  [^cite_note-64]

  &#x20;Construction of the first ship is expected to start in 2028.
