A **foreshock** is an [earthquake](10106.md) that occurs before a larger seismic event – the [mainshock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainshock) – and is related to it in both time and space. The designation of an earthquake as *foreshock*, *mainshock* or [aftershock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aftershock) is only possible after the full sequence of events has happened.[^cite_note-Gates-1]

## Occurrence

Foreshock activity has been detected for about 40% of all moderate to large earthquakes,[^cite_note-NRS-2] and about 70% for events of M>7.0.[^cite_note-Kayal-3] They occur from a matter of minutes to days or even longer before the main shock; for example, the [2002 Sumatra earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_Sumatra_earthquake) is regarded as a foreshock of the [2004 Indian Ocean earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Indian_Ocean_earthquake) with a delay of more than two years between the two events.[^cite_note-Vallée-4]

Some great earthquakes (M>8.0) show no foreshock activity at all, such as the M8.6 [1950 India–China earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1950_Assam%E2%80%93Tibet_earthquake).[^cite_note-Kayal-3]

The increase in foreshock activity is very difficult to quantify for individual earthquakes but becomes apparent when combining the results of many different events. From such combined observations, the increase before the mainshock is observed to be of [inverse power law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_law) type. This may either indicate that foreshocks [cause stress changes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb_stress_transfer) resulting in the mainshock or that the increase is related to a general increase in stress in the region.[^cite_note-Maeda-5]

## Mechanics

The observation of foreshocks associated with many earthquakes suggests that they are part of a preparation process prior to [nucleation](10106.md#Rupture_dynamics).[^cite_note-NRS-2] In one model of earthquake rupture, the process forms as a cascade, starting with a very small event that triggers a larger one, continuing until the main shock rupture is triggered. However, analysis of some foreshocks has shown that they tend to relieve stress around the fault. In this view, foreshocks and aftershocks are part of the same process. This is supported by an observed relationship between the rate of foreshocks and the rate of aftershocks for an event.[^cite_note-Felzer-6] In practice, there are two main conflicting theories about foreshocks: earthquake triggering process (described in SOC models and ETAS-like models) and the loading process by aseismic slip (nucleation models). This debate about the prognostic value of foreshocks is well known as Foreshock Hypothesis.[^cite_note-Mignan-7]

## Earthquake prediction

An increase in seismic activity in an area has been used as a method of [predicting earthquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthquake_prediction), most notably in the case of the [1975 Haicheng earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1975_Haicheng_earthquake) in China, where an evacuation was triggered by an increase in activity. However, most earthquakes lack obvious foreshock patterns and this method has not proven useful, as most small earthquakes are not foreshocks, leading to probable false alarms.[^cite_note-Ludwin-8] Earthquakes along oceanic [transform faults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transform_fault) do show repeatable foreshock behaviour, allowing the prediction of both the location and timing of such earthquakes.[^cite_note-McGuire-9] [Ring shaped patterns of foreshocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring-shaped_seismicity_structures) may precede strong earthquakes.
