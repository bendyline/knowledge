The **polar bear** (***Ursus maritimus***) is a large [bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bear) native to the [Arctic](36971.md) and nearby areas. It is closely related to the [brown bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_bear), and the two species can [interbreed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_\(biology\)). The polar bear is the largest extant species of bear and land [carnivore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnivore) by body mass, with adult males weighing 300–800 kg (660–1,760 lb). The species is [sexually dimorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexually_dimorphic), as adult females are much smaller. The polar bear is white or yellowish-furred with black skin and a [thick layer of fat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blubber). It is more slender than the brown bear, with a narrower skull, longer neck and lower shoulder hump. Its teeth are sharper and more adapted to cutting [meat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meat). The paws are large and allow the bear to walk on ice and paddle in the water.

Polar bears are both terrestrial and [pagophilic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pagophily) (ice-living) and are considered [marine mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_mammal) because of their dependence on [marine ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_ecosystem). They prefer the annual [sea ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_ice) but live on land when the ice melts in the summer. They are mostly carnivorous and specialized for preying on [seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinniped), particularly [ringed seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ringed_seal). Such prey is typically taken by ambush; the bear may stalk its prey on the ice or in the water, but also will stay at a breathing hole or ice edge to wait for prey to swim by. The bear primarily feeds on the seal's energy-rich [blubber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blubber). Other prey include [walruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walrus), [beluga whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beluga_whale) and some terrestrial animals. Polar bears are usually solitary but can be found in groups when on land. During the breeding season, male bears guard females and defend them from rivals. Mothers give birth to cubs in [maternity dens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maternity_den) during the winter. Young stay with their mother for up to two and a half years.

The polar bear is considered a [vulnerable species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulnerable_species) by the [International Union for Conservation of Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Union_for_Conservation_of_Nature) (IUCN) with an estimated total population of 22,000 to 31,000 individuals. Its biggest threats are [climate change](5042951.md), [pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollution) and [energy development](533487.md). Climate change has caused a [decline in sea ice](40659364.md), giving the polar bear less access to its favoured prey and increasing the risk of [malnutrition](258979.md), starvation, and eventually death. Less sea ice also means that the bears must spend more time on land, increasing [conflicts with humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_bear_attack).[^cite_note-5] Polar bears have been hunted, both by [native](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumpolar_peoples) and non-native peoples, for their coats, meat and other items. They have been kept in [captivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captivity) in [zoos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoo) and [circuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circus) and are prevalent in [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art), [folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folklore), religion and [modern culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_culture).

## Naming

The polar bear was given its common name by [Thomas Pennant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Pennant) in *A Synopsis of Quadrupeds* (1771). It was known as the "white bear" in Europe between the 13th and 18th centuries, as well as "ice bear", "sea bear" and "[Greenland](12118.md) bear". The [Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norsemen) referred to it as *isbjørn* 'ice bear' and *hvitebjørn* 'white bear'. The bear is called *nanook* by the [Inuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit). The [Netsilik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netsilik) cultures additionally have different names for bears based on certain factors, such as sex and age: these include adult males (*anguraq*), single adult females (*tattaq*), gestating females (*arnaluk*), newborns (*hagliaqtug*), large adolescents (*namiaq*) and dormant bears (*apitiliit*).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard201713,_31,_68–69,_122,_253-6] The scientific name *Ursus maritimus* is [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) for 'sea bear'.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard201748-7][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher20121-8]

## Taxonomy

[Carl Linnaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Linnaeus) classified the polar bear as a type of [brown bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_bear) (*Ursus arctos*), labelling it as *Ursus maritimus albus-major, arcticus* ('mostly-white sea bear, arctic') in the [1758 edition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/10th_edition_of_Systema_Naturae) of his work *[Systema Naturae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systema_Naturae)*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFee201948-9] [Constantine John Phipps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_John_Phipps) formally described the polar bear as a distinct species, *Ursus maritimus* in 1774, following his [1773 voyage towards the North Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1773_Phipps_expedition_towards_the_North_Pole).[^cite_note-Phipps1774-4][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFee201941-10] Because of its adaptations to a marine environment, some taxonomists, such as Theodore Knottnerus-Meyer, have placed the polar bear in its own genus, *Thalarctos*.[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200967-12] However *Ursus* is widely considered to be the valid genus for the species on the basis of the fossil record and the fact that it [can breed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grizzly%E2%80%93polar_bear_hybrid) with the brown bear.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200967-12][^cite_note-ReferenceC-13]

Different [subspecies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspecies) have been proposed including *Ursus maritimus maritimus* and *U. m. marinus*.[^cite_note-15][^cite_note-Wilson1976-14] However, these are not supported, and the polar bear is considered to be [monotypic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotypic_taxon).[^cite_note-\:0-16] One possible fossil subspecies, *[U. m. tyrannus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ursus_maritimus_tyrannus)*, was posited in 1964 by [Björn Kurtén](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bj%C3%B6rn_Kurt%C3%A9n), who reconstructed the subspecies from a single fragment of an ulna which was approximately 20 percent larger than expected for a polar bear.[^cite_note-ReferenceC-13] However, re-evaluation in the 21st century has indicated that the fragment likely comes from a giant brown bear.[^cite_note-Harington-17][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201237-18]

### Evolution

The polar bear is one of eight extant species in the [bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bear) family, Ursidae, and of six extant species in the subfamily [Ursinae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ursinae). One possible phylogeny is based on complete [mitochondrial DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondrial_DNA) sequences from Yu et al. (2007).[^cite_note-19] The polar bear and the brown bear form a close grouping, while the relationships of the other species are not very well resolved.[^cite_note-Servheen1999-20]

Fossils of polar bears are uncommon.[^cite_note-ReferenceC-13][^cite_note-Harington-17] The oldest known fossil is a 130,000- to 110,000-year-old jaw bone, found on [Prince Charles Foreland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Charles_Foreland), Norway, in 2004.[^cite_note-Lindqvist-22][^cite_note-polarresearch-1] Scientists in the 20th century surmised that polar bears directly descended from a population of brown bears, possibly in eastern [Siberia](29639.md) or [Alaska](624.md).[^cite_note-ReferenceC-13][^cite_note-Harington-17] [Mitochondrial DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondrial_DNA) studies in the 1990s and 2000s supported the status of the polar bear as a derivative of the brown bear, finding that some brown bear populations were more closely related to polar bears than to other brown bears, particularly the [ABC Islands bears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABC_Islands_bear) of [Southeast Alaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Alaska).[^cite_note-Lindqvist-22][^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24] A 2010 study estimated that the polar bear lineage split from other brown bears around 150,000 years ago.[^cite_note-Lindqvist-22]



[Polar/brown bear hybrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grizzly%E2%80%93polar_bear_hybrid) taxidermy specimen on display at [Natural History Museum at Tring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_History_Museum_at_Tring) in [Hertfordshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertfordshire), England

More extensive genetic studies have refuted the idea that polar bears are directly descended from brown bears and found that the two species are separate [sister lineages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sister_group). The genetic similarities between polar bears and some brown bears were found to be the result of interbreeding.[^cite_note-Hailer-25][^cite_note-Lan-26] A 2012 study estimated the split between polar and brown bears as occurring around 600,000 years ago.[^cite_note-Hailer-25] A 2022 study estimated the divergence as occurring even earlier at over one million years ago.[^cite_note-Lan-26] [Glaciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaciation) events over hundreds of thousands of years led to both the origin of polar bears and their subsequent interactions and hybridizations with brown bears.[^cite_note-Hassanin-27]

Studies in 2011 and 2012 concluded that [gene flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_flow) went from brown bears to polar bears during hybridization.[^cite_note-Hailer-25][^cite_note-Edwards-28] In particular, a 2011 study concluded that living polar bear populations derived their [maternal lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matriline) from now-extinct Irish brown bears.[^cite_note-Edwards-28] Later studies have clarified that gene flow went from polar to brown bears rather than the reverse.[^cite_note-Hassanin-27][^cite_note-Cahill-29][^cite_note-30] Up to 9 percent of the genome of ABC bears was transferred from polar bears,[^cite_note-31] while Irish bears had up to 21.5 percent polar bear origin.[^cite_note-Cahill-29] Mass hybridization between the two species appears to have stopped around 200,000 years ago. [Modern hybrids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grizzly%E2%80%93polar_bear_hybrid) are relatively rare in the wild.[^cite_note-Lan-26]

Analysis of the [number of variations of gene copies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copy_number_variation) in polar bears compared with brown bears and [American black bears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_black_bears) shows distinct adaptions. Polar bears have a less diverse array of [olfactory receptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olfactory_receptor) genes, a result of there being fewer odours in their Arctic habitat. With its carnivorous, high-fat diet the species has fewer copies of the gene involved in making [amylase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amylase), an enzyme that breaks down [starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starch), and more selection for genes for fatty acid breakdown and a more efficient [circulatory system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulatory_system). The polar bear's thicker coat is the result of more copies of genes involved in [keratin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keratin)-creating proteins.[^cite_note-32]

## Description



Polar bear skeleton



Bear skull

The polar bear is the largest living species of bear and land [carnivore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnivore), though some brown bear subspecies like the [Kodiak bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kodiak_bear) can rival it in size.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201210-33][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200974-34] Males are generally 200–250 cm (6.6–8.2 ft) long with a weight of 300–800 kg (660–1,760 lb). Females are smaller at 180–200 cm (5.9–6.6 ft) with a weight of 150–300 kg (330–660 lb).[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11] [Sexual dimorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_dimorphism) in the species is particularly high compared with most other mammals.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200975-35] Male polar bears also have proportionally larger heads than females.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200980-36] The weight of polar bears fluctuates during the year, as they can bulk up on fat and increase their mass by 50 percent.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201210-33] A fattened, pregnant female can weigh as much as 500 kg (1,100 lb).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling201138-37] Adults may stand 130–160 cm (4.3–5.2 ft) tall at the shoulder. The tail is 76–126 mm (3.0–5.0 in) long.[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11] The largest polar bear on record, reportedly weighing 1,002 kg (2,209 lb), was a male shot at [Kotzebue Sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kotzebue_Sound) in northwestern Alaska in 1960.[^cite_note-Wood-38]

Compared with the brown bear, this species has a more slender build, with a narrower, flatter and smaller skull, a longer neck, and a lower shoulder hump.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201210-33][^cite_note-Slater2010-39] The snout profile is curved, resembling a "[Roman nose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_nose)".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201210-33] They have 34–42 teeth including 12 [incisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incisor), 4 [canines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canine_tooth), 8–16 [premolars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premolar) and 10 [molars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_\(tooth\)). The teeth are adapted for a more carnivorous diet than that of the brown bear, having longer, sharper and more spaced out canines, and smaller, more pointed [cheek teeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheek_teeth) (premolars and molars).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200975-35][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201217-40][^cite_note-Slater2010-39] The species has a large space or [diastema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diastema) between the canines and cheek teeth, which may allow it to better bite into prey.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201217-40][^cite_note-Figueirido-41] Since it normally preys on animals much smaller than it, the polar bear does not have a particularly strong bite.[^cite_note-Figueirido-41] Polar bears have large paws, with the front paws being broader than the back. The feet are hairier than in other bear species, providing warmth and friction when stepping on snow and sea ice.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201222-42] The claws are small but sharp and hooked and are used both to snatch prey and climb onto ice.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201221-43][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200979-44]



Polar bear jumping on floating ice at [Svalbard](27441.md)

The coat consists of dense [underfur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underfur) around 5 cm (2.0 in) long and [guard hairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guard_hair) around 15 cm (5.9 in) long.[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11] Males have long hairs on their forelegs, which is thought to signal their [fitness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitness_\(biology\)) to females.[^cite_note-Dimorph-45] The outer surface of the hairs has a scaly appearance, and the guard hairs are hollow, which allows the animals to trap heat and float in the water.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher20127–8-46] The transparent guard hairs [forward scatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forward_scatter) ultraviolet light between the underfur and the skin, leading to a cycle of absorption and re-emission, keeping them warm.[^cite_note-Khattab-47] The fur appears white because of the [backscatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backscatter) of [incident light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_\(optics\)#Interaction_with_surfaces) and the absence of pigment.[^cite_note-Khattab-47][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher20127-48] Polar bears gain a yellowish colouration as they are exposed more to the sun. This is reversed after they [moult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moulting). It can also be grayish or brownish.[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11] Their light fur provides [camouflage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camouflage) in their snowy environment. After emerging from the water, the bear can easily shake itself dry since the hairs are resistant to tangling when wet.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200965,_72-49] Oil secretions prevent the hair from freezing.[^cite_note-50] The skin, including the nose and lips, is black and absorbs heat.[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11][^cite_note-Khattab-47] Polar bears have a 5–10 cm (2.0–3.9 in) thick layer of fat underneath the skin,[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11] which provides both warmth and energy.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201212-51] Polar bears maintain their core body temperature at about 36.9 °C (98.4 °F).[^cite_note-52] Overheating is countered by a layer of highly vascularized [striated muscle tissue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Striated_muscle_tissue) and finely controlled blood vessels. Bears also cool off by entering the water.[^cite_note-Khattab-47][^cite_note-53]

The eyes of a polar bear are close to the top of the head, which may allow them to stay out of the water when the animal is swimming at the surface. They are relatively small, which may be an adaption against blowing snow and [snow blindness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photokeratitis). Polar bears are [dichromats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dichromacy), and lack the [cone cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_cell) for seeing medium, mainly green, wavelengths. They have many [rod cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rod_cell), which allow them to see at night. The ears are small, allowing them to better retain heat and not get [frostbitten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frost_bite).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201214,_16,_18–19-54] They can hear best at frequencies of 11.2–22.5 kHz, a wider frequency range than expected given that their prey mostly makes low-frequency sounds.[^cite_note-55] The [nasal concha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasal_concha) creates a large surface area, so more warm air can move through the nasal passages.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201214-56] Their [olfactory system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olfactory_system) is also large and adapted for smelling prey over vast distances.[^cite_note-57] The animal has [reniculate kidneys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reniculate_kidney) which filter out the salt in their food.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201226-58]

## Distribution and habitat



Map of 19 polar bear subpopulations. The Queen Elizabeth Islands (QE) subpopulation is not universally accepted.

[^cite_note-statusreport-59]

Polar bears inhabit the [Arctic](36971.md) and adjacent areas. Their range includes Greenland, Canada, Alaska, Russia and the [Svalbard Archipelago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Svalbard_Archipelago) of Norway.[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200973,_140-60][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher20123-61] Polar bears have been recorded as close as 25 km (16 mi) from the [North Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pole).[^cite_note-62] The southern limits of their range include [James Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Bay) and [Newfoundland and Labrador](21980.md) in Canada and [St. Matthew Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Matthew_Island) and the [Pribilof Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pribilof_Islands) of Alaska.[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11] They are not permanent residents of Iceland but have been recorded visiting there if they can reach it via sea ice.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis2009122–124-63] As there has been minimal human encroachment on the bears' remote habitat, they can still be found in much of their original range, more of it than any other large land carnivore.[^cite_note-derocher2004-64]

Polar bears have been divided into at least 18 subpopulations labelled East Greenland (ES), [Barents Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barents_Sea) (BS), [Kara Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kara_Sea) (KS), [Laptev Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laptev_Sea) (LVS), [Chukchi Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chukchi_Sea) (CS), northern and southern [Beaufort Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaufort_Sea) (SBS and NBS), [Viscount Melville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscount_Melville_Sound) (VM), [M'Clintock Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M'Clintock_Channel) (MC), [Gulf of Boothia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Boothia) (GB), [Lancaster Sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancaster_Sound) (LS), [Norwegian Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Bay) (NB), [Kane Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kane_Basin) (KB), [Baffin Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baffin_Bay) (BB), [Davis Strait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davis_Strait) (DS), [Foxe Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foxe_Basin) (FB) and the western and southern [Hudson Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudson_Bay) (WHB and SHB) populations.[^cite_note-Peacock2015-65][^cite_note-statusreport-59] Bears in and around the [Queen Elizabeth Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Elizabeth_Islands) have been proposed as a subpopulation but this is not universally accepted.[^cite_note-statusreport-59] A 2022 study has suggested that the bears in southeast Greenland should be considered a different subpopulation based on their geographic isolation and genetics.[^cite_note-66] Polar bear populations can also be divided into four [gene clusters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_cluster): Southern Canadian, [Canadian Archipelago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_Archipelago), Western Basin (northwestern Canada west to the [Russian Far East](173135.md)) and Eastern Basin (Greenland east to Siberia).[^cite_note-Peacock2015-65]

The polar bear is dependent enough on the ocean to be considered a [marine mammal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_mammal).[^cite_note-\:0-16][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011XIII-67] It is [pagophilic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pagophilic) and mainly inhabits annual [sea ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_ice) covering [continental shelves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_shelf) and between islands of [archipelagos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archipelagos). These areas, known as the "Arctic Ring of Life", have high [biological productivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Productivity_\(ecology\)).[^cite_note-derocher2004-64][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling20119–10-68] The species tends to frequent areas where sea ice meets water, such as [polynyas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynya) and [leads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_\(sea_ice\)), to hunt the [seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinniped) that make up most of its diet.[^cite_note-69] Polar bears travel in response to changes in ice cover throughout the year. They are forced onto land in summer when the sea ice disappears.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling20119-70] Terrestrial habitats used by polar bears include forests, mountains, rocky areas, lakeshores and creeks.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201267–68-71] In the Chukchi and Beaufort seas, most polar bears follow the retreating summer ice north, though 15–40% now remain on land—a trend observed since the 1980s.[^cite_note-72] Some areas have thick [multiyear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_ice#Old_sea_ice) ice that does not completely melt and the bears can stay on all year,[^cite_note-Vongraven-73][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling20114-74] though this type of ice has fewer seals and allows for less productivity in the water.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling20114-74]

## Behaviour and ecology

Polar bears may travel areas as small as 3,500 km<sup>2</sup> (1,400 sq mi) to as large as 38,000 km<sup>2</sup> (15,000 sq mi) in a year, while drifting ice allows them to move further.[^cite_note-75] Depending on ice conditions, a bear can travel an average of 12 km (7.5 mi) per day.[^cite_note-76] These movements are powered by their energy-rich diet.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201212-51] Polar bears move by walking and [galloping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galloping) and do not [trot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trot).[^cite_note-77] Walking bears tilt their front paws towards each other.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201221-43] They can run at estimated speeds of up to 40 km/h (25 mph)[^cite_note-Brook-78] but typically move at around 5.5 km/h (3.4 mph).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011140-79] Polar bears are also capable swimmers and can swim at up to 6 km/h (3.7 mph).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200988-80] One study found they can swim for an average of 3.4 days at a time and travel an average of 154.2 km (95.8 mi).[^cite_note-81] They can dive for as long as three minutes.[^cite_note-Stirling_&_van_Meurs_2015-82] When swimming, the broad front paws do the paddling, while the hind legs play a role in steering and diving.[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201221-43]



Mother bear and cubs sleeping

Most polar bears are active year-round. [Hibernation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibernation) occurs only among pregnant females.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201228-83] Non-hibernating bears typically have a normal [24-hour cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circadian_rhythm) even during days of [all darkness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_night) or [all sunlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midnight_sun), though cycles less than a day are more common during the former.[^cite_note-84] The species is generally [diurnal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diurnality), being most active early in the day.[^cite_note-85] Polar bears sleep close to eight hours a day on average.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011141-86] They will sleep in various positions, including curled up, sitting up, lying on one side, on the back with limbs spread, or on the belly with the rump elevated.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200979-44][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011140-79] On sea ice, polar bears snooze at [pressure ridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_ridge_\(ice\)) where they dig on the sheltered side and lie down. After a snowstorm, a bear may rest under the snow for hours or days. On land, the bears may dig a resting spot on gravel or sand beaches.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011140–141-87] They will also sleep on rocky outcrops.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201268-88] In mountainous areas on the coast, mothers and subadults will sleep on slopes where they can better spot another bear coming.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011141-86] Adult males are less at risk from other bears and can sleep nearly anywhere.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201268-88]

### Social life

Young bears play-fighting

Polar bears are typically solitary, aside from mothers with cubs and mating pairs.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011105-89] On land, they are found closer together and gather around food resources. Adult males, in particular, are more tolerant of each other in land environments and outside the breeding season.[^cite_note-Derocher1990-90][^cite_note-Ferguson1997-91] They have been recorded forming stable "alliances", travelling, resting and playing together. A [dominance hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominance_hierarchy) exists among polar bears with the largest mature males ranking at the top. Adult females outrank subadults and adolescents and younger males outrank females of the same age. In addition, cubs with their mothers outrank those on their own.[^cite_note-Ovsyanikov2005-92] Females with dependent offspring tend to stay away from males,[^cite_note-Ferguson1997-91] but are sometimes associated with other female–offspring units, creating "composite families".[^cite_note-Ovsyanikov2005-92]

Polar bears are generally quiet but can produce various sounds.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201230-93] [Chuffing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prusten), a soft pulsing call, is made by mother bears presumably to keep in contact with their young.[^cite_note-Wemmer1976-94] During the breeding season, adult males will chuff at potential mates.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201231-95] Unlike other animals where chuffing is passed through the nostrils, in polar bears it is emitted through a partially open mouth.[^cite_note-Wemmer1976-94] Cubs will cry for attention and produce humming noises while nursing.[^cite_note-96] Teeth chops, jaw pops, blows, huffs, moans, [growls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growling) and [roars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roar) are heard in more hostile encounters.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201231-95] A polar bear visually communicates with its eyes, ears, nose and lips.[^cite_note-Ovsyanikov2005-92] Chemical communication can also be important: bears secrete their scent from their foot pads into their tracks, allowing individuals to keep track of one another.[^cite_note-97]

### Diet and hunting



Bear feeding on a [bearded seal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bearded_seal)

The polar bear is a [hypercarnivore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercarnivore),[^cite_note-Sacco2004-98] and the most carnivorous species of bear.[^cite_note-Slater2010-39] It is an [apex predator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apex_predator) of the Arctic,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011155-99] preying on [ice-living seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_seal) and consuming their energy-rich [blubber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blubber).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201269-100] The most commonly taken species is the [ringed seal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ringed_seal), but they also prey on [bearded seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bearded_seal) and [harp seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harp_seal).[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11] Ringed seals are ideal prey as they are abundant and small enough to be overpowered by even small bears.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011155–156-101] Bearded seal adults are larger and are more likely to break free from an attacking bear, hence adult male bears are more successful in hunting them. Less common prey are [hooded seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooded_seal), [spotted seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spotted_seal), [ribbon seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribbon_seal) and the more temperate-living [harbour seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harbor_seal).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201273,_76–77-102] Polar bears, mostly adult males, will occasionally hunt [walruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walrus) both on land and ice. They mainly target young walruses, as adults, with their thick skin and long tusks, are too large and formidable.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011161-103]

Besides seals, bears will prey on [cetacean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cetacea) species such as [beluga whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beluga_whale) and [narwhals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narwhal), as well as [reindeer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reindeer), birds and their eggs, fish and [marine invertebrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_invertebrates).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201280–88-104] They rarely eat plant material as their digestive system is too specialized for animal matter,[^cite_note-105] though they have been recorded eating berries, moss, grass and [seaweed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seaweed).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201288–89-106] In their southern range, especially near Hudson Bay and James Bay, polar bears endure all summer without sea ice to hunt from and must subsist more on terrestrial foods.[^cite_note-ReferenceB-107] Fat reserves allow polar bears to survive for months without eating.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200989-108] [Cannibalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannibalism) is known to occur in the species.[^cite_note-Taylor1985-109]

Polar bears hunt their prey in several different ways. When a bear spots a seal [hauling out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hauling-out) on the sea ice, it slowly stalks it with the head and neck lowered, possibly to make its dark nose and eyes less noticeable. As it gets closer, the bear crouches more and eventually charges at a high speed, attempting to catch the seal before it can escape into its ice hole. Some stalking bears need to move through water; traversing through water cavities in the ice when approaching the seal or swimming towards a seal on an ice floe. The polar bear can stay underwater with its nose exposed. When it gets close enough, the animal lunges from the water to attack.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011170–171-110]

During a limited time in spring, polar bears will search for ringed seal pups in their birth lairs underneath the ice. Once a bear catches the scent of a hiding pup and pinpoints its location, it approaches the den quietly to not alert it. It uses its front feet to smash through the ice and then pokes its head in to catch the pup before it can escape. A ringed seal's lair can be more than 1 m (3 ft 3 in) below the surface of the ice and thus more massive bears are better equipped for breaking in. Some bears may simply stay still near a breathing hole or other spot near the water and [wait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sit-and-wait_predators) for prey to come by.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher2012127–129,_131-111] This can last hours and when a seal surfaces the bear will try to pull it out with its paws and claws.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200991-112] This tactic is the primary hunting method from winter to early spring.[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11]



Bear with whale carcass

Bears hunt walrus groups by provoking them into stampeding and then look for young that have been crushed or separated from their mothers during the turmoil.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011161-103] There are reports of bears trying to kill or injure walruses by throwing rocks and pieces of ice on them.[^cite_note-113] Belugas and narwhals are vulnerable to bear attacks when they are stranded in shallow water or stuck in isolated breathing holes in the ice.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201280–83-114] When stalking reindeer, polar bears will hide in vegetation before an ambush.[^cite_note-Brook-78] On some occasions, bears may try to catch prey in open water, swimming underneath a seal or [aquatic bird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquatic_bird). Seals in particular, however, are more agile than bears in the water.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201284–85,_132-115] Polar bears rely on raw power when trying to kill their prey, and will employ bites and paw swipes.[^cite_note-Sacco2004-98] They have the strength to pull a mid-sized seal out of the water or haul a beluga carcass for quite some distance.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis2009112-116] Polar bears only occasionally [store](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoarding_\(animal_behavior\)) food for later—burying it under snow—and only in the short term.[^cite_note-117]

Polar bears themselves will [scavenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scavenger) as facultative [necrophages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necrophage). Subadult bears will eat remains left behind by others. Females with cubs often abandon a carcass when they see an adult male approaching, though are less likely to if they have not eaten in a long time.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011191–193-118] [Whale](33777.md) carcasses are a valuable food source, particularly on land and after the sea ice melts, and several bears may tolerate each other while feeding on the large rare beached [carrion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrion).[^cite_note-Derocher1990-90][^cite_note-119] In one area in northeastern Alaska, polar bears have been recorded competing with [grizzly bears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grizzly_bear) for whale carcasses. Despite their smaller size, grizzlies are more aggressive and polar bears are likely to yield to them in confrontations.[^cite_note-120] [Arctic foxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_fox) routinely follow polar bears and scavenge scraps from their kills. The bears usually tolerate them but will charge a fox that gets too close when they are feeding. Polar bears will also scavenge at [garbage dumps](211285.md) during ice-free periods.[^cite_note-121][^cite_note-122]

### Reproduction and development



Courting male approaching female

Polar bear mating takes place on the sea ice and during spring, mostly between March and May.[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11][^cite_note-Ramsay1986-123][^cite_note-Derocher2010-124][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011105-89] Males search for females in [estrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estrus) and often travel in twisting paths which reduces the chances of them encountering other males while still allowing them to find females. The movements of females remain linear and they travel more widely.[^cite_note-125] The mating system can be labelled as female-defence [polygyny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygyny_in_animals), [serial monogamy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_monogamy) or [promiscuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promiscuity#Other_animals).[^cite_note-Derocher2010-124][^cite_note-Zey2009-126]

Upon finding a female, a male will try to isolate and guard her. Courtship can be somewhat aggressive, and a male will pursue a female if she tries to run away. It can take days for the male to mate with the female which [induces ovulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induced_ovulation_\(animals\)). After their first copulation, the couple bond. Undisturbed polar bear pairings typically last around two weeks during which they will sleep together and mate multiple times.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher2012141,_145–147-127] Competition for mates can be intense and this has led to [sexual selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_selection) for bigger males. Polar bear males often have scars from fighting.[^cite_note-Ramsay1986-123][^cite_note-Derocher2010-124] A male and female that have already bonded will flee together when another male arrives.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher2012145–147-128] A female mates with multiple males in a season and a single litter can have more than one father.[^cite_note-Zey2009-126]



Polar bear cubs

When the mating season ends, the female will build up more fat reserves to sustain both herself and her young. Sometime between August and October, the female constructs and enters a [maternity den](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maternity_den) for winter. Depending on the area, maternity dens can be found in sea ice just off the coastline or further inland and may be dug underneath snow, earth or a combination of both.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011112,_115,_120-129] The inside of these shelters can be around 1.5 m (4 ft 11 in) wide with a ceiling height of 1.2 m (3 ft 11 in) while the entrance may be 2.1 m (6 ft 11 in) long and 1.2 m (3 ft 11 in) wide. The temperature of a den can be much higher than the outside.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200985-130] Females hibernate and give birth to their cubs in the dens.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201228,_155-131] Hibernating bears [fast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasting) and internally recycle bodily waste. Polar bears experience [delayed implantation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delayed_implantation) and the fertilized embryo does not start development until the fall, between mid-September and mid-October.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011124-132] With delayed implantation, [gestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestation) in the species lasts seven to nine months but actual pregnancy is only two months.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher2012171-133]

Mother polar bears typically give birth to two cubs per litter. As with other bear species, newborn polar bears are tiny and [altricial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precociality_and_altriciality).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011124–125,_131-134] The newborns have woolly hair and pink skin, with a weight of around 600 g (21 oz).[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201210-33] Their eyes remain closed for a month.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200984-135] The mother's fatty milk fuels their growth, and the cubs are kept warm both by the mother's body heat and the den. The mother emerges from the den between late February and early April, and her cubs are well-developed and capable of walking with her.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011126–127-136] At this time they weigh 10–15 kg (22–33 lb).[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11] A polar bear family stays near the den for roughly two weeks; during this time the cubs will move and play around while the mother mostly rests. They eventually head out on the sea ice.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011128-137]

Mother nursing her young

Cubs under a year old stay close to their mother. When she hunts, they stay still and watch until she calls them back.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher2012173,_184-138] Observing and imitating the mother helps the cubs hone their hunting skills.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011186-139] After their first year they become more independent and explore. At around two years old, they are capable of hunting on their own.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher2012184-140] The young suckle their mother as she is lying on her side or sitting on her rump.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011128-137] A [lactating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactating) female cannot conceive and give birth,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher2012181-141] and cubs are weaned between two and two-and-a-half years.[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11] She may simply leave her weaned young or they may be chased away by a courting male.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher2012184-140] Polar bears reach sexual maturity at around four years for females and six years for males.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011128–129-142] Females reach their adult size at 4 or 5 years of age while males are fully grown at twice that age.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher2012185-143]

### Mortality

Polar bears can live up to 30 years.[^cite_note-DeMaster1981-11] The bear's long lifespan and ability to consistently produce young offsets cub deaths in a population. Some cubs die in the dens or the womb if the female is not in good condition. Nevertheless, the female has a chance to produce a surviving litter the next spring if she can eat better in the coming year. Cubs will eventually starve if their mothers cannot kill enough prey.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011204–207-144] Cubs also face threats from [wolves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolves)[^cite_note-145] and adult male bears. Males [kill cubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infanticide_\(zoology\)) to bring their mother back into estrus but also kill young outside the breeding season for food.[^cite_note-Taylor1985-109] A female and her cubs can flee from the slower male. If the male can get close to a cub, the mother may try to fight him off, sometimes at the cost of her life.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011212-146]

Subadult bears, who are independent but not quite mature, have a particularly rough time as they are not as successful hunters as adults. Even when they do succeed, their kill will likely be stolen by a larger bear. Hence subadults have to scavenge and are often underweight and at risk of starvation. At adulthood, polar bears have a high survival rate, though adult males suffer injuries from fights over mates.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011207–208-147] Polar bears are especially susceptible to *[Trichinella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichinella)*, a parasitic [roundworm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundworm) they contract through cannibalism.[^cite_note-148]

## Conservation status

Main article: [Polar bear conservation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_bear_conservation)



Map from the [U.S. Geological Survey](23814944.md) shows projected changes in polar bear habitat from 2001 to 2010 and 2041 to 2050.

[^cite_note-149]

In 2015, the [IUCN Red List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IUCN_Red_List) categorized the polar bear as *[vulnerable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulnerable_species)* because of a "decline in area of occupancy, extent of occurrence and/or quality of habitat". It estimated the total population to be between 22,000 and 31,000, and the current population trend is unknown. Threats to polar bear populations include [climate change](5042951.md), pollution and energy development.[^cite_note-iucn_status_19_November_2021-2] In 2021, the IUCN/SSC Polar Bear Specialist Group labelled four subpopulations (Barents and Chukchi Sea, Foxe Basin and Gulf of Boothia) as "likely stable", two (Kane Basin and M'Clintock Channel) as "likely increased" and three (Southern Beaufort Sea, Southern and Western Hudson Bay) as "likely decreased" over specific periods between the 1980s and 2010s. The remaining ten did not have enough data.[^cite_note-statusreport-59]

A 2008 study predicted two-thirds of the world's polar bears may disappear by 2050, based on the [reduction of sea ice](40659364.md), and only one population would likely survive in 50 years.[^cite_note-150] A 2016 study projected a likely decline in polar bear numbers of more than 30 percent over three generations. The study concluded that declines of more than 50 percent are much less likely.[^cite_note-151] A 2012 review suggested that polar bears may become regionally extinct in southern areas by 2050 if trends continue, leaving the Canadian Archipelago and northern Greenland as strongholds.[^cite_note-2012review-152] A 2020 study concluded that a [worst-case scenario pathway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_Concentration_Pathway#RCP_8.5) would lead to the majority of subpopulations disappearing by 2100, while an [intermediate pathway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_Concentration_Pathway#RCP_4.5) would still see the extirpation of some subpopulations within the same time period.[^cite_note-153] A 2025 study cautions that the 2020 global demographic projections were based on the Hudson Bay populations and calls for more population specific analysis due to their different ecologies.[^cite_note-154] A 2014, study (cited in the 2025 study) found that the Chukchi Sea polar bears were more resilient to declining ice than the neighboring Beaufort Sea population.[^cite_note-155]

The key danger from climate change is malnutrition or starvation due to [habitat loss](1707053.md). Polar bears hunt seals on the sea ice, and rising temperatures cause the ice to melt earlier in the year, driving the bears to shore before they have built sufficient fat reserves to survive the period of scarce food in the late summer and early fall. Thinner sea ice tends to break more easily, which makes it more difficult for polar bears to access seals. Insufficient nourishment leads to lower reproductive rates in adult females and lower survival rates in cubs and juvenile bears. Lack of access to seals also causes bears to find food on land which increases the risk of conflict with humans.[^cite_note-derocher2004-64][^cite_note-2012review-152] A 2024 study concluded that greater consumption of terrestrial foods during the longer warm periods are unlikely to provide enough nourishment for Hudson Bay bears, increasing the risk of starvation during ice-free periods. Subadult bears would be particularly vulnerable.[^cite_note-156] Conversely, a 2026 study of polar bears at Svalbard found that they were able to fatten up and survive on less sea ice-dependent prey during prolonged ice-free periods.[^cite_note-157]



Polar bear swimming. The loss of sea ice has led to more open water and more pressure on the bears to swim great distances.

[^cite_note-derocher2004-64]

[^cite_note-2012review-152]

Reduction in sea ice cover also forces bears to swim longer distances, which further depletes their energy stores and occasionally leads to drowning. Increased ice mobility may result in less stable sites for dens or longer distances for mothers travelling to and from dens on land. Thawing of [permafrost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permafrost) would lead to more fire-prone roofs for bears denning underground. Less snow may affect insulation while more rain could cause more cave-ins.[^cite_note-derocher2004-64][^cite_note-2012review-152] The maximum corticosteroid-binding capacity of [corticosteroid-binding globulin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corticosteroid-binding_globulin) in polar bear serum correlates with stress in polar bears, and this has increased with climate warming.[^cite_note-158] Disease-causing [bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteria) and [parasites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasite) would flourish more readily in a warmer climate.[^cite_note-2012review-152]

Oil and gas development also affects polar bear habitat. The Chukchi Sea Planning Area of northwestern Alaska, which has had many drilling leases, was found to be an important site for non-denning female bears.[^cite_note-159] [Oil spills](158705.md) are also a risk. A 2018 study found that ten percent or less of prime bear habitat in the Chukchi Sea is vulnerable to a potential spill, but a spill at full reach could impact nearly 40 percent of the polar bear population.[^cite_note-160] Polar bears [accumulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioaccumulation) high levels of [persistent organic pollutants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistent_organic_pollutant) such as [polychlorinated biphenyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polychlorinated_biphenyl) (PCBs) and chlorinated [pesticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticides), because of their position at the top of the [ecological pyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_pyramid). Many of these chemicals have been internationally banned as a result of the recognition of their harm to the environment. Traces of them have slowly dwindled in polar bears but persist and have even increased in some populations.[^cite_note-161]

Polar bears receive some legal protection in all the countries they inhabit. The species has been labelled as *threatened* under the [US Endangered Species Act](532055.md) since 2008,[^cite_note-162] while the [Committee on the Status of Endangered Wildlife in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_on_the_Status_of_Endangered_Wildlife_in_Canada) listed it as of 'Special concern' since 1991.[^cite_note-163] In 1973, the [Agreement on the Conservation of Polar Bears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agreement_on_the_Conservation_of_Polar_Bears) was signed by all five nations with polar bear populations, Canada, Denmark (of which Greenland is an autonomous territory), Russia (then USSR), Norway and the US. This banned most harvesting of polar bears, allowed indigenous hunting using traditional methods, and promoted the preservation of bear habitat.[^cite_note-International1973-164] The [Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CITES) lists the species under Appendix II,[^cite_note-CITES-3] which allows regulated trade.[^cite_note-165]

## Relationship with humans

Polar bears have coexisted and interacted with [circumpolar peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumpolar_peoples) for millennia.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFee201925–26-166] "White bears" are mentioned as commercial items in the Japanese book *[Nihon Shoki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nihon_Shoki)* in the seventh century. It is not clear if these were polar bears or white-coloured brown bears.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard201730-167] During the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), Europeans considered white bears to be a novelty and were more familiar with brown- and black-coloured bears.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200913-168] The first known written account of the polar bear in its natural environment is found in the 13th-century anonymous Norwegian text *[Konungs skuggsjá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konungs_skuggsj%C3%A1)*, which mentions that "the white bear of Greenland wanders most of the time on the ice of the sea, hunting seals and whales and feeding on them" and says the bear is "as skillful a swimmer as any seal or whale".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard201753-169]



*[Nelson and the Bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nelson_and_the_Bear)*, by [Richard Westall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Westall) (1809)

Over the next centuries, several European explorers would mention polar bears and describe their habits.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard201753–66-170][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllis200914–23-171] Such accounts became more accurate after the [Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment), and both living and dead specimens were brought back. Nevertheless, some fanciful reports continued, including the idea that polar bears cover their noses during hunts. A relatively accurate drawing of a polar bear is found in [Henry Ellis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Ellis_\(governor\))'s work *A Voyage to Hudson's Bay* (1748).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard201749,_51–52-172] Polar bears were formally classified as a species by Constantine Phipps after his 1773 voyage to the Arctic. Accompanying him was a young [Horatio Nelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horatio_Nelson), who was said to have wanted to get a polar bear coat for his father but failed in his hunt.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFee201941-10] In his 1785 edition of *[Histoire Naturelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histoire_Naturelle)*, [Comte de Buffon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges-Louis_Leclerc_de_Buffon) mentions and depicts a "sea bear", clearly a polar bear, and "land bears", likely brown and black bears. This helped promote ideas about [speciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speciation). Buffon also mentioned a "white bear of the forest", possibly a [Kermode bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kermode_bear).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard201750-173]

### Exploitation

Further information: [Bear hunting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bear_hunting)



Hunter with polar bear slain with bow and arrow in Alaska (1924)

Polar bears were hunted as early as 8,000 years ago, as indicated by archaeological remains at [Zhokhov Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhokhov_Island) in the [East Siberian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Siberian_Sea). The oldest graphic depiction of a polar bear shows it being hunted by a man with three dogs. This [rock art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_art) was among several [petroglyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroglyph) found at [Pegtymel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pegtymel) in Siberia and dates from the fifth to eighth centuries. Before access to firearms, native people used lances, bows and arrows and hunted in groups accompanied by dogs. Though hunting typically took place on foot, some people killed swimming bears from boats with a harpoon. Polar bears were sometimes killed in their dens. Killing a polar bear was considered a [rite of passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rite_of_passage) for boys in some cultures. Native people respected the animal and hunts were subject to strict rituals.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard2017122–124,_130,_133-174] Bears were harvested for the fur, meat, fat, tendons, bones and teeth.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFee201928-175][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard2017128-176] The fur was worn and slept on, while the bones and teeth were made into tools. For the Netsilik, the individual who finally killed the bear had the right to its fur while the meat was passed to all in the party. Some people kept the cubs of slain bears.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard2017127–128,_132-177]



Skins of hunted bears in Greenland

Norsemen in Greenland traded polar bear furs in the Middle Ages.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard201734–35-178] Russia traded polar bear products as early as 1556, with [Novaya Zemlya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novaya_Zemlya) and [Franz Josef Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Josef_Land) being important commercial centres. Large-scale hunting of bears at Svalbard occurred since at least the 18th century, when no less than 150 bears were killed each year by Russian explorers. In the next century, more Norwegians were harvesting the bears on the island. From the 1870s to the 1970s, around 22,000 of the animals were hunted in total. Over 150,000 polar bears in total were either killed or captured in Russia and Svalbard, from the 18th to the 20th century. In the Canadian Arctic, bears were harvested by commercial whalers especially if they could not get enough whales. The [Hudson's Bay Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudson's_Bay_Company) is estimated to have sold 15,000 polar bear coats between the late 19th century and early 20th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStirling2011246–249-179] In the mid-20th century, countries began to regulate polar bear harvesting, culminating in the 1973 agreement.[^cite_note-International1973-164]

Polar bear meat was commonly eaten as rations by explorers and sailors in the Arctic, to widely varying appraisal. Some have called it too coarse and strong-smelling to eat, while others have praised it as a "royal dish".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard2017141-180] The liver was known for being too toxic to eat. This is due to the accumulation of [vitamin A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin_A) from the bears' prey.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDerocher201227-181] Polar bear fat was also used in lamps when other fuel was unavailable.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard2017141-180] Polar bear rugs were almost ubiquitous on the floors of Norwegian churches by the 13th and 14th centuries. In more modern times, classical Hollywood actors would pose on bearskin rugs, notably [Marilyn Monroe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marilyn_Monroe). Such images often had sexual connotations.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFee201932,_131–133-182]

### Conflicts

Further information: [Bear danger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bear_danger) and [Bear attack](20632326.md)



Road sign warning about the presence of polar bears. The Norwegian text translates into "Applies to all of Svalbard".

When the sea ice melts, polar bears, particularly subadults, conflict with humans over resources on land.[^cite_note-Heenskerk2020-183] They are attracted to the smell of human-made foods, particularly at garbage dumps and may be shot when they encroach on private property.[^cite_note-184] In [Churchill, Manitoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churchill,_Manitoba), local authorities maintain a "[polar bear jail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_bear_jail)" where nuisance bears are held until the sea ice freezes again.[^cite_note-185] Climate change has increased conflicts between the two species.[^cite_note-Heenskerk2020-183] Over 50 polar bears [swarmed a town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_mass_invasion_of_Russian_polar_bears) in Novaya Zemlya in February 2019, leading local authorities to declare a state of emergency.[^cite_note-186]

From 1870 to 2014, there were an estimated 73 polar bear attacks on humans, which led to 20 deaths. The majority of attacks were by hungry males, typically subadults, while female attacks were usually in defence of the young. In comparison to brown and American black bears, attacks by polar bears were more often near and around where humans lived. This may be due to the bears getting desperate for food and thus more likely to seek out human settlements. As with the other two bear species, polar bears are unlikely to target more than two people at once. Though popularly thought of as the most dangerous bear, the polar bear is no more aggressive to humans than other species.[^cite_note-Wilder2017-187]

### Captivity



Visitors observing polar bears underneath a plexiglass tunnel at the [Detroit Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit_Zoo)

The polar bear was for long a particularly sought-after species for exotic animal collectors, since it was relatively rare and remote living and had a reputation as a ferocious beast.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard201796-188] It is one of the few marine mammals that will reproduce well in captivity.[^cite_note-Perrin-189] They were originally kept only by royals and elites. The [Tower of London](31165.md) got [a polar bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_bear_of_Henry_III) as early as 1252 under [King Henry III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_III_of_England). In 1609, [James VI and I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_VI_and_I) of Scotland, England and Ireland was given two polar bear cubs by the sailor [Jonas Poole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonas_Poole), who got them during a trip to [Svalbard](27441.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFee201932,_103,_105-190] At the end of the 17th century, [Frederick I of Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_I_of_Prussia) housed polar bears in [menageries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menagerie) with other wild animals. He had their claws and canines removed to allow them to perform mock fights safely. Around 1726, [Catherine I of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_I_of_Russia) gifted two polar bears to [Augustus II the Strong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus_II_the_Strong) of Poland, who desired them for his animal collection.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard201795-191] Later, polar bears were displayed to the public in [zoos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoo) and [circuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circus).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFee2019103,_108-192] In early 19th century, the species was exhibited at the [Exeter Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_Exchange) in London, as well as menageries in Vienna and Paris. The first zoo in North America to exhibit a polar bear was the [Philadelphia Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Zoo) in 1859.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard2017xii,_96–97-193]

Polar bear exhibits were innovated by [Carl Hagenbeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Hagenbeck), who replaced cages and pits with settings that mimicked the animal's natural environment. In 1907, he revealed a complex [panoramic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panoramic) structure at the [Tierpark Hagenbeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tierpark_Hagenbeck) Zoo in Hamburg consisting of exhibits made of artificial snow and ice separated by moats. Different polar animals were displayed on each platform, giving the illusion of them living together. Starting in 1975, [Hellabrunn Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellabrunn_Zoo) in Munich housed its polar bears in an exhibit which consisted of a glass barrier, a house, concrete platforms mimicking ice floes and a large pool. Inside the house were maternity dens, and rooms for the staff to prepare and store the food. The exhibit was connected to an outdoor yard for extra room. Similar naturalistic and "immersive" exhibits were opened in the early 21st century, such as the "Arctic Ring of Life" at the [Detroit Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit_Zoo) and Ontario's [Cochrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochrane,_Ontario) Polar Bear Habitat.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard20177,_101,_105–106-194][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFee2019118-195] Many zoos in Europe and North America have stopped keeping polar bears because of the size and costs of their complex exhibits.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFee2019120–121-196] In North America, the population of polar bears in zoos reached its zenith in 1975 with 229 animals and declined in the 21st century.[^cite_note-197]



Performing polar bear at the 1973 *[Nationaal Songfestival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationaal_Songfestival)* in the Netherlands

Polar bears have been trained to perform in circuses. Bears in general, being large, powerful, easy to train and human-like in form, were widespread in circuses, and the white coat of polar bears made them particularly attractive. Circuses helped change the polar bear's image from a fearsome monster to something more comical. Performing polar bears were used in 1888 by [Circus Krone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circus_Krone) in Germany and later in 1904 by the [Bostock and Wombwell Menagerie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_C._Bostock) in England. Circus director Wilhelm Hagenbeck trained up to 75 polar bears to slide into a large tank through a chute. He began performing with them in 1908 and they had a particularly well-received show at the [Hippodrome in London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippodrome,_London). Other circus tricks performed by polar bears involved tightropes, balls, roller skates and motorcycles. One of the most famous polar bear trainers in the second half of the twentieth century was the East German Ursula Böttcher, whose small stature contrasted with that of the large bears. Starting in the late 20th century, most polar bear acts were retired, and the use of these bears for the circus is prohibited in the US.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard2017109–111,_116–119-198]

Several captive polar bears gained celebrity status in the late 20th and early 21st century, notably [Knut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knut_\(polar_bear\)) of the [Berlin Zoological Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Zoological_Garden), who was rejected by his mother and had to be hand-reared by zookeepers. Another bear, [Binky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binky_\(polar_bear\)) of the [Alaska Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Zoo) in Anchorage, became famous for attacking two visitors who got too close.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard201721–24,_105-199][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFee2019123–124,_145-200] Captive polar bears may pace back and forth, a [stereotypical behaviour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereotypy_\(non-human\)). In one study, they were recorded to have spent 14 percent of their days pacing.[^cite_note-201] [Gus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gus_\(bear\)) of the [Central Park Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Park_Zoo) was prescribed [Prozac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prozac) by a therapist for constantly swimming in his pool.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard201724-202] To reduce stereotypical behaviours, zookeepers provide the bears with enrichment items to trigger their play behaviour.[^cite_note-203] In sufficiently warm conditions, [algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae) concentrated in the medulla of their fur's guard hairs may cause zoo polar bears to appear green.[^cite_note-204]

### Cultural significance

Further information: [Cultural depictions of bears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_depictions_of_bears)



[Ivory carving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory_carving) of a swimming polar bear from the [Dorset culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorset_culture), northern Canada



The [coat of arms of Greenland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coat_of_arms_of_Greenland)

Polar bears have prominent roles in [Inuit culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit_culture) and [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit_religion). The deity [Torngarsuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torngarsuk) is sometimes imagined as a giant polar bear. He resides underneath the sea floor in an [underworld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underworld) of the dead and has power over sea creatures. [Kalaallit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalaallit) shamans would worship him through singing and dancing and were expected to be taken by him to the sea and consumed if he considered them worthy. Polar bears were also associated with the goddess [Nuliajuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuliajuk) who was responsible for their creation, along with other sea creatures. It is believed that shamans could reach the Moon or the bottom of the ocean by riding on a [guardian spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutelary_deity) in the form of a polar bear. Some folklore involves people turning into or disguising themselves as polar bears by donning their skins or the reverse, with polar bears removing their skins. In [Inuit astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit_astronomy), the [Pleiades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleiades) star cluster is conceived of as a polar bear trapped by dogs while [Orion's Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orion's_Belt), the [Hyades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyades_\(star_cluster\)) and [Aldebaran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldebaran) represent hunters, dogs and a wounded bear respectively.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard2017152–153,_156–162-205]

[Nordic folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordic_folklore) and [literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scandinavian_literature) have also featured polar bears. In *[The Tale of Auðun of the West Fjords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tale_of_Au%C3%B0un_of_the_West_Fjords)*, written around 1275, a poor man named Auðun spends all his money on a polar bear in Greenland, but ends up wealthy after giving the bear to the king of Denmark.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFee201932-206] In the 14th-century manuscript [Hauksbók](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hauksb%C3%B3k), a man named Odd kills and eats a polar bear that killed his father and brother. In the story of *The Grimsey Man and the Bear*, a mother bear nurses and rescues a farmer stuck on an ice floe and is repaid with sheep meat. 18th-century Icelandic writings mention the legend of a "polar bear king" known as the *bjarndýrakóngur*. This beast was depicted as a polar bear with "ruddy cheeks" and a [unicorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicorn)-like horn, which glows in the dark. The king could understand when humans talk and was considered to be very astute.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard2017165–166,_181–182-207] Two Norwegian fairy tales, "[East of the Sun and West of the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_of_the_Sun_and_West_of_the_Moon)" and "[White-Bear-King-Valemon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-Bear-King-Valemon)", involve white bears turning into men and seducing women.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFee201998-208]

Drawings of polar bears have been featured on maps of the northern regions. Possibly the earliest depictions of a polar bear on a map is the Swedish *[Carta marina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carta_marina)* of 1539, which has a white bear on Iceland or "Islandia". A 1544 map of North America includes two polar bears near [Quebec](7954867.md). Notable paintings featuring polar bears include [François-Auguste Biard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois-Auguste_Biard)'s *Fighting Polar Bears* (1839) and [Edwin Landseer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Landseer)'s *[Man Proposes, God Disposes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man_Proposes,_God_Disposes)* (1864). Polar bears have also been filmed for cinema. An Inuit polar bear hunt was shot for the 1932 documentary *[Igloo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igloo_\(1932_film\))*, while the 1974 film *[The White Dawn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_White_Dawn)* filmed a simulated stabbing of a trained bear for a scene. In the film *[The Big Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Big_Show_\(1961_film\))* (1961), two characters are killed by a circus polar bear. The scenes were shot using animal trainers instead of the actors. In modern literature, polar bears have been characters in both [children's fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children's_literature), like Hans Beer's *[Little Polar Bear and the Whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Little_Polar_Bear)* and Sakiasi Qaunaq's *The Orphan and the Polar Bear*, and fantasy novels, like [Philip Pullman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Pullman)'s *[His Dark Materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/His_Dark_Materials)* series. In radio, [Mel Blanc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mel_Blanc) provided the vocals for [Jack Benny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Benny)'s pet polar bear Carmichael on *[The Jack Benny Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jack_Benny_Program)*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard2017xi–xii,_36,_82–83,_100,_116,_184,_215-209] The polar bear is featured on flags and coats of arms, like the [coat of arms of Greenland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coat_of_arms_of_Greenland), and in many advertisements, notably [for Coca-Cola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coca-Cola_polar_bears) since 1922.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFee201932,_133–135-210]

As [charismatic megafauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charismatic_megafauna), polar bears have been used to [raise awareness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consciousness_raising) of the dangers of climate change. Aurora the polar bear is a giant [marionette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marionette) created by [Greenpeace](12233.md) for climate protests.[^cite_note-Born2019-211] The [World Wide Fund for Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Fund_for_Nature) has sold [plush polar bears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teddy_bear) as part of its "Arctic Home" campaign.[^cite_note-212] Photographs of polar bears have been featured in *[National Geographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Geographic)* and *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_\(magazine\))* magazines, including ones of them standing on ice floes, while the climate change documentary and advocacy film *[An Inconvenient Truth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Inconvenient_Truth)* (2006) includes an animated bear swimming.[^cite_note-Born2019-211] Automobile manufacturer [Nissan](21240.md) used a polar bear in one of its commercials, hugging a man for using an electric car.[^cite_note-213] To make a statement about global warming, in 2009 a Copenhagen ice statue of a polar bear with a bronze skeleton was purposely left to melt in the sun.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngelhard2017xiii-214]
