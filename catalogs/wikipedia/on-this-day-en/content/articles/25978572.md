The **University of Cambridge** is a [collegiate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collegiate_university) [public](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_university) [research university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_university) in [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge), England. Founded in 1209, the University of Cambridge is the second-oldest university in the [English-speaking world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English-speaking_world) and the [world's third-oldest university in continuous operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_oldest_universities_in_continuous_operation). The university's founding followed the arrival of scholars who left the [University of Oxford](31797.md) for Cambridge after a dispute with local townspeople.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13] The two [ancient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_university) English universities, although sometimes described as rivals, share many common features and are often jointly referred to as *[Oxbridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxbridge)*.

In 1231, 22 years after its founding, the university was recognised with a [royal charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_charter), granted by [King Henry III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_III_of_England). The University of Cambridge includes [31 semi-autonomous constituent colleges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colleges_of_the_University_of_Cambridge) and [more than 150 academic departments, faculties, and other institutions organised into six schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schools_of_the_University_of_Cambridge). The largest department is [Cambridge University Press and Assessment](68846510.md), which contains the oldest [university press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_press) in the world, with £1 billion of annual revenue and with 100 million learners.[^cite_note-14] All of the colleges are self-governing institutions within the university, managing their own personnel and policies, and all students are required to have a college affiliation within the university. Undergraduate teaching at Cambridge is centred on weekly small-group [supervisions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutorial_system) in the colleges with lectures, seminars, laboratory work, and occasionally further supervision provided by the central university faculties and departments.[^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16]

The university operates eight cultural and scientific museums, including the [Fitzwilliam Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitzwilliam_Museum) and [Cambridge University Botanic Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Botanic_Garden). [Cambridge's 116 libraries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libraries_of_the_University_of_Cambridge) hold a total of approximately 16 million books, around 9 million of which are in [Cambridge University Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Library), a [legal deposit library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_deposit) and one of the world's largest [academic libraries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_library).

Among the university's [notable alumni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_University_of_Cambridge_people) are reigning [King Charles III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Charles_III), [Francis Bacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Bacon), [Lord Byron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Byron), [Oliver Cromwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Cromwell), [Charles Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin), [Rosalind Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosalind_Franklin), [Rajiv Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajiv_Gandhi), [John Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Harvard), [Stephen Hawking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Hawking), [John Maynard Keynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes), [John Milton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Milton), [Vladimir Nabokov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Nabokov), [Jawaharlal Nehru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jawaharlal_Nehru), [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton), [Salman Rushdie](28722.md), [Bertrand Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell), [Alan Turing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Turing), [Ludwig Wittgenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Wittgenstein), [William Wordsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Wordsworth), 15 British prime ministers including the sitting prime minister [Andy Burnham](414818.md) and 194 [Olympic medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_medal)-winning athletes.[^cite_note-Hawks-17] Its alumni, academics, and affiliates have won 126 [Nobel Prizes](21201.md).[^cite_note-18]

## History

### Founding

See also: [Timeline of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_Cambridge)

Prior to the founding of the University of Cambridge in 1209, [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge) and the area surrounding it already had developed a scholarly and ecclesiastical reputation largely due to the intellectual and academic contributions of monks from the nearby [Ely Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ely_Cathedral). The founding of the university, however, mostly originated with an incident at the [University of Oxford](31797.md) during which two Oxford scholars, following the death of a local woman, were [hanged](155019.md) by the town's secular authorities. The local ecclesiastical authorities, who were not consulted, would have traditionally been inclined to pardon scholars in such cases. During this time, Oxford's town authorities were in conflict with [King John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John,_King_of_England). Fearing more violence from Oxford townsfolk, University of Oxford scholars began leaving Oxford for more hospitable cities, including Paris, [Reading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reading,_Berkshire), and [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge). Enough scholars ultimately took residence in Cambridge to form, along with the many scholars already there, the nucleus for the new university's foundation.[^cite_note-Early_records-19][^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21]

By 1225, a chancellor of the university was appointed, and writs issued by [King Henry III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_III_of_England) in 1231 established that rents in Cambridge were to be set *secundum consuetudinem universitatis*, according to the custom of the university, and established a panel of two masters and two townsmen to determine these. A letter from [Pope Gregory IX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Gregory_IX) two years later to the chancellor and the guild of scholars granted the new university *ius non trahi extra*, or the right not to be drawn out, for three years, meaning its members could not be summoned to a court outside of the diocese of Ely.[^cite_note-22] After Cambridge was described as a *[studium generale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Studium_generale)* in a letter from [Pope Nicholas IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Nicholas_IV) in 1290,[^cite_note-Hackett-23] and confirmed as such by [Pope John XXII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_John_XXII)'s 1318 [papal bull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papal_bull),[^cite_note-24] it became common for researchers from other European [medieval universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_university) to visit Cambridge to study or give lectures.[^cite_note-Hackett-23]

### Foundation of the colleges

See also: [Colleges of the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colleges_of_the_University_of_Cambridge) and [Oxford and Cambridge Act 1571](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_and_Cambridge_Act_1571)



[Peterhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peterhouse,_Cambridge), Cambridge's first college, founded in 1284



[Emmanuel College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmanuel_College,_Cambridge) Chapel

The [31 colleges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colleges_of_the_University_of_Cambridge) of the present-day University of Cambridge were originally an incidental feature of the university; no college within the University of Cambridge is as old as the university itself. The colleges within the university were initially endowed fellowships of scholars. There were also institutions without endowments, called hostels, which were gradually absorbed by the colleges over the centuries, and they have left some traces, including the naming of Garret Hostel Lane and [Garret Hostel Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garret_Hostel_Bridge), a street and bridge in Cambridge.[^cite_note-25]

The University of Cambridge's first college, [Peterhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peterhouse,_Cambridge), was founded in 1284 by [Hugh de Balsham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_de_Balsham), the [Bishop of Ely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Ely). Multiple additional colleges were founded during the 14th and 15th centuries, and colleges continued to be established during modern times, though there was a 204-year gap between the founding of [Sidney Sussex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Sussex_College,_Cambridge) in 1596 and that of [Downing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downing_College,_Cambridge) in 1800. The most recent college to be established is [Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robinson_College,_Cambridge), which was built in the late 1970s. Most recently, in March 2010, [Homerton College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homerton_College,_Cambridge) achieved full university college status, making it the university's newest full college.[^cite_note-26]

In [medieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) times, many colleges were founded so that their members could [pray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prayer) for the [souls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul) of the founders. University of Cambridge colleges were often associated with chapels or [abbeys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey). The colleges' focus began to shift in 1536, however, with the [dissolution of the monasteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries) and [Henry VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VIII)'s order that the university disband the [canon law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_law) that governed the university's faculty[^cite_note-27] and stop teaching [scholastic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism). In response, colleges changed their curricula from canon law to [classics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classics), the Bible, and mathematics.

Nearly a century later, the university found itself at the centre of a [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant) schism. Many nobles, intellectuals, and also commoners saw the [Church of England](5955.md) as too similar to the [Catholic Church](606848.md) and felt that it was being used by [The Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crown) to usurp the counties' rightful powers. [East Anglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Anglia) emerged as the centre of what ultimately became the [Puritan movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puritans). In Cambridge, the Puritan movement was particularly strong at [Emmanuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmanuel_College,_Cambridge), [St Catharine Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Catharine's_College,_Cambridge), [Sidney Sussex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Sussex_College), and [Christ's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ's_College,_Cambridge).[^cite_note-28] These colleges produced many [nonconformist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonconformist_\(Protestantism\)) graduates who greatly influenced, by social position or preaching, some 20,000 Puritans who ultimately left England for [New England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England) and especially the [Massachusetts Bay Colony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Bay_Colony) during the [Great Migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puritan_migration_to_New_England_\(1620%E2%80%931640\)) decade of the 1630s.

### Mathematics and mathematical physics

See also: [Lucasian Professor of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucasian_Professor_of_Mathematics) and [Mathematical Tripos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Tripos)

The university quickly established itself as a global leader in the study of mathematics. The university's examination in mathematics, known as the [Mathematical Tripos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Tripos),[^cite_note-29] was initially compulsory for all undergraduates studying for the Bachelor of Arts degree, the most common degree first offered at Cambridge. From the time of [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton) in the late 17th century until the mid-19th century, the university maintained an especially strong emphasis on [applied mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_mathematics), and especially [mathematical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_physics). Students awarded [first class honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_undergraduate_degree_classification) after completing the mathematics Tripos exam are called [wranglers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrangler_\(University_of_Cambridge\)), and the top student among them is known as the [Senior Wrangler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senior_Wrangler), a position that has been described as "the greatest intellectual achievement attainable in Britain."[^cite_note-Forfar_1996-30]

The Cambridge Mathematical Tripos is highly competitive and has helped produce some of the most famous names in British science, including [James Clerk Maxwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell), [Lord Kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thomson,_1st_Baron_Kelvin), and [Lord Rayleigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_William_Strutt,_3rd_Baron_Rayleigh).[^cite_note-31] However, some famous students, such as [G. H. Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._H._Hardy), disliked the Tripos system, feeling that students were becoming too focused on accumulating high exam marks at the expense of the subject itself.

Pure mathematics at the University of Cambridge in the 19th century achieved great things, though it largely missed out on substantial developments in French and German mathematics. By the early 20th century, however, pure mathematical research at Cambridge reached the highest international standard, thanks largely to G. H. Hardy and his collaborators, [J. E. Littlewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._E._Littlewood) and [Srinivasa Ramanujan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srinivasa_Ramanujan). [W. V. D. Hodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._V._D._Hodge) and others helped establish Cambridge as a global leader in [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry) in the 1930s.

### Modern period

See also: [Cambridge movement (philosophy)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_movement_\(philosophy\))



[Selwyn College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selwyn_College,_Cambridge), founded in 1882

The [Cambridge University Act 1856](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Act_1856) formalised the university's organisational structure and introduced the study of many new subjects, including theology, history, and [Modern languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_language).[^cite_note-32] Resources necessary for new courses in the arts, architecture, and [archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archeology) were donated by [Viscount Fitzwilliam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_FitzWilliam,_7th_Viscount_FitzWilliam) of [Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge), who also founded [Fitzwilliam Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitzwilliam_Museum) in 1816.[^cite_note-33] In 1847, [Prince Albert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert,_Prince_Consort) was elected the university's chancellor in a close contest with the [Earl of Powis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_of_Powis). As chancellor, Albert reformed university curricula beyond its initial focus on mathematics and classics, adding [modern era history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_era) and the [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science). Between 1896 and 1902, [Downing College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downing_College,_Cambridge) sold part of its land to permit the construction of [Downing Site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downing_Site), the university's grouping of scientific laboratories for the study of [anatomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatomy), [genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics), and [Earth sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Earth_Sciences,_University_of_Cambridge).[^cite_note-34] During this period, the [New Museums Site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Museums_Site) was erected, including the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory), which has since moved to [West Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Cambridge), and other [departments for chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yusuf_Hamied_Department_of_Chemistry) and medicine.[^cite_note-35] The University of Cambridge began to award PhD degrees in the first third of the 20th century; the first Cambridge PhD in mathematics was awarded in 1924.[^cite_note-36]

The university contributed significantly to the [Allies' forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_I) in [World War I](4764461.md) with 13,878 members of the university serving and 2,470 being [killed in action](368513.md) during the war. Teaching, and the fees it earned, nearly came to a halt during World War I, and severe financial difficulties followed. As a result, the university received its first systematic state support in 1919, and a [Royal commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_commission) was appointed in 1920 to recommend that the university (but not its colleges) begin receiving an annual grant.[^cite_note-37] Following [World War II](32927.md), the university experienced a rapid expansion in applications and enrolment, partly due to the success and popularity gained by many Cambridge scientists.[^cite_note-38] This was not without controversies, however. For example, Cambridge researchers were accused in 2023 of helping to develop weapon systems for Iran.[^cite_note-39]

#### Parliamentary representation

Main article: [Cambridge University (UK Parliament constituency)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\))

See also: [Category\:Members of the Parliament of the United Kingdom for the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Members_of_the_Parliament_of_the_United_Kingdom_for_the_University_of_Cambridge)

The University of Cambridge was one of only two universities to hold parliamentary seats in the [Parliament of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_England) and was later one of 19 represented in the [Parliament of the United Kingdom](13964.md). The constituency was created by a [Royal charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_charter) of 1603 and returned two members of parliament until 1950 when it was abolished by the [Representation of the People Act 1948](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representation_of_the_People_Act_1948). The constituency was not a geographical area; rather, its electorate consisted of university graduates. Before 1918, the franchise was restricted to male graduates with a [doctorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) or [MA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts_\(Oxbridge_and_Dublin\)) degree.

### Women's education



[Newnham College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newnham_College,_Cambridge), one of two colleges at the university which only admit female students

For the first several centuries of its existence, as was the case broadly in England and the world, the University of Cambridge was only open to male students. The first colleges established for women were [Girton College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girton_College,_Cambridge), founded by [Emily Davies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emily_Davies) in 1869, [Newnham College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newnham_College,_Cambridge), founded by [Anne Clough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Clough) and [Henry Sidgwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Sidgwick) in 1872, [Hughes Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hughes_Hall,_Cambridge), founded in 1885 by [Elizabeth Phillips Hughes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Phillips_Hughes) as the Cambridge Teaching College for Women, [Murray Edwards College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murray_Edwards_College), founded in 1954 by [Rosemary Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosemary_Murray) as [New Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murray_Edwards_College,_Cambridge), and [Lucy Cavendish College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucy_Cavendish_College,_Cambridge), founded in 1965. Prior to ultimately being permitted admission to the university in 1948, female students were granted the right to take University of Cambridge exams beginning in the late 19th century.[^cite_note-40] Women were also allowed to study courses, take examinations, and have prior exam results recorded retroactively, dating back to 1881; for a brief period after the turn of the 20th century, this allowed the [steamboat ladies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steamboat_ladies) to receive *[ad eundem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ad_eundem_gradum)* degrees from the [University of Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Dublin).[^cite_note-41] In 1998, a special graduation ceremony was held in which the women who attended Cambridge before admission was allowed in 1948 were finally conferred their degrees.[^cite_note-42]

Beginning in 1921, women were awarded diplomas that conferred the title associated with the Bachelor of Arts degree. But since women were not yet admitted to the Bachelor of Arts degree program, they were excluded from the university's governance structure. Since University of Cambridge students must belong to a college, and since established colleges remained closed to women, women found admissions restricted to the few university colleges that had been established only for them. [Darwin College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darwin_College,_Cambridge), the first graduate college of the university, matriculated both male and female students from its inception in 1964 and elected a mixed fellowship. Undergraduate colleges, starting with [Churchill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churchill_College,_Cambridge), [Clare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clare_College,_Cambridge), and [King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College,_Cambridge)'s colleges, began admitting women between 1972 and 1988. Among women's colleges at the university, [Girton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girton_College,_Cambridge) began admitting male students in 1979, and [Lucy Cavendish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucy_Cavendish_College,_Cambridge) began admitting men in 2021. But the other female-only colleges have remained female-only colleges as of 2023. As a result of [St Hilda's College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Hilda's_College,_Oxford), ending its ban on male students in 2008, Cambridge is now the only remaining university in the [United Kingdom](31717.md) with female-only colleges; [Newnham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newnham_College,_Cambridge) and [Murray Edwards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murray_Edwards_College,_Cambridge) only admit female students.[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44] As of the 2019–2020 academic year, the university's male to female enrolment, including post-graduates, was nearly balanced with its total student population being 53% male and 47% female.[^cite_note-45]

In 2018 and later years, the university has come under some criticism and faced legal challenges over alleged sexual harassment at the university.[^cite_note-46][^cite_note-47] In 2019, for example, former student Danielle Bradford, represented by sexual harassment lawyer [Ann Olivarius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ann_Olivarius), sued the university for its handling of her sexual misconduct complaint. "I was told that I should think about it very carefully because making a complaint could affect my place in my department", Bradford alleged in 2019.[^cite_note-48] In 2020, hundreds of current and former students accused the university in a letter, citing "a complete failure" to deal with sexual misconduct complaints.[^cite_note-49]

### Town and gown

Main article: [Town and gown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Town_and_gown)

Further information: [Away with the learning of clerks, away with it!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Away_with_the_learning_of_clerks,_away_with_it!) and [Silicon Fen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_Fen)

The relationship between the university and the city of [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge) has sometimes been uneasy. The phrase [town and gown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Town_and_gown) continues to be employed to distinguish between Cambridge residents (town) and University of Cambridge students (gown), who historically wore [academical dress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_dress). Ferocious rivalry between Cambridge's residents and university students has periodically erupted over the centuries. During the [Peasants' Revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peasants'_Revolt) of 1381, strong clashes led to attacks and [looting](209811.md) of university properties as locals contested the privileges granted by the British government to the University of Cambridge's academic staff. Residents burned university property in [Market Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_Hill,_Cambridge) to the famed rallying cry "[Away with the learning of clerks, away with it!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Away_with_the_learning_of_clerks,_away_with_it!)".[^cite_note-50] Following these events, the [University of Cambridge's Chancellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_chancellors_of_the_University_of_Cambridge) was given special powers allowing him to prosecute criminals and reestablish order in the city. Attempts at reconciliation between the city's residents and students followed; in the 16th century, agreements were signed to improve the quality of streets and student accommodation around the city. However, this was followed by new confrontations when the [plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_\(disease\)) reached Cambridge in 1630 and colleges refused to assist those affected by the disease by locking their sites.[^cite_note-51]

Such conflicts between Cambridge's residents and university students have largely disappeared since the 16th century, and the university has grown as a source of enormous employment and expanded wealth in [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge) and the region.[^cite_note-52] The university also has proven a source of extraordinary growth in [high tech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_tech) and [biotech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biotech) start-ups and established companies and associated providers of services to these companies. The economic growth associated with the university's high tech and biotech growth has been labeled the Cambridge Phenomenon, and has included the addition of 1,500 new companies and as many as 40,000 new jobs added between 1960 and 2010, mostly at [Silicon Fen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_Fen), a business cluster launched by the university in the late 20th century.[^cite_note-53]

### Myths, legends and traditions

Main article: [University of Cambridge legends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge_legends)

Further information: [Nine Lessons and Carols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nine_Lessons_and_Carols) and [wooden spoon (award)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wooden_spoon_\(award\))



[Mathematical Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Bridge) crossing [River Cam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Cam) at [Queens' College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queens'_College,_Cambridge)

Partly because of the University of Cambridge's extensive history, which now exceeds 800 years, the university has developed a large number of traditions, myths, and legends. Some are true, some are not, and some were true but have been discontinued, or have still been propagated nonetheless by generations of students and tour guides.

One such discontinued tradition is that of the [wooden spoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wooden_spoon_\(award\)), the prize awarded to the student with the lowest passing honours grade in the final examinations of the university's [Mathematical Tripos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Tripos). The last of these spoons was awarded in 1909 to Cuthbert Lempriere Holthouse, an oarsman of the Lady Margaret Boat Club at [St John's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Cambridge). It was more than one metre in length and had an oar blade for a handle. It can now be seen outside the Senior Combination Room of St John's College. Since 1908, examination results have been published alphabetically within class rather than in strict order of merit, which made it difficult to ascertain the student with the lowest passing grade deserving of the spoon, leading to discontinuation of the tradition.

Each Christmas Eve, [The Festival of Nine Lessons and Carols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nine_Lessons_and_Carols), sung by the [Choir of King's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choir_of_King's_College,_Cambridge), are broadcast globally on [BBC World Service](37627.md) television and radio and syndicated to hundreds of additional radio stations in the U.S. and elsewhere. The radio broadcast has been a national Christmas Eve tradition since 1928, though the festival has existed since 1918 and the celebration itself originated even earlier at [Truro Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truro_Cathedral) in Cornwall in 1880.[^cite_note-guardian-truro-54] The first television broadcast of the festival was in 1954.[^cite_note-55][^cite_note-56]

## Organisation and administration

See also: [Colleges of the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colleges_of_the_University_of_Cambridge) and [Schools of the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schools_of_the_University_of_Cambridge)



View over [Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge), [Gonville and Caius College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonville_and_Caius_College,_Cambridge), [Trinity Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_Hall,_Cambridge), and [Clare College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clare_College,_Cambridge) towards [King's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College,_Cambridge) Chapel seen from [St John's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Cambridge) Chapel on the left. In front of King's College Chapel is the [Senate House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_House_\(University_of_Cambridge\)).

Cambridge is a [collegiate university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collegiate_university), which means that its colleges are self-governing and independent, each with its own property, endowments, and income. Most colleges bring together academics and students from a broad range of disciplines. Each faculty, school, or department at the university includes academics affiliated with differing colleges. The university is legally structured as an [exempt charity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exempt_charity) and a common law corporation. Its corporate titles include the [Chancellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_chancellors_of_the_University_of_Cambridge), Masters, and Scholars of the University of Cambridge.[^cite_note-fullname-4]

The college faculties are responsible for giving lectures, arranging seminars, performing research, and determining the [syllabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllabus) for teaching, all of which is overseen by the university's general board. Together with the central administration headed by the [Vice-Chancellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_vice-chancellors_of_the_University_of_Cambridge), the college faculties make up the University of Cambridge. Facilities such as libraries are provided on all these levels by the university. The [Cambridge University Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Library) is the university's largest and primary library. [Squire Law Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faculty_of_Law,_University_of_Cambridge) is the primary library for the university's [students of law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faculty_of_Law,_University_of_Cambridge). Individual colleges each maintain a multi-discipline library designed for each college's respective undergraduates. College libraries tend to operate 24/7 and their usage in generally restricted to members of the college. Conversely, libraries operated by departments are generally open to all students of the university, regardless of subject.

### Colleges

The colleges are self-governing institutions with their own endowments and property, each founded as components of the university. All students and most academics are attached to a college. The colleges' importance lies in the housing, welfare, social functions, and undergraduate teaching they provide. All faculties, departments, research centres, and laboratories belong to the university, which arranges lectures and awards degrees, but undergraduates receive their overall academic supervision within the colleges through small group teaching sessions, which often include just one student; though in many cases students go to other colleges for supervision if the teaching fellows at their college do not specialise in a student's particular area of academic focus. Each college appoints its own teaching staff and fellows, both of whom are members of a university department. The colleges also decide which undergraduates to admit to the university, in accordance with university standards and regulations. Costs to students for room and board vary considerably from college to college.[^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70] Similarly, the investment in student education by each college at the university varies widely between the colleges.[^cite_note-TCS_college_spending-71]

Cambridge has 31 colleges, two of which, [Murray Edwards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murray_Edwards_College,_Cambridge) and [Newnham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newnham_College,_Cambridge), only admit women. The other colleges are [mixed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed-sex_education). [Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darwin_College,_Cambridge) was the first college to admit both men and women. In 1972, [Churchill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churchill_College,_Cambridge), [Clare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clare_College,_Cambridge), and [King's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College,_Cambridge) were the first previously all-male colleges to admit female undergraduates. In 1988, [Magdalene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magdalene_College,_Cambridge) became the last all-male college to accept women.[^cite_note-72] [Clare Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clare_Hall,_Cambridge) and Darwin only admit postgraduates, and [Hughes Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hughes_Hall,_Cambridge), [St Edmund's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Edmund's_College,_Cambridge), and [Wolfson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfson_College,_Cambridge) only admit [mature undergraduate and graduate students](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adult_learner) who are 21 years old or older on the date of their [matriculation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matriculation). [Lucy Cavendish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucy_Cavendish_College,_Cambridge), which was previously a women-only mature college, began admitting both men and women in 2021.[^cite_note-73] All other colleges admit both undergraduate and postgraduate students without any age restrictions.

Colleges are not required to admit students in all subjects; some colleges choose not to offer subjects such as architecture, art history, or theology, but most offer a complete range of academic specialties and related courses. Some colleges maintain a relative strength and associated reputation for expertise in certain academic disciplines. Churchill, for example, has a reputation for its expertise and focus on sciences and engineering, in part due to the requirement imposed by [Winston Churchill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winston_Churchill) upon the college's founding that 70% of its students studied mathematics, engineering, and the sciences.[^cite_note-74] Other colleges have more informal academic focus and even demonstrate ideological focus, such as [King's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College,_Cambridge), which is known for its left-wing political orientation,[^cite_note-75] and Churchill and [Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robinson_College,_Cambridge), both of which have a reputation for academic focus on [sustainability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainability) and environmentalism.[^cite_note-76] Three theological colleges at the university, [Westcott House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westcott_House,_Cambridge), [Westminster College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_College_\(Cambridge\)), and [Ridley Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ridley_Hall,_Cambridge), are members of the [Cambridge Theological Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Theological_Federation) and associated in partnership with the university.[^cite_note-77]

### Schools, faculties and departments

Main article: [Schools of the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schools_of_the_University_of_Cambridge)



[Institute of Continuing Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge_Institute_of_Continuing_Education) at Madingley Hall



[Old Schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Schools) (left) houses the university's administrative offices.

In addition to the 31 colleges, the university maintains more than 150 departments, faculties, schools, syndicates, and other academic institutions.[^cite_note-79] Members of these are usually members of one of the colleges, and responsibility for the entire academic programme of the university is divided among them.

The university has a department dedicated to providing [continuing education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuing_education), the [Institute of Continuing Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge_Institute_of_Continuing_Education), which is based primarily in [Madingley Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madingley_Hall), a 16th-century manor house in [Cambridgeshire](6288.md). Its award-bearing programmes include both undergraduate certificates and part-time master's degrees.[^cite_note-80]

A school in the University of Cambridge is a broad administrative grouping of related faculties and other units. Each has an elected supervisory body known as a Council, composed of representatives of the various constituent bodies. The University of Cambridge maintains six such schools:[^cite_note-81]

- Arts and Humanities

- Biological Sciences

- Clinical Medicine

- Humanities and Social Sciences

- Physical Sciences

- Technology

Teaching and research at the university is organised by faculties. The faculties have varying organisational substructures that partly reflect their respective histories and the university's operational needs, which may include a number of departments and other institutions. A small number of bodies called syndicates hold responsibility for teaching and research, including for the [University of Cambridge Local Examinations Syndicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge_Local_Examinations_Syndicate), the [University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Press), and the [University Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Library).

### Central administration

#### Chancellor and vice-chancellor

See also: [List of chancellors of the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_chancellors_of_the_University_of_Cambridge) and [List of vice-chancellors of the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_vice-chancellors_of_the_University_of_Cambridge)



[Regent House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regent_House) officers following a July 2014 graduation ceremony



[Faculty of Divinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faculty_of_Divinity,_University_of_Cambridge) at the university

The [chancellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_chancellors_of_the_University_of_Cambridge) of the university is a ten-year position that is mainly ceremonial and is held currently by [Chris Smith, Baron Smith of Finsbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Smith,_Baron_Smith_of_Finsbury), who succeeded [David Sainsbury, Baron Sainsbury of Turville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Sainsbury,_Baron_Sainsbury_of_Turville) in 2025.[^cite_note-82]

The current [vice-chancellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_vice-chancellors_of_the_University_of_Cambridge) is [Deborah Prentice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deborah_Prentice), who began her role in July 2023.[^cite_note-83] While the chancellor's office is ceremonial, the vice-chancellor serves as the university's *de facto* principal administrative officer. The university's internal governance is carried out almost entirely by [Regent House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regent_House), augmented by some external representation from the audit committee and four external members of the [university's council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Council).[^cite_note-84]

#### Senate and the Regent House



Light show on [Senate House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_House,_Cambridge) for the 800th anniversary of the university's founding



Old Court at [Clare College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clare_College,_Cambridge)

The university senate consists of all holders of the [MA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts_\(Oxbridge_and_Dublin\)) or higher degrees and is responsible for electing the chancellor and the high steward. Until 1950, when the [Cambridge University constituency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)) was abolished, it was also responsible for electing two members of the [House of Commons](13828.md). Prior to 1926, the university senate was the university's governing body, fulfilling the functions that [Regent House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regent_House) has provided since.[^cite_note-85] Regent House is the university's governing body, comprising all resident senior members of the university and the colleges, the chancellor, the [high steward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Steward_\(academia\)), the deputy high steward, and the commissary.[^cite_note-86] Public representatives of Regent House are the two [proctors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proctor), elected to serve for one year terms upon their nominations by the colleges.

#### Council and general board



[Senate House Passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_Lane) in the snow with [Senate House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_House,_Cambridge) on the right and [Gonville and Caius College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonville_and_Caius_College,_Cambridge) on the left

Although [Cambridge University Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Council) is the university's principal executive and policy-making body, the council reports to, and is held accountable by, [Regent House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regent_House) through a variety of checks and balances. The council is obliged to advise Regent House on matters of general concern to the university, which it does by publishing notices to the *[Cambridge University Reporter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Reporter)*, the university's official journal.[^cite_note-87] In March 2008, Regent House voted to increase from two to four the number of external members on the council,[^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89] and this was approved by Her Majesty the Queen in July 2008.[^cite_note-90]

The general board of the faculties is responsible for the university's academic and educational policies[^cite_note-91] and is accountable to the council for its management of these affairs. Faculty boards are accountable to the general board; other boards and syndicates are accountable either to the general board or to the council. Under this organisational structure, the university's various arms are kept under supervision of both the central administration and Regent House.[^cite_note-92]

### Finances

#### Endowment

The Cambridge University Endowment Fund is the main vehicle of investment for the university.[^cite_note-93] In the fiscal year ending 31 July 2025, the university group, excluding colleges, reported a total endowment of £2.76 billion.[^cite_note-annualfinstat25-5]

#### Benefactions and fundraising

In the fiscal year ending 31 July 2025, the central university, excluding colleges, reported total group income of £2.66 billion, of which £605 million was from research grants and contracts.[^cite_note-annualfinstat25-5] In July 2022, the *Dear World, Yours Cambridge* Campaign for the university and colleges concluded, raising a total of £2.217 billion in commitments.[^cite_note-finstat23-94]

The university maintains multiple scholarship programs. The Stormzy Scholarship for Black UK Students covers tuition costs for two students and maintenance grants for up to four years.[^cite_note-95] In 2000, [Bill Gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Gates) of [Microsoft](19001.md) donated US\$210 million through the [Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_and_Melinda_Gates_Foundation) to endow [Gates Cambridge Scholarships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gates_Cambridge_Scholarship) for students from outside the United Kingdom to pursue full-time postgraduate study at Cambridge.[^cite_note-96]

In October 2021, the university suspended its £400m collaboration with the [United Arab Emirates](69328.md), citing allegations that UAE was involved in illegal hacking of the university's computer and storage systems using [NSO Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NSO_Group)'s [Pegasus](51424588.md) software. UAE also was behind the leak of more than 50,000 phone numbers, including hundreds belonging to British citizens. [Stephen Toope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Toope), the university's outgoing Vice-Chancellor, said the decision to suspend its collaboration with UAE also was a result of additional revelations about UAE's Pegasus software hacking.[^cite_note-97]

#### Bonds

The University of Cambridge borrowed £350 million in October 2012 by issuing 40-year [bonds](60737.md),[^cite_note-CN2012oct11ib-98] whose interest rate is approximately 0.6 percent higher than the [British government](25318118.md)'s 40-year bond.[^cite_note-99]

### Affiliations and memberships

The University of Cambridge is a member of the [Russell Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Group) of research-led [British universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universities_in_the_United_Kingdom), the [G5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G5_\(education\)), the [League of European Research Universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_European_Research_Universities), the [International Alliance of Research Universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Alliance_of_Research_Universities), and it is part of the so-called [golden triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_triangle_\(universities\)) of [research intensive universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_university) in the [south of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_England).[^cite_note-100] It is also closely linked to the [high tech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_tech) business cluster known as [Silicon Fen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_Fen) and is part of [Cambridge University Health Partners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Health_Partners), Europe's largest [academic health science centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_health_science_centre).

#### Process

St John's New Court

[St John's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Cambridge) New Court



[Great Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_Great_Court) of [Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge), dating back to the 16th Century

Admission to the University of Cambridge is extremely competitive. In 2022, for instance, around 15% of applicants were admitted.[^cite_note-115][^cite_note-116] In 2021, Cambridge introduced an over-subscription clause to its offers of admission, which also permits the university to withdraw acceptances if too many students meet its selective entrance criteria. The clause can be invoked in the event of circumstances outside the reasonable control of the university. The clause was introduced following a record number of [A-level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GCE_Advanced_Level) pupils who obtained the highest grades from teacher assessment, which was introduced due to the cancellation of A-level examinations during the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md).[^cite_note-117][^cite_note-118]

The university's standard offer for most courses is set at A\*AA,[^cite_note-119][^cite_note-120] with A\*A\*A for science courses, or equivalent in other examination systems, e.g. 7,6,6 or 7,7,6 in IB. The university is consistently designated as a 'high-tariff' institution by the [Department for Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_for_Education), with the average undergraduate entrant to the university in recent years amassing between 165 and 166 [UCAS Tariff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCAS_Tariff) points (out of a maximum of 168) in their top three pre-university qualifications – the equivalent of A\*A\*A\* at [A-Level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A-level_\(United_Kingdom\)).[^cite_note-Mean_Tariff-108] Based on 2022/23 [HESA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_Education_Statistics_Agency) entry standards data published in domestic league tables, which include a broad range of qualifications beyond the top three exam grades, the average student at the University of Cambridge achieved 206 points.[^cite_note-CUG_Entry-106]

Due to a high proportion of applicants receiving the highest school grades, an interview process was introduced as a component of consideration for admission. Interviews are performed by College Fellows, who evaluate candidates on unexamined factors including potential for [original thinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_thinking) and creativity.[^cite_note-intguide-121] Prior to 2020 these interviews were normally held in person but moved online during the COVID-19 pandemic and have, at most colleges, remained online since.[^cite_note-122] For exceptional candidates, a matriculation offer is sometimes offered, requiring only two A-levels at grade E or above. [Sutton Trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutton_Trust) maintains that the [University of Oxford](31797.md) and the University of Cambridge recruit disproportionately from eight schools, which account for 1,310 [Oxbridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxbridge) places over three years contrasted with 1,220 from 2,900 other schools.[^cite_note-123]

Strong applicants who are not successful in being admitted to their chosen college at the university may be placed in the [Winter Pool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_Pool), where they can be considered for admission to other university colleges, which maintains consistency throughout the colleges, some of which receive more applicants than others.

Undergraduate applications are processed through [UCAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCAS), and the deadline for their submission currently is mid-October in the year before prior to beginning. Until the 1980s, candidates for all subjects were required to take special entrance examinations,[^cite_note-124] which have since been replaced by additional tests for some subjects, such as the Thinking Skills Assessment and Cambridge Law Test.[^cite_note-125] The university has at times considered reintroducing an admissions exam for all subjects.[^cite_note-126]

Graduate admission is first decided by the faculty or department responsible for the applicant's respective academic subject. An offer of acceptance effectively guarantees admission to the university, though not necessarily the applicant's preferred choice of college.[^cite_note-127]

#### Winter pool

The **Winter Pool** or **inter-College Pool** is part of the undergraduate application process intended to ensure that the best applicants are offered admission.[^cite_note-128] Approximately 20–25% of undergraduate admissions are awarded through the Pool. Each college can place applicants in the Winter Pool. These applicants' applications are then considered by Admissions Tutors or Directors of Studies during the pool, which takes place over three days in January prior to the release of the university's admissions decisions.[^cite_note-\:0a-129]

For each subject, colleges create an ordered list of the pooled applicants they seek to admit, and take turns choosing applicants. Colleges with specific student requirements, such as mature colleges and women-only colleges, are given priority over applicants eligible for their colleges. Some applicants are selected from the pool by the college that originally pooled them.[^cite_note-\:0a-129] Once all the colleges have selected as many applicants as they need, the pool ends. Some applicants are then interviewed a second time by the colleges before final admissions decisions are made.

Colleges can pool any candidate, either because the college has no space but believes the applicant is strong enough to get a place, or because the college wants to compare that applicant to other pooled applicants. Most applicants in the pool are pooled at their original college's discretion, but some candidates meet the compulsory pooling criteria.[^cite_note-130]

There were, as of the 2020–21 admissions cycle, only two grounds for compulsory pooling. For post-qualified applicants, their achieved grades at A level or equivalent and, for applicants with overseas interviews, an interview score of at least eight is achieved in all interviews. The second criterion does not apply to medicine applicants.[^cite_note-WhatDoTheyKno.comw-2020-131] Previously, [AS-Level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GCE_Advanced_Subsidiary_Level) UMS have been used as pooling criteria, but after [A-levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GCE_Advanced_Level) became linear this was discontinued.[^cite_note-WhatDoTheyKno.comw-2020-131]

As of 2012, there is only one specifically identified category for pooled applicants, which is known as **S**, meaning that the application is in special need of reassessment. This is used when candidates whose initial interview scores are of questionable accuracy, such as when a candidate received very different scores from different interviewers, experienced technical issues with interviews conducted over the internet, or was impacted by significant extenuating circumstances such as illness or the loss of a family member.[^cite_note-WhatDoTheyKno.comw-2020-131]

Pooled applicants who are fished by a college may be offered a place immediately or may be invited for an interview. In 2020, just 89 applicants were invited for second interviews, 34 of whom received offers of admission.[^cite_note-\:0a-129]

Each year, approximately 3,500 applicants receive offers from their preference college and a further 1,000 to 1,100 applicants are offered admission by another college through the pool. On average, one in five applicants is pooled and around one in four pooled applicants receives an offer of admission.[^cite_note-\:0a-129]

Statistics released by the university show that some colleges regularly receive particularly high numbers of applicants, and these colleges tend to take fewer applicants from the pool. Other colleges regularly draw a greater proportion of their undergraduate intake from the pool.[^cite_note-132]

#### Access



Percent of state school students at Cambridge and [Oxford](31797.md)

[^cite_note-133]

[^cite_note-134]

Public debate in the United Kingdom exists over whether admissions processes used at the [University of Oxford](31797.md) and Cambridge are entirely merit-based and fair, whether enough students from [state schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_school#United_Kingdom) are encouraged to apply, and whether these students are offered sufficient admission. In 2020–21, 71% of all successful applicants were from [state schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_school#United_Kingdom)[^cite_note-135] compared to approximately 93% of all students in the UK who attended state schools and 82% of post-16 students.[^cite_note-136]

Critics have argued that the relative lower percent of state school applicants with the required grades for admission to Cambridge and Oxford has had a negative impact on Oxford and Cambridge's collective reputation, though both universities have encouraged pupils from state schools to apply to help redress the perceived imbalance.[^cite_note-137] Others counter that government pressure to increase state school admissions constitutes inappropriate [social engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_engineering_\(political_science\)).[^cite_note-138][^cite_note-139] The proportion of undergraduates drawn from independent schools has dropped over the years, constituting, as of 2020, 26% of total admissions among the university's 3,436 applicants from independent schools compared to 23% of the 9,237 applications from state schools.[^cite_note-140] Cambridge, together with Oxford and [Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_University), was among those universities that adopted formulae in 2009 to rate the [GCSE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Certificate_of_Secondary_Education) performance of schools, using data from the [Department for Children, Schools and Families](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_for_Children,_Schools_and_Families), and took this into account when assessing university applicants.[^cite_note-141]

With the release of admissions figures, *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* reported in 2013 that ethnic minority candidates had lower success rates in individual subjects even when they had the same grades as white applicants. The university was criticised for what was seen as institutional discrimination against ethnic minority applicants in favour of white applicants. The university denied the claims of institutional discrimination, stating the figures did not take into account other variables.[^cite_note-Guardian20130226-142] A subsequent article reported that, in the years 2010 to 2012, ethnic minority applicants to medicine with 3 A\* grades or higher were 20% less likely to gain admission than white applicants with similar grades. The university refused to provide figures for a wider range of subjects, claiming that assembling and releasing such information was excessively costly.[^cite_note-143]

Given the competitive nature of gaining admission to the University of Cambridge, a number of educational consultancies have emerged to offer support with the application process. Some claim they can improve chances for admission, though these claims have never been independently verified. None of these companies are affiliated with or endorsed by the University of Cambridge. The university informs applicants that all necessary information regarding the application process is publicly available through the university and none of these services is providing any insight not already publicly available to applicants.[^cite_note-144]

The University of Cambridge has been criticised for admitting a lower percentage of black students, though many apply. Of the 31 colleges at Cambridge, six of them admitted fewer than 10 Black or mixed race students between 2012 and 2016.[^cite_note-145] Similar criticism exists over a relatively lower admission rate for white working class applicants; in 2019, only 2% of admitted students were white working class.[^cite_note-146]

In January 2021, Cambridge created foundation courses for disadvantaged students.[^cite_note-Media-2021-147] While the usual entry requirements are A\*AA in [A-Levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GCE_Advanced_Level), the one-year foundation course has 50 places for students who achieve BBB.[^cite_note-148] If successful on the course, students receive a recognised [CertHE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Certificate_of_Higher_Education) qualification and can progress to degrees in the arts, humanities, and social sciences at the university.[^cite_note-Media-2021-147] Candidates include those who have been in care, who are estranged from their families, who have missed significant periods of learning because of health issues, those from low-income backgrounds, and those from schools with few students attending universities.[^cite_note-Media-2021-147]

### Teaching

See also: [Cambridge School (intellectual history)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_School_\(intellectual_history\)) and [List of professorships at the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_professorships_at_the_University_of_Cambridge)



Results for the [Cambridge Mathematical Tripos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Tripos) are read aloud at [Senate House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_House,_Cambridge) and then tossed from the balcony in accordance with a tradition that began in the 18th century.

The University of Cambridge academic year is divided into three academic terms determined by the statutes of the university.[^cite_note-149] [Michaelmas term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michaelmas_term) lasts from October to December; the [Lent term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lent_term) last from January to March; and the [Easter term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_term) last from April to June.

Within these terms, undergraduate teaching takes place during eight-week periods called full terms. According to university statutes, it is a requirement during these periods that all students live within three miles of the [Church of St Mary the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_St_Mary_the_Great,_Cambridge), which is known as keeping term. Students eligible for graduation must fulfil this condition for nine terms (three years) while pursuing a Bachelor of Arts or twelve terms (four years) when pursuing a Master of Science, engineering, or mathematics degree.[^cite_note-150]

These terms are shorter than those of many other British universities.[^cite_note-151] Undergraduates are also expected to prepare heavily in the three holidays known as the Christmas, Easter, and Long Vacation holiday periods, which are referred to by the university as vacations rather than holidays; students vacate the premises during these periods but are still expected to be pursuing studies and assignments.

The [Tripos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripos) exam involves a mixture of lectures organised by the university department) and [supervised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supervision_system) and organised by the colleges. Science subjects involve laboratory sessions organised by the departments. The relative importance of these methods of teaching varies according to the needs of the subject. Supervisions are typically weekly hour-long sessions in which small groups of students, usually between one and three students, who meet with a member of the teaching staff or with a doctoral student. Students are normally required to complete an assignment in advance of this supervision, which they then discuss with the supervisor during the session. The assignment is often an essay on a subject assigned by the supervisor, or a problem sheet set by the lecturer. Depending on the subject and college, students sometimes receive between one and four supervisory sessions each week.[^cite_note-152] This [pedagogical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutorial_system) is often cited as being unique to Oxford, where supervisions are known as [tutorials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutorial),[^cite_note-153] and Cambridge and is sometimes credited with the exceptional nature generally associated with the education at these two world-renowned universities. A tutor named [William Farish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Farish_\(professor\)) developed the concept of grading students' work quantitatively at the University of Cambridge in 1792.[^cite_note-154]

### Research

See also: [Category\:Departments of the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Departments_of_the_University_of_Cambridge)

The University of Cambridge has research departments and teaching faculties in nearly every academic discipline, with research and lectures conducted by university departments. The colleges are charged with giving or arranging most supervisions, student accommodation, and funding most extracurricular activities. During the 1990s, the University of Cambridge added a substantial number of new specialist research laboratories on several sites around the city, and major expansion continues.[^cite_note-155] From 2000 to 2006, the University of Cambridge maintained a research partnership with [MIT](18879.md) in the United States, known as the [Cambridge–MIT Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge%E2%80%93MIT_Institute), which was discontinued after evolving into what is now called the CMI Partnership Programme.

### Graduation tradition and ceremony



Graduands enter [Senate House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_House,_Cambridge) at a graduation ceremony.



Vice-Chancellor Professor Sir [Leszek Borysiewicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leszek_Borysiewicz) in academic dress, after conferring degrees at the Senate House in July 2014

The university's governing body, [Regent House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regent_House), manages and votes on graduations. A formal meeting of Regent House, known as a [congregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congregation_\(university\)), is held for this purpose,[^cite_note-Ceremony-156] which is typically the final act during which all university procedures for undergraduate and graduate students and other degrees are finalised. After degrees are approved, candidates for graduation are required to request their respective college presents them during commencement congregation.

Graduates receiving an undergraduate degree wear the [academic dress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_dress_of_the_University_of_Cambridge) to which they are entitled prior to graduating; for example, most students becoming Bachelor of Arts graduates wear undergraduate gowns and not BA gowns. Graduates receiving a post-graduate degree wear the academic dress that they were entitled to before graduating if their first degree was also from the University of Cambridge; if their first degree was from another university, they wear the academic dress of the degree that they are about to receive. The BA gown without the strings is worn if the graduate is 24 years old or younger, and the MA gown without strings is worn if the graduate is 24 years old or older.[^cite_note-157] Graduates are presented their degrees in [Senate House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_House,_Cambridge) by each respective college in order of foundation or recognition by the university, except for the university's royal colleges.

During the University of Cambridge's congregation ceremony, graduands are brought forth by the [Praelector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praelector) of their respective college, who takes them by the right hand and presents them to the vice-chancellor to receive the degree they have earned. The Praelector presents graduands with the following [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) statement, substituting "\_\_\_\_" with the name of the degree and substituting "woman" for "man" if the graduate is female:

> "*Dignissima domina, Domina Procancellaria et tota Academia praesento vobis hunc virum quem scio tam moribus quam doctrina esse idoneum ad gradum assequendum \_\_\_\_\_; idque tibi fide mea praesto totique Academiae.*

The Latin statement translates in English as, "Most worthy Vice-Chancellor and the whole University, I present to you this man whom I know to be suitable as much by character as by learning to proceed to the degree of \_\_\_\_; for which I pledge my faith to you and to the whole University."

After presentation, the graduate is called by name and kneels before the vice-chancellor and proffers their hands to the vice-chancellor, who clasps them and then confers the degree through the following Latin statement, known as the [Trinitarian formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinitarian_formula) (*in nomine Patris*), which may be omitted at the request of the graduand:

"*Auctoritate mihi commissa admitto te ad gradum \_\_\_\_, in nomine Patris et Filii et Spiritus Sancti*, which translates in English as: "By the authority committed to me, I admit you to the degree of \_\_\_\_, in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit."

The new graduate then rises, bows, and leaves the Senate House through the Doctor's door in [Senate House Passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_Lane), where they receive their degree certificate.[^cite_note-Ceremony-156]

### Libraries and museums

Main article: [Libraries of the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libraries_of_the_University_of_Cambridge)

See also: [Category\:Museums of the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Museums_of_the_University_of_Cambridge)



[Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge)'s [Wren Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wren_Library)



[Fitzwilliam Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitzwilliam_Museum), the art and antiquities museum of the University of Cambridge

The University of Cambridge has [116 libraries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libraries_of_Cambridge_University).[^cite_note-158] [Cambridge University Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Library), which holds more than eight million volumes, is the central research library. It is a [legal deposit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_deposit) library, which entitles it to request a free copy of every book published in the UK and Ireland.[^cite_note-159]

In addition to the University Library and its dependents, almost every faculty or department has a specialised library; for example, the History Faculty's [Seeley Historical Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seeley_Historical_Library) houses in excess of 100,000 books. Every college also maintains a library, partly for the purpose of undergraduate teaching; older colleges often possess many early books and manuscripts in a separate library. For example, [Trinity College's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge) [Wren Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wren_Library,_Cambridge) houses more than 200,000 books printed before 1800 and [Corpus Christi College's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_Christi_College,_Cambridge) [Parker Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parker_Library,_Corpus_Christi_College) has more than 600 [medieval manuscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illuminated_manuscript), representing one of the largest such collections in the world. [Churchill Archives Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churchill_Archives_Centre) on the campus of [Churchill College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churchill_College) houses the official papers of former British prime ministers [Winston Churchill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winston_Churchill) and [Margaret Thatcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Thatcher).

The university operates eight arts, cultural, and scientific museums, and a [botanical garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botanical_garden).[^cite_note-160] [Fitzwilliam Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitzwilliam_Museum) is the art and antiquities museum; [Kettle's Yard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kettle's_Yard) is the university's [contemporary art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemporary_art) gallery; the [Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Archaeology_and_Anthropology,_University_of_Cambridge) houses the university's collections of local antiquities along with archaeological and [ethnographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnography) artefacts from around the world; [Cambridge University Museum of Zoology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Museum_of_Zoology) houses a wide range of [zoological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoology) specimens from around the world and is known for its iconic [finback whale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finback_whale) skeleton that hangs outside the museum. Cambridge University Museum of Zoology also holds specimens collected by [Charles Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin), an 1831 University of Cambridge alumnus. Other museums include the [Museum of Classical Archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Classical_Archaeology,_Cambridge), [Whipple Museum of the History of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whipple_Museum_of_the_History_of_Science), [Sedgwick Museum of Earth Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedgwick_Museum_of_Earth_Sciences), the university's geology museum, which displays some of Darwin's geological specimens and equipment (Darwin had studied under Adam Sedgwick, and wrote "I a geologist" in a notebook in 1838), and [Polar Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott_Polar_Research_Institute#The_Polar_Museum), part of the [Scott Polar Research Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott_Polar_Research_Institute), which is dedicated to [Captain Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captain_Scott) and his men and focuses on the exploration of the [Polar Regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_regions_of_Earth). [Cambridge University Botanic Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Botanic_Garden), created in 1831, is the university's botanical garden.

### Publishing and assessments

See also: [Cambridge University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Press)

The university's publishing arm, [Cambridge University Press & Assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Press_&_Assessment), is the oldest printer and publisher in the world and the second largest [university press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_press) in the world.[^cite_note-161][^cite_note-162] It is also the largest department of the university by financial income, reporting income above £800 million.[^cite_note-163]

The university established its Local Examination Syndicate in 1858, now known as [Cambridge University Press & Assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Press_&_Assessment) after its merger with Cambridge University Press. It is the largest assessment agency in Europe. Cambridge University Press & Assessment plays a leading role in researching, developing, and delivering assessments around the world.[^cite_note-164]

## Student life

See also: [Academic dress of the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_dress_of_the_University_of_Cambridge) and [List of social activities at the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_social_activities_at_the_University_of_Cambridge)

### Formal halls and May balls



The bridge over the [River Cam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Cam) at [Clare College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clare_College,_Cambridge) during the 2005 [May Ball in Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Ball_in_Cambridge)



The University Centre main dining hall

One privilege of student life at the University of Cambridge is the opportunity to attend formal dinners at a student's respective college, known as [Formal Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_\(university\)) that are held regularly during academic terms and daily at some of the university's colleges. During Formal Hall, students typically sit down for a meal in their [gowns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_gown) while [fellows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow) and sometimes guests eat separately at a so-called High Table. The beginning and end of the function is usually marked with [grace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_\(prayer\)), which is said in [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin). Special Formal Halls are organised for Christmas and the Commemoration of Benefactors.[^cite_note-181]

After the exam period, [May Week](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Week) is held during which it is customary to celebrate by attending [May Balls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Ball), which are all-night lavish parties held in the colleges where food, drinks, and entertainment are provided. So-called Suicide Sunday, the first day of May Week, is a popular date for [garden parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_party).[^cite_note-182]

### JCR and MCR

In addition to university-wide representation, students can participate in their own college student unions, which are known as [Junior Combination Room](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_room_\(university\)) (JCR) for undergraduates and Middle Combination Room (MCR) for post-graduates. These serve as a link between college staff and members and include officers elected annually between the fellow students; individual JCR and MCRs also report to [Cambridge Students' Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Students'_Union), which offers training courses for some of the positions within the body.[^cite_note-183]

### Societies

Main page: [Category\:Clubs and societies of the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Clubs_and_societies_of_the_University_of_Cambridge)

See also: [Cambridge Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Union) and [Cambridge University Moral Sciences Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Moral_Sciences_Club)



[Stephen Fry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Fry) at [Cambridge Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Union), the world's oldest continuing running debate society

Numerous student-run societies exist at the University of Cambridge designed to encourage students who share common passions or interests to periodically meet or discuss these interests. As of 2010, there were 751 registered societies at the university.[^cite_note-184] In addition to these, individual colleges often promote their own societies and sports teams.

Although technically independent from the university, [Cambridge Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Union), a globally renowned debate organisation and the oldest debate organisation in the world, offers students high-level debate and public speaking experience. Drama societies include the [Cambridge University Amateur Dramatic Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Amateur_Dramatic_Club) (CUADC) and the comedy club [Footlights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Footlights), whose alumni include many well-known show business personalities. The university's [Chamber Orchestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamber_Orchestra), composed entirely of university students, offers a range of orchestra programs, including symphonies.

### Sports

Main page: [Category\:Sport at the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Sport_at_the_University_of_Cambridge)

The boathouse of the [Cambridge University Boat Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Boat_Club)

[Rowing](25736.md) is one of the most popular sports at the University of Cambridge, and there are competitions between colleges, notably the [bumps races](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bumps_race). The University of Cambridge's rowing competition against [Oxford](31797.md) is known as the [Boat Race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boat_Race). [Varsity matches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_British_and_Irish_varsity_matches) against Oxford also exist in other sports, including [cricket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Cricket_Club), [rugby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_R.U.F.C.), [chess](5134.md), and [tiddlywinks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiddlywinks). Athletes who representing the university in a varsity match are entitled to a [Blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Blue) or a Half Blue, depending on the sport and other criteria.[^cite_note-Hawks-2-185]

The [University of Cambridge Sports Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge_Sports_Centre) opened in August 2013. The university has an ice hockey club called [Cambridge University Ice Hockey Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Ice_Hockey_Club). The [Hawks' Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawks'_Club) is a private members' club for the university's leading sportsmen.[^cite_note-Hawks-2-185] The Ospreys are the equivalent female club.

### Student newspapers and radio

See also: [Category\:Publications associated with the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Publications_associated_with_the_University_of_Cambridge)

Cambridge's oldest student newspaper is *[Varsity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varsity_\(Cambridge\))*. Established in 1947, notable figures who have edited the newspaper include [Jeremy Paxman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Paxman), [BBC](19344654.md) media editor [Amol Rajan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amol_Rajan), and *[Vogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vogue_\(magazine\))* international editor [Suzy Menkes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzy_Menkes). The student newspaper also has featured the early writings of [Zadie Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zadie_Smith), who appeared in *Varsity*'s literary anthology offshoot *[The Mays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mays)*, [Robert Webb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Webb), [Tristram Hunt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tristram_Hunt), and [Tony Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Wilson).

*Varsity* has a circulation of 9,000 and is the only student publication published weekly. News stories from *Varsity* have appeared in *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)*, *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times)*, *[The Sunday Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sunday_Times)*, *[The Daily Telegraph](23797577.md)*, *[The Independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Independent)* and *[The i Paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_i_Paper)*.

Other student publications include *[The Cambridge Student](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cambridge_Student)*, which is funded by [Cambridge Students' Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Students'_Union) and is published fortnightly, *[The Tab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tab)*, and *[The Mays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mays)* Founded by two University of Cambridge students in 2009, *The Tab* is an online media outlet featuring light-hearted features content. *The Mays* is a literary anthology including student prose, poetry, and visual art from both University of Cambridge and [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University) students. Founded in 1992 by three Cambridge students, the anthology publishes once a year and is overseen by Varsity Publications Ltd., the same body responsible for *Varsity*. Another literary journal, *Notes*, is published roughly twice per term. Additionally, many colleges have their own student-run publications.

The student radio station, [Cam FM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cam_FM), is run jointly by University of Cambridge and [Anglia Ruskin University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglia_Ruskin_University) students. The station holds an [FM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FM_broadcasting) licence (frequency 97.2 MHz), and hosts a mixture of music, talk, and sports shows.

### Student Union

Main article: [Cambridge Students' Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Students'_Union)

All students at the University of Cambridge are represented by [Cambridge Students' Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Students'_Union), which was founded in 2020 as a merger of two student unions, [Cambridge University Students' Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Students'_Union) (CUSU) and the Graduate Union (GU). CUSU previously represented all University students, and GU represented graduate students.[^cite_note-187][^cite_note-188]

The eight most important positions in Cambridge Students' Union are occupied by [sabbatical officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabbatical_officer).[^cite_note-189] In 2020, the sabbatical officers were elected with a turnout of 20.88% of the whole student body.[^cite_note-190]

In 2021, Cambridge Students' Union launched a petition opposing the financial collaboration between the university and the government of [United Arab Emirates](69328.md) that was worth £400m. The Union cited a "values gap" and threat to "academic freedom and institutional autonomy" following the release of internal UAE documents. Citing UAE's history of violating international human rights laws, it warned that university staff were vulnerable under the partnership to repression by gender, sexuality, or freedom of expression.[^cite_note-191]

In 2023, 72% of the Students' Union voted in favour of hosting talks regarding the removal of all animal products from cafes and canteens operated by the university's catering services. The students backed [vegan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veganism) food in response to threats to the climate and biodiversity. The vote is non-binding since the university controls the catering service. The vote was supported by the student chapter of [Plant-Based Universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegan_school_meal#Plant-Based_Universities_campaign).[^cite_note-192] After the vote, [Darwin College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darwin_College,_Cambridge) decided to serve only vegan food at its [May Ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Ball_in_Cambridge) in 2023.[^cite_note-193]

### Politics

A protest in [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge) with an attendance of more than a thousand students and residents – the city's largest demonstration – called on the University of Cambridge to [divest](981983.md) from Israel over Israel's actions in the [Gaza Strip](12047.md) during the [Gaza war](74998519.md).[^cite_note-varoctlarge-194][^cite_note-195] Students and staff also walked out of lectures in protest over the same issues.[^cite_note-196]

[Students and staff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_University_of_Cambridge_people) at the University of Cambridge wrote an open letter to the university, with more than 1,400 signatories, demanding it acknowledge the "slaughter of innocent Palestinians", "sever financial ties with Israel" as it had with Russia following the [invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine), and demanding it investigate its financial ties with [arms manufacturers](608296.md) that potentially supplied to Israel, mentioning, among others, [Plasan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasan) and [Caterpillar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caterpillar_Inc.).[^cite_note-varoctlarge-194][^cite_note-varNov-197]

[Charles Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin), evolutionary biologist

[Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton), mathematician and physicist who developed [classical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics) and [calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus)



[Abdus Salam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdus_Salam), a theoretical physicist

[Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton), who conducted many of his experiments on the grounds of Trinity College, ranks among the most famed University of Cambridge alumni. Other alumni of the university include [Francis Bacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Bacon), who developed the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method) of inquiry, mathematicians [John Dee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dee) and [Brook Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brook_Taylor), [pure mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_mathematics) [G. H. Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._H._Hardy), [John Edensor Littlewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Edensor_Littlewood), [Mary Cartwright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Cartwright), and [Augustus De Morgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus_De_Morgan); [Michael Atiyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Atiyah), a geometry specialist; [William Oughtred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Oughtred), inventor of the [logarithmic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_scale); [John Wallis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wallis), first to explain the law of acceleration; [Srinivasa Ramanujan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srinivasa_Ramanujan), a genius who made substantial contributions to [mathematical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_analysis), [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory), [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series), and [continued fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continued_fractions); and [James Clerk Maxwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell), who brought about the second great unification of physics (the first being accredited to Newton) with his classical theory of [electromagnetic radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_radiation). In 1890, mathematician [Philippa Fawcett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippa_Fawcett), a University of Cambridge student, registered the highest score in the Cambridge Mathematical Tripos exams but as a woman was then ineligible to claim the title [Senior Wrangler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senior_Wrangler).

In biology, University of Cambridge alumni include [Charles Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin), famous for developing the theory of [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection) and explaining evolution, is an alumnus of [Christ's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ's_College,_Cambridge). Biologists [Francis Crick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crick) and [James Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_D._Watson) developed the model explaining the three-dimensional structure of [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA) while working at the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory); University of Cambridge graduates [Maurice Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Wilkins) and especially [Rosalind Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosalind_Franklin) produced key [X-ray crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography) data, which was shared with Watson by Wilkins. Wilkins went on to verify the proposed structure and win the Nobel Prize with Watson and Crick. More recently, [Ian Wilmut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Wilmut) was part of the team responsible for the first cloning of a mammal ([Dolly the Sheep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolly_the_Sheep) in 1996), naturalist and broadcaster [David Attenborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Attenborough), ethologist [Jane Goodall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Goodall), expert on chimpanzees was a PhD student at the university, anthropologist [Dame Alison Richard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alison_Richard), former vice-chancellor of the university, and [Frederick Sanger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Sanger), a biochemist known for developing [Sanger sequencing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanger_sequencing) and receiving two Nobel prizes.

Despite the university's delay in admitting women to its full degree programs, women associated with the University of Cambridge have been at the heart of scientific research throughout the 20th century. Notable female scientists include biochemist [Marjory Stephenson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marjory_Stephenson), plant physiologist [Gabrielle Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabrielle_Howard), social anthropologist [Audrey Richards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audrey_Richards), psychoanalyst [Alix Strachey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alix_Strachey), who with her husband translated the works of [Sigmund Freud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_Freud), [Kavli Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kavli_Prize)-winner [Brenda Milner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brenda_Milner), responsible for co-discovering specialised brain networks for memory and cognition. Veterinary epidemiologist [Sarah Cleaveland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Cleaveland) has contributed to advances in eliminating [rabies](16946852.md) in the [Serengeti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serengeti).[^cite_note-200]

The university is widely considered the birthplace of the computer; mathematician and father of the computer [Charles Babbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Babbage) designed the world's first computing system as early as the mid-1800s. Cambridge alumnus [Alan Turing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Turing) devised the basis for modern computing, and [Maurice Wilkes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Wilkes) later created the first programmable computer. The [webcam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Webcam) was also invented at the University of Cambridge, showing the [Trojan Room coffee pot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_Room_coffee_pot) in the university's computer laboratories.[^cite_note-201]

In physics, [Ernest Rutherford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rutherford), regarded as the father of [nuclear physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_physics), spent much of his life at the university, where he worked closely with [E. J. Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evan_James_Williams) and [Niels Bohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr), a major contributor to the understanding of the [atom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom), [J. J. Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._Thomson), discoverer of the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron), [James Chadwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Chadwick), discoverer of the [neutron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron), and [John Cockcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cockcroft) and [Ernest Walton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Walton), responsible for first splitting the atom. [J. Robert Oppenheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Robert_Oppenheimer), leader of the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project) that developed the [atomic bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bomb), also studied under Rutherford and Thomson. [Joan Curran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Curran) devised the [chaff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaff_\(countermeasure\)) technique used during [World War II](32927.md) to disrupt [radar](25676.md) on [Axis powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_powers)' planes.

University of Cambridge alumni in astronomy include [John Herschel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Herschel), [Arthur Eddington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Eddington), and [Paul Dirac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Dirac), discoverer of [antimatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimatter) and one of the pioneers of [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics); [Stephen Hawking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Hawking), theoretical physicist and the university's long-serving [Lucasian Professor of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucasian_Professor_of_Mathematics) until 2009; and [Martin Rees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Rees,_Baron_Rees_of_Ludlow), the current [Astronomer Royal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer_Royal) and former Master of Trinity College. [John Polkinghorne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Polkinghorne), a mathematician before his ordination to the [Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican) [ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_\(Christianity\)), received the [Templeton Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Templeton_Prize) for his work reconciling science and religion.

Other significant university alumni in science include [Henry Cavendish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Cavendish), who discovered [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen); [Frank Whittle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Whittle), co-inventor of the jet engine; [William Thomson (Lord Kelvin)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thomson,_1st_Baron_Kelvin), who formulated the original [Laws of Thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_of_Thermodynamics); [William Fox Talbot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Fox_Talbot), who invented the camera, [Alfred North Whitehead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_North_Whitehead), Einstein's major opponent; [Jagadish Chandra Bose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jagadish_Chandra_Bose), one of the fathers of radio science; [Lord Rayleigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_William_Strutt,_3rd_Baron_Rayleigh), who made extensive contributions to both theoretical and experimental physics in the 20th century; and [Georges Lemaître](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Lema%C3%AEtre), who first proposed the [Big Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bang) theory.

### Politics



[Oliver Cromwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Cromwell), statesman, politician, and soldier who served as [Lord Protector of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Protector)

The University of Cambridge has a strong reputation in the field of politics, having educated:[^cite_note-202]

- [Oliver Cromwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Cromwell), [Lord Protector of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Protector_of_England) (1653–58)

  [^cite_note-203]

- 15 [British Prime Ministers](24150.md), including [Robert Walpole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Walpole), who is widely regarded as the first British Prime Minister, as well as the incumbent Prime Minister [Andy Burnham](414818.md).

- At least 30 foreign heads of state or government, including presidents of India, Ireland, Trinidad and Tobago, Uganda, South Korea, Singapore, and Zambia; along with prime ministers of Australia, Bahrain, Burma, France, India, Jordan, Luxembourg, Malaysia, Malta, Thailand, Pakistan, Poland, Singapore, South Africa, Sri Lanka, and New Zealand

- At least nine monarchs, including Kings [Edward VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_VII), [George VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_VI), and (current King) [Charles III](125248.md) of the United Kingdom, King [Peter II of Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_II_of_Yugoslavia), Queen [Margrethe II of Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margrethe_II_of_Denmark), and [Queen Sofía of Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Sof%C3%ADa_of_Spain). The university has also educated a large number of royals, including [Prince Edward, Earl of Wessex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Edward,_Earl_of_Wessex)

- Three signatories of the [United States Declaration of Independence](31874.md), [Thomas Lynch Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Lynch_Jr.), [Arthur Middleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Middleton), and [Thomas Nelson Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Nelson_Jr.)

  [^cite_note-204]

### Sports

By 2016, athletes who are university graduates or attendees had won 194 Olympic medals, including 88 gold medals.[^cite_note-Hawks-17] Alumni of the university include Chinese six-time world table tennis champion [Deng Yaping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deng_Yaping); sprinter and athletics hero [Harold Abrahams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Abrahams); inventors of the modern game of football, [H. de Winton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._de_Winton) and [J. C. Thring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._C._Thring); Indian cricketer Colonel H. H. Shri Sir [Ranjitsinhji Vibhaji II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranjitsinhji); and [George Mallory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Mallory), the mountaineer.

### Technology

University of Cambridge alumni have gone on to found or co-found leading technology companies, including [Demis Hassabis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demis_Hassabis), the co-founder and chief executive officer of [DeepMind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DeepMind), an [artificial intelligence](1164.md) subsidiary company of [Alphabet Inc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabet_Inc) responsible for [AlphaGo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AlphaGo) and [Sophie Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophie_Wilson) and [Steve Furber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Furber), co-founders of [ARM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arm_Ltd.), a British semiconductor and software design company based in Cambridge.[^cite_note-205][^cite_note-206]

### Notable current academics

The academic staff at the University of Cambridge includes several notable scholars, including [Sriya Iyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sriya_Iyer), known for her research in the economics of religion and development;[^cite_note-207][^cite_note-208][^cite_note-209] [Graham Virgo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Virgo), specialising in equity and the law of restitution;[^cite_note-210][^cite_note-211][^cite_note-212] [Aaron Koller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaron_Koller), a specialist in [Semitic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitic_languages) and Jewish intellectual history;[^cite_note-213][^cite_note-214] [Mihaela van der Schaar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihaela_van_der_Schaar), known for her research in AI-enabled personalised medicine.[^cite_note-215]

## In literature and popular culture

See also: [List of fictional Cambridge colleges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fictional_Cambridge_colleges); [A Disappearing Number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Disappearing_Number); [Cambridge Spies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Spies); [Chariots of Fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chariots_of_Fire); [Chariots of Fire (play)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chariots_of_Fire_\(play\)); [High Table, Lower Orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Table,_Lower_Orders); [Category\:Novels set in the University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Novels_set_in_the_University_of_Cambridge); [Porterhouse Blue (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porterhouse_Blue_\(TV_series\)); [Psmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psmith); [Rock 'n' Roll (play)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_'n'_Roll_\(play\)); [The Reeve's Tale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Reeve's_Tale); and [The Riddle of the Sphinx (Inside No. 9)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Riddle_of_the_Sphinx_\(Inside_No._9\))

Throughout its history, the University of Cambridge has frequently been featured in literature, artistic works, television, and film. Cambridge was mentioned as early as the 14th century in [Geoffrey Chaucer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Chaucer)'s *[The Canterbury Tales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Canterbury_Tales)*. In *[The Reeve's Tale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Reeve's_Tale)*, the two main fictional characters are students at a University of Cambridge college called Soler Halle, which is believed to refer to [King's Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_Hall,_Cambridge) and is now part of [Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge).[^cite_note-216] The university has been the setting for all or parts of numerous novels, including [Douglas Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Adams)' *[Dirk Gently's Holistic Detective Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirk_Gently's_Holistic_Detective_Agency)*, [Rose Macaulay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rose_Macaulay)'s *[They Were Defeated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/They_Were_Defeated)*,[^cite_note-217] and [Tom Sharpe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Sharpe)'s *[Porterhouse Blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porterhouse_Blue)*.[^cite_note-218]

Other notable examples of the University of Cambridge in popular culture include:

- [Xu Zhimo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xu_Zhimo)'s best-known poem, "On Leaving Cambridge" ([simplified Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplified_Chinese_characters): 再别康桥; [traditional Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_characters): 再別康橋), published in 1928, is part of [China's national curriculum](407843.md) taught to all schoolchildren and has generated a tremendous amount of adoration of the University of Cambridge in China.

  [^cite_note-219]

- In the *[Psmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psmith)* series, a collection of novels published between 1908 and 1923 by [P. G. Wodehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._G._Wodehouse), the [title character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert_Psmith) and [Mike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_%22Mike%22_Jackson), his closest friend, study at the University of Cambridge.

- The 1981 film *[Chariots of Fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chariots_of_Fire)* is partly set at Cambridge between 1919 and 1924 when protagonist [Harold Abrahams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Abrahams) (played by [Ben Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Cross)) was a student there.

- The 1983 film *[Monty Python's The Meaning of Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monty_Python's_The_Meaning_of_Life)* features [Churchill College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churchill_College,_Cambridge) in the film's church scene.

- The film *[Maurice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_\(1987_film\))*, based on the 1971 novel [Maurice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_\(novel\)), is partly filmed at the University of Cambridge.

- The 1989 film *[Dead Poets Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Poets_Society)* features character John Keating (played by [Robin Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Williams)), who is a Welton Academy English teacher described in his senior annual as "Cambridge bound".
