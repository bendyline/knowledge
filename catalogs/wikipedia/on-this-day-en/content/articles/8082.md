**Diamond** is a mineral [form of the element carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allotropes_of_carbon) with its atoms arranged in a [crystal structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_structure) called [diamond cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_cubic). Diamond is a tasteless, odorless, strong, brittle solid, a poor conductor of electricity, colorless in pure form, and insoluble in water. Another solid form of carbon known as [graphite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphite) is the [chemically stable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_stability) form of carbon at [room temperature and pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_temperature_and_pressure), but diamond is [metastable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metastable) and converts to it at a negligible rate under those conditions. Diamond has the highest [hardness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_hardness) and [thermal conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_conductivity) of any natural material, properties that are used in major industrial applications such as cutting and polishing tools.

Because the arrangement of atoms in diamond is extremely rigid, few types of impurity can contaminate it (two exceptions are [boron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron) and [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen)). Small numbers of [defects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_defect) or impurities—about one per million of lattice atoms—can color a diamond blue (boron), yellow (nitrogen), brown (defects), green (radiation exposure), purple, pink, orange, or red. Diamond also has a very high [refractive index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refractive_index) and a relatively high [optical dispersion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_dispersion).

Most natural diamonds have ages between 1 billion and 3.5 billion years. Most were formed at depths between 150 and 250 kilometres (93 and 155 mi) in the Earth's [mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantle_\(geology\)), although a few have come from as deep as 800 kilometres (500 mi). Under high pressure and temperature, carbon-containing fluids dissolved various minerals and replaced them with diamonds. Much more recently (hundreds to tens of million years ago), they were carried to the surface in [volcanic eruptions](8921015.md) and deposited in [igneous rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igneous_rock) known as [kimberlites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimberlite) and [lamproites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamproite).

[Synthetic diamonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_diamond) can be grown from high-purity carbon under high pressures and temperatures or from [hydrocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) gases by [chemical vapor deposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_vapor_deposition) (CVD). Natural and synthetic diamonds are most commonly distinguished using optical techniques or thermal conductivity measurements.

## Etymology, earliest use and composition discovery

The name *diamond* is derived from [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language): ἀδάμας (*adámas*), 'proper, unalterable, unbreakable, untamed', from [ἀ-](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E1%BC%80-#Ancient_Greek) (*a-*), 'not' + δαμάω (*damáō*), 'to overpower, tame'.[^cite_note-6] Diamonds are thought to have been first recognized and mined in [India](14533.md), where significant [alluvial deposits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alluvial_deposit) of the stone could be found many centuries ago along the rivers [Penner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penner_River), [Krishna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krishna_River), and [Godavari](47988.md). Diamonds have been known in India for at least 3,000 years but most likely 6,000 years.[^cite_note-hershey-7]

Diamonds have been treasured as gemstones since their use as [religious icons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icon) in [ancient India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdoms_of_Ancient_India). Their usage in engraving tools also dates to early [human history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_history).[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-ancient_China-9] The popularity of diamonds has risen since the 19th century due to increased supply, improved cutting and polishing techniques, growth in the world economy, and innovative and successful advertising campaigns.[^cite_note-sell-10]

In 1772, the French scientist [Antoine Lavoisier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Lavoisier) used a lens to concentrate the rays of the sun on a diamond in an atmosphere of [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen), and showed that the only product of the combustion was [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide), proving that diamond is composed of carbon.[^cite_note-11] Later, in 1797, the English chemist [Smithson Tennant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smithson_Tennant) repeated and expanded that experiment.[^cite_note-12] By demonstrating that burning diamond and graphite releases the same amount of gas, he established the chemical equivalence of these substances.[^cite_note-hazen-13]

## Properties

Main article: [Material properties of diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Material_properties_of_diamond)

Diamond is a solid form of pure carbon with its atoms arranged in a crystal. Solid carbon comes in different forms known as [allotropes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allotrope) depending on the type of chemical bond. The two most common [allotropes of pure carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allotropes_of_carbon) are diamond and [graphite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphite). In graphite, the bonds are sp<sup>2</sup> [orbital hybrids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_hybridisation) and the atoms form in planes, with each bound to three nearest neighbors, 120 degrees apart. In diamond, they are sp<sup>3</sup> and the atoms form tetrahedral, with each bound to four nearest neighbors.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15] Tetrahedra are rigid, the bonds are strong, and, of all known substances, diamond has the greatest number of atoms per unit volume, which is why it is both the hardest and the least [compressible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compressibility).[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-ChemThermo-17] It also has a high density, ranging from 3150 to 3530 kilograms per cubic metre (over three times the density of water) in natural diamonds and 3520 kg/m<sup>3</sup> in pure diamond.[^cite_note-mindat-4] In graphite, the bonds between nearest neighbors are even stronger, but the bonds between parallel adjacent planes are weak, so the planes easily slip past each other. Thus, graphite is much softer than diamond. However, the stronger bonds make graphite less flammable.[^cite_note-18]

Diamonds have been adopted for many uses because of the material's exceptional physical characteristics. It has the highest [thermal conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_conductivity) and the highest sound velocity. It has low adhesion and friction, and its coefficient of [thermal expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_expansion) is extremely low. Its optical transparency extends from the [far infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_infrared) to the deep [ultraviolet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet), and it has high [optical dispersion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_dispersion). It also has high electrical resistance. It is chemically inert, not reacting with most corrosive substances, and has excellent biological compatibility.[^cite_note-19]

### Thermodynamics



&#x20;Theoretically predicted phase diagram of carbon, from 1989 and updated with newer work

[^cite_note-20]

The equilibrium pressure and temperature conditions for a transition between graphite and diamond are well established theoretically and experimentally. The equilibrium pressure varies linearly with temperature, between 1.7 [GPa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_\(unit\)) at 0 K and 12 GPa at 5000 K (the diamond/graphite/liquid [triple point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triple_point)).[^cite_note-Bundy-21][^cite_note-22] However, the phases have a wide region about this line where they can coexist. At [standard temperature and pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_temperature_and_pressure), 20 °C (293 K) and 1 standard atmosphere (0.10 MPa), the stable phase of carbon is graphite, but diamond is [metastable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metastable), with a significant kinetic energy barrier that the atoms must overcome to reach the lower energy state,[^cite_note-baird-23] and its rate of conversion to graphite is negligible, with a timescale of millions to billions of years.[^cite_note-ChemThermo-17][^cite_note-baird-23] However, at temperatures above about 4500 K, diamond rapidly converts to graphite. Experiments have found that diamond, in the presence of H<sub>2</sub>O passes through an intermediate linear carbon phase.[^cite_note-24]

Rapid conversion of graphite to diamond requires pressures well above the equilibrium line: at 2000 K, a pressure of 35 GPa (about 350,000 standard atmospheres) is needed.[^cite_note-Bundy-21]

Above the graphite–diamond–liquid carbon triple point, the melting point of diamond increases slowly with increasing pressure, but at pressures of hundreds of GPa, it decreases.[^cite_note-25] At high pressures, [silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon) and [germanium](12242.md) have a BC8 [body-centered cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_crystal_system) crystal structure, and a similar structure is predicted for carbon at high pressures. At 0 K, the transition is predicted to occur at 1100 GPa.[^cite_note-26]

Results published in *[Nature Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Physics)* in 2010 suggest that, at ultra-high pressures and temperatures (about 10 million atmospheres or 1 TPa and 50,000 °C), diamond melts into a metallic fluid. The extreme conditions required for this to occur are present in the [ice giant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_giant) [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet) [Neptune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptune) and [Uranus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranus), both of which are made up of approximately 10 percent carbon and could hypothetically contain oceans of liquid carbon. Since large quantities of metallic fluid can affect the magnetic field, this could serve to explain why the geographic and magnetic poles of the two planets are not aligned.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28]

### Crystal structure

See also: [Crystallographic defects in diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallographic_defects_in_diamond)



Diamond unit cell, showing the tetrahedral structure

The most common crystal structure of diamond is called [diamond cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_cubic). It is formed of [unit cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_cell) stacked together. Although there are 18 atoms in the figure, each corner atom is shared by eight unit cells and each atom in the center of a face is shared by two, so there are a total of eight atoms per unit cell.[^cite_note-29] The length of each side of the unit cell is denoted by *a* and is 3.567 [angstroms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angstrom).[^cite_note-Ashcroft-30]

The nearest neighbor distance in the diamond lattice is 1.732*a*/4 where *a* is the lattice constant, usually given in Angstrøms as *a* = 3.567 Å, which is 0.3567 nm.

A diamond cubic lattice can be thought of as two interpenetrating [face-centered cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face-centered_cubic) lattices with one displaced by 1⁄4 of the diagonal along a cubic cell, or as one lattice with two atoms associated with each lattice point.[^cite_note-Ashcroft-30] Viewed from a <1 1 1> [crystallographic direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_index), it is formed of layers stacked in a repeating ABCABC ... pattern. Diamonds can also form an ABAB ... structure, which is known as hexagonal diamond or [lonsdaleite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lonsdaleite), but this is far less common and is formed under different conditions from cubic carbon.[^cite_note-31]

### Crystal habit

A triangular facet of a crystal having triangular etch pits with the largest having a base length of about 0.2 millimetres (0.0079 in)

One face of an uncut octahedral diamond, showing trigons (of positive and negative relief) formed by natural chemical etching

Diamonds occur most often as [euhedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euhedral) or rounded [octahedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octahedron) and [twinned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_twinning) octahedra known as *[macles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macle)*. As diamond's crystal structure has a cubic arrangement of the atoms, they have many [facets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facet) that belong to a [cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube_\(geometry\)), octahedron, [rhombicosidodecahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhombicosidodecahedron), [tetrakis hexahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrakis_hexahedron), or [disdyakis dodecahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disdyakis_dodecahedron). The crystals can have rounded-off and unexpressive edges and can be elongated. Diamonds (especially those with rounded crystal faces) are commonly found coated in *nyf*, an opaque gum-like skin.[^cite_note-32]

Some diamonds contain opaque fibers. They are referred to as *opaque* if the fibers grow from a clear substrate or *fibrous* if they occupy the entire crystal. Their colors range from yellow to green or gray, sometimes with cloud-like white to gray impurities. Their most common shape is cuboidal, but they can also form octahedra, dodecahedra, macles, or combined shapes. The structure is the result of numerous impurities with sizes between 1 and 5 microns. These diamonds probably formed in kimberlite magma and sampled the volatiles.[^cite_note-Cartigny-33]

Diamonds can also form polycrystalline aggregates. There have been attempts to classify them into groups with names such as [boart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boart), [ballas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballas), stewartite, and framesite, but there is no widely accepted set of criteria.[^cite_note-Cartigny-33] [Carbonado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonado), a type in which the diamond grains were [sintered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sintering) (fused without melting by the application of heat and pressure), is black in color and tougher than single crystal diamond.[^cite_note-34] It has never been observed in a volcanic rock. There are many theories for its origin, including formation in a star, but no consensus.[^cite_note-Cartigny-33][^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36]

### Mechanical

#### Hardness



The extreme hardness of diamond in certain orientations makes it useful in materials science, as in this pyramidal diamond embedded in the working surface of a [Vickers hardness tester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vickers_hardness_test).

Diamond is the hardest material on the [qualitative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qualitative_property) [Mohs scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohs_scale_of_mineral_hardness). To conduct the [quantitative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_measurement) [Vickers hardness test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vickers_hardness_test), samples of materials are struck with a pyramid of standardized dimensions using a known force – a diamond crystal is used for the pyramid to permit a wide range of materials to be tested. From the size of the resulting indentation, a Vickers hardness value for the material can be determined. Diamond's great hardness relative to other materials has been known since antiquity, and is the source of its name. This does not mean that it is infinitely hard, indestructible, or unscratchable.[^cite_note-37] Indeed, diamonds can be scratched by other diamonds[^cite_note-38] and worn down over time even by softer materials, such as vinyl [phonograph records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonograph_record).[^cite_note-39]

Diamond hardness depends on its purity, crystalline perfection, and orientation: hardness is higher for flawless, pure crystals oriented to the [<111>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_index#Case_of_cubic_structures) direction (along the longest diagonal of the cubic diamond lattice).[^cite_note-40] Therefore, whereas it might be possible to scratch some diamonds with other materials, such as [boron nitride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron_nitride), the hardest diamonds can only be scratched by other diamonds and [nanocrystalline diamond aggregates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aggregated_diamond_nanorod).

The hardness of diamond contributes to its suitability as a gemstone. Because it can only be scratched by other diamonds, it maintains its polish extremely well. Unlike many other gems, it is well-suited to daily wear because of its resistance to scratching—perhaps contributing to its popularity as the preferred gem in [engagement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engagement_ring) or [wedding rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedding_ring), which are often worn every day.

The hardest natural diamonds mostly originate from the [Copeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copeton_Dam) and [Bingara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bingara) fields located in the [New England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England_\(Australia\)) area in [New South Wales](21654.md), Australia. These diamonds are generally small, perfect to semiperfect octahedra, and are used to polish other diamonds. Their hardness is associated with the [crystal growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_growth) form, which is single-stage crystal growth. Most other diamonds show more evidence of multiple growth stages, which produce inclusions, flaws, and defect planes in the crystal lattice, all of which affect their hardness. It is possible to treat regular diamonds under a combination of high pressure and high temperature to produce diamonds that are harder than the diamonds used in hardness gauges.[^cite_note-41]

Diamonds cut glass, but this does not positively identify a diamond because other materials, such as quartz, also lie above glass on the [Mohs scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohs_scale) and can also cut it. Diamonds can scratch other diamonds, but this can result in damage to one or both stones. Hardness tests are infrequently used in practical gemology because of their potentially destructive nature.[^cite_note-read-42] The extreme hardness and high value of diamond means that gems are typically polished slowly, using painstaking traditional techniques and greater attention to detail than is the case with most other gemstones;[^cite_note-hazen-13] these tend to result in extremely flat, highly polished facets with exceptionally sharp facet edges. Diamonds also possess an extremely high refractive index and fairly high dispersion. Taken together, these factors affect the overall appearance of a polished diamond and most [diamantaires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamantaire) still rely upon skilled use of a [loupe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loupe) (magnifying glass) to identify diamonds "by eye".[^cite_note-43]

#### Toughness

Somewhat related to hardness is another mechanical property *toughness*, which is a material's ability to resist breakage from forceful impact. The [toughness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toughness) of natural diamond has been measured as 50–65 [MPa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megapascal)·m<sup>1/2</sup>.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45] This value is good compared to other ceramic materials, but poor compared to most engineering materials such as engineering alloys, which typically exhibit toughness over 80 MPa·m<sup>1/2</sup>. As with any material, the macroscopic geometry of a diamond contributes to its resistance to breakage. Diamond has a [cleavage plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleavage_plane) and is therefore more fragile in some orientations than others. [Diamond cutters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_cutting) use this attribute to cleave some stones before faceting them.[^cite_note-harlow-46] "Impact toughness" is one of the main indexes to measure the quality of synthetic industrial diamonds.

#### Yield strength

Diamond has a compressive [yield strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yield_strength) of 130–140 GPa.[^cite_note-47] This exceptionally high value, along with the hardness and transparency of diamond, are the reasons that [diamond anvil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_anvil) cells are the main tool for high pressure experiments.[^cite_note-Dubrovinsky-48] These anvils have reached pressures of 600 GPa.[^cite_note-Wogan2012-49] Much higher pressures may be possible with [nanocrystalline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanocrystalline_material) diamonds.[^cite_note-Dubrovinsky-48][^cite_note-Wogan2012-49]

#### [Elasticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_\(physics\)) and [tensile strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensile_strength)

Usually, attempting to deform bulk diamond crystal by tension or bending results in brittle fracture. However, when single crystalline diamond is in the form of micro/nanoscale wires or needles (\~100–300 nanometers in diameter, micrometers long), they can be elastically stretched by as much as 9–10 percent tensile strain without failure,[^cite_note-50] with a maximum local tensile stress of about 89–98 GPa,[^cite_note-51] very close to the theoretical limit for this material.[^cite_note-52]

### Electrical conductivity

Other specialized applications also exist or are being developed, including use as [semiconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor): some [blue diamonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_diamond) are natural semiconductors, in contrast to most diamonds, which are excellent [electrical insulators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insulator_\(electricity\)). The conductivity and blue color originate from boron impurity. Boron substitutes for carbon atoms in the diamond lattice, donating a hole into the [valence band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_band).[^cite_note-boron-53]

Substantial conductivity is commonly observed in nominally [undoped](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doping_\(semiconductor\)) diamond grown by [chemical vapor deposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_vapor_deposition_of_diamond). This conductivity is associated with [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen)-related species adsorbed at the surface, and it can be removed by [annealing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annealing_\(metallurgy\)) or other surface treatments.[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55]

Thin needles of diamond can be made to vary their electronic [band gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Band_gap) from the normal 5.6 eV to near zero by selective mechanical deformation.[^cite_note-56]

High-purity diamond wafers 5 cm in diameter exhibit perfect resistance in one direction and perfect conductance in the other, creating the possibility of using them for quantum data storage. The material contains only 3 parts per million of nitrogen. The diamond was grown on a stepped substrate, which eliminated cracking.[^cite_note-57]

### Surface property

Diamonds are naturally [lipophilic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipophilicity) and [hydrophobic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrophobe), which means the diamonds' surface cannot be wet by water, but can be easily wet and stuck by oil. This property can be utilized to extract diamonds using oil when making synthetic diamonds. However, when diamond surfaces are chemically modified with certain ions, they are expected to become so [hydrophilic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrophile) that they can stabilize multiple layers of [water ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice) at [human body temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_body_temperature).[^cite_note-58]

The surface of diamonds is partially oxidized. The oxidized surface can be reduced by heat treatment under hydrogen flow. That is to say, this heat treatment partially removes oxygen-containing functional groups. But diamonds (sp<sup>3</sup>C) are unstable against high temperature (above about 400 °C (752 °F)) under atmospheric pressure. The structure gradually changes into sp<sup>2</sup>C above this temperature. Thus, diamonds should be reduced below this temperature.[^cite_note-59]

### Chemical stability

At room temperature, diamonds do not react with any chemical reagents including strong acids and bases.

In an atmosphere of pure oxygen, diamond has an [ignition point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignition_point) that ranges from 690 °C (1,274 °F) to 840 °C (1,540 °F); smaller crystals tend to burn more easily. It increases in temperature from red to white heat and burns with a pale blue flame, and continues to burn after the source of heat is removed. By contrast, in air the combustion will cease as soon as the heat is removed because the oxygen is diluted with nitrogen. A clear, flawless, transparent diamond is completely converted to carbon dioxide; any impurities will be left as ash.[^cite_note-60] Heat generated from cutting a diamond will not ignite the diamond,[^cite_note-61] and neither will a cigarette lighter,[^cite_note-62] but house fires and blow torches are hot enough. Jewelers must be careful when molding the metal in a diamond ring.[^cite_note-63]

Diamond powder of an appropriate grain size (around 50 microns) burns with a shower of sparks after ignition from a flame. Consequently, [pyrotechnic compositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrotechnic_composition) based on [synthetic diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_diamond) powder can be prepared. The resulting sparks are of the usual red-orange color, comparable to charcoal, but show a very linear trajectory which is explained by their high density.[^cite_note-64] Diamond also reacts with fluorine gas above about 700 °C (1,292 °F).

### Color

Main article: [Diamond color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_color)

A museum display of jewelry items. Three brooches each consist of a large brown central gem surrounded by many clear small stones. A necklace has a large brown gem at its bottom and its string is all covered with small clear gems. A cluster-shaped decoration contains many brown gems.

Brown diamonds at the [National Museum of Natural History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Natural_History) in [Washington, D.C.](108956.md)

Picture of a diamond

The most famous colored diamond, the [Hope Diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hope_Diamond)

Diamond has a wide [band gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Band_gap) of 5.5 [eV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronvolt) corresponding to the deep [ultraviolet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet) wavelength of 225 nanometers. This means that pure diamond should transmit visible light and appear as a clear colorless crystal. Colors in diamond originate from lattice defects and impurities. The diamond crystal lattice is exceptionally strong, and only atoms of [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen), [boron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron), and [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) can be introduced into diamond during the growth at significant concentrations (up to atomic percents). Transition metals [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel) and [cobalt](24580536.md), which are commonly used for growth of synthetic diamond by high-pressure high-temperature techniques, have been detected in diamond as individual atoms; the maximum concentration is 0.01% for nickel[^cite_note-65] and even less for cobalt. Virtually any element can be introduced to diamond by ion implantation.[^cite_note-66]

Nitrogen is by far the most common impurity found in gem diamonds and is responsible for the yellow and brown color in diamonds. Boron is responsible for the blue color.[^cite_note-67] Color in diamond has two additional sources: irradiation (usually by alpha particles), that causes the color in green diamonds, and [plastic deformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_deformation) of the diamond crystal lattice. Plastic deformation is the cause of color in some brown[^cite_note-68] and perhaps pink and red diamonds.[^cite_note-69] In order of increasing rarity, yellow diamond is followed by brown, colorless, then by blue, green, black, pink, orange, purple, and red.[^cite_note-harlow-46] "Black", or [carbonado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonado), diamonds are not truly black, but rather contain numerous dark inclusions that give the gems their dark appearance. Colored diamonds contain impurities or structural defects that cause the coloration, while pure or nearly pure diamonds are transparent and colorless. Most diamond impurities replace a carbon atom in the [crystal lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_lattice), known as a [carbon flaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_flaw). The most common impurity, nitrogen, causes a slight to intense yellow coloration depending upon the type and concentration of nitrogen present.[^cite_note-harlow-46] The [Gemological Institute of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemological_Institute_of_America) (GIA) classifies low saturation yellow and brown diamonds as diamonds in the *normal color range*, and applies a grading scale from "D" (colorless) to "Z" (light yellow). Yellow diamonds of high color saturation or a different color, such as pink or blue, are called *fancy colored* diamonds and fall under a different grading scale.[^cite_note-harlow-46]

In 2008, the [Wittelsbach Diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wittelsbach_Diamond), a 35.56-carat (7.112 g) [blue diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_diamond) once belonging to the King of Spain, fetched over US\$24 million at a Christie's auction.[^cite_note-70] In May 2009, a 7.03-carat (1.406 g) blue diamond fetched the highest price per carat ever paid for a diamond when it was sold at auction for 10.5 million Swiss francs (6.97 million euros, or US\$9.5 million at the time).[^cite_note-71] That record was, however, beaten the same year: a 5-carat (1.0 g) vivid pink diamond was sold for US\$10.8 million in Hong Kong on December 1, 2009.[^cite_note-72]

### Clarity

Clarity is one of the 4C's (color, clarity, cut and carat weight) that helps in identifying the quality of diamonds. The [Gemological Institute of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemological_Institute_of_America) (GIA) developed 11 clarity scales to decide the quality of a diamond for its sale value. The GIA clarity scale spans from Flawless (FL) to included (I) having internally flawless (IF), very, very slightly included (VVS), very slightly included (VS) and slightly included (SI) in between. Impurities in natural diamonds are due to the presence of natural minerals and oxides. The clarity scale grades the diamond based on the color, size, location of impurity and quantity of clarity visible under 10x magnification.[^cite_note-73] Inclusions in diamond can be extracted by optical methods. The process is to take pre-enhancement images, identifying the inclusion removal part and finally removing the diamond facets and noises.[^cite_note-74]

### Fluorescence



Necklace of rough light brown diamonds under [UV light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blacklight) (top) and normal light (bottom)

Between 25% and 35% of natural diamonds exhibit some degree of fluorescence when examined under invisible long-wave ultraviolet light or higher energy radiation sources such as X-rays and lasers.[^cite_note-75] Incandescent lighting will not cause a diamond to fluoresce. Diamonds can fluoresce in a variety of colors including blue (most common), orange, yellow, white, green and very rarely red and purple. Although the causes are not well understood, variations in the atomic structure, such as the number of nitrogen atoms present are thought to contribute to the phenomenon.

### Thermal conductivity

Diamonds can be identified by their high thermal conductivity (900–2320 W·m<sup>−1</sup>·K<sup>−1</sup>).[^cite_note-76] Their high [refractive index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refractive_index) is also indicative, but other materials have similar refractivity.

## Geology

Diamonds are extremely rare, with concentrations of at most parts per billion in source rock.[^cite_note-Cartigny-33] Before the 20th century, most diamonds were found in [alluvial deposits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alluvial_deposit). Loose diamonds are also found along existing and ancient [shorelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shore), where they tend to accumulate because of their size and density.[^cite_note-AMNH-77]<sup>: 149 </sup> Rarely, they have been found in [glacial till](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glacial_till) (notably in [Wisconsin](33127.md) and [Indiana](21883857.md)), but these deposits are not of commercial quality.[^cite_note-AMNH-77]<sup>: 19 </sup> These types of deposit were derived from localized igneous [intrusions](1106531.md) through [weathering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weathering) and [transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sediment_transport) by [wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind) or [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water).[^cite_note-Shirey2013-78]

Most diamonds come from the [Earth's mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantle_\(geology\)), and most of this section discusses those diamonds. However, there are other sources. Some blocks of the crust, or [terranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrane), have been buried deep enough as the crust thickened so they experienced [ultra-high-pressure metamorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultra-high-pressure_metamorphism). These have evenly distributed *microdiamonds* that show no sign of transport by magma. In addition, when meteorites strike the ground, the shock wave can produce high enough temperatures and pressures for *microdiamonds* and *[nanodiamonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detonation_nanodiamond)* to form.[^cite_note-Shirey2013-78] Impact-type microdiamonds can be used as an indicator of ancient impact craters.[^cite_note-79] [Popigai impact structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popigai_impact_structure) in Russia may have the world's largest diamond deposit, estimated at trillions of carats, and formed by an asteroid impact.[^cite_note-80]

A common misconception is that diamonds form from highly compressed [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal). Coal is formed from buried prehistoric plants, and most diamonds that have been dated are far older than the first [land plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embryophyte). It is possible that diamonds can form from coal in [subduction zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subduction_zone), but diamonds formed in this way are rare, and the carbon source is more likely [carbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonate) rocks and organic carbon in sediments, rather than coal.[^cite_note-81][^cite_note-82]

### Surface distribution



[Geologic provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologic_province) of the world. The pink and orange areas are [shields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shield_\(geology\)) and [platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platform_\(geology\)), which together constitute cratons.

Diamonds are far from evenly distributed over the Earth. A rule of thumb known as Clifford's rule states that they are almost always found in [kimberlites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimberlite) on the oldest part of [cratons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craton), the stable cores of continents with typical ages of 2.5 billion years or more.[^cite_note-Shirey2013-78][^cite_note-83]<sup>: 314 </sup> However, there are exceptions. The [Argyle diamond mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argyle_diamond_mine) in [Australia](4689264.md), the largest producer of diamonds by weight in the world, is located in a *mobile belt*, also known as an *[orogenic belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orogeny)*,[^cite_note-84] a weaker zone surrounding the central craton that has undergone compressional tectonics. Instead of kimberlite, the host rock is [lamproite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamproite). Lamproites with diamonds that are not economically viable are also found in the United States, India, and Australia.[^cite_note-Shirey2013-78] In addition, diamonds in the [Wawa belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algoman_orogeny) of the [Superior province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superior_craton) in [Canada](5042916.md) and microdiamonds in the [island arc of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeastern_Japan_arc) are found in a type of rock called [lamprophyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamprophyre).[^cite_note-Shirey2013-78]

[Kimberlites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimberlite) can be found in narrow (1 to 4 meters) dikes and sills, and in pipes with diameters that range from about 75 m to 1.5 km. Fresh rock is dark bluish green to greenish gray, but after exposure rapidly turns brown and crumbles.[^cite_note-85] It is hybrid rock with a chaotic mixture of small minerals and rock fragments ([clasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clastic_rock)) up to the size of watermelons. They are a mixture of [xenocrysts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenocryst) and [xenoliths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenolith) (minerals and rocks carried up from the lower crust and mantle), pieces of surface rock, altered minerals such as [serpentine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serpentine_subgroup), and new minerals that crystallized during the eruption. The texture varies with depth. The composition forms a continuum with [carbonatites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonatite), but the latter have too much oxygen for carbon to exist in a pure form. Instead, it is locked up in the mineral [calcite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcite) ([Ca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium)[C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon)[O](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen)<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub>).[^cite_note-Shirey2013-78]

All three of the diamond-bearing rocks (kimberlite, lamproite and lamprophyre) lack certain minerals ([melilite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melilite) and [kalsilite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalsilite)) that are incompatible with diamond formation. In [kimberlite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimberlite), [olivine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivine) is large and conspicuous, while lamproite has Ti-[phlogopite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phlogopite) and lamprophyre has [biotite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biotite) and [amphibole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphibole). They are all derived from magma types that erupt rapidly from small amounts of melt, are rich in [volatiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volatility_\(chemistry\)) and [magnesium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium_oxide), and are less [oxidizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) than more common mantle melts such as [basalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basalt). These characteristics allow the melts to carry diamonds to the surface before they dissolve.[^cite_note-Shirey2013-78]

### Exploration



[Diavik Mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diavik_Mine), on an island in Lac de Gras in northern Canada

[Kimberlite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimberlite) pipes can be difficult to find. They weather quickly (within a few years after exposure) and tend to have lower topographic relief than surrounding rock. If they are visible in outcrops, the diamonds are never visible because they are so rare. In any case, kimberlites are often covered with vegetation, sediments, soils, or lakes. In modern searches, [geophysical methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysical_survey) such as [aeromagnetic surveys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeromagnetic_survey), [electrical resistivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_resistivity_tomography), and [gravimetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravimetry), help identify promising regions to explore. This is aided by isotopic dating and modeling of the geological history. Then surveyors must go to the area and collect samples, looking for kimberlite fragments or *indicator minerals*. The latter have compositions that reflect the conditions where diamonds form, such as extreme melt depletion or high pressures in [eclogites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclogite). However, indicator minerals can be misleading; a better approach is [geothermobarometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geothermobarometry), where the compositions of minerals are analyzed as if they were in equilibrium with mantle minerals.[^cite_note-Shirey2013-78]

Finding kimberlites requires persistence, and only a small fraction contain diamonds that are commercially viable. The only major discoveries since about 1980 have been in Canada. Since existing mines have lifetimes of as little as 25 years, there could be a shortage of new natural diamonds in the future.[^cite_note-Shirey2013-78]

### Ages

Diamonds are dated by analyzing inclusions using the decay of radioactive isotopes. Depending on the elemental abundances, one can look at the decay of [rubidium to strontium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubidium%E2%80%93strontium_dating), [samarium to neodymium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samarium%E2%80%93neodymium_dating), [uranium to lead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium%E2%80%93lead_dating), [argon-40 to argon-39](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argon%E2%80%93argon_dating), or [rhenium to osmium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhenium%E2%80%93osmium_dating). Those found in kimberlites have ages ranging from 1 to 3.5 billion years, and there can be multiple ages in the same kimberlite, indicating multiple episodes of diamond formation. The kimberlites themselves are much younger. Most of them have ages between tens of millions and 300 million years old, although there are some older exceptions (Argyle, [Premier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premier_Mine) and Wawa). Thus, the kimberlites formed independently of the diamonds and served only to transport them to the surface.[^cite_note-Cartigny-33][^cite_note-Shirey2013-78] Kimberlites are also much younger than the cratons they have erupted through. The reason for the lack of older kimberlites is unknown, but it suggests there was some change in mantle chemistry or tectonics. No kimberlite has erupted in human history.[^cite_note-Shirey2013-78]

### Origin in mantle



[Eclogite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclogite) with centimeter-size [garnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garnet) crystals



Red garnet inclusion in a diamond

[^cite_note-DCOdecadal-86]

Most gem-quality diamonds come from depths of 150–250 km in the [lithosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithosphere). Such depths occur below cratons in *mantle keels*, the thickest part of the lithosphere. These regions have high enough pressure and temperature to allow diamonds to form and they are not convecting, so diamonds can be stored for billions of years until a kimberlite eruption samples them.[^cite_note-Shirey2013-78]

Host rocks in a mantle keel include [harzburgite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harzburgite) and [lherzolite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lherzolite), two type of [peridotite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peridotite). The most dominant rock type in the [upper mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_mantle_\(Earth\)), peridotite is an [igneous rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igneous_rock) consisting mostly of the minerals [olivine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivine) and [pyroxene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroxene); it is low in [silica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_dioxide) and high in [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium). However, diamonds in peridotite rarely survive the trip to the surface.[^cite_note-Shirey2013-78] Another common source that does keep diamonds intact is [eclogite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclogite), a [metamorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamorphic) rock that typically forms from [basalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basalt) as an oceanic plate plunges into the mantle at a [subduction zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subduction).[^cite_note-Cartigny-33]

A smaller fraction of diamonds (about 150 have been studied) come from depths of 330–660 km, a region that includes the [transition zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_zone_\(Earth\)). They formed in eclogite but are distinguished from diamonds of shallower origin by inclusions of [majorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majorite) (a form of [garnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garnet) with excess silicon). A similar proportion of diamonds comes from the lower mantle at depths between 660 and 800 km.[^cite_note-Cartigny-33]

Diamond is thermodynamically stable at high pressures and temperatures, with the phase transition from [graphite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphite) occurring at greater temperatures as the pressure increases. Thus, underneath continents it becomes stable at temperatures of 950 degrees Celsius and pressures of 4.5 gigapascals, corresponding to depths of 150 kilometers or greater. In subduction zones, which are colder, it becomes stable at temperatures of 800 °C and pressures of 3.5 gigapascals. At depths greater than 240 km, iron–nickel metal phases are present and carbon is likely to be either dissolved in them or in the form of [carbides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbide). Thus, the deeper origin of some diamonds may reflect unusual growth environments.[^cite_note-Cartigny-33][^cite_note-Shirey2013-78]

In 2018 the first known natural samples of a phase of ice called [Ice VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_VII) were found as inclusions in diamond samples. The inclusions formed at depths between 400 and 800 km, straddling the upper and lower mantle, and provide evidence for water-rich fluid at these depths.[^cite_note-87][^cite_note-Perkins-88]

### Carbon sources

The mantle has roughly one billion [gigatonnes](31185.md) of carbon (for comparison, the atmosphere-ocean system has about 44,000 gigatonnes).[^cite_note-89] Carbon has two [stable isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_nuclide), [<sup>12</sup>C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon-12) and [<sup>13</sup>C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon-13), in a ratio of approximately 99\:1 by mass.[^cite_note-Shirey2013-78] This ratio has a wide range in meteorites, which implies that it also varied a lot in the early Earth. It can also be altered by surface processes like [photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis). The fraction is generally compared to a standard sample using a ratio [δ<sup>13</sup>C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopic_signature#Carbon_isotopes) expressed in parts per thousand. Common rocks from the mantle such as basalts, carbonatites, and kimberlites have ratios between −8 and −2. On the surface, organic sediments have an average of −25 while carbonates have an average of 0.[^cite_note-Cartigny-33]

Populations of diamonds from different sources have distributions of δ<sup>13</sup>C that vary markedly. Peridotitic diamonds are mostly within the typical mantle range; eclogitic diamonds have values from −40 to +3, although the peak of the distribution is in the mantle range. This variability implies that they are not formed from carbon that is *primordial* (having resided in the mantle since the Earth formed). Instead, they are the result of tectonic processes, although (given the ages of diamonds) not necessarily the same tectonic processes that act in the present.[^cite_note-Shirey2013-78] Diamond-forming carbon originates in the top 700 kilometers (430 mi) or so of the upper mantle closest to the surface, known as the [asthenosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asthenosphere).[^cite_note-Cartigny-33]

### Formation and growth



Age zones in a diamond

[^cite_note-DCOdecadal-86]

Diamonds in the mantle form through a *[metasomatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metasomatism)* process where a C–O–H–N–S fluid or melt dissolves minerals in a rock and replaces them with new minerals. (The vague term C–O–H–N–S is commonly used because the exact composition is not known.) Diamonds form from this fluid either by reduction of oxidized carbon (e.g., CO<sub>2</sub> or CO<sub>3</sub>) or oxidation of a reduced phase such as [methane](18582230.md).[^cite_note-Cartigny-33]

Using probes such as polarized light, [photoluminescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoluminescence), and [cathodoluminescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathodoluminescence), a series of growth zones can be identified in diamonds. The characteristic pattern in diamonds from the lithosphere involves a nearly concentric series of zones with very thin oscillations in luminescence and alternating episodes where the carbon is resorbed by the fluid and then grown again. Diamonds from below the lithosphere have a more irregular, almost polycrystalline texture, reflecting the higher temperatures and pressures as well as the transport of the diamonds by convection.[^cite_note-Shirey2013-78]

### Transport to the surface



Diagram of a volcanic pipe

Geological evidence supports a model in which kimberlite magma rises at 4–20 meters per second, creating an upward path by [hydraulic fracturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic_fracturing) of the rock. As the pressure decreases, a vapor phase [exsolves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exsolution) from the magma, and this helps to keep the magma fluid. At the surface, the initial eruption explodes out through fissures at high speeds (over 200 m/s (450 mph)). Then, at lower pressures, the rock is eroded, forming a pipe and producing fragmented rock ([breccia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breccia)). As the eruption wanes, there is [pyroclastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroclastic_rock) phase and then metamorphism and hydration produces [serpentinites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serpentinite).[^cite_note-Shirey2013-78]

### Double diamonds

In rare cases, diamonds have been found that contain a cavity within which is a second diamond. The first double diamond, the [Matryoshka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matryoshka_\(diamond\)), was found by [Alrosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alrosa) in [Yakutia](57905.md), Russia, in 2019.[^cite_note-90] Another one was found in the [Ellendale Diamond Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellendale_Diamond_Field) in [Western Australia](33613.md) in 2021.[^cite_note-91]

### In space

Main article: [Extraterrestrial diamonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraterrestrial_diamonds)

Although diamonds on [Earth](9228.md) are rare, they are very common in space. In [meteorites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorite), about three percent of the carbon is in the form of [nanodiamonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanodiamond), having diameters of a few nanometers. Sufficiently small diamonds can form in the cold of space because their lower [surface energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_energy) makes them more stable than graphite. The isotopic signatures of some nanodiamonds indicate they were formed outside the Solar System in stars.[^cite_note-92]

High pressure experiments predict that large quantities of diamonds condense from [methane](18582230.md) into a "diamond rain" on the ice giant planets [Uranus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranus) and [Neptune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptune).[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94][^cite_note-95] Some extrasolar planets may be almost entirely composed of diamond.[^cite_note-96]

Diamonds may exist in carbon-rich stars, particularly [white dwarfs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_dwarf). One theory for the origin of [carbonado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonado), the toughest form of diamond, is that it originated in a white dwarf or [supernova](27680.md).[^cite_note-97][^cite_note-98] Diamonds formed in stars may have been the first minerals.[^cite_note-99]

## Industry

See also: [Diamonds as an investment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamonds_as_an_investment), [List of countries by diamond production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_diamond_production), and [Clean Diamond Trade Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_Diamond_Trade_Act)

A clear faceted gem supported in four clamps attached to a wedding ring

A round [brilliant cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brilliant_\(diamond_cut\)) diamond set in a ring

The most familiar uses of diamonds today are as gemstones used for [adornment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adornment), and as industrial abrasives for cutting hard materials. The markets for gem-grade and industrial-grade diamonds value diamonds differently.

### Gem-grade diamonds

Main article: [Diamond (gemstone)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_\(gemstone\))

The [dispersion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispersion_\(optics\)) of white light into [spectral colors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_color) is the primary gemological characteristic of gem diamonds. In the 20th century, experts in gemology developed methods of grading diamonds and other gemstones based on the characteristics most important to their value as a gem. Four characteristics, known informally as the *four Cs*, are now commonly used as the basic descriptors of diamonds: these are its mass in *[carats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carat_\(unit\))* (a carat being equal to 0.2 grams), *[cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_cut)* (quality of the cut is graded according to [proportions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspect_ratio), [symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry) and [polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polishing)), *[color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_color)* (how close to white or colorless; for fancy diamonds how intense is its hue), and *[clarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_clarity)* (how free is it from [inclusions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusion_\(mineral\))). A large, flawless diamond is known as a [paragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paragon_\(diamond\)).[^cite_note-100]

A large trade in gem-grade diamonds exists. Although most gem-grade diamonds are sold newly polished, there is a well-established market for resale of polished diamonds (e.g. pawnbroking, auctions, second-hand jewelry stores, diamantaires, bourses, etc.). One hallmark of the trade in gem-quality diamonds is its remarkable concentration: wholesale trade and diamond cutting is limited to just a few locations; in 2003, 92% of the world's diamonds were cut and polished in [Surat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surat), [India](14533.md).[^cite_note-101] Other important centers of diamond cutting and trading are the [Antwerp diamond district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerp_diamond_district) in [Belgium](3343.md), where the [International Gemological Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Gemological_Institute) is based, [London](17867.md), the [Diamond District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_District) in [New York City](645042.md), the [Diamond Exchange District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Exchange_District) in [Tel Aviv](31453.md) and [Amsterdam](844.md). One contributory factor is the geological nature of diamond deposits: several large primary kimberlite-pipe mines each account for significant portions of market share (such as the [Jwaneng mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jwaneng_diamond_mine) in Botswana, which is a single large-pit mine that can produce between 12,500,000 and 15,000,000 carats (2,500 and 3,000 kg) of diamonds per year[^cite_note-102]).

The production and distribution of diamonds is largely consolidated in the hands of a few key players, and concentrated in traditional diamond trading centers, the most important being Antwerp, where 80% of all [rough diamonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rough_diamond), 50% of all cut diamonds and more than 50% of all rough, cut and industrial diamonds combined are handled.[^cite_note-India-103] This makes Antwerp a de facto "world diamond capital".[^cite_note-104] The city of Antwerp also hosts the [Antwerpsche Diamantkring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerpsche_Diamantkring), created in 1929 to become the first and biggest diamond bourse dedicated to rough diamonds.[^cite_note-105] Another important diamond center is New York City, where almost 80% of the world's diamonds are sold, including auction sales.[^cite_note-India-103]

The [De Beers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Beers) company, as the world's largest diamond mining company, holds a dominant position in the industry, and has done so since soon after its founding in 1888 by the British businessman [Cecil Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecil_Rhodes). De Beers is currently the world's largest operator of diamond production facilities (mines) and [distribution channels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_\(business\)) for gem-quality diamonds. The Diamond Trading Company (DTC) is a subsidiary of De Beers and markets rough diamonds from De Beers-operated mines. De Beers and its subsidiaries own mines that produce some 40% of annual world diamond production. For most of the 20th century over 80% of the world's rough diamonds passed through De Beers,[^cite_note-106] but by 2001–2009 the figure had decreased to around 45%,[^cite_note-107] and by 2013 the company's market share had further decreased to around 38% in value terms and even less by volume.[^cite_note-idexonline-108] De Beers sold off the vast majority of its diamond stockpile in the late 1990s – early 2000s[^cite_note-109] and the remainder largely represents working stock (diamonds that are being sorted before sale).[^cite_note-110] This was well documented in the press[^cite_note-111] but remains little known to the general public.

As a part of reducing its influence, De Beers withdrew from purchasing diamonds on the open market in 1999 and ceased, at the end of 2008, purchasing Russian diamonds mined by the largest Russian diamond company [Alrosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alrosa).[^cite_note-112] As of January 2011, De Beers states that it only sells diamonds from the following four countries: Botswana, Namibia, South Africa and Canada.[^cite_note-113] Apart from Alrosa, other important diamond mining companies include [BHP](200825.md), which is the world's largest mining company;[^cite_note-114] [Rio Tinto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rio_Tinto_\(corporation\)), the owner of the [Argyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argyle_diamond_mine) (100%), [Diavik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diavik_Diamond_Mine) (60%), and [Murowa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murowa_diamond_mine) (78%) diamond mines;[^cite_note-115] and [Petra Diamonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petra_Diamonds), the owner of several major diamond mines in Africa.



Diamond polisher in Amsterdam

Further down the supply chain, members of The [World Federation of Diamond Bourses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Federation_of_Diamond_Bourses) (WFDB) act as a medium for wholesale diamond exchange, trading both polished and rough diamonds. The WFDB consists of independent diamond bourses in major cutting centers such as Tel Aviv, Antwerp, Johannesburg and other cities across the US, Europe and Asia.[^cite_note-harlow-46] In 2000, the WFDB and The International Diamond Manufacturers Association established the [World Diamond Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Diamond_Council) to prevent the trading of diamonds used to fund war and inhumane acts. WFDB's additional activities include sponsoring the [World Diamond Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Diamond_Congress) every two years, as well as the establishment of the [International Diamond Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Diamond_Council) (IDC) to oversee diamond grading.[^cite_note-116]

Once purchased by Sightholders (which is a trademark term referring to the companies that have a three-year supply contract with DTC), diamonds are cut and polished in preparation for sale as gemstones ('industrial' stones are regarded as a by-product of the gemstone market; they are used for abrasives).[^cite_note-polish-117] The cutting and polishing of rough diamonds is a specialized skill that is concentrated in a limited number of locations worldwide.[^cite_note-polish-117] Traditional diamond cutting centers are Antwerp, [Amsterdam](844.md), Johannesburg, New York City, and Tel Aviv. Recently, diamond cutting centers have been established in China, India, [Thailand](30128.md), Namibia and Botswana.[^cite_note-polish-117] Cutting centers with lower cost of labor, notably Surat in [Gujarat, India](53707.md), handle a larger number of smaller carat diamonds, while smaller quantities of larger or more valuable diamonds are more likely to be handled in Europe or North America. The recent expansion of this industry in India, employing low cost labor, has allowed smaller diamonds to be prepared as gems in greater quantities than was previously economically feasible.[^cite_note-India-103]

Diamonds prepared as gemstones are sold on diamond exchanges called *[bourses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_\(organized_market\))*. There are 28 registered diamond bourses in the world.[^cite_note-118] Bourses are the final tightly controlled step in the diamond supply chain; wholesalers and even retailers are able to buy relatively small lots of diamonds at the bourses, after which they are prepared for final sale to the consumer. Diamonds can be sold already set in jewelry, or sold unset ("loose"). According to the Rio Tinto, in 2002 the diamonds produced and released to the market were valued at US\$9 billion as rough diamonds, US\$14 billion after being cut and polished, US\$28 billion in wholesale diamond jewelry, and US\$57 billion in retail sales.[^cite_note-119]

#### Cutting

Main articles: [Diamond cutting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_cutting) and [Diamond cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_cut)

A large rectangular pink multifaceted gemstone, set in a decorative surround. The decoration includes a row of small clear faceted gemstones around the main gem's perimeter, and clusters of gems forming a crest on one side. The crest comprises a three-pointed crown faced by two unidentifiable animals.

The [Daria-i-Noor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daria-i-Noor) Diamond—an example of unusual diamond cut and jewelry arrangement.

Mined rough diamonds are converted into gems through a multi-step process called "cutting". Diamonds are extremely hard, but also brittle and can be split up by a single blow. Therefore, diamond cutting is traditionally considered as a delicate procedure requiring skills, scientific knowledge, tools and experience. Its final goal is to produce a faceted jewel where the specific angles between the facets would optimize the diamond luster, that is dispersion of white light, whereas the number and area of facets would determine the weight of the final product. The weight reduction upon cutting is significant and can be of the order of 50%.[^cite_note-x50-120] Several possible shapes are considered, but the final decision is often determined not only by scientific, but also practical considerations. For example, the diamond might be intended for display or for wear, in a ring or a necklace, singled or surrounded by other gems of certain color and shape.[^cite_note-antique-121] Some of them may be considered as classical, such as [round](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_cut), [pear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pear_Cut_Diamond), [marquise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marquise_Cut_Diamond), [oval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oval_Cut_Diamond), [hearts and arrows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearts_and_arrows) diamonds, etc. Some of them are special, produced by certain companies, for example, [Phoenix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Cut_Diamond?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Cushion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cushion_Cut_Diamond?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Sole Mio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sole_Mio_Cut_Diamond?action=edit\&redlink=1) diamonds, etc.[^cite_note-122]

The most time-consuming part of the cutting is the preliminary analysis of the rough stone. It needs to address a large number of issues, bears much responsibility, and therefore can last years in case of unique diamonds. The following issues are considered:

- The hardness of diamond and its ability to cleave strongly depend on the crystal orientation. Therefore, the crystallographic structure of the diamond to be cut is analyzed using [X-ray diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_diffraction) to choose the optimal cutting directions.

- Most diamonds contain visible non-diamond inclusions and crystal flaws. The cutter has to decide which flaws are to be removed by the cutting and which could be kept.

- Splitting a diamond with a hammer is difficult, a well-calculated, angled blow can cut the diamond, piece-by-piece, but it can also ruin the diamond itself. Alternatively, it can be cut with a [diamond saw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_saw), which is a more reliable method.

  [^cite_note-antique-121]

  [^cite_note-123]

After initial cutting, the diamond is shaped in numerous stages of polishing. Unlike cutting, which is a responsible but quick operation, polishing removes material by gradual erosion and is extremely time-consuming. The associated technique is well developed; it is considered as a routine and can be performed by technicians.[^cite_note-124] After polishing, the diamond is reexamined for possible flaws, either remaining or induced by the process. Those flaws are concealed through various [diamond enhancement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_enhancement) techniques, such as repolishing, crack filling, or clever arrangement of the stone in the jewelry. Remaining non-diamond inclusions are removed through laser drilling and filling of the voids produced.[^cite_note-read-42]

#### Marketing



Diamond balance scale 0.01–25 carat jeweler's measuring tool

Marketing has significantly affected the image of diamond as a valuable commodity.

[N. W. Ayer & Son](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N._W._Ayer_&_Son), the advertising firm retained by [De Beers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Beers) in the mid-20th century, succeeded in reviving the American diamond market and the firm created new markets in countries where no diamond tradition had existed before. N. W. Ayer's marketing included [product placement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_placement), advertising focused on the diamond product itself rather than the De Beers brand, and associations with celebrities and royalty. Without advertising the De Beers brand, De Beers was advertising its competitors' diamond products as well,[^cite_note-125] but this was not a concern as De Beers dominated the diamond market throughout the 20th century. De Beers' market share dipped temporarily to second place in the global market below Alrosa in the aftermath of the global economic crisis of 2008, down to less than 29% in terms of carats mined, rather than sold.[^cite_note-jckonline.com-126] The campaign lasted for decades but was effectively discontinued by early 2011. De Beers still advertises diamonds, but the advertising now mostly promotes its own brands, or licensed product lines, rather than completely "generic" diamond products.[^cite_note-jckonline.com-126] The campaign was perhaps best captured by the slogan "[a diamond is forever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_diamond_is_forever)".[^cite_note-sell-10] This slogan is now being used by De Beers Diamond Jewelers,[^cite_note-127] a jewelry firm which is a 50/50% joint venture between the De Beers mining company and [LVMH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LVMH), the luxury goods conglomerate.

Brown-colored diamonds constituted a significant part of the diamond production, and were predominantly used for industrial purposes. They were seen as worthless for jewelry (not even being assessed on the [diamond color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_color) scale). After the development of Argyle diamond mine in Australia in 1986, and marketing, brown diamonds have become acceptable gems.[^cite_note-128][^cite_note-129] The change was mostly due to the numbers: the Argyle mine, with its 35,000,000 carats (7,000 kg) of diamonds per year, makes about one-third of global production of natural diamonds;[^cite_note-130] 80% of Argyle diamonds are brown.[^cite_note-131]

### Industrial-grade diamonds

A diamond scalpel consisting of a yellow diamond blade attached to a pen-shaped holder

A [scalpel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalpel) with synthetic diamond blade

A polished metal blade embedded with small diamonds

Close-up photograph of an [angle grinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_grinder) blade with tiny diamonds shown embedded in the metal



A diamond knife blade used for cutting ultrathin sections (typically 70 to 350 nm) for transmission [electron microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_microscopy)

Industrial diamonds are valued mostly for their hardness and thermal conductivity, making many of the gemological characteristics of diamonds, such as the [4 Cs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4_Cs), irrelevant for most applications. Eighty percent of mined diamonds (equal to about 135,000,000 carats (27,000 kg) annually) are unsuitable for use as gemstones and are used industrially.[^cite_note-132] In addition to mined diamonds, synthetic diamonds found industrial applications almost immediately after their invention in the 1950s; in 2014, 4,500,000,000 carats (900,000 kg) of synthetic diamonds were produced, 90% of which were produced in China. Approximately 90% of diamond [grinding grit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grinding_\(abrasive_cutting\)) is currently of synthetic origin.[^cite_note-usgs-133]

The boundary between gem-quality diamonds and industrial diamonds is poorly defined and partly depends on market conditions (for example, if demand for polished diamonds is high, some lower-grade stones will be polished into low-quality or small gemstones rather than being sold for industrial use). Within the category of industrial diamonds, there is a sub-category comprising the lowest-quality, mostly opaque stones, which are known as [bort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bort).[^cite_note-spear-134]

Industrial use of diamonds has historically been associated with their hardness, which makes diamond the ideal material for cutting and grinding tools. As the hardest known naturally occurring material, diamond can be used to polish, cut, or wear away any material, including other diamonds. Common industrial applications of this property include diamond-tipped [drill bits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drill_bit) and saws, and the use of diamond powder as an [abrasive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrasive). Less expensive industrial-grade diamonds (bort) with more flaws and poorer color than gems, are used for such purposes.[^cite_note-135] Diamond is not suitable for machining [ferrous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrous) [alloys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alloy) at high speeds, as carbon is soluble in iron at the high temperatures created by high-speed machining, leading to greatly increased wear on diamond tools compared to alternatives.[^cite_note-136]

Specialized applications include use in laboratories as containment for [high-pressure experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_experiment) (see [diamond anvil cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_anvil_cell)), high-performance [bearings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bearing_\(mechanical\)), and limited use in specialized [windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Window).[^cite_note-spear-134] With the continuing advances being made in the production of synthetic diamonds, future applications are becoming feasible. The high [thermal conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_conductivity) of diamond makes it suitable as a [heat sink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_sink) for integrated circuits in [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics).[^cite_note-137]

### Mining

See also: [List of diamond mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_diamond_mines) and [Exploration diamond drilling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration_diamond_drilling)

Approximately 130,000,000 carats (26,000 kg) of diamonds are mined annually, with a total value of nearly US\$9 billion, and about 100,000 kg (220,000 lb) are synthesized annually.[^cite_note-yarnell-138]

Roughly 49% of diamonds originate from [Central](539606.md) and [Southern Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Africa), although significant sources of the mineral have been discovered in [Canada](5042916.md), [India](14533.md), [Russia](25391.md), [Brazil](3383.md), and [Australia](4689264.md).[^cite_note-usgs-133] They are mined from kimberlite and lamproite volcanic pipes, which can bring diamond crystals, originating from deep within the Earth where high pressures and temperatures enable them to form, to the surface. The mining and distribution of natural diamonds are subjects of frequent controversy such as concerns over the sale of *[blood diamonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_diamond)* or *conflict diamonds* by African [paramilitary](146730.md) groups.[^cite_note-conflict-139] The diamond supply chain is controlled by a limited number of powerful businesses, and is also highly concentrated in a small number of locations around the world.

Only a very small fraction of the diamond ore consists of actual diamonds. The ore is crushed, during which care is required not to destroy larger diamonds, and then sorted by density. Today, diamonds are located in the diamond-rich density fraction with the help of [X-ray fluorescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_fluorescence), after which the final sorting steps are done by hand. Before the use of [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray) became commonplace,[^cite_note-x50-120][^cite_note-140] the separation was done with grease belts; diamonds have a stronger tendency to stick to grease than the other minerals in the ore.[^cite_note-harlow-46]



[Siberia](29639.md)'s Udachnaya diamond mine

A clear octahedral stone protrudes from a black rock

The slightly misshapen octahedral shape of this rough diamond crystal in matrix is typical of the mineral. Its lustrous faces also indicate that this crystal is from a primary deposit

Historically, diamonds were found only in [alluvial deposits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alluvial_deposit) in [Guntur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guntur_district) and [Krishna district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krishna_district) of the [Krishna River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krishna_River) delta in [Southern India](678583.md).[^cite_note-141] India led the world in diamond production from the time of their discovery in approximately the 9th century BC[^cite_note-hershey-7][^cite_note-142] to the mid-18th century AD, but the commercial potential of these sources had been exhausted by the late 18th century and at that time India was eclipsed by Brazil where the first non-Indian diamonds were found in 1725.[^cite_note-hershey-7] Currently, one of the most prominent Indian mines is located at [Panna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panna_District).[^cite_note-143]

Diamond extraction from primary deposits (kimberlites and lamproites) started in the 1870s after the discovery of the [Diamond Fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Fields) in South Africa.[^cite_note-144] Production has increased over time and now an accumulated total of 4,500,000,000 carats (900,000 kg) have been mined since that date.[^cite_note-giasummer2007-145] Twenty percent of that amount has been mined in the last five years, and during the last 10 years, nine new mines have started production; four more are waiting to be opened soon. Most of these mines are located in Canada, Zimbabwe, Angola, and one in Russia.[^cite_note-giasummer2007-145]

In the U.S., diamonds have been found in [Arkansas](1930.md), [Colorado](5399.md), [New Mexico](21649.md), Wyoming, and [Montana](19978.md).[^cite_note-DGemGLorenz-146][^cite_note-Montana-147] In 2004, the discovery of a microscopic diamond in the U.S. led to the January 2008 bulk-sampling of [kimberlite pipes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimberlite_pipe) in a remote part of Montana. The [Crater of Diamonds State Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crater_of_Diamonds_State_Park) in Arkansas is open to the public, and is the only mine in the world where members of the public can dig for diamonds.[^cite_note-Montana-147]

Today, most commercially viable diamond deposits are in Russia (mostly in [Sakha Republic](57905.md), for example [Mir pipe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mir_Mine) and [Udachnaya pipe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Udachnaya_pipe)), [Botswana](3464.md), Australia ([Northern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Australia) and [Western Australia](33613.md)) and the [Democratic Republic of the Congo](76762.md).[^cite_note-148] In 2005, Russia produced almost one-fifth of the global diamond output, according to the [British Geological Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Geological_Survey). Australia boasts the richest diamantiferous pipe, with production from the Argyle diamond mine reaching peak levels of 42 metric tons per year in the 1990s.[^cite_note-DGemGLorenz-146][^cite_note-149] There are also commercial deposits being actively mined in the [Northwest Territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Territories) of Canada and Brazil.[^cite_note-usgs-133] Diamond prospectors continue to search the globe for diamond-bearing kimberlite and lamproite pipes.

#### Political issues

Main articles: [Kimberley Process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimberley_Process), [Blood diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_diamond), and [Child labour in the diamond industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_labour_in_the_diamond_industry)

Unsustainable diamond mining in Sierra Leone. Documentary as part of the Vrinda Project for Wikibooks

In some of the more politically unstable [central African](539606.md) and [west African](67393.md) countries, revolutionary groups have taken control of [diamond mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_diamond_mines), using proceeds from diamond sales to finance their operations. Diamonds sold through this process are known as *conflict diamonds* or *blood diamonds*.[^cite_note-conflict-139]

In response to public concerns that their diamond purchases were contributing to war and [human rights abuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_abuses), the [United Nations](31769.md), the diamond industry and diamond-trading nations introduced the [Kimberley Process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimberley_Process) in 2002.[^cite_note-kimb-150] The Kimberley Process aims to ensure that conflict diamonds do not become intermixed with the diamonds not controlled by such rebel groups. This is done by requiring diamond-producing countries to provide proof that the money they make from selling the diamonds is not used to fund criminal or revolutionary activities. Although the Kimberley Process has been moderately successful in limiting the number of conflict diamonds entering the market, some still find their way in. According to the International Diamond Manufacturers Association, conflict diamonds constitute 2–3% of all diamonds traded.[^cite_note-151] Two major flaws still hinder the effectiveness of the Kimberley Process: (1) the relative ease of smuggling diamonds across African borders, and (2) the violent nature of diamond mining in nations that are not in a technical state of war and whose diamonds are therefore considered "clean".[^cite_note-kimb-150]

The Canadian Government has set up a body known as the Canadian Diamond Code of Conduct[^cite_note-152] to help authenticate Canadian diamonds. This is a stringent tracking system of diamonds and helps protect the "conflict free" label of Canadian diamonds.[^cite_note-153]

Mineral resource exploitation in general causes irreversible environmental damage, which must be weighed against the socio-economic benefits to a country.[^cite_note-154]

## Synthetics, simulants, and enhancements

### Synthetics

Main article: [Synthetic diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_diamond)

Synthetic diamonds are diamonds manufactured in a laboratory, as opposed to diamonds mined from the Earth. The gemological and industrial uses of diamond have created a large demand for rough stones. This demand has been satisfied in large part by synthetic diamonds, which have been manufactured by various processes for more than half a century. However, in recent years it has become possible to produce gem-quality synthetic diamonds of significant size.[^cite_note-AMNH-77] It is possible to make colorless synthetic gemstones that, on a molecular level, are identical to natural stones and so visually similar that only a gemologist with special equipment can tell the difference.[^cite_note-bain-155]

The majority of commercially available synthetic diamonds are yellow and are produced by so-called *high-pressure high-temperature* ([HPHT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HPHT)) processes.[^cite_note-156] The yellow color is caused by [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen) impurities. Other colors may also be reproduced such as blue, green or pink, which are a result of the addition of [boron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron) or from [irradiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemstone_irradiation) after synthesis.[^cite_note-157]

Another popular method of growing synthetic diamond is [chemical vapor deposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_vapor_deposition) (CVD). The growth occurs under low pressure (below atmospheric pressure). It involves feeding a mixture of gases (typically 1 to 99 [methane](18582230.md) to [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen)) into a chamber and splitting them into chemically active [radicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_\(chemistry\)) in a [plasma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_\(physics\)) ignited by [microwaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwaves), [hot filament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot_filament), [arc discharge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_arc), [welding torch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welding_torch), or [laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser).[^cite_note-158] This method is mostly used for coatings, but can also produce single crystals several millimeters in size (see picture).[^cite_note-yarnell-138]

As of 2010, nearly all 5,000 million carats (1,000 tonnes) of synthetic diamonds produced per year are for industrial use. Around 50% of the 133 million carats of natural diamonds mined per year end up in industrial use.[^cite_note-bain-155][^cite_note-159] Mining companies' expenses average 40 to 60 US dollars per carat for natural colorless diamonds, while synthetic manufacturers' expenses average \$2,500 per carat for synthetic, gem-quality colorless diamonds.[^cite_note-bain-155]<sup>: 79 </sup> However, a purchaser is more likely to encounter a synthetic when looking for a fancy-colored diamond because only 0.01% of natural diamonds are fancy-colored, while most synthetic diamonds are colored in some way.[^cite_note-160]

- Six crystals of cubo-octahedral shapes, each about 2 millimeters in diameter. Two are pale blue, one is pale yellow, one is green-blue, one is dark blue and one green-yellow.

  Synthetic diamonds of various colors grown by the high-pressure high-temperature technique

- A round, clear gemstone with many facets, the main face being hexagonal, surrounded by many smaller facets.

  Colorless gem cut from diamond grown by chemical vapor deposition

### Simulants

Main article: [Diamond simulant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_simulant)

A round sparkling, clear gemstone with many facets.

Gem-cut synthetic silicon carbide set in a ring

A diamond simulant is a non-diamond material that is used to simulate the appearance of a diamond, and may be referred to as diamante. [Cubic zirconia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_zirconia) is the most common. The gemstone [moissanite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moissanite) ([silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide)) can be treated as a diamond simulant, though more costly to produce than cubic zirconia. Both are produced synthetically.[^cite_note-161]

### Enhancements

Main article: [Diamond enhancement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_enhancement)

Diamond enhancements are specific treatments performed on natural or synthetic diamonds (usually those already cut and polished into a gem), which are designed to better the gemological characteristics of the stone in one or more ways. These include laser drilling to remove inclusions, application of sealants to fill cracks, treatments to improve a white diamond's color grade, and treatments to give fancy color to a white diamond.[^cite_note-162]

Coatings are increasingly used to give a diamond simulant such as cubic zirconia a more "diamond-like" appearance. One such substance is [diamond-like carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond-like_carbon)—an amorphous carbonaceous material that has some physical properties similar to those of the diamond. Advertising suggests that such a coating would transfer some of these diamond-like properties to the coated stone, hence enhancing the diamond simulant. Techniques such as [Raman spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raman_spectroscopy) should easily identify such a treatment.[^cite_note-163]

### Identification



[Cathodoluminescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathodoluminescence) image of a diamond, taken in a [scanning electron microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scanning_electron_microscope)

Early diamond identification tests included a scratch test relying on the superior hardness of diamond. This test is destructive, as a diamond can scratch another diamond, and is rarely used nowadays. Instead, diamond identification relies on its superior thermal conductivity. Electronic thermal probes are widely used in the gemological centers to separate diamonds from their imitations. These probes consist of a pair of battery-powered [thermistors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermistor) mounted in a fine copper tip. One thermistor functions as a heating device while the other measures the temperature of the copper tip: if the stone being tested is a diamond, it will conduct the tip's thermal energy rapidly enough to produce a measurable temperature drop. This test takes about two to three seconds.[^cite_note-164]

Whereas the thermal probe can separate diamonds from most of their simulants, distinguishing between various types of diamond, for example synthetic or natural, irradiated or non-irradiated, etc., requires more advanced, optical techniques. Those techniques are also used for some diamonds simulants, such as [silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide), which pass the thermal conductivity test. Optical techniques can distinguish between natural diamonds and synthetic diamonds. They can also identify the vast majority of treated natural diamonds.[^cite_note-raman-165] "Perfect" crystals (at the atomic lattice level) have never been found, so both natural and synthetic diamonds always possess characteristic imperfections, arising from the circumstances of their crystal growth, that allow them to be distinguished from each other.[^cite_note-spot-166]

Laboratories use techniques such as spectroscopy, microscopy, and luminescence under shortwave ultraviolet light to determine a diamond's origin.[^cite_note-raman-165] They also use specially made instruments to aid them in the identification process. Two screening instruments are the *DiamondSure* and the *DiamondView*, both produced by the [DTC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Trading_Company) and marketed by the GIA.[^cite_note-167]

Several methods for identifying synthetic diamonds can be performed, depending on the method of production and the color of the diamond. CVD diamonds can usually be identified by an orange fluorescence. D–J colored diamonds can be screened through the [Swiss Gemmological Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_Gemmological_Institute)'s[^cite_note-168] Diamond Spotter. Stones in the D–Z color range can be examined through the DiamondSure UV/visible spectrometer, a tool developed by De Beers.[^cite_note-spot-166] Similarly, natural diamonds usually have minor imperfections and flaws, such as inclusions of foreign material, that are not seen in synthetic diamonds.

Screening devices based on diamond type detection can be used to make a distinction between diamonds that are certainly natural and diamonds that are potentially synthetic. Those potentially synthetic diamonds require more investigation in a specialized lab. Examples of commercial screening devices are D-Screen (WTOCD / HRD Antwerp), Alpha Diamond Analyzer (Bruker / HRD Antwerp), and D-Secure (DRC Techno).
