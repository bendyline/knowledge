**Bristol** ([/ˈbrɪstəl/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/f/fb/En-uk-Bristol.ogg/En-uk-Bristol.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/f/fb/En-uk-Bristol.ogg/En-uk-Bristol.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>) is a [city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_status_in_the_United_Kingdom) and [ceremonial county](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceremonial_county) in [South West England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_West_England). It is on the [River Avon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Avon,_Bristol), and bordered by [Gloucestershire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloucestershire) to the north and [Somerset](51763.md) to the south, with a short coastline on the [Bristol Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Channel) to the west. The county includes most of the [Bristol conurbation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Bristol), but the urban area extends into [South Gloucestershire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Gloucestershire), [Bath and North East Somerset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bath_and_North_East_Somerset), and [North Somerset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Somerset).

The county is almost entirely urbanised and had an estimated population of 494,399 in 2024, making Bristol the most populous city in the region.[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7] For local government purposes the county is a [unitary authority area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_authorities_of_England) governed by [Bristol City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_City_Council). The council is a member of the [West of England Combined Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_of_England_Combined_Authority), which allows it to collaborate with [South Gloucestershire Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Gloucestershire_Council) and [Bath and North East Somerset Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bath_and_North_East_Somerset_Council).

[Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age) [hillforts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillfort) and [Roman villas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_villa) were built near the [confluence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confluence) of the rivers [Frome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Frome,_Bristol) and Avon. Bristol received a [royal charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_charter) in 1155, but was [historically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historic_counties_of_England) divided between Gloucestershire and Somerset until it became a [county corporate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_corporate) in 1373. From the 13th to the 18th centuries, Bristol was among the top three English cities, after London, in tax receipts. A major port, Bristol was a starting place for early voyages of exploration to the New World. At the height of the [Bristol slave trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_slave_trade), from 1700 to 1807, more than 2,000 slave ships carried an estimated 500,000 people from Africa to slavery in the Americas. The [Port of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Bristol) has since moved from [Bristol Harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Harbour) in the city centre to the [Severn Estuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severn_Estuary) at [Avonmouth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avonmouth) and [Royal Portbury Dock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Portbury_Dock).

The city's modern economy is built on the creative media, electronics, and [aerospace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerospace) industries; the city-centre docks have been redeveloped as cultural and heritage centres. There are a variety of artistic and sporting organisations and venues, including the [Royal West of England Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_West_of_England_Academy), the [Arnolfini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnolfini,_Bristol), [Ashton Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashton_Gate_Stadium) and the [Memorial Ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memorial_Ground). The city has two universities: the [University of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bristol) and the [University of the West of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_the_West_of_England) (UWE Bristol). It is connected to the world by [Bristol Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Airport); to the rest of [Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain) via [Bristol Temple Meads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Temple_Meads_railway_station) and [Bristol Parkway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Parkway_railway_station) mainline rail stations; and by road by both the south-west to [West Midlands](351076.md) [M5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M5_motorway) and the London to South Wales [M4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M4_motorway) (which connect to the city centre by the [Portway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portway,_Bristol) and [M32](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M32_motorway)).

## Toponymy

The name derives from the [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English) *Brycgstow*, meaning "assembly place by the bridge" or simply "site of the bridge", presumably in reference to a crossing over the Avon.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9] The final 'L' is an unetymological addition that first appears in the 12th century, which may be due to the unique "Terminal L" found in the [Bristol dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_in_Bristol#Dialect). An older form of the name survives as the surname [Bristow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristow_\(surname\)), which derives from the city.[^cite_note-10]

## History

Main articles: [History of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Bristol) and [Timeline of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_Bristol)

[Archaeological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological) finds, including flint tools believed to be between 300,000 and 126,000 years old made with the [Levallois technique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levallois_technique), indicate the presence of [Neanderthals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neanderthal) in the [Shirehampton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shirehampton) and [St Annes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brislington_West_\(ward\)) areas of Bristol during the [Middle Palaeolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Palaeolithic).[^cite_note-11] [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age) [hill forts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill_fort) near the city are at [Leigh Woods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leigh_Woods) and [Clifton Down](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton_Down), on the side of the [Avon Gorge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avon_Gorge), and on [Kings Weston Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kings_Weston_Hill) near [Henbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henbury,_Bristol).[^cite_note-12] A [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Britain) port, *Portus Abonae* (Avonport) – abbreviated to *Abona* in the [Antonine Itinerary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonine_Itinerary),[^cite_note-13] existed at what is now [Sea Mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_Mills,_Bristol) (connected to [Bath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bath,_Somerset) and [Gloucester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloucester) by [Roman roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_road)); another settlement was at the present-day [Inns Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filwood_\(ward\)#Inns_Court). Isolated [Roman villas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_villa) and small [forts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castra) and settlements were also scattered throughout the area.[^cite_note-14]

### Middle Ages

Bristol was founded by 1000; by about 1020, it was a trading centre with a [mint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mint_\(coin\)) producing silver pennies bearing its name.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELobelCarus-Wilson19752–3-15] By 1067, Brycgstow was a well-fortified *[burh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burh)*, and that year the townsmen beat back a raiding party from Ireland led by three of [Harold Godwinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Godwinson)'s sons.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELobelCarus-Wilson19752–3-15] Under [Norman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normans) rule, the town had one of the strongest [castles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Castle) in [southern England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_England).[^cite_note-16] Bristol was the place of exile for [Diarmait Mac Murchada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diarmait_Mac_Murchada), the Irish [king of Leinster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_of_Leinster), after being overthrown. The Bristol merchants subsequently played a prominent role in funding [Richard Strongbow de Clare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Strongbow_de_Clare) and the [Norman invasion of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_invasion_of_Ireland).[^cite_note-17]

The port developed in the 11th century around the confluence of the [Rivers Frome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Frome,_Bristol) and [Avon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Avon_\(Bristol\)), adjacent to [Bristol Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Bridge) just outside the town walls.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrace197613–15-19] By the 12th century, there was an important [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_England) community in Bristol which survived through to the late 13th century when all Jews were [expelled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edict_of_Expulsion) from England.[^cite_note-20] The stone bridge built in 1247 was replaced by the current bridge during the 1760s.[^cite_note-21] The town incorporated neighbouring suburbs and became a [county](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_corporate) in 1373,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELiddy200513-22] the first town in England to be given this status.[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERayfield198517–23-24][^cite_note-25] During this period, Bristol became a shipbuilding and manufacturing centre.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECarus-Wilson1933183–246-26] By the 14th century, Bristol, [York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/York) and [Norwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwich) were England's largest [medieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval) towns after London.[^cite_note-27] One-third to one-half of the population died in the [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death) of 1348–49,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcCulloch1839398–399-28] which checked population growth, and its population remained between 10,000 and 12,000 for most of the 15th and 16th centuries.[^cite_note-29]

### 15th and 16th centuries

A stone-built Victorian Gothic building with two square towers and a central arched entrance underneath a circular ornate window. A Victorian street lamp stands in front of the building and on the right part of a leafless tree, with blue skies behind.

West front of [Bristol Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Cathedral)

During the 15th century, Bristol was the second most important port in the country, trading with Ireland,[^cite_note-30] Iceland[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECarus-Wilson1933155–182-31] and [Gascony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gascony).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECarus-Wilson1933183–246-26] It was the starting point for many voyages, including [Robert Sturmy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Sturmy)'s (1457–58) unsuccessful attempt to break the Italian [monopoly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopoly) of Eastern Mediterranean trade.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJenks20061-32] New exploration voyages were launched by Venetian [John Cabot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cabot), who in 1497 made landfall in North America.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJonesCondon2016-33] A 1499 voyage, led by merchant [William Weston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Weston_\(explorer\)) of Bristol, was the first expedition commanded by an Englishman to [North America](21139.md).[^cite_note-34] During the first decade of the 16th century, Bristol's merchants undertook a series of exploration voyages to North America and even founded a commercial organisation, 'The Company Adventurers to the New Found Land', to assist their endeavours.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJonesCondon201657–70-35] However, they seem to have lost interest in North America after 1509, having incurred great expenses and made little profit.

During the 16th century, Bristol merchants concentrated on developing trade with Spain and its American colonies.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEConnell-Smith195410-36] This included the [smuggling](28645.md) of prohibited goods, such as food and guns, to Iberia[^cite_note-37] during the [Anglo-Spanish War (1585–1604)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Spanish_War_\(1585%E2%80%931604\)).[^cite_note-38] Bristol's illicit trade grew enormously after 1558, becoming integral to its economy.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJones2012-39]

The original [Diocese of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Bristol) was founded in 1542,[^cite_note-40] when the former [Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey) of [St. Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Canterbury) (founded by [Robert Fitzharding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Fitzharding) four hundred years earlier)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBettey19961–5-41] became [Bristol Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Cathedral). Bristol also gained [city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_status_in_the_United_Kingdom) status that year.[^cite_note-42]

### 17th and 18th centuries



The 17th-century Old Dutch House, High Street, Bristol, before destruction in the Blitz, 1940

In the 1640s, during the [English Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Civil_War), [the city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_in_the_English_Civil_War) was occupied by [Royalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavalier), who built the [Royal Fort House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Fort_House) on the site of an earlier [Parliamentarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundhead) stronghold.[^cite_note-43]

Fishermen from Bristol, who had fished the [Grand Banks of Newfoundland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Banks_of_Newfoundland) since the 16th century,[^cite_note-44] began settling Newfoundland permanently in larger numbers during the 17th century, establishing colonies at [Bristol's Hope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol's_Hope) and [Cuper's Cove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuper's_Cove). Growth of the city and trade came with the rise of England's [American colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_colonization_of_the_Americas) in the 17th century. Bristol's location on the west side of Great Britain gave its ships an advantage in sailing to and from the New World, and the city's merchants made the most of it, with the city becoming one of the two leading outports in all of England by the middle of the 18th century.[^cite_note-45] Bristol was the slave capital of England: In 1755, it had the largest number of slave traders in the country with 237, as against London's 147.[^cite_note-46] It was a major supplier of slaves to [South Carolina](27956.md) before 1750.[^cite_note-47]

The 18th century saw an expansion of Bristol's population (45,000 in 1750)[^cite_note-48] and its role in the [Atlantic trade](49005.md) in Africans taken for [slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery) to the Americas. Bristol and later [Liverpool](18081.md) became centres of the [Triangular Trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangular_Trade).[^cite_note-49] Manufactured goods were shipped to West Africa and exchanged for Africans; the enslaved captives were transported across the Atlantic to the Americas in the [Middle Passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Passage) under brutal conditions.[^cite_note-nmm-50] Plantation goods such as sugar, tobacco, rum, rice, cotton and a few slaves (sold to the aristocracy as house servants) returned across the Atlantic to England.[^cite_note-nmm-50] Some household slaves were baptised in the hope this would lead them to be freed. The [Somersett Case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somersett_Case) of 1772 clarified that slavery was illegal in England.[^cite_note-51] At the height of the [Bristol slave trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_slave_trade) from 1700 to 1807, more than 2,000 slave ships carried a conservatively estimated 500,000 people from Africa to slavery in the Americas.[^cite_note-52]

In 1739, [John Wesley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wesley) founded the first [Methodist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodist) chapel, the [New Room](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Room), in Bristol.[^cite_note-53] Wesley, along with his brother [Charles Wesley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Wesley) and [George Whitefield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Whitefield), preached to large congregations in Bristol and the neighbouring village of [Kingswood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingswood,_South_Gloucestershire), often in the open air.[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55]

Wesley published a pamphlet on slavery, titled *Thoughts Upon Slavery,* in 1774[^cite_note-56] and the [Society of Friends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_Friends) began lobbying against slavery in Bristol in 1783. The city's scions remained nonetheless strongly anti-abolitionist. [Thomas Clarkson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Clarkson) came to Bristol to study the slave trade and gained access to the [Society of Merchant Venturers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_Merchant_Venturers) records.[^cite_note-BRS#1-57] One of his contacts was the owner of the [Seven Stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Stars,_Bristol) [public house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_house), who boarded sailors Clarkson sought to meet. Through these sailors he was able to observe how slaver captains and first mates "plied and stupefied seamen with drink" to sign them up.[^cite_note-BRS#1-57][^cite_note-58] Other informants included ship surgeons and seamen seeking redress. When [William Wilberforce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Wilberforce) began his parliamentary abolition campaign on 12 May 1788, he recalled the history of the [Irish slave trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_slave_trade) from Bristol, which he provocatively claimed continued into the reign of [Henry VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VII_of_England).[^cite_note-BRS#1-57] [Hannah More](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannah_More), originally from Bristol, and a good friend of both Wilberforce and Clarkson, published "Slavery, A Poem" in 1788, just as Wilberforce began his parliamentary campaign.[^cite_note-59] His major speech on 2 April 1792 likewise described the Bristol slave trade specifically, and led to the arrest, trial and subsequent acquittal of a local slaver captain named Kimber.[^cite_note-BRS#1-57]

### 19th century

The city was associated with Victorian engineer [Isambard Kingdom Brunel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isambard_Kingdom_Brunel), who designed the [Great Western Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Western_Railway) between Bristol and [London Paddington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Paddington), two pioneering Bristol-built oceangoing [steamships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steamboat) ([SS *Great Britain*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Great_Britain) and [SS *Great Western*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Great_Western)), and the [Clifton Suspension Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton_Suspension_Bridge). The new railway replaced the [Kennet and Avon Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennet_and_Avon_Canal), which had fully opened in 1810, as the main route for the transport of goods between Bristol and London.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEClew197079–80-60] Competition from Liverpool (beginning around 1760), disruptions of maritime commerce due to war with France (1793) and the abolition of the slave trade (1807) contributed to Bristol's failure to keep pace with the newer manufacturing centres of [Northern England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_England) and the [West Midlands](351076.md). The tidal Avon Gorge, which had secured the port during the Middle Ages, had become a liability. An 1804–09 plan to improve the city's port with a [floating harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Harbour) designed by [William Jessop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Jessop) was a costly error, requiring high harbour fees.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuchananCossons196932–33-61]



Black-and-white etching showing the towers of [St Stephen's Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Stephen's_Church,_Bristol), [St Augustine the Less Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Augustine_the_Less_Church,_Bristol) and [Bristol Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Cathedral), published c. 1850

During the 19th century, [Samuel Plimsoll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Plimsoll), known as "the sailor's friend", campaigned to make the seas safer; shocked by overloaded vessels, he successfully fought for a compulsory [load line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Load_line_\(vessel\)) on ships.[^cite_note-62]

By 1867, ships were getting larger and the meanders in the river Avon prevented boats over 300 ft (90 m) from reaching the harbour, resulting in falling trade.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECoules2006194–195-63] The port facilities were migrating downstream to [Avonmouth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avonmouth) and new industrial complexes were founded there.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuchananCossons1969224–225-64] Some of the traditional industries including copper and brass manufacture went into decline,[^cite_note-65] but the import and processing of [tobacco](30942.md) flourished with the expansion of the [W.D. & H.O. Wills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W.D._&_H.O._Wills) business.[^cite_note-66]

Supported by new industry and growing commerce, Bristol's population (66,000 in 1801), quintupled during the 19th century,[^cite_note-67] resulting in the creation of new suburbs such as [Clifton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton,_Bristol) and [Cotham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotham,_Bristol). These provide architectural examples from the Georgian to the Regency style, with many fine terraces and villas facing the road, and at right angles to it. In the early 19th century, the romantic [medieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval) [gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_revival) style appeared, partially as a reaction against the [symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry) of [Palladianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palladianism), and can be seen in buildings such as the [Bristol City Museum and Art Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_City_Museum_and_Art_Gallery),[^cite_note-68] the [Royal West of England Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_West_of_England_Academy),[^cite_note-69] and [The Victoria Rooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Victoria_Rooms).[^cite_note-Victoria_Rooms-70] [Riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_riots) broke out in 1793[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHunt1818-71] and 1831; the first over the renewal of [tolls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toll_road) on Bristol Bridge, and the second against the rejection of the second [Reform Bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reform_Act_1832) by the [House of Lords](13658.md).[^cite_note-72] The population by 1841 had reached 140,158.[^cite_note-73]

The Diocese of Bristol had undergone several boundary changes by 1897 when it was "reconstituted" into the configuration which has lasted into the 21st century.[^cite_note-74]

### 20th century

An old ordnance survey map of Bristol, showing roads, railways, rivers and contours.

A 1946 map of Bristol

From a population of about 330,000 in 1901, Bristol grew steadily during the 20th century, peaking at 428,089 in 1971.[^cite_note-visiontime-75] Its Avonmouth docklands were enlarged during the early 1900s by the Royal Edward Dock.[^cite_note-76] Another new dock, the [Royal Portbury Dock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Portbury_Dock), opened across the river from Avonmouth during the 1970s.[^cite_note-77] As air travel grew in the first half of the century, aircraft manufacturers built factories.[^cite_note-78] The unsuccessful [Bristol International Exhibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_International_Exhibition) was held on Ashton Meadows in the [Bower Ashton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bower_Ashton) area in 1914.[^cite_note-79] After the premature closure of the exhibition the site was used, until 1919, as [barracks](1033489.md) for the [Gloucestershire Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloucestershire_Regiment) during [World War I](4764461.md).[^cite_note-80][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurlton201460–90-81]



St Mary le Port Church, destroyed on 24 November 1940

[Bristol was heavily damaged by Luftwaffe raids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Blitz) during [World War II](32927.md); about 1,300 people living or working in the city were killed, and nearly 100,000 buildings were damaged, at least 3,000 beyond repair.[^cite_note-82][^cite_note-83] The original central shopping area, near the bridge and castle, [is now a park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle_Park,_Bristol) containing two bombed churches and fragments of the castle. A third bomb-damaged church nearby, [St Nicholas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Nicholas_Church,_Bristol), was restored and, after a period as a museum, has now re-opened as a church.[^cite_note-84] It houses a 1756 [William Hogarth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Hogarth) [triptych](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triptych) painted for the high altar of [St Mary Redcliffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mary_Redcliffe). The church also has statues of [King Edward I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Edward_I) (moved from [Arno's Court Triumphal Arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arno's_Court_Triumphal_Arch)) and [King Edward III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Edward_III) (taken from Lawfords' Gate in the city walls when they were demolished about 1760), and 13th-century statues of [Robert, 1st Earl of Gloucester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert,_1st_Earl_of_Gloucester) (builder of [Bristol Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Castle))[^cite_note-85] and [Geoffrey de Montbray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_de_Montbray) (who built the city's walls) from Bristol's [Newgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newgate,_Bristol).[^cite_note-86]



Ambrose Road, in the Cliftonwood neighbourhood

The rebuilding of [Bristol city centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_city_centre) was characterised by 1960s and 1970s [skyscrapers](29485.md), [mid-century modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-century_modern) architecture and [road building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20th_Century_Road_Schemes_in_Bristol). Beginning in the 1980s some [main roads were closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20th_Century_Road_Schemes_in_Bristol#Queen_Square,_Redcliffe_Way_and_The_Centre), the [Georgian-era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_era) [Queen Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Square,_Bristol) and [Portland Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portland_Square,_Bristol) were restored, the [Broadmead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadmead) shopping area regenerated, and one of the city centre's tallest mid-century towers was demolished.[^cite_note-87] Bristol was bombed twice by the [IRA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provisional_IRA), in [1974](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1974_Bristol_bombing) and again in [1978](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_terrorist_incidents_in_Great_Britain#1970s).[^cite_note-88]

The 20th-century relocation of the docks to [Avonmouth Docks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avonmouth_Docks) and [Royal Portbury Dock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Portbury_Dock), 7 mi (11 km) downstream from the city centre, has allowed the redevelopment of the old dock area (the Floating Harbour).[^cite_note-89] Although the docks' existence was once in jeopardy (since the area was seen as a derelict industrial site), the inaugural 1996 [International Festival of the Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Festival_of_the_Sea,_1996) held in and around the docks affirmed the area as a leisure asset of the city.[^cite_note-90]

### 21st century

Main article: [Statue of Edward Colston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue_of_Edward_Colston)

On 7 June 2020 a [Statue of Edward Colston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue_of_Edward_Colston) was pulled down from its city centre plinth by protestors and pushed into the harbour.[^cite_note-91] The statue was recovered on 11 June and has become a museum exhibit.[^cite_note-92] The action followed more than a decade of debate over the statue and the wording of its plaque, which commemorated Colston's philanthropic work in the city while making no reference to his role in the [Royal African Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_African_Company) and the [Bristol Slave Trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Slave_Trade).[^cite_note-93]

Between 2012 and 2024, the council was led by a directly elected mayor, the [Mayor of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_Bristol). The post was abolished following a 2022 referendum, in which voters chose to replace it with a council committee system.[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-97]

The [lord mayor of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mayors_of_Bristol) is a figurehead elected each May by the city council. The lord mayor (2026) is Henry Michallat.[^cite_note-bbc_lord_mayor_2026-3]

In the [House of Commons](13828.md), Bristol is represented by five constituencies, [East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_East_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)), [North East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_North_East_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)), [North West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_North_West_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)), [South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_South_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)), and [Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Central_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)).[^cite_note-98]

The city has a tradition of political activism. [Edmund Burke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Burke), MP for the [Bristol constituency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)) for six years beginning in 1774, insisted that he was a Member of Parliament first and a representative of his constituents' interests second.[^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100] Women's-rights advocate [Emmeline Pethick-Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmeline_Pethick-Lawrence) (1867–1954) was born in Bristol,[^cite_note-101] and the [left-winger](18499.md) [Tony Benn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Benn) served as MP for [Bristol South East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_South_East) from 1950 to 1960 and again from 1963 to 1983.[^cite_note-102] In 1963 the [Bristol Bus Boycott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Bus_Boycott), following the [Bristol Omnibus Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Omnibus_Company)'s refusal to hire black drivers and conductors, drove the passage of the UK's 1965 [Race Relations Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Race_Relations_Act_1965).[^cite_note-103] The [1980 St Pauls riot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_St_Pauls_riot) protested against racism and police harassment and showed mounting dissatisfaction with the socioeconomic circumstances of the city's Afro-Caribbean residents. Local support of [fair trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_trade) was recognised in 2005, when Bristol became a [fairtrade zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairtrade_zone).[^cite_note-104]

Bristol is both a city and a county, since [King Edward III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Edward_III) granted it a county charter in 1373.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELiddy200513-22] The county was expanded in 1835 to include suburbs such as [Clifton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton,_Bristol), and it was named a [county borough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_borough) in 1889 when that designation was introduced.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERayfield198517–23-24]

### Former county of Avon

Main article: [Avon (county)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avon_\(county\))

On 1 April 1974, Bristol became a local government district of the administrative county of [Avon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avon_\(county\)).[^cite_note-105] On 1 April 1996, Avon was abolished and Bristol became a [unitary authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_authority).[^cite_note-106]

The former Avon area, called [Greater Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Bristol) by the Government Office of the South West (now abolished) and others,[^cite_note-107] refers to the city and the three neighbouring local authorities‍—‌[Bath and North East Somerset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bath_and_North_East_Somerset), [North Somerset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Somerset) and [South Gloucestershire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Gloucestershire) previously in Avon.

The [North Fringe of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Fringe_of_Bristol), a developed area between the Bristol city boundary and the M4, M5 and M32 motorways (now in South Gloucestershire) was so named as part of a 1987 [plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_plan) prepared by the [Northavon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northavon) District Council of Avon county.[^cite_note-108]

### West of England Combined Authority

Main article: [West of England Combined Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_of_England_Combined_Authority)

The [West of England Combined Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_of_England_Combined_Authority) was created on 9 February 2017.[^cite_note-SI_2017_No_126-109] Covering Bristol and the rest of the old Avon county with the exception of North Somerset, the new [combined authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combined_authority) has responsibility for regional planning, roads, and local transport, and to a lesser extent, education and business investment. The authority is led by the [Mayor of the West of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_the_West_of_England), [Helen Godwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_Godwin).

## Geography

### Boundaries

See also: [Subdivisions of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subdivisions_of_Bristol)

Suspension bridge between two brick built towers, over a wooded gorge, showing mud and water at the bottom. In the distance are hills.

[Brunel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isambard_Kingdom_Brunel)'s [Clifton Suspension Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton_Suspension_Bridge)

Bristol's boundaries can be defined in several ways, including those of the city itself, the developed area, or [Greater Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Bristol).

The [city council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_Bristol) boundary is the narrowest definition of the city itself. However, it unusually includes a large, roughly rectangular section of the western [Severn Estuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severn_Estuary) ending at (but not including) the islands of [Flat Holm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_Holm) (in [Cardiff](5882.md), Wales) and [Steep Holm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steep_Holm).[^cite_note-110] This "seaward extension" can be traced back to the original boundary of the County of Bristol laid out in the charter[^cite_note-111] granted to the city by [Edward III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_III) in 1373.[^cite_note-112]

The [Office for National Statistics](324029.md) (ONS) has defined a [Bristol Urban Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Urban_Area), which includes developed areas adjoining Bristol but outside the city-council boundary, such as [Kingswood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingswood,_South_Gloucestershire), [Mangotsfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mangotsfield), [Stoke Gifford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoke_Gifford), [Winterbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winterbourne,_Gloucestershire), [Almondsbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almondsbury), [Easton in Gordano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easton_in_Gordano), [Whitchurch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitchurch,_Bristol) village, [Filton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filton), [Patchway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patchway) and [Bradley Stoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradley_Stoke), but excludes undeveloped areas within that boundary.[^cite_note-113]

### Geography

Bristol lies within a [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone) area running from the [Mendip Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendip_Hills) in the south to the [Cotswolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotswolds) in the northeast.[^cite_note-114] The rivers Avon and Frome cut through the limestone to the underlying clay, creating Bristol's characteristically hilly landscape. The Avon flows from Bath in the east, through [flood plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flood_plains) and areas which were marshes before the city's growth. To the west, the Avon cuts through the limestone to form the Avon Gorge, formed largely by glacial meltwater after the [last ice age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternary_glaciation).[^cite_note-10.1144/GSL-115]

The gorge, which helped protect Bristol Harbour, has been quarried for stone to build the city, and its surrounding land has been protected from development as [The Downs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Downs,_Bristol) and Leigh Woods. The Avon [estuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estuary) and the gorge form the county boundary with [North Somerset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Somerset), and the river flows into the [Severn Estuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severn_Estuary) at [Avonmouth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avonmouth). A smaller gorge, cut by the [Hazel Brook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazel_Brook) which flows into the [River Trym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Trym), crosses the [Blaise Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaise_Castle) estate in northern Bristol.[^cite_note-10.1144/GSL-115]

Bristol is sometimes described, by its inhabitants, as being built on seven hills, like Rome. From 18th-century guidebooks, these seven hills were known as simply Bristol (the Old Town), Castle Hill, College Green, Kingsdown, St Michael's Hill, [Brandon Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandon_Hill,_Bristol) and Redcliffe Hill.[^cite_note-116] Other local hills include Red Lion Hill, [Barton Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barton_Hill,_Bristol), [Lawrence Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Hill,_Bristol), Black Boy Hill, Constitution Hill, [Staple Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staple_Hill,_Gloucestershire), [Windmill Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windmill_Hill,_Bristol), Malborough Hill, Nine Tree Hill, Talbot, Brook Hill and Granby Hill.

Bristol is 106 mi (171 km) west of London, 77 mi (124 km) south-southwest of [Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham) and 26 mi (42 km) east of the Welsh capital [Cardiff](5882.md). Areas adjoining the city fall within a loosely defined area known as [Greater Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Bristol). [Bath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bath,_Somerset) is located 11 mi (18 km) southeast of the city centre, [Weston-super-Mare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weston-super-Mare) is 18 mi (29 km) to the southwest and the Welsh city of [Newport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newport,_Wales) is 19 mi (31 km) to the north west.

### Climate

The climate is [oceanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_climate) ([Köppen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification): *Cfb)*, milder than most places in England and United Kingdom.[^cite_note-117][^cite_note-118] Located in southern England, Bristol is one of the warmest cities in the UK with a mean annual temperature of approximately 11 °C (52 °F).[^cite_note-119][^cite_note-120] It is among the sunniest, with more than 1,600 hours of sunshine per year.[^cite_note-121] Although the city is partially sheltered by the Mendip Hills, it is exposed to the Severn Estuary and the [Bristol Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Channel). Rain is fairly evenly distributed throughout the year, with autumn and winter the wetter seasons. The Atlantic Ocean influences Bristol's weather, keeping its average temperature above freezing throughout the year, but winter frosts are frequent and snow occasionally falls from early November to late April. Summers are warm and drier, with variable sunshine, rain and clouds, and spring weather is unsettled.[^cite_note-122][^cite_note-123]

The weather stations nearest Bristol for which long-term climate data are available are Long Ashton (about 5 mi (8 km) south west of the city centre) and Bristol Weather Station, in the city centre. Data collection at these locations ended in 2002 and 2001, respectively, and following the closure of Filton Airfield, Almondsbury is the nearest weather station to the city.[^cite_note-MO_stations_2024-124] Temperatures at Long Ashton from 1959 to 2002 ranged from 33.5 °C (92.3 °F) in July 1976[^cite_note-125] to −14.4 °C (6.1 °F) in January 1982.[^cite_note-126] Monthly high temperatures since 2002 at Filton exceeding those recorded at Long Ashton include 25.7 °C (78.3 °F) in April 2003,[^cite_note-127] 34.5 °C (94.1 °F) in July 2006[^cite_note-128] and 26.8 °C (80.2 °F) in October 2011.[^cite_note-129] The lowest recent temperature at Filton was −10.1 °C (13.8 °F) in December 2010.[^cite_note-130] Although large cities in general experience an [urban heat island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island) effect, with warmer temperatures than their surrounding rural areas, this phenomenon is minimal in Bristol.[^cite_note-131]

### Environment

Main article: [Environment in Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environment_in_Bristol)

Bristol was ranked as Britain's most sustainable city (based on its environmental performance, quality of life, [future-proofing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future-proofing) and approaches to climate change, recycling and biodiversity), topping environmental charity [Forum for the Future](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forum_for_the_Future)'s 2008 [Sustainable Cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_city) Index.[^cite_note-136][^cite_note-137] Local initiatives include [Sustrans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustrans) (creators of the [National Cycle Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Cycle_Network), founded as Cyclebag in 1977)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECottonGrimshaw2002-138] and Resourcesaver, a non-profit business established in 1988 by Avon Friends of the Earth.[^cite_note-139] The city regularly features in the annual *[Sunday Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunday_Times)* lists of best places in Britain in which to live, topping the list of cities in 2014 and 2017.[^cite_note-140][^cite_note-141] The city received the 2015 [European Green Capital Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Green_Capital_Award), becoming the first UK city to receive this award.[^cite_note-142]

In 2019 Bristol City Council voted in favour of banning all privately owned [diesel cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_engine) from the city centre.[^cite_note-bbc._Bris-143] Since then, the plans have been revised in favour of a [clean air zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_air_zone) whereby older and more polluting vehicles will be charged to drive through the city centre. The Clean Air Zone came into effect in November 2022.[^cite_note-144]

### Green belt

Main article: [Avon Green Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avon_Green_Belt)

The city has a green belt mainly along its southern fringes, taking in small areas within the [Ashton Court Estate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashton_Court), South Bristol crematorium and cemetery, High Ridge common and Whitchurch, with a further area around Frenchay Farm. The belt extends outside the city boundaries into surrounding counties and districts, for several miles in places, to afford protection from [urban sprawl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_sprawl) to surrounding villages and towns.

### Bristol conurbation

The population of [Bristol's contiguous urban area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Urban_Area) was put at 551,066 by the ONS based on Census 2001 data.[^cite_note-ANameSoThatWeDontHaveToUseIbidForTheNextRef-163] In 2006 the ONS estimated Bristol's urban-area population at 587,400,[^cite_note-164] making it England's sixth-most populous city and tenth-most populous urban area.[^cite_note-ANameSoThatWeDontHaveToUseIbidForTheNextRef-163] At 3,599/km<sup>2</sup> (9,321/sq mi) it has the seventh-highest population density of any English district.[^cite_note-165] According to data from 2019, the urban area has the 11th-largest population in the UK, with a population of 670,000.[^cite_note-UrbanPopulation-166]

In 2007 the [European Spatial Planning Observation Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Spatial_Planning_Observation_Network) (ESPON) defined Bristol's [functional urban area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_urban_area) as including Weston-super-Mare, Bath and Clevedon with a total population of 1.04 million, the twelfth largest of the UK.[^cite_note-167]

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Bristol)

Two ornate metal pillars with large dishes on top in a paved street, with an eighteenth-century stone building behind, upon which can be seen the words "Tea Blenders Estabklishec 177-". People sitting at café-style tables outside. On the right are iron railings.

Two of the four Nails (bronze tables used for conducting business) in [Corn Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_Street)

Bristol has a long history of trade, originally exporting wool cloth and importing fish, wine, grain and dairy products;[^cite_note-168] later imports were tobacco, tropical fruits and plantation goods. Major imports are motor vehicles, grain, timber, [produce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Produce) and petroleum products.[^cite_note-169]

The city's economy also relies on the [aerospace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerospace), defence, media, information technology, financial services and tourism industries.[^cite_note-170][^cite_note-171] The [Ministry of Defence](203793.md) (MoD)'s Procurement Executive, later known as the [Defence Procurement Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defence_Procurement_Agency) and [Defence Equipment and Support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defence_Equipment_and_Support), moved to its headquarters to [Abbey Wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOD_Abbey_Wood), Filton, in 1995. This organisation, with a staff of 12,000 to 13,000, procures and supports MoD equipment.[^cite_note-172] One of the UK's most popular tourist destinations, Bristol was selected in 2009 as one of the world's top-ten cities by international travel publishers [Dorling Kindersley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorling_Kindersley) in their *[Eyewitness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyewitness_Books)* guides for young adults.[^cite_note-173]

Bristol is one of the eight-largest regional English cities that make up the [Core Cities Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core_Cities_Group), and is ranked as a Gamma level [global city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_city) by the [Globalization and World Cities Research Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globalization_and_World_Cities_Research_Network), the fourth-highest-ranked English city.[^cite_note-174] In 2017 Bristol's [gross domestic product](12594.md) was £88.448 billion.[^cite_note-subregionalgva-175][^cite_note-176] Its [per capita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_capita) GDP was £46,000 (\$65,106, €57,794), which was some 65% above the national average, the third-highest of any English city (after London and [Nottingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nottingham)) and the sixth-highest of any city in the United Kingdom (behind London, [Edinburgh](9602.md), Glasgow, [Belfast](5046.md) and Nottingham).[^cite_note-subregionalgva-175] According to the 2011 census, Bristol's unemployment rate (claiming [Jobseeker's Allowance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jobseeker's_Allowance)) was three per cent, compared with two per cent for [South West England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_West_England) and the national average of four per cent.[^cite_note-177]

Although Bristol's economy no longer relies upon its [port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Bristol), which was moved to docks at Avonmouth during the 1870s[^cite_note-178] and to the Royal Portbury Dock in 1977 as ship size increased, it is the largest importer of cars to the UK. Until 1991, the port was publicly owned; it is leased, with £330 million invested and its annual tonnage increasing from 3.9 million long tons (4 million tonnes) to 11.8 million (12 million).[^cite_note-179] Tobacco importing and cigarette manufacturing have ceased, but the importation of wine and spirits continues.[^cite_note-180]

The financial services sector employs 59,000 in the city,[^cite_note-181] and 50 micro-electronics and silicon design companies employ about 5,000. In 1983, [Hewlett-Packard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hewlett-Packard) opened its national research laboratory in Bristol.[^cite_note-182][^cite_note-183] In 2014 the city was ranked seventh in the "top 10 UK destinations" by [TripAdvisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TripAdvisor).[^cite_note-184]

During the 20th century, Bristol's manufacturing activities expanded to include aircraft production at Filton by the [Bristol Aeroplane Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Aeroplane_Company) and aircraft-engine manufacturing by Bristol Aero Engines (later [Rolls-Royce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolls-Royce_Holdings)) at Patchway. Bristol Aeroplane was known for their World War I [Bristol Fighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_F.2_Fighter)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyne2002105-185] and World War II [Blenheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Blenheim) and [Beaufighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaufighter) planes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyne2002105-185] During the 1950s, they were a major English manufacturer of civilian aircraft, known for the [Freighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Freighter), [Britannia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Britannia) and [Brabazon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Brabazon). The company diversified into automobile manufacturing during the 1940s, producing hand-built [luxury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luxury_vehicle) [Bristol Cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Cars) at their factory in Filton, and the Bristol Cars company was spun off in 1960.[^cite_note-186] The city also gave its name to Bristol buses, which were manufactured in the city from 1908 to 1983: by Bristol Tramways until 1955, and from 1955 to 1983 by [Bristol Commercial Vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Commercial_Vehicles).[^cite_note-187]

A view from below of an aeroplane in flight, with a slender fuselage and swept back wings.

Final [Concorde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concorde) flight on 26 November 2003, shortly before landing on the Filton runway

Filton played a key role in the Anglo-French [Concorde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concorde) supersonic airliner project during the 1960s. The British Concorde prototype made its maiden flight from Filton to [RAF Fairford](475013.md) on 9 April 1969, five weeks after the French test flight.[^cite_note-188] In 2003 [British Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Airways) and [Air France](14998012.md) decided to discontinue Concorde flights, retiring the aircraft to locations (primarily museums) worldwide. On 26 November 2003 Concorde 216 made the final Concorde flight, returning to [Bristol Filton Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Filton_Airport) as the centrepiece of a proposed air museum which is planned to include the existing Bristol Aero collection (including a Bristol Britannia).[^cite_note-189]

The aerospace industry remains a major sector of the local economy.[^cite_note-190] Major aerospace companies in Bristol include [BAE Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BAE_Systems), a merger of [Marconi Electronic Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marconi_Electronic_Systems) and [BAe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BAe) (the latter a merger of BAC, Hawker Siddeley and Scottish Aviation). [Airbus](26220236.md)[^cite_note-191] and Rolls-Royce are also based at Filton, and aerospace engineering is an area of research at the University of the West of England. Another aviation company in the city is [Cameron Balloons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameron_Balloons), who manufacture [hot air balloons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot_air_balloon);[^cite_note-192] each August the city hosts the [Bristol International Balloon Fiesta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_International_Balloon_Fiesta), one of Europe's largest hot-air balloon festivals.[^cite_note-193]

In 2005, Bristol was named by the UK government one of England's six science cities.[^cite_note-194][^cite_note-195] A £500 million shopping centre, [Cabot Circus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabot_Circus), opened in 2008 amidst predictions by developers and politicians that the city would become one of England's top ten retail destinations.[^cite_note-196] The [Bristol Temple Quarter Enterprise Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Temple_Quarter_Enterprise_Zone), focused on creative, high-tech and low-carbon industries around [Bristol Temple Meads railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Temple_Meads_railway_station),[^cite_note-BP1-197] was announced in 2011[^cite_note-198] and launched the following year.[^cite_note-BP1-197] The 70 ha (170-acre) [Urban Enterprise Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_Enterprise_Zone) has streamlined [planning procedures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_permission) and reduced [business rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_rates_in_England_and_Wales). Rates generated by the zone are channelled to five other designated enterprise areas in the region:[^cite_note-199] Avonmouth, Bath, [Bristol and Bath Science Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_and_Bath_Science_Park) in Emersons Green, Filton, and Weston-super-Mare. Bristol is the only big city whose wealth per capita is higher than that of Britain as a whole. The wider region has one of the biggest aerospace hubs in the UK, centred on Airbus, Rolls-Royce and GKN at Filton airfield.[^cite_note-200]

Between 2012 and 2020, the city had the largest circulating [community currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_currency) in the UK, the [Bristol Pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Pound), which was [pegged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_exchange_rate_system) to the [pound sterling](270673.md) before it ceased operation.

## Culture

Main article: [Culture of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Bristol)

### Arts

An imposing eighteenth-century building with three entrance archways, large first-floor windows and an ornate peaked gable end above.

The Coopers Hall, part of the Bristol Old Vic Theatre Royal complex

A long two-storey building with 4 cranes in front on the quayside. Two tugboats are moored at the quay.

Site of the former [Bristol Industrial Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Industrial_Museum), now the [M Shed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M_Shed)

Bristol has a thriving arts scene. Some of the modern venues and modern digital production companies have merged with legacy production companies based in old buildings around the city. In 2008 the city was a finalist for the 2008 [European Capital of Culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Capital_of_Culture), although the title was awarded to Liverpool.[^cite_note-201] The city was designated "City of Film" by [UNESCO](21786641.md) in 2017 and has been a member of the [Creative Cities Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creative_Cities_Network) since then.[^cite_note-202]

The [Bristol Old Vic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Old_Vic), founded in 1946 as an offshoot of [The Old Vic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Old_Vic) in London, occupies the 1766 Theatre Royal (607 seats) on [King Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Street,_Bristol) and the Weston Studio in the adjacent Coopers' Hall (built in 1743), with a 2018 bar and foyer area. The Theatre Royal, a grade I [listed building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Listed_building),[^cite_note-203][^cite_note-gradei-204] is the oldest continuously operating theatre in England.[^cite_note-205] The [Bristol Old Vic Theatre School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Old_Vic_Theatre_School) (which originated in King Street) is a separate company, and the [Bristol Hippodrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Hippodrome) is a 1,951-seat theatre for national touring productions. Other smaller theatres include the [Tobacco Factory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_Factory), [QEH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Elizabeth's_Hospital), the Redgrave Theatre at [Clifton College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton_College), The Wardrobe Theatre, Bristol Improv Theatre, and the Alma Theatre. Bristol's theatre scene features several companies as well as the Old Vic, including [Show of Strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Show_of_Strength_Theatre_Company), [Shakespeare at the Tobacco Factory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare_at_the_Tobacco_Factory) and Travelling Light. Theatre Bristol is a partnership between the city council, [Arts Council England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arts_Council_England) and local residents to develop the city's theatre industry.[^cite_note-206] Several organisations support Bristol theatre; the Residence (an artist-led community) provides office, social and rehearsal space for theatre and performance companies,[^cite_note-207] and [Equity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equity_\(trade_union\)) has a branch in the city.[^cite_note-208]

A painting on a building showing a naked man hanging by one hand from a window sill. A man in a suit looks out of the window, shading his eyes with his right hand, behind him stands a woman in her underwear.

*[Well Hung Lover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well_Hung_Lover)*, one of many [Banksy](396241.md) artworks in the city, which has since been vandalised with blue paint (partially cleaned by the city council)

The city has many venues for live music, its largest being the 2,000-seat [Bristol Beacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Beacon), previously Colston Hall, named after [Edward Colston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Colston). Others include the [Bristol Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O2_Academy_Bristol), [The Fleece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wool_Hall,_Bristol), [The Croft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Croft_\(music_venue\)?action=edit\&redlink=1), the Exchange, Fiddlers, the [Victoria Rooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_Rooms,_Bristol), Rough Trade, [Trinity Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_Centre), [St George's Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_George's_Bristol) and several pubs, from the jazz-oriented [The Old Duke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Old_Duke,_Bristol) to rock at the Fleece and [indie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_music) bands at the Louisiana.[^cite_note-209][^cite_note-210] In 2010 [PRS for Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PRS_for_Music) called Bristol the UK's most musical city, based on the number of its members born there relative to the city's population.[^cite_note-211]

Since the late 1970s, Bristol has been home to bands combining [punk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punk_rock), [funk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funk_rock), [dub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dub_music) and [political consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_consciousness). With [trip hop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trip_hop) and [Bristol Sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Sound) artists such as [Tricky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tricky_\(musician\)),[^cite_note-212] [Portishead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portishead_\(band\))[^cite_note-213] and [Massive Attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massive_Attack),[^cite_note-214] the [list of bands from Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_bands_from_Bristol) is extensive. The city is a stronghold of [drum and bass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_and_bass), with artists such as [Roni Size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roni_Size)'s [Mercury Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_Prize)-winning [Reprazent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reprazent),[^cite_note-215] as [DJ Krust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DJ_Krust),[^cite_note-216] [More Rockers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/More_Rockers)[^cite_note-217] and [TC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TC_\(musician\)).[^cite_note-218] Musicians were at the centre of the broader Bristol urban-culture scene which received international media attention during the 1990s.[^cite_note-219] [The Downs Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Downs_Festival) is also a yearly occurrence where both local and well-known bands play. Since its inception in 2016, it has become a major event in the city.

The [Bristol Museum and Art Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Museum_and_Art_Gallery) houses a collection encompassing [natural history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_history), archaeology, local glassware, [Chinese ceramics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_ceramics) and art. The [M Shed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M_Shed) museum opened in 2011 on the site of the former [Bristol Industrial Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Industrial_Museum).[^cite_note-220] Both are operated by Bristol Culture and Creative Industries, which also runs three historic houses‍—‌the [Tudor Red Lodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Lodge_Museum,_Bristol), the [Georgian House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_House,_Bristol) and [Blaise Castle House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaise_Castle_House); and [Bristol Archives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Archives).[^cite_note-221] The 18th- and 19th-century portrait painter [Thomas Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Lawrence), 19th-century architect [Francis Greenway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Greenway) (designer of many of Sydney's first buildings) were born in the city. The [graffiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graffiti) artist [Banksy](396241.md) is believed to be from Bristol, and many of his works are on display in the city.



Clay models of [Wallace & Gromit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallace_&_Gromit) on display at St. Georges, Bristol

The [Watershed Media Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watershed_Media_Centre) and [Arnolfini gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnolfini_gallery) (both in dockside warehouses) exhibit contemporary art, photography and cinema, and the city's oldest gallery is at the [Royal West of England Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_West_of_England_Academy) in Clifton.[^cite_note-222] The nomadic [Antlers Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antlers_Gallery) opened in 2010, moving into empty spaces on [Park Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park_Street,_Bristol), on [Whiteladies Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whiteladies_Road) and in the Purifier House on Bristol's Harbourside.[^cite_note-223] [Stop-motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stop-motion) animation films and commercials (produced by [Aardman Animations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aardman_Animations)) are made in Bristol, such as *[Wallace & Gromit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallace_&_Gromit)* and *[Chicken Run](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicken_Run)*, while Aardman has also branched out into computer-animation, such as *[Arthur Christmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Christmas)*.[^cite_note-224][^cite_note-225] [Robert Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Newton), [Bobby Driscoll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Driscoll) and other cast members of the 1950 [Walt Disney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Disney) film *[Treasure Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_Island_\(1950_film\))* (some scenes were filmed along the [harbourside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Harbour)) visited the city along with Disney himself. Bristol is home to the [regional headquarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcasting_House,_Bristol) of [BBC West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_West) and the [BBC Natural History Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Natural_History_Unit).[^cite_note-226] Locations in and around Bristol have featured in the BBC's natural-history programmes, including *[Animal Magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_Magic_\(TV_series\))* (filmed at [Bristol Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Zoo)).[^cite_note-227]

Bristol is the birthplace of 18th-century poets [Robert Southey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Southey)[^cite_note-228] and [Thomas Chatterton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Chatterton).[^cite_note-229] Southey (born on [Wine Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wine_Street,_Bristol) in 1774) and his friend, [Samuel Taylor Coleridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Taylor_Coleridge), married the Fricker sisters from the city.[^cite_note-230] [William Wordsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Wordsworth) spent time in Bristol,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENewlyn20017-231] where [Joseph Cottle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Cottle) published *[Lyrical Ballads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyrical_Ballads)* in 1798. Actor [Cary Grant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cary_Grant) was born in Bristol, and comedians from the city include [Justin Lee Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Lee_Collins),[^cite_note-232] [Lee Evans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Evans_\(comedian\)),[^cite_note-233] [Russell Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Howard)[^cite_note-234] and writer-comedian [Stephen Merchant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Merchant).[^cite_note-235]

The author [John Betjeman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Betjeman) wrote a poem called "Bristol".[^cite_note-JB_1-236] It begins:

> Green upon the flooded Avon shone the after-storm-wet-sky,\
> &#x20;Quick the struggling withy branches let the leaves of autumn fly,\
> &#x20;And a star shone over Bristol, wonderfully far and high.

— John Betjeman, Bristol

### Architecture

Main article: [Buildings and architecture of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buildings_and_architecture_of_Bristol)

Large, square two-storey house at the end of a dirt path

Garden front of John Vanbrugh's [Kings Weston House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kings_Weston_House), Bristol

A seventeenth-century timber-framed building with three gables and a traditional inn sign showing a picture of a sailing barge. Some drinkers sit at benches outside on a cobbled street. Other old buildings are further down the street, and in the background, part of a modern office building can be seen.

The [Llandoger Trow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llandoger_Trow), a historic Bristol pub

Bristol has 51 [Grade I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grade_I_listed_buildings_in_Bristol),[^cite_note-gradei-204] 500 [Grade II\*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grade_II*_listed_buildings_in_Bristol) and over 3,800 [Grade II listed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grade_II_listed_buildings_in_Bristol) [buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categories_of_listed_building)[^cite_note-237] in a variety of [architectural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architectural) styles, from [medieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_architecture) to modern. During the mid-19th century, [Bristol Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Byzantine), a style unique to the city, was developed, and several examples have survived. Buildings from most [architectural periods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architectural_periods) of the United Kingdom can be seen in the city. Surviving elements of the fortifications and castle date to the medieval period,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurrough19703-238] and the Church of St James dates back to the 12th century.[^cite_note-239]

The oldest Grade I listed buildings in Bristol are religious. [St James' Priory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_James'_Priory,_Bristol) was founded in 1129 as a [Benedictine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedictine) priory by Earl [Robert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert,_1st_Earl_of_Gloucester) of Gloucester, the illegitimate son of [Henry I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_I_of_England).[^cite_note-240] The second-oldest is [Bristol Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Cathedral) and its associated [Great Gatehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Gatehouse).[^cite_note-241] Founded in 1140 as an [Augustinian](144980.md) monastery, the church became the seat of the [bishop](4092.md) and cathedral of the new Diocese of Bristol in 1542. Most of the medieval stonework, particularly the Elder Lady Chapel, is made from limestone taken from quarries around [Dundry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dundry) and [Felton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felton,_Somerset), with [Bath stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bath_stone) being used in other areas.[^cite_note-242] Amongst the other churches included in the list is the 12th-century [St Mary Redcliffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mary_Redcliffe) which is the second tallest building in Bristol. The church was described by [Queen Elizabeth I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Elizabeth_I) as "the fairest, goodliest, and most famous parish church in England."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurrough197013–14-243]

Secular buildings include [The Red Lodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Lodge_Museum,_Bristol), built in 1580 for John Yonge as a [lodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gatehouse) for a larger house that once stood on the site of the present [Bristol Beacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Beacon), previously known as Colston Hall. It was added to in [Georgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_architecture) times and restored in the early 20th century.[^cite_note-244] [St Bartholomew's Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Bartholomew's_Hospital,_Bristol) is a 12th-century town house which was incorporated into a monastery hospital. It was founded in 1240 by [Sir John la Warr, 2nd Baron De La Warr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_De_La_Warr) (c. 1277–1347), and became [Bristol Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Grammar_School) from 1532 to 1767, and then [Queen Elizabeth's Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Elizabeth's_Hospital) 1767–1847. The round piers predate the hospital, and may come from an aisled hall, the earliest remains of domestic architecture in the city, which was then adapted to form the hospital chapel.[^cite_note-245] Three 17th-century town houses which were attached to the hospital were incorporated into model workers' flats in 1865, and converted to offices in 1978. [St Nicholas's Almshouses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Nicholas's_Almshouses) were built in 1652[^cite_note-246] to provide care for the poor. Several public houses were also built in this period, including the [Llandoger Trow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llandoger_Trow)[^cite_note-247] on King Street and the [Hatchet Inn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatchet_Inn,_Bristol).[^cite_note-248]

[Manor houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manor_house) include [Goldney Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldney_Hall), where the highly decorated [Grotto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grotto_at_Goldney_House) dates from 1739.[^cite_note-249] Commercial buildings such as the Exchange[^cite_note-250] and [Old Post Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Post_Office,_Bristol)[^cite_note-251] from the 1740s are also included in the list. Residential buildings include the Georgian Portland Square[^cite_note-252] and the complex of small cottages around a green at [Blaise Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaise_Hamlet), which was built around 1811 for retired employees of [Quaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaker) [banker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banker) and [philanthropist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philanthropist) [John Scandrett Harford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Scandrett_Harford), who owned [Blaise Castle House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaise_Castle_House).[^cite_note-253]

The 18th-century [Kings Weston House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kings_Weston_House), in northern Bristol, was designed by [John Vanbrugh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Vanbrugh) and is the only Vanbrugh building in any UK city outside London. [Almshouses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almshouse)[^cite_note-254] and pubs from the same period[^cite_note-255] intermingle with modern development. Several Georgian [squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_square) were designed for the middle class as prosperity increased during the 18th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFoyle200419–21-256]

During World War II, the city centre was heavily bombed during the [Bristol Blitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Blitz).[^cite_note-257] The central shopping area near [Wine Street and Castle Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle_Park,_Bristol) was particularly hard-hit, and [the Dutch House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dutch_House,_Bristol) and [St Peter's Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Peter's_Hospital,_Bristol) were destroyed. Nevertheless, in 1961 [John Betjeman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Betjeman) called Bristol "the most beautiful, interesting and distinguished city in England".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWinstone1985124-258]

### Dialect

Further information: [Culture in Bristol § Dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_in_Bristol#Dialect)

An ornate brick tower surrounded by trees. The tower has balconies and is surmounted by a pitched roof with an ornate figure at the apex.

[Cabot Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabot_Tower,_Bristol), seen from the Brandon Hill park

A dialect of English ([West Country English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Country_English)), known as Bristolian, is spoken by longtime residents, who are known as Bristolians.[^cite_note-259] Bristol natives have a [rhotic accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhotic_accent), in which the post-vocalic *r* in *car* and *card* is pronounced (unlike in [Received Pronunciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Received_Pronunciation)). The city is regarded as one of the last locations in England, along with [Blackburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackburn), to preserve the traditional English rhotic R sound.[^cite_note-260] The unique feature of this accent is the 'Bristol (or terminal) l', in which *l* is appended to words ending in *a* or *o*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHughes201286–88-261] Whether this is a broad *l* or a *w* is a subject of debate,[^cite_note-262] with *area* pronounced 'areal' or 'areaw'. The ending of *Bristol* is another example of the Bristol *l*. Bristolians pronounce *-a* and *-o* at the end of a word as *-aw* (*cinemaw*). To non-natives, the pronunciation suggests an *l* after the vowel.[^cite_note-263][^cite_note-264]

Bristolian is characterised by use of *cassn't* instead of *can't* and *canst* and *dissn't* instead of *didst* and *didn't*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlack1996172-265] The second-person singular was retained and children were admonished with "Thee and thou, the Welshman's cow". In Bristolian, as in French and German, the second-person singular was not used when speaking to a superior (except by the egalitarian [Quakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quakers)). The pronoun *thee* is also used in the subject position ("What bist thee doing?"), and *I* or *he* in the object position ("Give he to I.").[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStrohmeyer200913-266] Linguist [Stanley Ellis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Ellis_\(linguist\)), who found that many dialect words in the Filton area were linked to aerospace work, described Bristolian as "a cranky, crazy, crab-apple tree of language and with the sharpest, juiciest flavour that I've heard for a long time".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEElmes200539-267]

## Sport

Main article: [Sport in Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_in_Bristol)

In the foreground, twentieth-century housing can be seen amidst trees, and on the right, a tower block of flats. [Ashton Gate Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashton_Gate_Stadium), with the [Clifton Suspension Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton_Suspension_Bridge) over the [Avon Gorge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avon_Gorge) in the background

Bristol is represented by professional teams in all the major national sports. [Bristol City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_City) and [Bristol Rovers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Rovers) are the city's main [football](10568.md) clubs. [Bristol Bears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Bears) ([rugby union](25405.md)) and [Gloucestershire County Cricket Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloucestershire_County_Cricket_Club) are also based in the city.

The two [Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_League) clubs are Bristol City and Bristol Rovers‍—‌the former being the only club from the city to play in the precursor to the [Premier League](11250.md). Bristol City, formed in 1894, were Division One runners-up in 1907 and lost the FA Cup final in 1909. In the First Division in 1976, they then sank to the bottom professional tier before reforming after a 1982 bankruptcy. They have been above Bristol Rovers in the Football League since 2000, and were promoted to the second tier of English football in 2007.[^cite_note-268] [Bristol City Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_City_Women) were formerly based at [Twerton Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twerton_Park), but now share [Ashton Gate Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashton_Gate_Stadium) as a home venue with the men's team.[^cite_note-269]



[The Memorial Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memorial_Stadium_\(Bristol\)), home of [Bristol Rovers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Rovers)

Bristol Rovers, the oldest professional football team in the city, were formed in 1883 and promoted back into the Football League in 2015. They were third-tier champions twice ([Division Three South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_Three_South) in 1952–53 and [Division Three](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_Three) in 1989–90), [Watney Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watney_Cup) Winners (1972) and runners-up for the [Johnstone's Paint Trophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnstone's_Paint_Trophy) (2006–07), although they have never played in England's top division.

[Bristol Bears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Bears) was formed in 1888 and has often competed at the highest level of rugby union.[^cite_note-270] The club played at the [Memorial Ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memorial_Ground), which it shared with Bristol Rovers from 1996, until moving to Ashton Gate Stadium in 2014.[^cite_note-271][^cite_note-272] They changed their name from Bristol Rugby to Bristol Bears to coincide with their return to [Premiership Rugby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premiership_Rugby) in [the 2018–19 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018-19_English_Premiership).

The [first-class cricket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-class_cricket) club [Gloucestershire County Cricket Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloucestershire_County_Cricket_Club)[^cite_note-273] has its headquarters and plays the majority of its home games at the [Bristol County Ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_County_Ground), the only major international sports venue in the south-west of England. It was formed by the family of [W. G. Grace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._G._Grace).[^cite_note-274]

A large number of hot air balloons taking off from a field which is surrounded by tents and stalls. The sun is low in the sky, and balloons can be seen flying into the distance.

[Bristol International Balloon Fiesta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_International_Balloon_Fiesta)

Bristol sponsors the annual [Bristol Half Marathon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Half_Marathon) and hosted the [2001 IAAF World Half Marathon Championships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_IAAF_World_Half_Marathon_Championships).[^cite_note-275] Bristol has staged finishes and starts of the [Tour of Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tour_of_Britain) cycle race,[^cite_note-276] and facilities in the city were used as training camps for the [2012 London Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_London_Olympics).[^cite_note-277] The [Bristol International Balloon Fiesta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_International_Balloon_Fiesta), a major UK hot-air ballooning event, is held each summer at Ashton Court.[^cite_note-278]

Bristol is the birthplace of the 2025 [Formula 1 World Champion](3437295.md) [Lando Norris](48978813.md).[^cite_note-279]

## Media

Main article: [Media in Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_in_Bristol)



BBC Broadcasting House as seen from Whiteladies Road

Bristol is home to the regional headquarters of [BBC West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_West) and the BBC Natural History Unit based at Broadcasting House, which produces television, radio and online content with a [natural history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_history) or [wildlife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildlife) theme. The city has a long association with [David Attenborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Attenborough)'s authored documentaries, including *[Life on Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_on_Earth_\(TV_series\))*,[^cite_note-280] *[The Blue Planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Blue_Planet)* and *[Planet Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet_Earth_\(franchise\))*.

The BBC announced in 2021 that it was moving the production of many of its programmes from Broadcasting House to [Finzels Reach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finzels_Reach) in the centre of Bristol.[^cite_note-281]

Bristol has two daily newspapers, the *[Western Daily Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Daily_Press)* and the *[Bristol Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Post)* (both owned by [Reach plc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reach_plc)); and a Bristol edition of the free [*Metro*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metro_\(British_newspaper\)) newspaper (owned by [DMGT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DMGT)). *[The Bristol Cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bristol_Cable)* specialises in investigative journalism with a quarterly print edition and website.

Aardman Animations is a Bristol-based animation studio, known for the characters [Wallace & Gromit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallace_&_Gromit) and [Morph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morph_\(animation\)).

Local radio stations, including [BBC Radio Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_Bristol), [Heart West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_West), [Greatest Hits Radio Bristol & The South West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_Hits_Radio_Bristol_&_The_South_West), [Hits Radio Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_FM_\(Bristol\)), [Kiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiss_\(UK_radio_station\)), and [BCfm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCfm), a community based station. Television programmes produced in the city include *[Points West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Points_West)*, Endemol productions such as *[Deal or No Deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deal_or_No_Deal)*, *[The Crystal Maze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crystal_Maze)*, *[Tipping Point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tipping_Point_\(game_show\))* and *[ITV News West Country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITV_News_West_Country)*. Coming-of-age drama *[Skins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skins_\(British_TV_series\))* and supernatural comedy-drama *[Being Human](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Being_Human_\(British_TV_series\))* were also shot in the city. Bristol was the former home of long-running BBC medical soap opera *[Casualty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casualty_\(TV_series\))* until 2011. In October 2018, [Channel 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_4) announced that Bristol would be home to one of its 'Creative Hubs', as part of their move to produce more content outside of London.[^cite_note-282]

Publishers in the city have included 18th-century Bristolian Joseph Cottle, who helped introduce [Romanticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism) by publishing the works of William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMadden1972419-283] During the 19th century, J.W. Arrowsmith published the Victorian comedies *[Three Men in a Boat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Men_in_a_Boat)* (by [Jerome K. Jerome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome_K._Jerome)) and *[The Diary of a Nobody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Diary_of_a_Nobody)* by [George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Grossmith) and [Weedon Grossmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weedon_Grossmith).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJerome1889-284] The contemporary Redcliffe Press has published over 200 books covering all aspects of the city.[^cite_note-285] Bristol is home to [YouTube](3524766.md) video developers and stylists [The Yogscast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Yogscast), with founders Simon Lane and Lewis Brindley having moved their operations from [Reading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reading,_Berkshire) to Bristol in 2012.[^cite_note-286]

## Education

Main article: [Education in Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_Bristol)

&#x20;A Palladian style nineteenth century stone building with a large colonnaded porch. In front a large metal statue on a pedestal and fountains with decorations.

The Victoria Rooms, owned by the University of Bristol

Bristol has two major institutions of higher education: the [University of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bristol), a [redbrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redbrick) chartered in 1909;[^cite_note-287] and the [University of the West of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_the_West_of_England), opened as Bristol [Polytechnic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_technology) in 1969, which became a university in 1992.[^cite_note-288] [The University of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_University_of_Law) also has a campus in the city. Bristol has two [further education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Further_education) institutions ([City of Bristol College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Bristol_College) and [South Gloucestershire and Stroud College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Gloucestershire_and_Stroud_College)) and two [theological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theological) colleges: [Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Bristol), and [Bristol Baptist College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Baptist_College).&#x20;

Bristol has 129 [infant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant_school), [junior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junior_school) and primary schools,[^cite_note-289] [17 secondary schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_schools_in_Bristol),[^cite_note-290] and three learning centres. After a section of north London, Bristol has England's second-highest number of [private school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_schools_in_the_United_Kingdom) places.[^cite_note-curtis-291] Independent schools in Bristol include [Clifton College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton_College), [Clifton High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton_High_School,_Bristol), [Badminton School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badminton_School), [Bristol Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Grammar_School), [Queen Elizabeth's Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Elizabeth's_Hospital) (the only all-boys school) and the [Redmaids' School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redmaids'_School) (founded in 1634 by John Whitson, which claims to be England's oldest girls' school).[^cite_note-292]

### Science city

In 2005, [Chancellor of the Exchequer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_of_the_Exchequer) [Gordon Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Brown) named Bristol one of six English 'science cities',[^cite_note-296] and a £300 million science park was planned at [Emersons Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emersons_Green).[^cite_note-297] Research is conducted at the two universities, the [Bristol Royal Infirmary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Royal_Infirmary) and [Southmead Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southmead_Hospital), and science outreach is practised at [We The Curious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/We_The_Curious), the Bristol Zoo, the [Bristol Festival of Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Festival_of_Nature) and the CREATE Centre.[^cite_note-298]

The city has produced a number of scientists, including 19th-century chemist [Humphry Davy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humphry_Davy)[^cite_note-299] (who worked in [Hotwells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotwells)). Physicist [Paul Dirac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Dirac) (from [Bishopston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishopston,_Bristol)) received the 1933 Nobel Prize for his contributions to [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics).[^cite_note-300] [Cecil Frank Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecil_Frank_Powell) was the Melvill Wills Professor of Physics at the University of Bristol when he received the 1950 Nobel Prize for, among other discoveries, his photographic method of studying nuclear processes. [Colin Pillinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Pillinger)[^cite_note-301] was the planetary scientist behind the [Beagle 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beagle_2) project, and neuropsychologist [Richard Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Gregory) founded the Exploratory (a hands-on science centre which was the predecessor of At-Bristol/We The Curious).[^cite_note-302]

Initiatives such as the [Flying Start Challenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_Start_Challenge) encourage an interest in science and engineering in Bristol secondary-school pupils; links with aerospace companies impart technical information and advance student understanding of design.[^cite_note-303] The [Bloodhound LSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloodhound_LSR) project to break the [land speed record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_speed_record) is based at the Bloodhound Technology Centre on the city's harbourside.[^cite_note-304]

Bristol has two principal railway stations. [Bristol Temple Meads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Temple_Meads), near the city centre, has [Great Western Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Western_Railway_\(train_operating_company\)) services which include high-speed trains to [London Paddington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paddington_railway_station) and local, regional and [CrossCountry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CrossCountry) trains. [Bristol Parkway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Parkway_railway_station), north of the city in Gloucestershire, but within the conurbation, has high-speed Great Western Railway services to [Swansea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swansea_railway_station), [Cardiff Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Central_railway_station) and London Paddington, and CrossCountry services reaching [Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_New_Street_railway_station), [Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Piccadilly_railway_station) and [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh_Waverley_railway_station). There are scheduled coach links to most major UK cities.[^cite_note-progrep-305]



[Bristol Temple Meads station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Temple_Meads_railway_station)

Bristol's principal surviving suburban railway is the [Severn Beach Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severn_Beach_Line) to Avonmouth and [Severn Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severn_Beach). Although [Portishead Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portishead_Railway)'s passenger service was a casualty of the [Beeching cuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beeching_cuts), freight service to the Royal Portbury Dock was restored from 2000 to 2002. The [MetroWest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MetroWest_\(Bristol\)) scheme, formerly known as The Greater Bristol Metro, proposes to increase the city's rail capacity[^cite_note-306] including the restoration of a further 3 mi (5 km) of track on the line to [Portishead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portishead,_Somerset) (a [dormitory town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dormitory_town) with one connecting road), and a further commuter rail line from Bristol Temple Meads to [Henbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henbury,_Bristol), on an [existing freight line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henbury_Loop_Line). Following numerous delays, the two lines are due to be opened in 2026.[^cite_note-307][^cite_note-308]

### Roads

The M4 motorway connects the city on an east–west axis from London to [West Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Wales), and the M5 is a north–southwest axis from Birmingham to Exeter. The [M49 motorway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M49_motorway) is a shortcut between the M5 in the south and the M4 [Severn Crossing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Severn_Crossing) in the west, and the [M32](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M32_motorway) is a spur from the M4 to the city centre.[^cite_note-progrep-305] The [Portway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portway,_Bristol) connects the M5 to the city centre, and was the most expensive road in Britain when opened in 1926.[^cite_note-309][^cite_note-310]

Royal Portbury Dock.

The [Port of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Bristol)

Private car use is high in the city, leading to traffic congestion costing an estimated £350 million per year.[^cite_note-ltp06.1-311] Bristol allows motorcycles to use most of the city's bus lanes and provides secure, free parking for them.[^cite_note-312] In 2022, Bristol implemented a [Clean Air Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_Air_Zone) to reduce pollution by charging the most polluting vehicles to enter the city centre, with the money raised reinvested in transport.[^cite_note-bp-20220706-313]

### Public transport

Further information: [Buses in Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buses_in_Bristol)

Public transport in the city consists primarily of a [First West of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_West_of_England) bus network. Other providers are Abus,[^cite_note-314] [Stagecoach West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagecoach_West), and [Stagecoach South West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagecoach_South_West).[^cite_note-315][^cite_note-316] Bristol's bus service has been criticised as unreliable and expensive, and in 2005 FirstGroup was fined for delays and safety violations.[^cite_note-317][^cite_note-318]

Although the city council has included a [light rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_rail) system in its [local transport plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_transport_plan) since 2000, it has been unsuccessful in funding the project.[^cite_note-319] Instead, Bristol has a network of five [bus rapid transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus_rapid_transit) routes, named [MetroBus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MetroBus_\(Bristol\)), which in part run on dedicated infrastructure. The Metrobus project aimed to provide a faster and more reliable service than standard buses, improving transport infrastructure and reducing congestion,[^cite_note-320] but has been criticised as a limited success because the routes are not fully segregated from other traffic.[^cite_note-321] The first routes opened in 2018,[^cite_note-322] and as of 2025, they connect the city centre with [Emersons Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emersons_Green), [Bristol Parkway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Parkway), [Cribbs Causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cribbs_Causeway), [Hengrove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hengrove), and a [park and ride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park_and_ride_bus_services_in_the_United_Kingdom) near [Long Ashton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Ashton).[^cite_note-323]

Three [park and ride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park_and_ride) sites serve Bristol.[^cite_note-324] The city centre has water transport operated by [Bristol Ferry Boats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Ferry_Boats), [Bristol Packet Boat Trips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Packet_Boat_Trips) and Number Seven Boat Trips, providing leisure and commuter service in the harbour.[^cite_note-325]

### Cycling

Bristol was designated as England's first "cycling city" in 2008 and one of England's 12 "Cycling demonstration" areas.[^cite_note-326] It is home to [Sustrans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustrans), the sustainable transport charity. The [Bristol and Bath Railway Path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_and_Bath_Railway_Path) links it to Bath, and was the first part of the [National Cycle Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Cycle_Network). The city also has urban cycle routes and links with National Cycle Network routes to the rest of the Country. Cycling trips increased by 21% from 2001 to 2005.[^cite_note-ltp06.1-311]

In 2023, [Bristol Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Airport) (BRS), located in neighbouring [North Somerset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Somerset), was ranked the eighth [busiest airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Busiest_airports_in_the_United_Kingdom_by_total_passenger_traffic) in the United Kingdom.
