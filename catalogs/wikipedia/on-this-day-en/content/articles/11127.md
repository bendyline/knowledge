The **Federal Bureau of Investigation** (**FBI**) is the domestic [intelligence](81014.md) and [security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_agency) service of the [United States](3434750.md) and [its principal federal law enforcement agency](16252377.md). An agency of the [United States Department of Justice](52563.md), the FBI is a member of the [U.S. Intelligence Community](501244.md) and reports to both the [attorney general](44002.md) and the [director of national intelligence](456082.md).[^cite_note-intel-3] A leading American [counterterrorism](83392.md), [counterintelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterintelligence), and criminal investigative organization, the FBI has [jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction) over violations of more than 200 categories of [federal crimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_crime_in_the_United_States).[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-quickfacts-5] The FBI maintains a [list of its top 10 most wanted fugitives](237871.md).

Although many of the FBI's functions are unique, its activities in support of [national security](240468.md) are comparable to those of the British [MI5](100076.md) and [NCA](28153284.md) and the Russian [FSB](385990.md). Unlike the [Central Intelligence Agency](5183633.md) (CIA), which has no law enforcement authority and is focused on intelligence collection abroad, the FBI is primarily a domestic agency, maintaining 56 [field offices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_FBI_field_offices) in major cities throughout the United States, and more than 400 resident agencies in smaller cities and areas across the nation. At an FBI field office, a senior-level FBI officer concurrently serves as the representative of the director of national intelligence.[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7]

Despite its domestic focus, the FBI also maintains a significant international footprint, operating 60 Legal Attache (LEGAT) offices and 15 sub-offices in [U.S. embassies and consulates](6654039.md) across the globe. These foreign offices exist primarily for the purpose of coordination with foreign security services and do not usually conduct unilateral operations in the host countries.[^cite_note-8] The FBI can and does at times carry out secret activities overseas,[^cite_note-9] just as the CIA has a [limited domestic function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Resources_Division). These activities generally require coordination across government agencies.

The FBI was established in 1908 as the Bureau of Investigation, the BOI or BI for short. Its name was changed to the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) in 1935.[^cite_note-10] The FBI headquarters is the [J. Edgar Hoover Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Edgar_Hoover_Building) in [Washington, D.C.](108956.md)

## Mission, priorities and budget



FBI Domestic Investigations and Operations Guide (.pdf file)

### Mission

The mission of the FBI is to "protect the American people and uphold the [Constitution of the United States](31644.md)".[^cite_note-\:0-2][^cite_note-\:2-11]

### Priorities

Currently, the FBI's top priorities are:[^cite_note-\:2-11]

- Protect the United States from [terrorist attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrorist_attacks_in_the_United_States)

- Protect the United States against foreign intelligence operations, espionage, and cyber operations

- Combat significant cybercriminal activity

- Combat public [corruption](66220.md) at all levels

- Protect [civil rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_rights_in_the_United_States)

- Combat transnational criminal enterprises

- Combat major [white-collar crime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-collar_crime)

- Combat significant [violent crime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violent_crime_in_the_United_States)

### Budget

In the fiscal year 2019, the Bureau's total budget was approximately \$9.6 billion.[^cite_note-mission-12]

In the Authorization and Budget Request to Congress for fiscal year 2021,[^cite_note-13] the FBI asked for \$9,800,724,000. Of that money, \$9,748,829,000 would be used for Salaries and Expenses (S\&E) and \$51,895,000 for Construction.[^cite_note-\:0-2] The S\&E program saw an increase of \$199,673,000.

## History

### Background

In 1896, the [National Bureau of Criminal Identification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Criminal_Identification) was founded, providing agencies across the country with information to identify known criminals. The [1901 assassination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination_of_William_McKinley) of President [William McKinley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_McKinley) created a perception that the United States was under threat from [anarchists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarchism_in_the_United_States). The [Departments of Justice](52563.md) and [Labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Labor) had been keeping records on anarchists for years, but President [Theodore Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Roosevelt) wanted more power to monitor them.[^cite_note-Weiner-ch2-14]

The Justice Department had been tasked with [the regulation of interstate commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_Commerce_Act_of_1887) since 1887, though it lacked the staff to do so. It had made little effort to relieve its staff shortage until the [Oregon land fraud scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oregon_land_fraud_scandal) at the turn of the 20th century. President Roosevelt instructed Attorney General [Charles Bonaparte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Joseph_Bonaparte) to organize an autonomous investigative service that would report only to the [attorney general](44002.md).[^cite_note-FindlayMemo1943-15]

Bonaparte contacted other agencies, including the [U.S. Secret Service](58221.md), for personnel, investigators in particular. On May 27, 1908, Congress forbade this use of Treasury employees by the Justice Department, citing fears that the new agency would serve as a [secret police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_police) department.[^cite_note-AGreport1908-16] Again at Roosevelt's urging, Bonaparte moved to organize a formal **Bureau of Investigation**, which would then have its own staff of [special agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_agent_\(United_States\)).[^cite_note-Weiner-ch2-14]

### Creation of BOI

The Bureau of Investigation (BOI) was created on July 26, 1908.[^cite_note-17] Attorney General Bonaparte, using Department of Justice expense funds,[^cite_note-Weiner-ch2-14] hired thirty-four people, including some veterans of the Secret Service,[^cite_note-historicdates-18][^cite_note-langeluttig-p9-19] to work for a new investigative agency. Its first "chief" (the title is now "director") was [Stanley Finch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Finch). Bonaparte notified the Congress of these actions in December 1908.[^cite_note-Weiner-ch2-14]

The bureau's first official task was visiting and making surveys of the houses of prostitution in preparation for enforcing the "White Slave Traffic Act" or [Mann Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mann_Act), passed on June 25, 1910. In 1932, the bureau was renamed the United States Bureau of Investigation.

### Creation of FBI

The following year, 1933, the BOI was linked to the [Bureau of Prohibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Prohibition) and rechristened the Division of Investigation (DOI); it became an independent service within the Department of Justice in 1935.[^cite_note-historicdates-18] In the same year, its name was officially changed from the Division of Investigation to the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI).

### J. Edgar Hoover as FBI director



[J. Edgar Hoover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Edgar_Hoover), FBI director from 1924 to 1972

[J. Edgar Hoover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Edgar_Hoover) served as FBI director from 1924 to 1972, a combined 48 years with the BOI, DOI, and FBI. He was chiefly responsible for creating the Scientific Crime Detection Laboratory, or the [FBI Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_Laboratory), which officially opened in 1932, as part of his work to professionalize investigations by the government. Hoover was substantially involved in most major cases and projects that the FBI handled during his tenure. But as detailed below, his tenure as Bureau director proved to be highly controversial, especially in its later years. After Hoover's death, Congress passed legislation that limited the tenure of future FBI directors to ten years.

Early homicide investigations of the new agency included the [Osage Indian murders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osage_Indian_murders). During the "War on Crime" of the 1930s, FBI agents apprehended or killed a number of notorious criminals who committed kidnappings, bank robberies, and murders throughout the nation, including [John Dillinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dillinger), ["Baby Face" Nelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baby_Face_Nelson), [Kate "Ma" Barker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma_Barker), [Alvin "Creepy" Karpis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvin_Karpis), and [George "Machine Gun" Kelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_Gun_Kelly_\(gangster\)).

Other activities of its early decades focused on the scope and influence of the [white supremacist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_supremacist) group [Ku Klux Klan](16779.md), a group with which the FBI was evidenced to be working in the [Viola Liuzzo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viola_Liuzzo) lynching case. Earlier, through the work of [Edwin Atherton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Atherton), the BOI claimed to have successfully apprehended an entire army of Mexican neo-revolutionaries under the leadership of General [Enrique Estrada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrique_Estrada) in the mid-1920s, east of San Diego, California.

Hoover began using [wiretapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone_tapping) in the 1920s during [Prohibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prohibition_in_the_United_States) to arrest bootleggers.[^cite_note-hnn-20] In the 1927 case *[Olmstead v. United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olmstead_v._United_States)*, in which a bootlegger was caught through telephone tapping, the [United States Supreme Court](31737.md) ruled that FBI wiretaps did not violate the [Fourth Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) as unlawful search and seizure, as long as the FBI did not break into a person's home to complete the tapping.[^cite_note-hnn-20] After Prohibition's repeal, [Congress](31756.md) passed the [Communications Act of 1934](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communications_Act_of_1934), which outlawed non-consensual phone tapping, but did allow bugging.[^cite_note-hnn-20] In the 1939 case *Nardone v. United States*, the court ruled that due to the 1934 law, evidence the FBI obtained by phone tapping was inadmissible in court.[^cite_note-hnn-20] After *[Katz v. United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katz_v._United_States)* (1967) overturned *Olmstead*, Congress passed the [Omnibus Crime Control Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnibus_Crime_Control_Act), allowing public authorities to tap telephones during investigations, as long as they obtained warrants beforehand.[^cite_note-hnn-20]

#### National security

Beginning in the 1940s and continuing into the 1970s, the bureau investigated cases of [espionage](10501.md) against the United States and its allies. Eight [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism) agents who had planned [sabotage](29462.md) operations against American targets were arrested, and six were executed (*[Ex parte Quirin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ex_parte_Quirin)*) under their sentences. Also during this time, a joint US/UK code-breaking effort called "the [Venona Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venona_project)"—with which the FBI was heavily involved—broke Soviet diplomatic and intelligence communications codes, allowing the US and British governments to read Soviet communications. This effort confirmed the existence of Americans working in the United States for Soviet intelligence.[^cite_note-nsa-21] Hoover was administering this project, but he failed to notify the [Central Intelligence Agency](5183633.md) (CIA) of it until 1952. Another notable case was the arrest of Soviet spy [Rudolf Abel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vilyam_Genrikhovich_Fisher) in 1957.[^cite_note-22] The discovery of Soviet spies operating in the US motivated Hoover to pursue his longstanding concern with the threat he perceived from the [American Left](28903916.md).

#### Japanese American internment

In 1939, the Bureau began compiling a [custodial detention list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_Index#Custodial_Detention_Index) with the names of those who would be taken into custody in the event of war with Axis nations. The majority of the names on the list belonged to [Issei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Issei) community leaders, as the FBI investigation built on an existing [Naval Intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Naval_Intelligence) index that had focused on [Japanese Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_American) in Hawaii and the West Coast, but many [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internment_of_German_Americans) and [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internment_of_Italian_Americans) nationals also found their way onto the [FBI Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_Index) list.[^cite_note-23] Robert Shivers, head of the Honolulu office, obtained permission from Hoover to start detaining those on the list on December 7, 1941, while bombs were still falling over [Pearl Harbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attack_on_Pearl_Harbor).[^cite_note-Niiya-FBI-24] Mass arrests and searches of homes, in most cases conducted without warrants, began a few hours after the attack, and over the next several weeks more than 5,500 Issei men were taken into FBI custody.[^cite_note-25]

On February 19, 1942, President [Franklin Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) issued [Executive Order 9066](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_9066), authorizing the removal of Japanese Americans from the West Coast. FBI Director Hoover opposed the subsequent mass removal and confinement of Japanese Americans authorized under Executive Order 9066, but Roosevelt prevailed.[^cite_note-26] The vast majority went along with the subsequent exclusion orders, but in a handful of cases where Japanese Americans refused to obey the new military regulations, FBI agents handled their arrests.[^cite_note-Niiya-FBI-24] The Bureau continued surveillance on Japanese Americans throughout the war, conducting background checks on applicants for resettlement outside camp, and entering the camps, usually without the permission of [War Relocation Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_Relocation_Authority) officials, and grooming informants to monitor dissidents and "troublemakers". After the war, the FBI was assigned to protect returning Japanese Americans from attacks by hostile white communities.[^cite_note-Niiya-FBI-24]

#### COMINFIL programs

The FBI's COMINFIL programs (COMINFIL was shorthand for "communist infiltration of") were initiated in the 1940s. COMINFIL investigations gathered intelligence under the following categories: "political activities, legislative activities, domestic administration issues, Negro question, youth matters, women's matters, farmers' matters, cultural activities, veterans matters, religion, education, industry"[^cite_note-27]

Writer Richard Steven Street maintains that COMINFIL programs were designed "to 'expose, disrupt, or otherwise neutralize' and purge Communist Party members and others considered subversive from positions of power."[^cite_note-28] COMINFIL programs allowed the FBI to surveil organizations and individuals, ranging from the Farmworkers' union to the Los Angeles PTA to entire industries, like radio and broadcasting [^cite_note-29]

#### Sex deviates program

According to Douglas M. Charles, the FBI's "sex deviates" program began on April 10, 1950, when J. Edgar Hoover forwarded to the White House, to the U.S. Civil Service Commission, and to branches of the armed services a list of 393 alleged federal employees who had allegedly been arrested in Washington, D.C., since 1947, on charges of "sexual irregularities". On June 20, 1951, Hoover expanded the program by issuing a memo establishing a "uniform policy for the handling of the increasing number of reports and allegations concerning present and past employees of the United States Government who assertedly \[sic] are sex deviates". The program was expanded to include non-government jobs. According to [Athan Theoharis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athan_Theoharis), "In 1951 he \[Hoover] had unilaterally instituted a Sex Deviates program to purge alleged homosexuals from any position in the federal government, from the lowliest clerk to the more powerful position of White House aide." On May 27, 1953, [Executive Order 10450](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_10450) went into effect. The program was expanded further by this executive order by making all federal employment of homosexuals illegal. On July 8, 1953, the FBI forwarded to the U.S. Civil Service Commission information from the sex deviates program. Between 1977 and 1978, 300,000 pages in the sex deviates program, collected between 1930 and the mid-1970s, were destroyed by FBI officials.[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31][^cite_note-32]

#### Civil rights movement

During the 1950s and 1960s, FBI officials became increasingly concerned about the influence of civil rights leaders, whom they believed either had communist ties or were unduly influenced by communists or "[fellow travelers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_traveler)". In 1956, for example, Hoover sent an open letter denouncing Dr. [T. R. M. Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._R._M._Howard), a civil rights leader, surgeon, and wealthy entrepreneur in Mississippi who had criticized FBI inaction in solving recent murders of [George W. Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Lee), [Emmett Till](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmett_Till), and other blacks in the South.[^cite_note-33]

The FBI carried out controversial [domestic surveillance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveillance) in an operation it called the [COINTELPRO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COINTELPRO), from "COunter-INTELligence PROgram".[^cite_note-coinpro-34] It was to investigate and disrupt the activities of dissident political organizations within the United States, including both militant and non-violent organizations. Among its targets was the [Southern Christian Leadership Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Christian_Leadership_Conference), a leading civil rights organization whose clergy leadership included the Rev. Dr. [Martin Luther King Jr.](20076.md).[^cite_note-latimes-35]

The FBI frequently investigated King. In the mid-1960s, King began to criticize the Bureau for giving insufficient attention to the use of terrorism by white supremacists. Hoover responded by publicly calling King the most "notorious liar" in the United States.[^cite_note-37] In his 1991 memoir, *[Washington Post](102226.md)* journalist [Carl Rowan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Rowan) asserted that the FBI had sent at least one anonymous letter to King encouraging him to commit suicide.[^cite_note-washingtonpost-38] Historian [Taylor Branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_Branch) documents an anonymous November 1964 "suicide package" sent by the Bureau that combined a letter to the civil rights leader telling him "You are done. There is only one way out for you." with audio recordings of King's sexual indiscretions.[^cite_note-39]

In March 1971, the residential office of an FBI agent in [Media, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media,_Pennsylvania), was burgled by a group calling itself the [Citizens' Commission to Investigate the FBI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizens'_Commission_to_Investigate_the_FBI). Numerous files were taken and distributed to a range of newspapers, including *[The Harvard Crimson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Harvard_Crimson)*.[^cite_note-'70s_2-40] The files detailed the FBI's extensive [COINTELPRO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COINTELPRO) program, which included investigations into lives of ordinary citizens—including a black student group at a Pennsylvania military college and the daughter of Congressman [Henry S. Reuss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_S._Reuss) of [Wisconsin](33127.md).[^cite_note-'70s_2-40] The country was "jolted" by the revelations, which included assassinations of political activists, and the actions were denounced by members of the Congress, including House Majority Leader [Hale Boggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hale_Boggs).[^cite_note-'70s_2-40] The phones of some members of the Congress, including Boggs, had allegedly been tapped.[^cite_note-'70s_2-40]

#### Kennedy's assassination

When President [John F. Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy) was shot and killed, the jurisdiction fell to the local police departments until President [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson) directed the FBI to take over the investigation.[^cite_note-history_postwar-41] To ensure clarity about the responsibility for investigation of homicides of federal officials, Congress passed a law in 1965 that included investigations of such deaths of federal officials, especially by homicide, within FBI jurisdiction.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44]

### Organized crime



An FBI surveillance photograph of [Joseph D. Pistone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_D._Pistone) (aka Donnie Brasco), [Benjamin "Lefty" Ruggiero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Ruggiero) and [Edgar Robb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Robb) (aka Tony Rossi), 1980s

In response to organized crime, on August 25, 1953, the FBI created the [Top Hoodlum Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top_Hoodlum_Program?action=edit\&redlink=1). The national office directed field offices to gather information on [mobsters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gangster) in their territories and to report it regularly to Washington for a centralized collection of intelligence on [racketeers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racketeering).[^cite_note-45] After the [Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racketeer_Influenced_and_Corrupt_Organizations_Act), for RICO Act, took effect, the FBI began investigating the former Prohibition-organized groups, which had become fronts for crime in major cities and small towns. All the FBI work was done undercover and from within these organizations, using the provisions provided in the RICO Act. Gradually the agency dismantled many of the groups. Although Hoover initially denied the existence of a [National Crime Syndicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Crime_Syndicate) in the United States, the Bureau later conducted operations against known organized crime syndicates and families, including those headed by [Sam Giancana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Giancana) and [John Gotti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gotti). The RICO Act is still used today for all [organized crime](22625.md) and any individuals who may fall under the act's provisions.

In 2003, a congressional committee called the FBI's organized crime [informant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Informant) program "one of the greatest failures in the history of federal law enforcement".[^cite_note-Murphy-46] The FBI allowed four innocent men to be convicted of the [March 1965 gangland murder of Edward "Teddy" Deegan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Barboza#False_testimony_against_rivals) in order to protect [Vincent Flemmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Flemmi), an FBI informant. Three of the men were sentenced to death (which was later reduced to life in prison), and the fourth defendant was sentenced to life in prison.[^cite_note-Murphy-46] Two of the four men died in prison after serving almost 30 years, and two others were released after serving 32 and 36 years. In July 2007, U.S. District Judge [Nancy Gertner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy_Gertner) in Boston found that the bureau had helped convict the four men using false witness accounts given by mobster [Joseph Barboza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Barboza). The U.S. Government was ordered to pay \$100 million in damages to the four defendants.[^cite_note-47]

### Special FBI teams



&#x20;[FBI SWAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_Special_Weapons_and_Tactics_Teams) agents in a training exercise

In 1982, the FBI formed an elite unit[^cite_note-history_rise-48] to help with problems that might arise at the [1984 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984_Summer_Olympics) to be held in Los Angeles, particularly [terrorism](30636.md) and major-crime. This was a result of the [1972 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1972_Summer_Olympics) in [Munich, Germany](19058.md), when [terrorists murdered the Israeli athletes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich_massacre). Named the [Hostage Rescue Team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hostage_Rescue_Team), or HRT, it acts as a dedicated FBI [SWAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SWAT) team dealing primarily with counter-terrorism scenarios. Unlike the special agents serving on local [FBI SWAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_SWAT) teams, HRT does not conduct investigations. Instead, HRT focuses solely on additional tactical proficiency and capabilities. Also formed in 1984 was the *Computer Analysis and Response Team*, or CART.[^cite_note-history_coldwarend-49]

From the late 1980s to the early 1990s, the FBI reassigned more than 300 agents from foreign counter-intelligence duties to violent crime, and made violent crime the sixth national priority. With cuts to other well-established departments, and because terrorism was no longer considered a threat after the end of the [Cold War](325329.md),[^cite_note-history_coldwarend-49] the FBI assisted local and state police forces in tracking fugitives who had crossed state lines, which is a federal offense. The FBI Laboratory helped develop [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA) testing, continuing its pioneering role in identification that began with its fingerprinting system in 1924.

### Notable efforts in the 1990s



FBI agent tagging the [cockpit voice recorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cockpit_voice_recorder) from [EgyptAir Flight 990](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EgyptAir_Flight_990) on the deck of [USNS *Grapple*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USNS_Grapple) at the crash site on November 13, 1999

On May 1, 1992, FBI SWAT and HRT personnel in [Los Angeles County, California](79734.md) aided local officials in securing peace within the area during the [1992 Los Angeles riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Los_Angeles_riots). HRT operators, for instance, spent 10 days conducting vehicle-mounted patrols throughout [Los Angeles](18110.md), before returning to Virginia.[^cite_note-50]

Between 1993 and 1996, the FBI increased its [counter-terrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter-terrorism) role following the first [1993 World Trade Center bombing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1993_World_Trade_Center_bombing) in [New York City](645042.md), the 1995 [Oklahoma City bombing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City_bombing), and the arrest of the [Unabomber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Kaczynski) in 1996. Technological innovation and the skills of FBI Laboratory analysts helped ensure that the three cases were successfully prosecuted.[^cite_note-history_wired-51] However, Justice Department investigations into the FBI's roles in the [Ruby Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruby_Ridge) and [Waco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waco_siege) incidents were found to have been obstructed by agents within the Bureau. During the [1996 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Summer_Olympics) in [Atlanta, Georgia](3138.md), the FBI was criticized for its investigation of the [Centennial Olympic Park bombing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centennial_Olympic_Park_bombing). It has settled a dispute with [Richard Jewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Jewell), who was a private security guard at the venue, along with some media organizations.[^cite_note-leak-52]

In response to the [1998 U.S. embassy bombings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_U.S._embassy_bombings) in [Kenya](188171.md) and [Tanzania](30118.md), the FBI developed its *Strategic Plan 1998–2003* to prioritize counterterrorism and national security.[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54] This plan introduced a three-tiered system to classify and prioritize investigative programs agency-wide. Tier One, the highest priority, included national security programs such as counterterrorism and counterintelligence; Tier Two encompassed major criminal investigations, such as organized crime and white-collar crime, while Tier Three covered lower-priority matters, including "the most significant" crimes against property and individuals.[^cite_note-55]

The September 11, 2001 attacks accelerated the FBI's implementation of the tier system, leading to a significant reallocation of resources toward Tier One programs. A 2003 audit by the Department of Justice [Office of the Inspector General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Inspector_General_\(United_States\)) (OIG) reported that the average number of agents assigned to terrorism-related investigations more than doubled—from 2,126 in [FY](5752099.md) 2000 to 4,680 by FY 2002—largely due to the expansion of the Counterterrorism Division's Joint Terrorism Task Forces and related initiatives.[^cite_note-56]

### September 11 attacks



September 11 attacks at the Pentagon

During the [September 11, 2001, attacks](5058690.md) on the [World Trade Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Trade_Center_\(1973%E2%80%932001\)), FBI agent [Leonard W. Hatton Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_W._Hatton_Jr.) was killed during the rescue effort while helping the rescue personnel evacuate the occupants of the South Tower, and he stayed when it collapsed. Within months after the attacks, FBI director [Robert Mueller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Mueller), who had been sworn in a week before the attacks, called for a re-engineering of FBI structure and operations. He made countering every federal crime a top priority, including the prevention of terrorism, countering foreign intelligence operations, addressing cybersecurity threats, other high-tech crimes, protecting civil rights, combating public corruption, organized crime, white-collar crime, and major acts of violent crime.[^cite_note-history_current-57]

In February 2001, [Robert Hanssen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hanssen) was caught selling information to the Russian government. It was later learned that Hanssen, who had reached a high position within the FBI, had been selling intelligence since as early as 1979. He pleaded guilty to [espionage](10501.md) and received a [life sentence](148681.md) in 2002, but the incident led many to question the security practices employed by the FBI. There was also a claim that Hanssen might have contributed information that led to the September 11, 2001, attacks.[^cite_note-9_11-58]

The [9/11 Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/9/11_Commission)'s final report on July 22, 2004, stated that the FBI and [Central Intelligence Agency](5183633.md) (CIA) were both partially to blame for not pursuing intelligence reports that could have prevented the September 11 attacks. In its most damning assessment, the report concluded that the country had "not been well served" by either agency and listed numerous recommendations for changes within the FBI.[^cite_note-abc-59] While the FBI did accede to most of the recommendations, including oversight by the new [director of National Intelligence](456082.md), some former members of the 9/11 Commission publicly criticized the FBI in October 2005, claiming it was resisting any meaningful changes.[^cite_note-cbsnews-60]

On July 8, 2007, *[The Washington Post](102226.md)* published excerpts from [UCLA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles) Professor Amy Zegart's book *Spying Blind: The CIA, the FBI, and the Origins of 9/11*.[^cite_note-cis-61] The *Post* reported, from Zegart's book, that government documents showed that both the CIA and the FBI had missed 23 potential chances to disrupt the terrorist attacks of September 11, 2001. The primary reasons for the failures included: agency cultures resistant to change and new ideas; inappropriate incentives for promotion; and a lack of cooperation between the FBI, CIA, and the rest of the [United States Intelligence Community](501244.md). The book blamed the FBI's decentralized structure, which prevented effective communication and cooperation among different FBI offices. The book suggested that the FBI had not evolved into an effective counter-terrorism or counter-intelligence agency, due in large part to deeply ingrained agency cultural resistance to change. For example, FBI personnel practices continued to treat all staff other than special agents as support staff, classifying [intelligence analysts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_analysis) alongside the FBI's auto mechanics and janitors.[^cite_note-wpz-62]

### Faulty bullet analysis

For over 40 years, the FBI crime lab in Quantico had believed that lead alloys used in bullets had unique chemical signatures. It was analyzing the bullets with the goal of matching them chemically, not only to a single batch of ammunition coming out of a factory, but also to a single box of bullets. The [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences) conducted an 18-month independent review of [comparative bullet-lead analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_bullet-lead_analysis). In 2003, its National Research Council published a report whose conclusions called into question 30 years of FBI testimony. It found the analytic model used by the FBI for interpreting results was deeply flawed, and the conclusion, that bullet fragments could be matched to a box of ammunition, was so overstated that it was misleading under the rules of evidence. One year later, the FBI decided to stop conducting bullet lead analyses.[^cite_note-63]

After a *[60 Minutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/60_Minutes)*/*[The Washington Post](102226.md)* investigation in November 2007, two years later, the Bureau agreed to identify, review, and release all pertinent cases, and notify prosecutors about cases in which faulty testimony was given.[^cite_note-64]

### Technology

In 2012, the FBI formed the [National Domestic Communications Assistance Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Domestic_Communications_Assistance_Center) to develop technology for assisting law enforcement with technical knowledge regarding communication services, technologies, and electronic surveillance.[^cite_note-65]

### January 6 United States Capitol attack

An FBI informant who participated in the [January 6 United States Capitol attack](66307490.md) on democratic institutions in Washington D.C. later testified in support of the [Proud Boys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proud_Boys), who were part of the plot. Revelations about the informant raised fresh questions about intelligence failures by the FBI before the riot. According to the [Brennan Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brennan_Center) and [United States Senate committees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Senate_committees), the FBI's response to white supremacist violence was "woefully inadequate". The FBI has long been suspected to have turned a blind eye towards right-wing extremists while disseminating conspiracy theories on the [origin of SARS-CoV-2](66389098.md).[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68]

## Legal authority



FBI badge and service pistol, a [Glock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glock) Model 22, .40 S\&W caliber

The FBI's mandate is established in [Title 28 of the United States Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_28_of_the_United_States_Code) (U.S. Code), Section 533, which authorizes the [Attorney General](44002.md) to "appoint officials to detect and prosecute crimes against the United States".[^cite_note-uscode-76] Other federal statutes give the FBI the authority and responsibility to investigate specific crimes.

The FBI's chief tool against [organized crime](22625.md) is the [Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racketeer_Influenced_and_Corrupt_Organizations_Act) (RICO) Act. The FBI is also charged with the responsibility of enforcing compliance of the United States [Civil Rights Act of 1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1964) and investigating violations of the act in addition to prosecuting such violations with the [United States Department of Justice](52563.md) (DOJ). The FBI also shares concurrent jurisdiction with the [Drug Enforcement Administration](146720.md) (DEA) in the enforcement of the [Controlled Substances Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_Substances_Act) of 1970.

The [USA PATRIOT Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriot_Act) increased the powers allotted to the FBI, especially in [wiretapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone_tapping) and monitoring of Internet activity. One of the most controversial provisions of the act is the so-called *[sneak and peek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sneak_and_peek_warrant)* provision, granting the FBI powers to search a house while the residents are away, and not requiring them to notify the residents for several weeks afterward. Under the PATRIOT Act's provisions, the FBI also resumed inquiring into the [library](17727.md) records[^cite_note-library-77] of those who are suspected of [terrorism](30636.md) (something it had supposedly not done since the 1970s).

In the early 1980s, Senate hearings were held to examine FBI undercover operations in the wake of the [Abscam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abscam) controversy, which had allegations of [entrapment](216694.md) of elected officials. As a result, in the following years a number of guidelines were issued to constrain FBI activities.

Information obtained through an FBI investigation is presented to the appropriate [U.S. Attorney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Attorney) or Department of Justice official, who decides if prosecution or other action is warranted.

The FBI often works in conjunction with other federal agencies, including the [U.S. Coast Guard](32223.md) (USCG) and [U.S. Customs and Border Protection](757040.md) (CBP) in seaport and airport security,[^cite_note-sea-78] and the [National Transportation Safety Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Transportation_Safety_Board) in investigating [airplane crashes](164536.md) and other critical incidents. [Immigration and Customs Enforcement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Immigration_and_Customs_Enforcement)'s Homeland Security Investigations (HSI) has nearly the same amount of investigative manpower as the FBI and investigates the largest range of crimes. In the wake of the [September 11 attacks](5058690.md), then–Attorney General Ashcroft assigned the FBI as the designated lead organization in terrorism investigations after the creation of the [U.S. Department of Homeland Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Department_of_Homeland_Security). HSI and the FBI are both integral members of the [Joint Terrorism Task Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_Terrorism_Task_Force).

## Infrastructure



The [J. Edgar Hoover Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Edgar_Hoover_Building), FBI headquarters



FBI Mobile Command Center, [Washington Field Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_FBI_field_offices)

The FBI is headquartered at the [J. Edgar Hoover Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Edgar_Hoover_Building) in [Washington, D.C.](108956.md), with 56 field offices[^cite_note-organization-85] in major cities across the United States. The FBI also maintains over 400 resident agencies across the United States, as well as over 50 legal attachés at United States [embassies](8970.md) and [consulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consul_\(representative\)). Many specialized FBI functions are located at facilities in [Quantico, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantico,_Virginia), as well as a "data campus" in [Clarksburg, West Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarksburg,_West_Virginia), where 96 million sets of fingerprints "from across the United States are stored, along with others collected by American authorities from prisoners in [Saudi Arabia](349303.md) and [Yemen](350939.md), [Iraq](7515928.md) and [Afghanistan](737.md)".[^cite_note-WaPo-86] The FBI is in the process of moving its Records Management Division, which processes [Freedom of Information Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_Information_Act_\(United_States\)) (FOIA) requests, to [Winchester, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winchester,_Virginia).[^cite_note-87]

According to *[The Washington Post](102226.md)*, the FBI "is building a vast repository controlled by people who work in a top-secret vault on the fourth floor of the J. Edgar Hoover Building in [Washington](108956.md). This one stores the profiles of tens of thousands of Americans and legal residents who are not accused of any crime. What they have done is appear to be acting suspiciously to a town sheriff, a traffic cop or even a neighbor."[^cite_note-WaPo-86]

The [FBI Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_Laboratory), established with the formation of the BOI,[^cite_note-labhistory-88] did not appear in the J. Edgar Hoover Building until its completion in 1974. The lab serves as the primary lab for most DNA, biological, and physical work. Public tours of FBI headquarters ran through the FBI laboratory workspace before the move to the J. Edgar Hoover Building. The services the lab conducts include Chemistry, Combined DNA Index System (CODIS), Computer Analysis and Response, DNA Analysis, Evidence Response, Explosives, Firearms and Tool marks, Forensic Audio, Forensic Video, Image Analysis, Forensic Science Research, Forensic Science Training, Hazardous Materials Response, Investigative and Prospective Graphics, Latent Prints, Materials Analysis, Questioned Documents, Racketeering Records, Special Photographic Analysis, Structural Design, and Trace Evidence.[^cite_note-labwork-89] The services of the FBI Laboratory are used by many state, local, and international agencies free of charge. The lab also maintains a second lab at the FBI Academy.

The [FBI Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_Academy), located in [Quantico, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantico,_Virginia), is home to the communications and computer laboratory the FBI utilizes. It is also where new agents are sent for training to become FBI special agents. Going through the 21-week course is required for every special agent.[^cite_note-school-90] First opened for use in 1972, the facility is located on 385 acres (156 hectares) of woodland. The Academy trains state and local law enforcement agencies, which are invited to the law enforcement training center. The FBI units that reside at Quantico are the Field and Police Training Unit, Firearms Training Unit, Forensic Science Research and Training Center, Technology Services Unit (TSU), Investigative Training Unit, Law Enforcement Communication Unit, Leadership and Management Science Units (LSMU), Physical Training Unit, New Agents' Training Unit (NATU), Practical Applications Unit (PAU), the Investigative Computer Training Unit, and the College of Analytical Studies.



The [FBI Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_Academy), located in [Quantico, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantico,_Virginia)

In 2000, the FBI began the Trilogy project to upgrade its outdated [information technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_technology) (IT) infrastructure. This project, originally scheduled to take three years and cost around \$380 million, ended up over budget and behind schedule.[^cite_note-vcf-91] Efforts to deploy modern computers and networking equipment were generally successful, but attempts to develop new investigation software, outsourced to [Science Applications International Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_Applications_International_Corporation) (SAIC), were not. [Virtual Case File](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_Case_File), or VCF, as the software was known, was plagued by poorly defined goals, and repeated changes in management.[^cite_note-vcf2-92]

In January 2005, more than two years after the software was originally planned for completion, the FBI abandoned the project. At least \$100 million, and much more by some estimates, was spent on the project, which never became operational. The FBI has been forced to continue using its decade-old Automated Case Support system, which [IT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_technology) experts consider woefully inadequate. In March 2005, the FBI announced it was beginning a new, more ambitious software project, code-named Sentinel, which they expected to complete by 2009.[^cite_note-sentinel-93]



The FBI Field Office in [Chelsea, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chelsea,_Massachusetts)

[Carnivore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnivore_\(software\)) was an electronic eavesdropping software system implemented by the FBI during the Clinton administration; it was designed to monitor email and electronic communications. After prolonged negative coverage in the press, the FBI changed the name of its system from "Carnivore" to "DCS1000". DCS is reported to stand for "Digital Collection System"; the system has the same functions as before. The [Associated Press](18935732.md) reported in mid-January 2005 that the FBI essentially abandoned the use of Carnivore in 2001, in favor of commercially available software, such as NarusInsight.

The [Criminal Justice Information Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_Criminal_Justice_Information_Services_Division) (CJIS) Division[^cite_note-cjis-94] is located in [Clarksburg, West Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarksburg,_West_Virginia). Organized beginning in 1991, the office opened in 1995 as the youngest agency division. The complex is the length of three football fields. It provides a main repository for information in various data systems. Under the roof of the CJIS are the programs for the *National Crime Information Center* (NCIC), *Uniform Crime Reporting* (UCR), *Fingerprint Identification*, *Integrated Automated Fingerprint Identification System* (IAFIS), *NCIC 2000*, and the *National Incident-Based Reporting System* (NIBRS). Many state and local agencies use these data systems as a source for their own investigations and contribute to the database using secure communications. FBI provides these tools of sophisticated identification and information services to local, state, federal, and international law enforcement agencies.

The FBI heads the [National Virtual Translation Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Virtual_Translation_Center), which provides "timely and accurate translations of foreign intelligence for all elements of the [Intelligence Community](501244.md)".[^cite_note-Virtual_Translators_and_FBI-95]

In June 2021, the FBI held a groundbreaking for its planned FBI Innovation Center, set to be built in [Huntsville, Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huntsville,_Alabama). The Innovation Center is to be part of a large, college-like campus costing a total of \$1.3 billion in [Redstone Arsenal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redstone_Arsenal) and will act as a center for [cyber threat intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyber_threat_intelligence), [data analytics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytics), and emerging threat training.[^cite_note-96]

### Hiring process

To apply to become an FBI agent, one must be between the ages of 23 and 37, unless one is a preference-eligible [veteran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veteran), in which case one may apply after age 37.[^cite_note-99] The applicant must also hold U.S. citizenship, be of high moral character, have a clean record, and hold at least a four-year [bachelor's degree](188874.md). At least three years of professional work experience prior to application is also required. All FBI employees require a Top Secret (TS) [security clearance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_clearance), and in many instances, employees need a TS/SCI ([Top Secret](3539962.md#Top_Secret)/[Sensitive Compartmented Information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collateral_clearance)) clearance.[^cite_note-tssci-100]

To obtain a security clearance, all potential FBI personnel must pass a series of [Single Scope Background Investigations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_Scope_Background_Investigation) (SSBI), which are conducted by the [Office of Personnel Management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Office_of_Personnel_Management).[^cite_note-opm-101] Special agent candidates also have to pass a Physical Fitness Test (PFT), which includes a 300-meter run, one-minute sit-ups, maximum push-ups, and a 1.5-mile (2.4 km) run. Personnel must pass a [polygraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygraph) test with questions including possible drug use.[^cite_note-FAQ-FBI_Jobs-102] Applicants who fail polygraphs may not gain employment with the FBI.[^cite_note-103] Up until 1975, the FBI had a minimum height requirement of 5 feet 7 inches (170 cm).[^cite_note-104]

### BOI and FBI directors

Main article: [Director of the Federal Bureau of Investigation](5861065.md)

[FBI directors](5861065.md) are [appointed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_positions_filled_by_presidential_appointment_with_Senate_confirmation) (nominated) by the [president of the United States](24113.md) and must be confirmed by the [United States Senate](24909346.md) to serve a term of office of ten years, subject to resignation or removal by the president at his/her discretion before their term ends. Additional terms are allowed following the same procedure.

[J. Edgar Hoover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Edgar_Hoover), appointed by President [Calvin Coolidge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvin_Coolidge) in 1924, was by far the longest-serving director, serving until his death in 1972. In 1968, Congress passed legislation, as part of the *Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act of 1968*, requiring Senate confirmation of appointments of future directors.[^cite_note-105] As the incumbent, this legislation did not apply to Hoover. The last FBI director was [Christopher Wray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_A._Wray). The current FBI director is [Kash Patel](62202727.md), appointed by President [Donald Trump](4848272.md).

The FBI director is responsible for the day-to-day operations at the FBI. Along with the [deputy director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deputy_Director_of_the_Federal_Bureau_of_Investigation), the director makes sure cases and operations are handled correctly. The director also is in charge of making sure the leadership in the FBI [field offices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_FBI_field_offices) is staffed with qualified agents. Before the [Intelligence Reform and Terrorism Prevention Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_Reform_and_Terrorism_Prevention_Act) was passed in the wake of the [September 11 attacks](5058690.md), the FBI director would directly brief the president of the United States on any issues that arise from within the FBI. Since then, the director now reports to the [director of national intelligence](456082.md) (DNI), who in turn reports to the President.

## Firearms



A Glock 22 pistol in .40 S\&W caliber

Upon qualification, an FBI special agent is issued a full-size [Glock 22](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glock_22) or compact Glock 23 [semi-automatic pistol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-automatic_pistol), both of which are chambered in the [.40 S\&W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/.40_S\&W) [cartridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartridge_\(firearms\)). In May 1997, the FBI officially adopted the Glock, in .40 S\&W, for general agent use, and first issued it to New Agent Class 98-1 in October 1997. At present, the Glock 23 "FG\&R" (finger groove and rail; either 3rd generation or "Gen4") is the issue sidearm.[^cite_note-106]

New agents are issued firearms, on which they must qualify, on successful completion of their training at the [FBI Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_Academy). The Glock 26 (subcompact [9×19mm Parabellum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/9%C3%9719mm_Parabellum)), Glock 23 and Glock 27 (.40 S\&W compact and subcompact, respectively) are authorized as secondary weapons. Special agents are also authorized to purchase and qualify with the [Glock 21](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glock_21) in [.45 ACP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/.45_ACP).[^cite_note-107]

Special agents of the FBI [Hostage Rescue Team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hostage_Rescue_Team) (HRT) and [regional SWAT teams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_Special_Weapons_and_Tactics_Teams) are issued the [Springfield Armory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springfield_Armory,_Inc.) [Professional Model 1911](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M1911_pistol) pistol in .45 ACP.[^cite_note-108][^cite_note-109][^cite_note-110]

In June 2016, the FBI awarded [Glock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glock_Ges.m.b.H.) a contract for new handguns. Unlike the currently issued .40 S\&W chambered Glock pistols, the new Glocks will be chambered for 9x19mm Parabellum. The contract is for the full-size Glock 17M and the compact Glock 19M. The "M" means the Glocks have been modified to meet government standards specified by a 2015 government [request for proposal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Request_for_proposal).[^cite_note-111][^cite_note-112][^cite_note-113][^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115]

## Publications

The *[FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_Law_Enforcement_Bulletin)* is published monthly by the FBI Law Enforcement Communication Unit,[^cite_note-lecu-116] with articles of interest to state and local [law enforcement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_enforcement_agency) personnel. First published in 1932 as *Fugitives Wanted by Police*,[^cite_note-history_newdeal-117] the *FBI Law Bulletin* covers topics including law enforcement technology and issues, such as [crime mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_mapping) and [use of force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Use_of_force), as well as recent [criminal justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_justice) research, and [ViCAP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violent_Criminal_Apprehension_Program) alerts, on wanted suspects and key cases.

The FBI also publishes some reports for both law enforcement personnel as well as regular citizens covering topics including law enforcement, [terrorism](30636.md), [cybercrime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_crime), [white-collar crime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-collar_crime), [violent crime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violent_crime), and statistics.[^cite_note-pubs-118] The vast majority of [federal government](195149.md) publications covering these topics are published by the [Office of Justice Programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Justice_Programs) agencies of the [United States Department of Justice](52563.md), and disseminated through the [National Criminal Justice Reference Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Criminal_Justice_Reference_Service).

### Crime statistics

During the 1920s the FBI began issuing crime reports by gathering numbers from local police departments.[^cite_note-'70s-119] Due to limitations of this system that were discovered during the 1960s and 1970s—victims often simply did not report crimes to the police in the first place—the [Department of Justice](52563.md) developed an alternative method of tallying crime, the victimization survey.[^cite_note-'70s-119]

#### Uniform Crime Reports

Main article: [Uniform Crime Reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_Crime_Reports)

The Uniform Crime Reports (UCR) compile data from over 17,000 law enforcement agencies across the country. They provide detailed data regarding the volume of crimes to include arrest, clearance (or closing a case), and law enforcement officer information. The UCR focuses its data collection on violent crimes, hate crimes, and property crimes.[^cite_note-pubs-118] Created in the 1920s, the UCR system has not proven to be as *uniform* as its name implies. The UCR data only reflect the most serious offense in the case of connected crimes and has a very restrictive definition of rape. Since about 93% of the data submitted to the FBI is in this format, the UCR stands out as the publication of choice as most states require law enforcement agencies to submit this data.

Preliminary Annual *Uniform Crime Report* for 2006 was released on June 4, 2006. The report shows violent crime offenses rose 1.3%, but the number of property crime offenses decreased 2.9% compared to 2005.[^cite_note-publications_ucr2006-120]

#### National Incident-Based Reporting System

Main article: [National Incident-Based Reporting System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Incident-Based_Reporting_System)

The National Incident-Based Reporting System (NIBRS) [crime statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_statistics) system aims to address limitations inherent in UCR data. The system is used by law enforcement agencies in the United States for collecting and reporting data on crimes. Local, state, and federal agencies generate NIBRS data from their records management systems. Data is collected on every incident and arrest in the Group A offense category. The Group A offenses are 46 specific crimes grouped in 22 offense categories. Specific facts about these offenses are gathered and reported in the NIBRS system. In addition to the Group A offenses, eleven Group B offenses are reported with only the arrest information. The NIBRS system is in greater detail than the summary-based UCR system. As of 2004, 5,271 law enforcement agencies submitted NIBRS data. That amount represents 20% of the United States population and 16% of the crime statistics data collected by the FBI.

## eGuardian

eGuardian is the name of an FBI system, launched in January 2009, to share tips about possible terror threats with local police agencies. The program aims to get law enforcement at all levels sharing data quickly about suspicious activity and people.[^cite_note-121]

eGuardian enables near real-time sharing and tracking of terror information and suspicious activities with local, state, tribal, and federal agencies. The eGuardian system is a spin-off of a similar but classified tool called Guardian that has been used inside the FBI, and shared with vetted partners since 2005.[^cite_note-122]

## Media portrayal

Main article: [FBI portrayal in media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_portrayal_in_media)



The popular TV series *[The X-Files](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_X-Files)* depicts the fictional FBI Special Agents [Dana Scully](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dana_Scully) ([Gillian Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gillian_Anderson)) and [Fox Mulder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_Mulder) ([David Duchovny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Duchovny)) who investigate [paranormal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paranormal) phenomena.

The FBI has been frequently depicted in popular media since the 1930s. The bureau has participated to varying degrees, which has ranged from direct involvement in the creative process of film or TV series development, to providing consultation on operations and closed cases.[^cite_note-162] A few of the notable portrayals of the FBI on television are the series *[The X-Files](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_X-Files)*, which started in 1993 and concluded its eleventh season in early 2018, and concerned investigations into [paranormal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paranormal) phenomena by five fictional special agents, and the fictional Counter Terrorist Unit (CTU) agency in the TV drama *[24](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/24_\(TV_series\))*, which is patterned after the [FBI Counterterrorism Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_Counterterrorism_Division).

The 1991 movie *[Point Break](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_Break_\(1991_film\))* depicts an undercover FBI agent who infiltrated a gang of bank robbers. The 1997 movie *[Donnie Brasco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donnie_Brasco_\(film\))* is based on the true story of undercover FBI agent [Joseph D. Pistone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_D._Pistone) infiltrating the Mafia. The 2005–2020 television series *[Criminal Minds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_Minds)*, that follows the team members of the FBI's [Behavioral Analysis Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_Analysis_Unit) (BAU) in the pursuit of serial killers. The 2017 TV series *[Riverdale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverdale_\(American_TV_series\))* where one of the main characters is an FBI agent. The 2015 TV series *[Quantico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantico_\(TV_series\))*, titled after the location of the Bureau's training facility, deals with probationary and special agents, not all of whom, within the show's format, may be fully reliable or even trustworthy.

The 2018 series *[FBI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_\(TV_series\))*, set in NYC that follows the personal and professional lives of the agents assigned to 26 Federal Plaza (NYC FBI field office). *FBI*'s first spin-off, titled *[FBI: Most Wanted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI:_Most_Wanted)* (2020), follows the FBI's Fugitive Task Force in chasing down the America's most wanted criminals, and the second spin-off, *[FBI: International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI:_International)* (2021), follows the FBI's International Fly Team that goes wherever they are needed in the world to protect the American's interests.
