**Athens**[^cite_note-7] is the [capital](181337.md) and [largest city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_and_towns_in_Greece) of [Greece](12108.md). A significant coastal urban area in the Mediterranean, Athens is also the capital of the [Attica region](26280929.md) and is the southernmost capital on the [European mainland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_mainland). With its urban area's population numbering over 3.6 million, it is the [eighth-largest urban area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_urban_areas_in_the_European_Union) in the [European Union](9317.md) (EU). The [Municipality of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipality_of_Athens) (also City of Athens), which constitutes a small administrative unit of the entire urban area, had a population of 643,452 in 2021,[^cite_note-census21-4] within its official limits, and a land area of 38.96 square kilometres (15.04 square miles).[^cite_note-stat01-8][^cite_note-area-9] Athens joined the UNESCO Global Network of Learning Cities in 2016.[^cite_note-UNESCO_UIL-GNLC-10]

Athens is one of the [world's oldest cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_oldest_continuously_inhabited_cities), with its [recorded history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recorded_history) spanning over 3,400 years,[^cite_note-11] and its earliest human presence beginning somewhere between the 11th and 7th millennia BC. According to [Greek mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mythology), the city was named after [Athena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena), the ancient Greek goddess of wisdom, but modern scholars generally agree that the goddess took her name after the city.[^cite_note-12] [Classical Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Athens) was one of the most powerful [city-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polis) in [ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece). It was a centre for [Ancient Greek democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_democracy), [the arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_art), education and [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_philosophy),[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14] and was highly influential throughout the [Hellenistic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_world) and the European continent, particularly in [ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome).[^cite_note-15] For this reason it is often regarded as the [cradle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cradle_of_civilization) of [Western civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_civilization) and the [birthplace of democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_democracy) in its own right independently from the rest of Greece.[^cite_note-britannica-16][^cite_note-bbc-17]

In modern times Athens is a large cosmopolitan [metropolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolis) and central to economic, financial, industrial, maritime, political and cultural life in Greece. It is a [Beta (+)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globalization_and_World_Cities_Research_Network#Beta) – status [global city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_city) according to the [Globalization and World Cities Research Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globalization_and_World_Cities_Research_Network),[^cite_note-18] and is one of the biggest economic centres in [Southeast Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Europe). It also has a large financial sector, and its port [Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus) is both the second-busiest passenger port in Europe[^cite_note-ESPO-19] and the thirteenth-largest container port in the world.[^cite_note-20] The [Athens metropolitan area](72650858.md)[^cite_note-21] extends beyond its administrative municipal city limits as well as its [urban agglomeration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_Agglomeration), with a population of 3,638,281 in 2021[^cite_note-census21-4][^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23] over an area of 2,928.717 km<sup>2</sup> (1,131 sq mi).[^cite_note-area-9]

The heritage of the [Classical Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Greece) is still evident in the city, represented by [ancient monuments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_monument), and works of art, the most famous of these being the [Parthenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon), considered a key landmark of early [Western culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture). Athens retains [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Greece), [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) and a smaller number of [Ottoman](22278.md) monuments, while its historical urban core features elements of continuity through its millennia of history. Athens contains two [World Heritage Sites](44940.md) recognised by [UNESCO](21786641.md): the [Acropolis of Athens](2076.md) and the medieval [Daphni Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daphni_Monastery). Athens is home to several museums and cultural institutions, such as the [National Archeological Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Archaeological_Museum,_Athens), featuring the world's largest collection of ancient Greek antiquities, the [Acropolis Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acropolis_Museum), the [Museum of Cycladic Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goulandris_Museum_of_Cycladic_Art), the [Benaki Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benaki_Museum) and the [Byzantine and Christian Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_and_Christian_Museum). Athens was the host city of the [first modern-day Olympic Games](48644.md) in 1896, and 108 years later it hosted the [2004 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Summer_Olympics), making it one of five cities to have hosted the Summer Olympics on more than one occasion.[^cite_note-oly-24]

## Etymology and names

Further information: [Names of European cities in different languages (A)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_European_cities_in_different_languages_\(A\))

In [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language), the city's name was Ἀθῆναι (*Athênai*, pronounced [\[atʰɛ̂ːnai̯\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Greek) in [Classical Attic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attic_Greek)), which is a plural word. In earlier Greek, such as [Homeric Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeric_Greek), the name had been current in the singular form though, as Ἀθήνη (*Athḗnē*).[^cite_note-25] It was possibly rendered in the plural later on, like those of Θῆβαι (*[Thêbai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thebes,_Greece)*) and Μυκῆναι (*[Μukênai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenae)*). The root of the word is probably not of Greek or [Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages) origin,[^cite_note-Beekes2009-26] and is possibly a remnant of the [Pre-Greek substrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Greek_substrate) of Attica.[^cite_note-Beekes2009-26]

In [classical antiquity](252905.md) it was debated whether Athens took its name from its patron goddess [Athena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena) ([Attic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attic_Greek) Ἀθηνᾶ, *Athēnâ*, [Ionic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_Greek) Ἀθήνη, *Athḗnē*, and [Doric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doric_Greek) Ἀθάνα, *Athā́nā*) or Athena took her name from the city.[^cite_note-Burkert1985-27] Modern scholars now generally agree that the goddess takes her name from the city,[^cite_note-Burkert1985-27] because the ending -*ene* is common in names of locations, but rare for personal names.[^cite_note-Burkert1985-27]

According to the ancient Athenian [founding myth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Founding_myth), Athena, the goddess of wisdom and war, competed against [Poseidon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poseidon), the God of the Seas, for patronage of the yet-unnamed city;[^cite_note-Kerényi1951-28] they agreed that whoever gave the Athenians the better gift would become their patron[^cite_note-Kerényi1951-28] and appointed [Cecrops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecrops_I), the king of Athens, as the judge.[^cite_note-Kerényi1951-28] According to the account given by [Pseudo-Apollodorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibliotheca_\(Pseudo-Apollodorus\)), Poseidon struck the ground with his [trident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trident_of_Poseidon) and a salt water spring welled up.[^cite_note-Kerényi1951-28] In an alternative version of the myth from [Virgil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil)'s poem *[Georgics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgics)*, Poseidon instead gave the Athenians the first horse.[^cite_note-Kerényi1951-28] In both versions, Athena offered the Athenians the first domesticated [olive tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive_tree).[^cite_note-Kerényi1951-28][^cite_note-Garland2008-29]

Cecrops accepted this gift[^cite_note-Kerényi1951-28] and declared Athena the patron goddess of Athens.[^cite_note-Kerényi1951-28][^cite_note-Garland2008-29] Eight different etymologies, now commonly rejected, have been proposed since the 17th century. [Christian Lobeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Lobeck) proposed as the root of the name the word ἄθος (*áthos*) or ἄνθος (*ánthos*) meaning "flower", to denote Athens as the "flowering city". [Ludwig von Döderlein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Christoph_Wilhelm_Ludwig_D%C3%B6derlein) proposed the stem of the verb θάω, stem θη- (*tháō*, *thē-*, "to suck") to denote Athens as having fertile soil.[^cite_note-30]

Athenians were called [cicada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicada)-wearers (Ancient Greek: Τεττιγοφόροι) because they used to wear pins of golden cicadas. A symbol of being [autochthonous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autochthon_\(ancient_Greece\)) (earth-born), because the legendary founder of Athens, [Erechtheus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erechtheus) was an autochthon or of being musicians, because the cicada is a "musician" insect.[^cite_note-31] In classical literature the city was sometimes referred to as the [City of the Violet Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_the_Violet_Crown), first documented in [Pindar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pindar)'s ἰοστέφανοι Ἀθᾶναι (*iostéphanoi Athânai*), or as τὸ κλεινὸν ἄστυ (*tò kleinòn ásty*, "the glorious city").

During the medieval period, the name of the city was rendered once again in the singular as Ἀθήνα. Variant names included Setines, Satine, and Astines, all derivations involving [false splitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_splitting) of prepositional phrases.[^cite_note-Bourne1887-32] King [Alphonse X of Castile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfonso_X_of_Castile) credits Ovid with the [false etymology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_etymology) 'the place without death' because of Athens' arts and sciences "that never die".[^cite_note-33] In Ottoman Turkish, it was called آتينا *Ātīnā*.[^cite_note-34]

### Antiquity

Main articles: [Classical Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Athens), [Hellenistic Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_Greece), and [Roman Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Greece)

The oldest known human presence in Athens is the Cave of Schist, which has been dated to between the 11th and 7th millennia BC.[^cite_note-ethnos.gr-35] Athens has been continuously inhabited for at least 5,000 years (3000 BC).[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-tung-37] By 1400 BC, the settlement had become an important centre of the [Mycenaean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenaean_Greece) civilisation, and the [Acropolis](2076.md) was the site of a major [Mycenaean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenae) fortress, whose remains can be recognised from sections of the characteristic [Cyclopean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclopean) walls.[^cite_note-38] Unlike other Mycenaean centres, such as [Mycenae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenae) and [Pylos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pylos), it is not known whether Athens suffered destruction in about 1200 BC, an event often attributed to a [Dorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorians) invasion, and the Athenians always maintained that they were pure [Ionians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionians) with no Dorian element. However, Athens, [like many other Bronze Age settlements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age_collapse), went into economic decline for around 150 years afterwards.[^cite_note-39] [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age) burials, in the [Kerameikos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerameikos)[^cite_note-40] and other locations, are often richly provided for and demonstrate that from 900 BC onwards Athens was one of the leading centres of trade and prosperity in the region.[^cite_note-41]

### Classical Athens

Main articles: [Classical Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Athens) and [Delian League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delian_League)

By the sixth century BC, widespread social unrest led to the reforms of [Solon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solon). These would pave the way for the eventual introduction of democracy by [Cleisthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleisthenes) in 508 BC. Athens had by this time become a significant naval power with a large fleet, and helped the [rebellion of the Ionian cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionian_Revolt) against [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire) rule. In the ensuing [Greco-Persian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Persian_Wars) Athens, together with Sparta and Plataea, led the coalition of Greek states that would eventually repel the Persians, defeating them decisively at [Marathon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Marathon) under the leadership of [Miltiades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miltiades) in 490 BC, and crucially at [Salamis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Salamis) under the leadership of [Themistocles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Themistocles) in 480 BC. However, this did not prevent Athens from being [captured and sacked twice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_destruction_of_Athens) by the Persians within one year, after a heroic but ultimately failed resistance at [Thermopylae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Thermopylae) by [Spartans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparta) and other Greeks led by [King Leonidas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonidas_I),[^cite_note-42] after both [Boeotia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeotia) and [Attica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attica) fell to the Persians.



The [Delian League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delian_League) under the leadership of Athens before the [Peloponnesian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnesian_War) in 431 BC

The decades that followed became known as the [Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth-century_Athens) of the [Athenian democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenian_democracy), during which time Athens became the leading city of [Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece), with its cultural achievements laying the foundations for [Western civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture).[^cite_note-britannica-16][^cite_note-bbc-17] The playwrights [Aeschylus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeschylus), [Sophocles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophocles) and [Euripides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euripides) flourished in Athens during this time, as did the historians [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus) and [Thucydides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thucydides), the physician [Hippocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocrates), and the philosophers [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates) and [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato). Guided by [Pericles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pericles), who promoted the arts and fostered democracy, Athens embarked on an ambitious building program that saw the construction of the Acropolis of Athens (including the [Parthenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon)), as well as empire-building via the [Delian League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delian_League). Originally intended as an association of [Greek city-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_city-state), which were led by [Cimon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimon), to continue the fight against the Persians, the league soon turned into a vehicle for Athens's own imperial ambitions. The resulting tensions brought about the [Peloponnesian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnesian_War) (431–404 BC), in which Athens was defeated by its rival Sparta.[^cite_note-43]



The [Parthenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon) on the [Acropolis](2076.md) hill of Athens, dedicated to [Athena Parthenos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena_Parthenos)

Nonetheless, the city reemerged soon as a major power in the Greek world, forming the [Second Athenian League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Athenian_League) during the time of the Spartan and Theban hegemonies. By the mid-4th century BC the northern Greek kingdom of [Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedon) was becoming dominant in Greek affairs. In 338 BC the armies of [Philip II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Macedon) and his son [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) defeated an alliance of some of the Greek city-states led by Athens and [Thebes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thebes,_Greece#Decline_and_destruction) at the [Battle of Chaeronea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Chaeronea_\(338_BC\)). After this defeat, Athens joined the [Hellenic League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Corinth) under Philip and then Alexander.

### Roman Empire

Main article: [Athens under Roman rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_under_Roman_rule)

Later, under Rome, Athens was given the status of a [free city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_city_\(classical_antiquity\)) because of its widely admired schools. In the second century AD, the Roman emperor [Hadrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadrian), himself an Athenian citizen,[^cite_note-44] ordered the construction of a number of public buildings. [Paul the Apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle) visited Athens on his second missionary journey. Athens was [sacked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sack_of_Athens_\(267_AD\)) in 267 AD by a Germanic tribe.

In the early 4th century AD the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) began to be governed from [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople), and with the construction and expansion of the imperial city, many of Athens's works of art were taken by the emperors to adorn it. The Empire became [Christianised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianised), and the use of [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) declined in favour of exclusive use of [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Greek); in the [Roman imperial period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_imperial_period_\(chronology\)), both languages had been used. In the later Roman period, Athens was ruled by the emperors continuing until the 13th century, its citizens identifying themselves as citizens of the Roman Empire ("*[Rhomaioi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhomaioi)*"). The conversion of the empire from paganism to Christianity greatly affected Athens, resulting in reduced reverence for the city.[^cite_note-tung-37] Ancient monuments such as the Parthenon, Erechtheion and the Hephaisteion (Theseion) were converted into churches. As the empire became increasingly anti-pagan, Athens became a provincial town and experienced fluctuating fortunes.

The city remained an important centre of learning, especially of [Neoplatonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism)—with notable pupils including [Gregory of Nazianzus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_of_Nazianzus), [Basil of Caesarea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basil_of_Caesarea) and the Roman emperor [Julian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_\(emperor\)) (r. 355–363)—and consequently a centre of paganism. Christian items do not appear in the archaeological record until the early 5th century.[^cite_note-ODB-45] The sack of the city by the [Herules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herules) in 267 and by the [Visigoths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visigoths) under their king [Alaric I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaric_I) (r. 395–410) in 396, however, dealt a heavy blow to the city's fabric and fortunes, and Athens was henceforth confined to a small fortified area that embraced a fraction of the ancient city.[^cite_note-ODB-45] The emperor [Justinian I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian_I) (r. 527–565) banned the teaching of philosophy by pagans in 529,[^cite_note-46] an event whose impact on the city is much debated,[^cite_note-ODB-45] but is generally taken to mark the end of the ancient history of Athens.

### Byzantine Athens

Main articles: [Byzantine empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_empire) and [Byzantine Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Greece)



The [Daphni Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daphni_Monastery), an 11th-century [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine) monastery northwest of central Athens, is a designated [UNESCO World Heritage Site](44940.md).

Athens was sacked by the [Slavs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavs) in 582, but remained in imperial hands thereafter, as highlighted by the visit of the emperor [Constans II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constans_II) (r. 641–668) in 662/3 and its inclusion in the [Theme of Hellas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theme_of_Hellas).[^cite_note-ODB-45] The city was threatened by [Saracen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saracen) raids in the 8th–9th centuries—in 896, Athens was raided and possibly occupied for a short period, an event which left some archaeological remains and elements of Arabic ornamentation in contemporary buildings[^cite_note-EI2-47]—but there is also evidence of a [mosque](19894.md) existing in the city at the time.[^cite_note-ODB-45] In the great dispute over [Byzantine Iconoclasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Iconoclasm), Athens is commonly held to have supported the [iconophile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iconophile) position, chiefly due to the role played by Empress [Irene of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irene_of_Athens) in the ending of the first period of Iconoclasm at the [Second Council of Nicaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Council_of_Nicaea) in 787.[^cite_note-ODB-45] A few years later, another Athenian, [Theophano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theophano_of_Athens), became empress as the wife of [Staurakios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staurakios) (r. 811–812).[^cite_note-ODB-45]

Invasion of the empire by the Turks after the [Battle of Manzikert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Manzikert) in 1071, and the ensuing civil wars, largely passed the region by and Athens continued its provincial existence unharmed. When the Byzantine Empire was rescued by the resolute leadership of the three [Komnenos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komnenos) emperors [Alexios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexios_I_Komnenos), [John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_II_Komnenos) and [Manuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_I_Komnenos), Attica and the rest of Greece prospered. Archaeological evidence tells us that the medieval town experienced a period of rapid and sustained growth, starting in the 11th century and continuing until the end of the 12th century.



The [Frankish Tower of the Acropolis of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankish_Tower_\(Acropolis_of_Athens\)) in 1874, the year before it was demolished, with the ruins of the [Propylaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propylaea_\(Acropolis_of_Athens\)) and view west over the Athenian plain towards [Mount Aigaleo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Aigaleo)

The [Agora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Agora_of_Athens) (marketplace) had been deserted since late antiquity, began to be built over, and soon the town became an important centre for the production of soaps and dyes. The growth of the town attracted the [Venetians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice), and various other traders who frequented the ports of the Aegean, to Athens. This interest in trade appears to have further increased the economic prosperity of the town.[^cite_note-48]

The 11th and 12th centuries were the Golden Age of [Byzantine art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_art) in Athens. Almost all of the most important Middle Byzantine churches in and around Athens were built during these two centuries, and this reflects the growth of the town in general. This medieval prosperity did not last.

### Duchy of Athens

Further information: [Duchy of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Athens)

In 1204, the [Fourth Crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Crusade) conquered Athens and the city was not recovered from the [Latins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholicism) before it was taken by the [Ottoman Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Turks). It did not become Greek in government again until the 19th century. From 1204 until 1458, Athens was ruled by Latins in three separate periods, following the [Crusades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades). The "Latins", or "[Franks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franks_\(Crusaders\))", were western Europeans and followers of the [Latin Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Church) brought to the [Eastern Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Mediterranean) during the Crusades. Along with the rest of Byzantine Greece, Athens was part of the series of feudal [fiefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiefs), similar to the [Crusader states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusader_states) established in [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria_\(region\)) and on [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Cyprus) after the [First Crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Crusade). This period is known as the *[Frankokratia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankokratia)*.

### Ottoman Athens

Main article: [Ottoman Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Greece)



The second [Parthenon mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon_mosque) in the ruined [Parthenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon), which was destroyed by a [Venetian bombardment in 1687](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_the_Acropolis_\(1687\)), depicted by [Pierre Peytier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Peytier) in the 1830s



[Tzistarakis Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tzistarakis_Mosque), an [Ottoman](22278.md) mosque, built in 1759, in [Monastiraki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monastiraki) Square

The first [Ottoman](22278.md) attack on Athens, which involved a short-lived occupation of the town, came in 1397, under the Ottoman generals [Yaqub Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaqub_Pasha?action=edit\&redlink=1) and Timurtash.[^cite_note-EI2-47] In 1458, Athens was captured by the Ottomans under the personal leadership of Sultan [Mehmed II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehmed_II).[^cite_note-EI2-47] As the Ottoman Sultan rode into the city, he was greatly struck by the beauty of its ancient monuments and issued a *[firman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firman)* (imperial edict) forbidding their looting or destruction, on pain of death. The [Parthenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon) was converted into the [main mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon_mosque) of the city.[^cite_note-tung-37]

Under Ottoman rule, Athens was denuded of any importance and its population severely declined, leaving it as a "small country town" ([Franz Babinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Babinger)).[^cite_note-EI2-47] From the early 17th century, Athens came under the jurisdiction of the [Kizlar Agha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kizlar_Agha), the chief black eunuch of the [Sultan's harem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Harem). The city had originally been granted by Sultan [Ahmed I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_I) (r. 1603–1617) to Basilica, one of his favourite concubines, who hailed from the city, in response of complaints of maladministration by the local governors. After her death, Athens came under the purview of the Kizlar Agha.[^cite_note-49]

The Turks began a practice of storing gunpowder and explosives in the Parthenon and [Propylaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propylaea_\(Acropolis_of_Athens\)). In 1640, a lightning bolt struck the Propylaea, causing its destruction.[^cite_note-50] In 1687, during the [Morean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morean_War), the Acropolis [was besieged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_the_Acropolis_\(1687\)) by the Venetians under [Francesco Morosini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Morosini), and the [temple of Athena Nike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Athena_Nike) was dismantled by the Ottomans to fortify the Parthenon. A shot fired during the bombardment of the Acropolis caused a powder magazine in the Parthenon to explode (26 September), and the building was severely damaged, giving it largely the appearance it has today. The Venetian occupation of Athens lasted for six months, and both the Venetians and the Ottomans participated in the looting of the Parthenon. One of its western pediments was removed, causing even more damage to the structure.[^cite_note-tung-37][^cite_note-EI2-47] During the Venetian occupation, the two mosques of the city were converted into Catholic and Protestant churches, but on 9 April 1688 the Venetians abandoned Athens again to the Ottomans.[^cite_note-EI2-47]

### Modern history

#### 1821 Greek Revolution

Main article: [Greek War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_War_of_Independence)



*[The Entry of King Otto of Greece into Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Entry_of_King_Otto_of_Greece_into_Athens)* by [Peter von Hess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_von_Hess), 1839

In 1822, a Greek insurgency [captured the city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_the_Acropolis_\(1821%E2%80%931822\)), but it fell to the Ottomans again in 1826, although the [Acropolis held till June 1827](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_the_Acropolis_\(1826%E2%80%931827\)). Again the ancient monuments suffered badly. The Ottoman forces remained in possession until March 1833, when they withdrew.

#### Capital of modern Greece

Main article: [Kingdom of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Greece)

Following the [Greek War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_War_of_Independence) and the establishment of the [Greek Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Kingdom), Athens was chosen to replace [Nafplio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nafplio) as the second capital of the newly independent Greek state in 1834, largely because of historical and sentimental reasons.[^cite_note-51] At the time, after the extensive destruction it had suffered during the war of independence, it was reduced to a town of about 4,000 people (less than half its earlier population) in a loose swarm of houses along the foot of the Acropolis. The first [King of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_of_Greece), [King Otto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_of_Greece) of Bavaria, commissioned the architects [Stamatios Kleanthis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stamatios_Kleanthis) and [Eduard Schaubert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eduard_Schaubert) to design a modern city plan fit for the capital of a state.

The first modern city plan consisted of a triangle defined by the Acropolis, the ancient cemetery of [Kerameikos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerameikos) and the new palace of the Bavarian king (now housing the [Greek Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Parliament)), so as to highlight the continuity between modern and ancient Athens. Neoclassicism, the international style of this epoch, was the architectural style through which Bavarian, French and Greek architects such as Hansen, Klenze, Boulanger or Kaftantzoglou designed the first important public buildings of the new capital.

In 1896, Athens hosted the first modern [Olympic Games](22576.md). In the 1920s a number of [Greek refugees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_refugees), expelled from [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia) after the [Greco-Turkish War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Turkish_War_\(1919%E2%80%931922\)) and [Population exchange between Greece and Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_exchange_between_Greece_and_Turkey), swelled Athens's population.&#x20;

#### Athens during World War II

Main articles: [Greek Resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Resistance) and [1 May 1944 Kaisariani executions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_May_1944_Kaisariani_executions)

Athens was [occupied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_occupation_of_Greece) by the Axis (primarily German soldiers) during [World War II](32927.md) and experienced terrible privations during the later years of the war. The [Great Famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_\(Greece\)) greatly affected the city during the occupation. Several resistance organizations were present inside Athens to fight against the occupation, with [EAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EAM_\(Greece\)) being the main movement of the [Greek Resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Resistance).&#x20;

Following the liberation of Greece from the [Axis powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_powers), [Dekemvriana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dekemvriana) rocked the city with heavy fighting between [communist](356444.md) forces and government forces backed by the British. ## Geography



The Athens Urban Area within the Attica Basin, seen from space

Athens sprawls across the central plain of Attica that is often referred to as the **Athens Basin** or the **Attica Basin** ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language): Λεκανοπέδιο Αθηνών/Αττικής, [romanised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Greek): *Lekanopédio Athinón/Attikís*). The basin is bounded by four large mountains: [Mount Aigaleo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Aigaleo) to the west, Mount [Parnitha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parnitha) to the north, [Mount Pentelicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Pentelicus) to the northeast and Mount [Hymettus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hymettus) to the east.[^cite_note-Focus_on_Athens-52] Beyond Mount Aegaleo lies the [Thriasian plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thriasian_plain), which forms an extension of the central plain to the west. The [Saronic Gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saronic_Gulf) lies to the southwest. Mount Parnitha is the tallest of the four mountains (1,413 metres or 4,636 feet),[^cite_note-53] and has been declared a [national park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_park). The Athens urban area spreads over 50 kilometres (31 mi) from [Agios Stefanos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agios_Stefanos,_Attica) in the north to [Varkiza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varkiza) in the south. The city is located in the north temperate zone, 38 degrees north of the equator.

Athens is built around a large number of hills. [Lycabettus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Lycabettus) is one of the tallest hills of the city proper and provides a view of the entire Attica Basin. The meteorology of Athens is deemed to be one of the most complex in the world because its mountains cause a [temperature inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inversion_\(meteorology\)) phenomenon which, along with the Greek government's difficulties controlling industrial pollution, was responsible for the air pollution problems the city has faced.[^cite_note-tung-37] This issue is not unique to Athens; for instance, Los Angeles and [Mexico City](18987.md) also suffer from similar atmospheric inversion problems.[^cite_note-tung-37]

The [Cephissus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cephissus_\(Athenian_plain\)) river, the [Ilisos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilisos) and the [Eridanos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eridanos_\(Athens\)) stream are the historical rivers of Athens.

### Environment



[Smog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smog) in Athens

By the late 1970s the pollution of Athens had become so destructive that according to the then Greek [Minister of Culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Culture_\(Greece\)), Constantine Trypanis, "...the carved details on the five the caryatids of the [Erechtheum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erechtheum) had seriously degenerated, while the face of the horseman on the Parthenon's west side was all but obliterated."[^cite_note-54] A series of measures taken by the authorities of the city throughout the 1990s resulted in the improvement of air quality; the appearance of smog (or *nefos* as the Athenians used to call it) has become less common.

Measures taken by the Greek authorities throughout the 1990s have improved the quality of air over the Attica Basin. Nevertheless, air pollution still remains an issue for Athens, particularly during the hottest summer days. In late June 2007,[^cite_note-outraged-55] the Attica region experienced a number of [brush fires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_Greek_forest_fires),[^cite_note-outraged-55] including a blaze that burned a significant portion of a large forested national park in [Mount Parnitha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parnitha),[^cite_note-ypexode-56] considered critical to maintaining a better air quality in Athens all year round.[^cite_note-outraged-55] Damage to the park has led to worries over a stalling in the improvement of air quality in the city.[^cite_note-outraged-55]

The major waste management efforts undertaken in the last decade, particularly the plant built on the small island of Psytalia, have greatly improved [water quality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_quality) in the Saronic Gulf, and the coastal waters of Athens are now accessible again to swimmers.

#### Parks



The [Pedion tou Areos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedion_tou_Areos) park



The entrance of the [National Gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Garden_of_Athens), commissioned by [Queen Amalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amalia_of_Oldenburg) in 1838 and completed by 1840

[Parnitha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parnitha) National Park is punctuated by well-marked paths, gorges, springs, torrents and caves dotting the protected area. Hiking and mountain-biking in all four mountains are popular outdoor activities for residents of the city. The [National Garden of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Garden_of_Athens) was completed in 1840 and is a green refuge of 15.5 hectares in the centre of the Greek capital. Located between the Parliament and [Zappeion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zappeion) buildings, the latter of which maintains its own garden of seven hectares. Parts of the City Centre have been redeveloped under a masterplan called the *Unification of Archeological Sites of Athens*, which has also gathered funding from the EU to help enhance the project.[^cite_note-EUfund-57][^cite_note-58]

The landmark [Dionysiou Areopagitou Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysiou_Areopagitou_Street) has been pedestrianised, forming a scenic route. The route starts from the [Temple of Olympian Zeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Olympian_Zeus_\(Athens\)) at Vasilissis Olgas Avenue, continues under the southern slopes of the Acropolis near [Plaka](251202.md), and finishes just beyond the [Temple of Hephaestus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Hephaestus) in [Thiseio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thiseio). The route in its entirety provides visitors with views of the Parthenon and the [Agora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Agora_of_Athens), the meeting point of ancient Athenians, away from the busy City Centre.

The hills of Athens also provide green space. [Lycabettus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Lycabettus), [Philopappos hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philopappos_Monument) and the area around it, including [Pnyx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pnyx) and [Ardettos hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ardettos_hill?action=edit\&redlink=1), are planted with pines and other trees, with the character of a small forest rather than typical metropolitan parkland. Also to be found is the [Pedion tou Areos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedion_tou_Areos) (*Field of Mars*) of 27.7 hectares, near the [National Archaeological Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Archaeological_Museum,_Athens).[^cite_note-59]

### Climate



Sunrise in Athens



Sunset over the Hill of the Nymphs, viewed from the [Propylaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propylaea_\(Acropolis_of_Athens\)), 2024

Athens has a hot-summer [Mediterranean climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_climate) ([Köppen climate classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification): *Csa*). Especially during summer the climate in Athens can be considered warmer than some cities that are similar or even closer to the Equator such as [Seoul](19159283.md), [Melbourne](17306237.md), [Buenos Aires](255919.md) and [Cape Town](6653.md). According to the meteorological station near the city centre which is operated by the [National Observatory of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Observatory_of_Athens), Athens has a mean annual temperature of 19.2 °C (66.6 °F) while other parts of the urban agglomeration are affected by the [urban heat island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island) effect more and thus have higher mean annual temperatures.[^cite_note-Climate_Atlas_of_Greece-60] [Fog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fog) is rare in the city centre, but somewhat more frequent in areas to the east, close to Mt. [Hymettus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hymettus).[^cite_note-Practical_Information_About_Athens-61] Advection fog can occur in spring, especially along the coastline.

The southern section of the [Athens metropolitan area](72650858.md) (i.e., [Elliniko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliniko)) lies in the transitional zone between [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_climate) (*Csa*) and hot [semi-arid climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-arid_climate) (*BSh*), with its port-city of [Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus) being the most extreme example, receiving just 331.9 millimetres (13.07 in) per year. The areas to the south generally see less extreme temperature variations as their climate is moderated by the [Saronic gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saronic_Gulf).[^cite_note-62] The northern part of the city (e.g. [Kifissia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kifissia)), owing to its higher elevation, features moderately lower temperatures and slightly increased precipitation year-round. The generally dry climate of the Athens basin compared to the precipitation amounts seen in a typical Mediterranean climate is due to the [rain shadow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rain_shadow) effect caused by the [Pindus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pindus) mountain range and the [Dirfys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirfys) and [Parnitha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parnitha) mountains, substantially drying the westerly[^cite_note-63] and northerly[^cite_note-Practical_Information_About_Athens-61] winds respectively.



Snowfall in Athens on 16 February 2021

Snowfall is relatively uncommon. It usually does not cause heavy disruption to daily life, in contrast to the northern parts of the city, where [blizzards](4548.md) occur on a more regular basis. The most recent examples include the snowstorms of 16 February 2021[^cite_note-64] and 24 January 2022,[^cite_note-65] when the entire urban area was blanketed in snow, apart, in the second case, to some areas adjacent to [Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus).

Athens may get particularly hot in the summer, owing partly to the strong [urban heat island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island) effect characterising the city.[^cite_note-66] In fact, Athens has been referred to as the hottest city in [mainland Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland_Europe),[^cite_note-Athens_will_be_the_first_European_city_to_appoint_a_chief_heat_officer-67] and is the first city in Europe to appoint a chief heat officer to deal with severe [heat waves](293270.md).[^cite_note-68] [Temperatures of 47.5°C and over](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extreme_temperatures_in_Greece) have been reported in several locations of the metropolitan area, including within the urban agglomeration. [Elefsina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elefsina), to the west of the city, was until 2021 the holder of the [World Meteorological Organization](33584.md) record for the highest temperature recorded in Europe with 48.0 °C (118.4 °F) which was recorded on 10 July 1977.[^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70]

## Administration



Athens City Hall



The [Old Royal Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Royal_Palace)

Athens became the capital of Greece in 1834, following [Nafplion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nafplion), which was the provisional capital from 1829. The municipality (city) of Athens is also the capital of the [Attica](26280929.md) region. The term *Athens* can refer either to the municipality of Athens, to [Greater Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Athens) or urban area, or to the entire [Athens Metropolitan Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Metropolitan_Area).

The large **city centre** ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language): Κέντρο της Αθήνας, [romanised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Greek): *Kéntro tis Athínas*) of the Greek capital falls directly within the **Municipality of Athens** ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language): Δήμος Αθηναίων, [romanised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Greek): *Dímos Athinaíon*), which is the largest in population size in Greece and forms the core of the Athens urban area is made up of a series of smaller Municipal Communities, followed by the **Municipality of [Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus)**, which forms a significant city centre on its own within the Athens urban area and it is the second largest in population size within it.

### Municipality of Athens

The **municipality of Athens** is divided into seven **municipal communities** ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language): δημοτικές κοινότητες) ,[^cite_note-82] which are themselves divided in a total of 53 **municipal districts** ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language): συνοικίες),[^cite_note-83] further divided into a total of 144 **municipal neighbourhoods**.[^cite_note-84]These **municipal neighbourhoods** generally take the name of a church, monument or square.&#x20;

The seven **municipal communities** are only named by numbers.

## Demographics



A map of Athens' population density

The Municipality of Athens had a population of 643,452 people in 2021.[^cite_note-census21-4] In the [2021 Population and Housing Census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Greek_census), the four regional units that make up the former Athens prefecture have a combined population of 2,611,713. They, together with the [regional unit of Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus_\(regional_unit\)) (sometimes referred to as Greater Piraeus) make up the dense Athens Urban Area, or Greater Athens, which reaches a total population of 3,059,764 inhabitants (in 2021).[^cite_note-census21-4]

The municipality (centre) of Athens is the [most populous in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_in_Greece), with a population of 643,452 people (in 2021)[^cite_note-census21-4] and an area of 38.96 km<sup>2</sup> (15.04 sq mi),[^cite_note-stat01-8] forming the core of the Athens Urban Area within the Attica Basin. The incumbent [Mayor of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mayors_of_Athens) is Charis Doukas of PASOK. The municipality is divided into seven municipal districts which are mainly used for administrative purposes.[^cite_note-statistics-93]

For the Athenians the most popular way of dividing the downtown is through its neighbourhoods such as [Pagkrati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pangrati), [Ampelokipoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambelokipi,_Athens), [Goudi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goudi), [Exarcheia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exarcheia), [Patisia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patisia), [Ilisia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilisia,_Athens), [Petralona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petralona), [Plaka](251202.md), [Anafiotika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anafiotika), [Koukaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koukaki), [Kolonaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolonaki) and [Kypseli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kypseli,_Athens), each with its own distinct history and characteristics.

[Romani people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romani_people) are concentrated in Acharnes, Ano Liosia, Agia Varvara, Zefeiri and Kamatero.[^cite_note-94]

There is a large Albanian community in Athens.[^cite_note-95]

The Athens Metropolitan Area, with an area of 2,928.717 km<sup>2</sup> (1,131 sq mi) and inhabited by 3,744,059 people in 2021,[^cite_note-census21-4] consists of the Athens Urban Area with the addition of the towns and villages of [East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Attica) and [West Attica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Attica), which surround the dense urban area of the Greek capital. It actually sprawls over the whole peninsula of Attica, which is the best part of the [region of Attica](26280929.md), excluding the [islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islands_\(regional_unit\)).

### Safety

Athens ranks in the lowest percentage for the risk on frequency and severity of terrorist attacks according to the EU Global Terrorism Database (EIU 2007–2016 calculations). The city also ranked 35th in Digital Security, 21st on Health Security, 29th on Infrastructure Security and 41st on Personal Security globally in a 2017 The [Economist Intelligence Unit](15303116.md) report.[^cite_note-96] It also ranks as a very safe city (39th globally out of 162 cities overall) on the ranking of the safest and most dangerous countries.[^cite_note-97] As November 2024 the crime index from [Numbeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numbeo) places Athens at 55.40 (moderate), while its safety index is at 44.60.[^cite_note-98][^cite_note-99] According to a [Mercer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercer_\(consulting_firm\)) 2019 Quality of Living Survey, Athens ranks 89th on the Mercer Quality of Living Survey ranking.[^cite_note-100]

Athens is the [financial capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_centre) of Greece. According to data from 2014, Athens as a metropolitan economic area produced US\$130 billion as GDP in [PPP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purchasing_power_parity), which consists of nearly half of the production for the whole country. Athens was ranked 102nd in that year's list of global economic metropolises, while GDP per capita for the same year was 32,000 [US dollars](18717338.md).[^cite_note-103]

Athens is one of the major economic centres in south-eastern Europe and is considered a regional economic power. The port of Piraeus, where big investments by [COSCO](51161421.md) have already been delivered during the recent decade, the completion of the new Cargo Centre in Thriasion,[^cite_note-104] the [expansion of the Athens Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_4_\(Athens_Metro\)) and the [Athens Tram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Tram), as well as the [Hellenikon metropolitan park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenikon_Metropolitan_Park) redevelopment in Elliniko and other urban projects, are the economic landmarks of the upcoming years.

Prominent Greek companies such as [Hellas Sat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellas_Sat), [Hellenic Aerospace Industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenic_Aerospace_Industry), [Mytilineos Holdings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mytilineos_Holdings), [Titan Cement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titan_Cement), [Hellenic Petroleum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenic_Petroleum), [Papadopoulos E.J.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papadopoulos_\(biscuits\)), [Folli Follie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folli_Follie), [Jumbo S.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jumbo_S.A.), [OPAP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OPAP), and [Cosmote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmote) have their headquarters in the metropolitan area of Athens. Multinational companies such as [Ericsson](9424.md), [Sony](26989.md), [Siemens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens), [Motorola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motorola), [Samsung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samsung), [Microsoft](19001.md), [Teleperformance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teleperformance), [Novartis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novartis), [Mondelez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mondelez) and [Coca-Cola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coca-Cola) also have their regional research and development headquarters in the city. The banking sector is represented by [National Bank of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bank_of_Greece), [Alpha Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_Bank), [Eurobank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurobank_Ergasias), and [Piraeus Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus_Bank), while the [Bank of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_Greece) is also situated in the City Centre. The [Athens Stock Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Exchange) was severely hit by the [Greek government-debt crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_government-debt_crisis) and the decision of the government to proceed into [capital controls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_controls_in_Greece) during summer 2015. As a whole the economy of Athens and Greece was strongly affected, while data showed a change from long recession to growth of 1.4% from 2017 onwards.[^cite_note-105]

Tourism is also a leading contributor to the economy of the city, as one of Europe's top destinations for city-break tourism, and also the gateway for excursions to both the islands and other parts of the mainland. Greece attracted 26.5 million visitors in 2015, 30.1 million visitors in 2017, and over 33 million in 2018, making Greece one of the [most visited countries in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Tourism_rankings#Europe) and the world, and contributing 18% to the country's GDP. Athens welcomed more than 5 million tourists in 2018, and 1.4 million were "city-breakers"; this was an increase by over a million city-breakers since 2013.[^cite_note-106]

### Tourism

Athens has been a destination for travellers since antiquity. Over the 2000s, the city's infrastructure and social amenities have improved, in part because of its successful bid to stage the [2004 Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Summer_Olympics).

The Greek Government, aided by the EU, has funded major infrastructure projects such as the state-of-the-art [Eleftherios Venizelos International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_International_Airport),[^cite_note-107] the expansion of the [Athens Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Metro) system,[^cite_note-EUfund-57] and the new [Attiki Odos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attiki_Odos) Motorway.[^cite_note-EUfund-57]

In recent years, Athens has become more dynamic with the addition of numerous new bars and cafés and a growing presence of [street art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_art) and [graffiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graffiti), enhancing its urban edge and adding more tourist options alongside the city's archaeological sites and museums.[^cite_note-108]

## Transport

Main article: [Public transport in Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_transport_in_Athens)



Athens railways network (Metro, Suburban Railway and Tram)

Athens is the country's major transportation hub. The city has Greece's largest airport and its largest port; Piraeus, too, is the largest container transport port in the Mediterranean, and the largest passenger port in Europe.

Athens is a major national hub for Intercity ([Ktel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KTEL_\(Greece\))) and international buses, as well as for domestic and international rail transport. Public transport is serviced by a variety of transportation means, making up the country's largest mass transit system. [Transport for Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_for_Athens) operates a large bus and [trolleybus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trolleybus) fleet, the city's [Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Metro) and a [tram network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Tram), connecting the southern suburbs to the city centre.[^cite_note-109] [Hellenic Train](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenic_Train) operates [Suburban Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Suburban_Railway) services.

### Bus transport

OSY ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language): ΟΣΥ) (Odikes Sygkoinonies S.A.), a subsidiary company of OASA (Athens urban transport organisation), is the main operator of buses and trolleybuses in Athens. As of 2017, its network consists of around 322 bus lines, spanning the Athens Metropolitan Area, and making up a fleet of 2,375 buses and trolleybuses. Of those 2,375, 619 buses run on [compressed natural gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compressed_natural_gas), making up the largest fleet of natural gas-powered buses in Europe, and 354 are electric-powered (trolleybuses). All of the 354 trolleybuses are equipped to run on diesel in case of [power failure](296636.md).[^cite_note-110]

International links are provided by a number of private companies. National and regional bus links are provided by [KTEL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KTEL_\(Greece\)) from two InterCity Bus Terminals; [Kifissos Bus Terminal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Peloponnese_Bus_Station) A and [Liosion Bus Terminal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Liosion_Bus_Station) B, both located in the north-western part of the city. *Kifissos* provides connections towards [Peloponnese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnese), North Greece, West Greece and some [Ionian Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionian_Islands), whereas *Liosion* is used for most of Central Greece. Both of these terminals will be replaced by a new Intercity Bus Terminal under construction in [Eleonas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elaionas) due to be completed by 2027.

### Railways

Main articles: [Athens Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Metro), [Proastiakos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proastiakos), and [Athens Tram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Tram)

Athens is the hub of the [country's national railway system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenic_Railways_Organisation) (OSE), connecting the capital with major cities across Greece and abroad ([Istanbul](3391396.md), [Sofia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sofia), [Belgrade](55904.md) and [Bucharest](36877.md)).



Suburban rail ([Proastiakos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proastiakos))

The [Athens Suburban Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Suburban_Railway), referred to as the *[Proastiakos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proastiakos)*, connects Athens International Airport to the city of [Kiato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiato), 106 km (66 mi)[^cite_note-proastiakos-111] west of Athens, via Larissa station, the city's central rail station and the port of Piraeus. The length of Athens's commuter rail network extends to 120 km (75 mi),[^cite_note-proastiakos-111] and is expected to stretch to 281 km (175 mi) by 2010.[^cite_note-proastiakos-111]



[Athens Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Metro) train at [Omonia metro station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omonia_metro_station)

The [Athens Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Metro) is operated by STASY S.A. ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language): ΣΤΑΣΥ) (Statheres Sygkoinonies S.A.), a subsidiary company of OASA (Athens urban transport organisation), which provides public transport throughout the Athens Urban Area. While its main purpose is transport, it also houses Greek artefacts found during the construction of the system.[^cite_note-112] The Athens Metro runs three metro lines, namely [Line 1 (Green Line)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Metro_Line_1), [Line 2 (Red Line)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Metro_Line_2) and [Line 3 (Blue Line)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Metro_Line_3) lines, of which the first was constructed in 1869, and the other two largely during the 1990s, with the initial new sections opened in January 2000. Line 1 mostly runs at ground level and the other two (Line 2 & 3) routes run entirely underground. A fleet of 42 trains, using 252 carriages, operates on the network,[^cite_note-xyz-113] with a daily occupancy of 1,353,000 passengers.[^cite_note-114]

*Line 1* (Green Line) serves 24 stations, and is the oldest line of the Athens metro network. It runs from [Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus) station to [Kifissia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kifissia) station and covers a distance of 25.6 km (15.9 mi). There are transfer connections with the Blue Line 3 at [Monastiraki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monastiraki) station and with the Red Line 2 at [Omonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omonoia_Square) and [Attiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attiki) stations. *Line 2* (Red Line) runs from [Anthoupoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthoupoli_metro_station) station to [Elliniko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliniko_metro_station) station and covers a distance of 17.5 km (10.9 mi).[^cite_note-xyz-113] The line connects the western suburbs of Athens with the southeast suburbs, passing through the centre of Athens. The Red Line has transfer connections with the Green Line 1 at [Attiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attiki_metro_station) and [Omonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omonia_metro_station) stations. There are also transfer connections with the Blue Line 3 at [Syntagma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntagma_Square) [station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntagma_metro_station) and with the tram at Syntagma, [Syngrou Fix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syngrou%E2%80%93Fix_station) and [Neos Kosmos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neos_Kosmos_station) stations. *Line 3* (Blue Line) runs from Dimotiko Theatro station, through the central Monastiraki and Syntagma stations to [Doukissis Plakentias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doukissis_Plakentias_station) avenue in the northeastern suburb of [Halandri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalandri).[^cite_note-xyz-113] It then ascends to ground level and continues to [Athens International Airport Eleftherios Venizelos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_International_Airport) using the suburban railway infrastructure, extending its total length to 39 km (24 mi).[^cite_note-xyz-113] The spring 2007 extension from Monastiraki westwards to [Egaleo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egaleo) connected some of the main [night life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nightlife) hubs of the city, namely those of Gazi ([Kerameikos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerameikos_metro_station) station) with Psirri ([Monastiraki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monastiraki_metro_station) station) and the city centre ([Syntagma station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntagma_station)).The new stations [Maniatika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maniatika_metro_station), [Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus_station) and [Dimotiko Theatro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimotiko_Theatro_metro_station), were completed on 10 October 2022,[^cite_note-Attiko_Metro-115][^cite_note-Athens_Greece_Guide-116] connecting the biggest port of Greece, the Port of Piraeus, with Athens International Airport, the biggest airport of Greece.



Vehicle of the [Athens Tram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Tram)

The [Athens Tram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Tram) is operated by STASY S.A. (Statheres Sygkoinonies S.A.), a subsidiary company of [Transport for Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_for_Athens) (OASA). It has a fleet of 35 [Sirio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitachi_Sirio) type vehicles[^cite_note-athenstram-117] and 25 [Alstom Citadis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alstom_Citadis) type vehicles[^cite_note-118] which serve 48 stations,[^cite_note-athenstram-117] employ 345 people with an average daily occupancy of 65,000 passengers.[^cite_note-athenstram-117] The tram network spans a total length of 27 km (17 mi) and covers ten Athenian suburbs.[^cite_note-athenstram-117] The network runs from [Syntagma Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntagma_Square) to the southwestern suburb of [Palaio Faliro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaio_Faliro), where the line splits in two branches; the first runs along the Athens coastline toward the southern suburb of [Voula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voula), while the other heads toward Piraeus. The network covers the majority of the Athens coastline.[^cite_note-EF-119]

### Athens International Airport

[Athens International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_International_Airport)

### Motorways

Further information: [National Roads and Motorways in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Roads_and_Motorways_in_Greece)

Two main motorways of Greece begin in Athens, namely the [A1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A1_motorway_\(Greece\))/[E75](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_route_E75), heading north towards Greece's second largest city, [Thessaloniki](40471.md) and the border crossing of [Evzonoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evzonoi); and the [A8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A8_motorway_\(Greece\))/[E94](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_route_E94) heading west, towards Greece's third largest city, [Patras](470691.md), which incorporated the [EO8a national road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_National_Road_8a). Before their completion, much of the road traffic used the [EO1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_National_Road_1) and [EO8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_National_Road_8) roads.

Athens's Metropolitan Area is served by the [Attiki Odos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attiki_Odos) toll motorway network: its main section, the [A6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A6_motorway_\(Greece\)), extends from the western industrial suburb of [Elefsina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleusina) to [Athens International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_International_Airport); while two beltways, namely the Aigaleo Beltway ([A65](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A65_motorway_\(Greece\))) and the Hymettus Beltway ([A62](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A62_motorway_\(Greece\))) serve parts of western and eastern Athens respectively. The span of the Attiki Odos in all its length is 65 km (40 mi),[^cite_note-122] making it the largest metropolitan motorway network in all of Greece.

## Education



Facade of the [Academy of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Athens_\(modern\))

Located on [Panepistimiou Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panepistimiou_Street), the old campus of the [University of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_and_Kapodistrian_University_of_Athens), the [National Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Library_of_Greece), and the [Athens Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Athens_\(modern\)) form the "Athens Trilogy" built in the mid-19th century. The largest and oldest university in Athens is the National and Kapodistrian University of Athens. Most of the functions of NKUA along National Technical University of Athens have been transferred to a campus in the eastern suburb of [Zografou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zografou). The [National Technical University of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Technical_University_of_Athens) old campus is located on Patision Street.

The [University of West Attica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_West_Attica) is the second largest university in Athens. The seat of the university is located in the western area of Athens, where the philosophers of Ancient Athens delivered lectures. All the activities of UNIWA are carried out in the modern infrastructure of the three University Campuses within the metropolitan region of Athens (Egaleo Park, Ancient Olive Groove and Athens), which offer modern teaching and research spaces, entertainment and support facilities for all students. Other universities that lie within Athens are the [Athens University of Economics and Business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_University_of_Economics_and_Business), the [Panteion University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panteion_University), the [Agricultural University of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_University_of_Athens) and the [University of Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Piraeus).

There are overall ten state-supported Institutions of Higher (or Tertiary) education located in the Athens Urban Area, these are by chronological order: [Athens School of Fine Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_School_of_Fine_Arts) (1837), [National Technical University of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Technical_University_of_Athens) (1837), [National and Kapodistrian University of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_and_Kapodistrian_University_of_Athens) (1837), [Agricultural University of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_University_of_Athens) (1920), [Athens University of Economics and Business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_University_of_Economics_and_Business) (1920), [Panteion University of Social and Political Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panteion_University) (1927), [University of Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Piraeus) (1938), [Harokopio University of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harokopio_University) (1990), [School of Pedagogical and Technological Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_of_Pedagogical_and_Technological_Education) (2002), [University of West Attica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_West_Attica) (2018). There are also several other private *colleges*, as they called formally in Greece, as the establishment of private universities is prohibited by the constitution. Many of them are accredited by a foreign state or university such as the [American College of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_College_of_Greece) and the [Athens Campus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Indianapolis_%E2%80%93_Athens_Campus) of the [University of Indianapolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Indianapolis).[^cite_note-123]

## Culture

Main article: [Culture of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Greece)

### Archaeological hub and museums

Further information: [List of museums in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_museums_in_Greece)



The [National Archaeological Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Archaeological_Museum,_Athens) in central Athens



The [Acropolis Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acropolis_Museum)

The city is a world centre of [archaeological research](18951655.md). Alongside national academic institutions, such as the [Athens University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_and_Kapodistrian_University_of_Athens) and the [Archaeological Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological_Society_of_Athens), it is home to multiple archaeological museums, taking in the [National Archaeological Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Archaeological_Museum,_Athens), the [Cycladic Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goulandris_Museum_of_Cycladic_Art), the [Epigraphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epigraphy) Museum, the [Byzantine & Christian Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_&_Christian_Museum), as well as museums at the ancient Agora, [Acropolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acropolis_Museum), [Kerameikos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerameikos), and the [Kerameikos Archaeological Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerameikos_Archaeological_Museum). The city is also the setting for the [Demokritos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democritus) laboratory for [Archaeometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological_science), alongside regional and national archaeological authorities forming part of the [Greek Department of Culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Culture_\(Greece\)).

Athens hosts 17 [Foreign Archaeological Institutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Foreign_Archaeological_Institutes_in_Greece) which promote and facilitate research by scholars from their home countries. As a result, Athens has more than a dozen archaeological libraries and three specialised archaeological laboratories, and is the venue of several hundred specialised lectures, conferences and seminars, as well as dozens of archaeological exhibitions each year. At any given time, hundreds of international scholars and researchers in all disciplines of archaeology are to be found in the city.

Athens's most important museums include:

- the [National Archaeological Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Archaeological_Museum,_Athens), the largest archaeological museum in the country, and one of the most important internationally, as it contains a vast collection of antiquities. Its artefacts cover a period of more than 5,000 years, from late [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic) Age to [Roman Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Greece);

- the [Benaki Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benaki_Museum) with its several branches for each of its collections including ancient, Byzantine, Ottoman-era, Chinese art and beyond;

- the [Byzantine and Christian Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_&_Christian_Museum), one of the most important museums of [Byzantine art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_art);

- the [National Art Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_\(Athens\)), the nation's eponymous leading gallery, which reopened in 2021 after renovation;

- the [National Museum of Contemporary Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_National_Museum_of_Contemporary_Art), which opened in 2000 in a former brewery building;

- the [Goulandris Museum of Contemporary Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goulandris_Museum_of_Contemporary_Art), which displays many of the modern art works amassed by shipowner [Basil Goulandris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basil_Goulandris) and his wife [Elise Karadontis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elise_Goulandris)

- the [Numismatic Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numismatic_Museum_of_Athens), housing a major collection of ancient and modern coins;

- the [Goulandris Museum of Cycladic Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goulandris_Museum_of_Cycladic_Art), home to an extensive collection of [Cycladic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycladic_art), including its famous figurines of white marble;

- the [New Acropolis Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acropolis_Museum), opened in 2009, and replacing the old museum on the Acropolis. The new museum has proved considerably popular; almost one million people visited during the summer period June–October 2009 alone. A number of smaller and privately owned museums focused on Greek culture and arts are also to be found.

- the [Kerameikos Archaeological Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerameikos_Archaeological_Museum), a museum which displays artefacts from the burial site of Kerameikos. Much of the pottery and other artefacts relate to Athenian attitudes towards death and the afterlife, throughout many ages.

- the [Jewish Museum of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Museum_of_Greece), a museum which describes the history and culture of [the Greek Jewish community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Greece).

### Architecture

See also: [Modern architecture in Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_architecture_in_Athens)



The [Cathedral of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Cathedral_of_Athens) (Athens Metropolis)

Athens incorporates [architectural styles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architectural_style) ranging from [Greco-Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Revival_architecture) and [Neoclassical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassical_architecture) to Modern. They are often to be found in the same areas, as Athens is not marked by a uniformity of architectural style. A visitor will quickly notice the absence of tall buildings: Athens has very strict [height restriction laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Height_restriction_laws) in order to ensure the Acropolis Hill is visible throughout the city. Despite the variety in styles, there is evidence of continuity in elements of the architectural environment throughout the city's history.[^cite_note-124]

For the greatest part of the 19th century Neoclassicism dominated Athens, as well as some deviations from it such as [Eclecticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclecticism), especially in the early 20th century. Thus, the [Old Royal Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Royal_Palace) was the first important public building to be built, between 1836 and 1843. Later in the mid and late 19th century, [Theophil Freiherr von Hansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron_Theophil_von_Hansen) and [Ernst Ziller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Ziller) took part in the construction of many neoclassical buildings such as the [Athens Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Athens_\(modern\)) and the [Zappeion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zappeion) Hall. Ziller also designed many private mansions in the centre of Athens which gradually became public, usually through donations, such as [Schliemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Schliemann)'s [Iliou Melathron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliou_Melathron).

Beginning in the 1920s, [modern architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_architecture) including [Bauhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bauhaus) and [Art Deco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Deco) began to exert an influence on almost all Greek architects, and buildings both public and private were constructed in accordance with these styles. Localities with a great number of such buildings include [Kolonaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolonaki), and some areas of the centre of the city; neighbourhoods developed in this period include [Kypseli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kypseli,_Athens).[^cite_note-125]

In the 1950s and 1960s during the extension and development of Athens, other modern movements such as the [International style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Style_\(architecture\)) played an important role. The centre of Athens was largely rebuilt, leading to the demolition of a number of neoclassical buildings. The architects of this era employed materials such as glass, marble and aluminium, and many blended modern and classical elements.[^cite_note-126] After World War II, internationally known architects to have designed and built in the city included [Walter Gropius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Gropius), with his design for the US Embassy, and, among others, [Eero Saarinen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eero_Saarinen), in his postwar design for the east terminal of the [Ellinikon Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellinikon_International_Airport).

### Urban sculpture



The [Old Parliament House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Parliament_House,_Athens) is now home to the National History Museum. View from [Stadiou Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stadiou_Street).

Across the city numerous statues or busts are to be found. Apart from the neoclassicals by [Leonidas Drosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonidas_Drosis) at the Academy of Athens (Plato, Socrates, Apollo and Athena), others in notable categories include the statue of [Theseus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theseus) by [Georgios Fytalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgios_Fytalis?action=edit\&redlink=1) at Thiseion; depictions of philhellenes such as [Lord Byron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Byron), [George Canning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Canning), and [William Gladstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Ewart_Gladstone); the equestrian statue of [Theodoros Kolokotronis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodoros_Kolokotronis) by [Lazaros Sochos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lazaros_Sochos) in front of the Old Parliament; statues of [Ioannis Kapodistrias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ioannis_Kapodistrias), [Rigas Feraios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigas_Feraios) and [Adamantios Korais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adamantios_Korais) at the university; of [Evangelos Zappas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelos_Zappas) and [Konstantinos Zappas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konstantinos_Zappas) at the Zappeion; [Ioannis Varvakis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ioannis_Varvakis) at the National Garden; the" Woodbreaker" by [Dimitrios Filippotis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimitrios_Filippotis?action=edit\&redlink=1); the equestrian statue of [Alexandros Papagos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandros_Papagos) in the Papagou district; and various busts of fighters of Greek independence at the [Pedion tou Areos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedion_tou_Areos). A significant landmark is also the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier in Syntagma.

### Entertainment and performing arts

Athens is home to 148 theatrical stages, more than any other city in the world, including the ancient [Odeon of Herodes Atticus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odeon_of_Herodes_Atticus), home to the [Athens Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Festival), which runs from May to October each year.[^cite_note-127][^cite_note-128] In addition to a large number of multiplexes, Athens plays host to open air garden cinemas. The city also supports music venues, including the [Athens Concert Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Concert_Hall) (*Megaro Moussikis*), which attracts world class artists.[^cite_note-129] The Athens [Planetarium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetarium),[^cite_note-130] located in [Andrea Syngrou Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_Syngrou_Avenue), in [Palaio Faliro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaio_Faliro)[^cite_note-131] is one of the largest and best equipped digital planetaria in the world.[^cite_note-132] The [Stavros Niarchos Foundation Cultural Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stavros_Niarchos_Foundation_Cultural_Center), inaugurated in 2016, will house the [National Library of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Library_of_Greece) and the [Greek National Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_National_Opera).[^cite_note-133] In 2018 Athens was designated as the [World Book Capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Book_Capital) by [UNESCO](21786641.md).[^cite_note-134]

Restaurants, tavernas and bars can be found in the entertainment hubs in [Plaka](251202.md) and the [Trigono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monastiraki) areas of the historic centre, the inner suburbs of [Gazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gazi,_Athens) and [Psyrri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psyri) are especially busy with nightclubs and bars, while [Kolonaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolonaki), [Exarchia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exarcheia), [Kypseli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kypseli,_Athens), [Metaxourgeio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaxourgeio), [Koukaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koukaki) and [Pangrati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pangrati) offer more of a cafe and restaurant scene. The coastal suburbs of [Microlimano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus), [Alimos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alimos) and [Glyfada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glyfada) include many tavernas, beach bars and busy summer clubs.



[The Stavros Niarchos Foundation Cultural Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stavros_Niarchos_Foundation_Cultural_Center), home of the [Greek National Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_National_Opera) and the new [National Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Library_of_Greece)

The most successful songs during the period 1870–1930 were the Athenian serenades (Αθηναϊκές καντάδες), based on the [Heptanesean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heptanese) [kantádhes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_folk_music#Ionian_Islands) (καντάδες '[serenades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serenade)'; sing.: καντάδα) and the songs performed on stage (επιθεωρησιακά τραγούδια 'theatrical revue songs') in [revues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revues), [musical comedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_theatre), [operettas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operettas) and [nocturnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nocturnes) that were dominating Athens's theatre scene.

In 1922, following the [Greek-Turkish war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Turkish_War_\(1919%E2%80%931922\)), [Greek genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_genocide) and later [population exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_exchange_between_Greece_and_Turkey) suffered by the Greek population of Asia Minor, many ethnic Greeks fled to Athens. They settled in poor neighbourhoods and brought with them [Rebetiko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebetiko) music, making it also popular in Greece, and which later became the base for the [Laïko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La%C3%AFko) music. Other forms of song popular today in Greece are elafrolaika, entechno, dimotika, and skyladika.[^cite_note-Tales_of_Orpheus-135] Greece's most notable, and internationally famous, composers of Greek song, mainly of the entechno form, are [Manos Hadjidakis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manos_Hadjidakis) and [Mikis Theodorakis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikis_Theodorakis). Both composers have achieved fame abroad for their composition of film scores.[^cite_note-Tales_of_Orpheus-135]

The renowned American-born Greek soprano [Maria Callas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Callas) spent her teenage years in Athens, where she settled in 1937.[^cite_note-136][^cite_note-137] Her professional opera career started in 1940 in Athens, with the [Greek National Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_National_Opera).[^cite_note-138] In 2018, the city's municipal Olympia Theatre was renamed the "[Olympia City Music Theatre 'Maria Callas'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympia_City_Music_Theatre_%22Maria_Callas%22)"[^cite_note-139][^cite_note-140] and in 2023, the Municipality inaugurated the [Maria Callas Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Callas_Museum), housing it in a [neoclassical building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassical_architecture) on 44 Mitropoleos street.[^cite_note-141]

### Sports

See also: [List of sports clubs in Athens Municipality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_sports_clubs_in_Athens_Municipality)



The Panathenaic Stadium of Athens ([Kallimarmaron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panathenaic_Stadium)), dating back to the 4th century BC, hosted the [first modern Olympic Games](48644.md) in 1896.



[Athens Olympic Sports Complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Olympic_Sports_Complex)



[Panathinaikos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panathinaikos_F.C.) vs [Sparta Prague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparta_Prague) in the [Athens Olympic Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Olympic_Stadium), 2008

Athens has a long tradition in sports and sporting events, serving as home to the most important clubs in [Greek sport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_in_Greece) and housing a large number of sports facilities. The city has also been host to sports events of international importance.

Athens has hosted the [Summer Olympic Games](27566.md) twice, in [1896](48644.md) and [2004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Summer_Olympics). The 2004 Summer Olympics required the development of the [Athens Olympic Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Stadium_\(Athens\)), which has since gained a reputation as one of the most beautiful stadiums in the world, and one of its most interesting modern monuments.[^cite_note-142] The biggest stadium in the country, it hosted two finals of the [UEFA Champions League](44220.md), in [1994](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1994_UEFA_Champions_League_Final) and [2007](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_UEFA_Champions_League_Final). Other major stadiums are the [Karaiskakis Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaiskakis_Stadium) located in the nearby city of [Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus), a sports and entertainment complex, host of the [1971 UEFA Cup Winners' Cup Final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_European_Cup_Winners'_Cup_Final), and [Agia Sophia Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agia_Sophia_Stadium) located in [Nea Filadelfeia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nea_Filadelfeia), host of the [2024 UEFA Europa Conference League final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_UEFA_Europa_Conference_League_final).

The [EuroLeague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EuroLeague) final has been hosted twice in 1985 and in [1993](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FIBA_European_Championship_1992%E2%80%9393) at the [Peace and Friendship Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_and_Friendship_Stadium), most known as SEF, a large indoor arena,[^cite_note-143] and the third time in [2007](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006%E2%80%9307_Euroleague) at the [Olympic Indoor Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Indoor_Hall). Events in other sports such as [athletics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Track_and_field_athletics), volleyball, [water polo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_polo) etc., have been hosted in the capital's venues.

Greater Athens is home to three widely supported and successful multi-sport clubs, [Panathinaikos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panathinaikos_A.O.), originated in the city of Athens, [Olympiacos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympiacos_CFP), originated in the port city of [Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus) and [AEK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A.E.K._\(sports_club\)), originated in the suburban town of [Nea Filadelfeia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nea_Filadelfeia). In [football](10568.md), [Olympiacos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympiacos_F.C.) is the dominant force at the national level and the only Greek club to have won a European competition, the [2023–24 UEFA Europa Conference League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023%E2%80%9324_UEFA_Europa_Conference_League), [Panathinaikos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panathinaikos_F.C.) made it to the [1971 European Cup Final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_European_Cup_Final), while [AEK Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AEK_Athens_F.C.) is the other member of the [big three](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P.O.K.). These clubs also have successful [basketball](3921.md) teams; [Panathinaikos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panathinaikos_B.C.) and [Olympiacos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympiacos_B.C.) are considered among the top powers in Europe, having won the [EuroLeague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EuroLeague) seven and three times respectively, whilst [AEK Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AEK_Athens_B.C.) was the first Greek team to win a European trophy in any team sport.

Other notable clubs within the region are [Athinaikos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athinaikos_F.C.), [Panionios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panionios_F.C.), [Atromitos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atromitos_F.C.), [Apollon Smyrnis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollon_Smyrnis_F.C.), [Panellinios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panellinios_G.S.), [Egaleo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egaleo_F.C.), [Ethinkos Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnikos_Piraeus_F.C.), [Maroussi BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maroussi_B.C.) and [Peristeri B.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peristeri_B.C.) Athenian clubs have also had domestic and international success in other sports.

The Athens area encompasses a variety of [terrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrain), notably hills and mountains rising around the city, and the capital is the only major city in Europe to be bisected by a [mountain range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_range). Four mountain ranges extend into city boundaries and thousands of kilometres of trails criss-cross the city and neighbouring areas, providing exercise and wilderness access [on foot](147313.md) and [on bicycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_biking).

Beyond Athens and across the prefecture of Attica, outdoor activities include [skiing](28478.md), [rock climbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_climbing), [hang gliding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hang_gliding) and windsurfing. Numerous outdoor clubs serve these sports, including the Athens Chapter of the [Sierra Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Club), which leads over 4,000 outings annually in the area.

Athens was awarded the 2004 Summer Olympics on 5 September 1997 in [Lausanne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lausanne), Switzerland, after having lost a previous bid to host the [1996 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Summer_Olympics), to [Atlanta](3138.md), United States.[^cite_note-oly-24] It was to be the second time Athens would host the games, following the inaugural event of 1896. After an unsuccessful bid in 1990, the 1997 bid was radically improved, including an appeal to Greece's Olympic history. In the last round of voting, Athens defeated Rome with 66 votes to 41.[^cite_note-oly-24] Prior to this round, the cities of [Buenos Aires](255919.md), Stockholm and [Cape Town](6653.md) had been eliminated from competition, having received fewer votes.[^cite_note-oly-24] Although the heavy cost was criticised, estimated at US\$1.5 billion, Athens was transformed into a more functional city that enjoys modern technology both in transportation and in modern [urban development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_planning).[^cite_note-olyy-144] The games welcomed over 10,000 athletes from 202 countries.[^cite_note-olyy-144]

### Precincts



The 58 precincts of Athens (2004–).

The municipality of Athens is divided in 58 electoral precincts. The current precinct limits were instated beginning with the 2004 election in order for the precincts to have a roughly equal electorate. Before 2004 there were 68 precincts, the limits of which corresponded to the Orthodox Parishes of Athens.
