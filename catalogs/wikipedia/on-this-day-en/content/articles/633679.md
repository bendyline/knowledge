**Highway 1** is a [provincial highway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provincial_highways_in_British_Columbia) in [British Columbia](3392.md), Canada, that carries the main route of the [Trans-Canada Highway](70775.md) (TCH). The highway is 1,047 kilometres (651 mi) long and connects [Vancouver Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vancouver_Island), the [Greater Vancouver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Vancouver) region in the [Lower Mainland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Mainland), and the [Interior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Columbia_Interior). It is the westernmost portion of the main TCH to be numbered "Highway 1", which continues through [Western Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Canada) and extends to the [Manitoba](18926.md)–[Ontario](22218.md) boundary. The portion of Highway 1 in the Lower Mainland is the second-busiest [freeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled-access_highway) in Canada, after [Toronto](64646.md)'s [Ontario Highway 401](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontario_Highway_401) .

The highway's western terminus is in the provincial capital of [Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria,_British_Columbia), which is also the Trans-Canada Highway's western terminus, where it serves as a city street and freeway in the suburbs. Highway 1 travels north to [Nanaimo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanaimo) and reaches the Lower Mainland at [Horseshoe Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horseshoe_Bay,_West_Vancouver) via a [BC Ferries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BC_Ferries) route across the [Strait of Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strait_of_Georgia). The highway bypasses [Vancouver](32706.md) on a freeway that travels through [Burnaby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnaby), northern [Surrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrey,_British_Columbia), and [Abbotsford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbotsford,_British_Columbia) while following the [Fraser River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraser_River) inland. The freeway ends in [Hope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hope,_British_Columbia), where Highway 1 turns north to [Cache Creek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cache_Creek,_British_Columbia) and later east to follow the Fraser and [Thompson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thompson_River) rivers into the Interior and through [Kamloops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamloops). The highway continues east across the [Columbia Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_Mountains), serving three [national parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_parks_of_Canada): [Mount Revelstoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Revelstoke_National_Park), [Glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glacier_National_Park_\(Canada\)), and [Yoho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoho_National_Park). [Highway 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberta_Highway_1) enters [Alberta](717.md) and [Banff National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banff_National_Park) at [Kicking Horse Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kicking_Horse_Pass), the highest point of the Trans-Canada Highway.

Highway 1 was preceded by several overland trails and [wagon roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagon_road) established in the mid-to-late 19th century, including the [Old Yale Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Yale_Road) in the [Fraser Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraser_Valley), the [Cariboo Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cariboo_Road), and the [Big Bend Highway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bend_Highway). The provincial government designated Highway 1 in 1941 on a portion of the [Island Highway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island_Highway) between Victoria and [Kelsey Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelsey_Bay) as well as the Vancouver–Banff highway. It was incorporated into the national Trans-Canada Highway program, which was established in 1949 and completed in 1962. Other sections of the highway were realigned in later years, including a new freeway in the Lower Mainland that opened in the 1960s and 1970s and was numbered [Highway 401](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Columbia_Highway_401).

## Vancouver Island section



The beginning of Hwy 1 at the Mile Zero monument in Victoria

The western terminus of Highway 1 and the 7,821-kilometre-long (4,860 mi) main route of the [Trans-Canada Highway](70775.md) (TCH) is at Dallas Road on the southern coast of [Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria,_British_Columbia), which faces the [Strait of Juan de Fuca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strait_of_Juan_de_Fuca).[^cite_note-MOT-Official-1] The terminus is marked by the [Mile Zero Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mile_Zero_Monument?action=edit\&redlink=1), a wooden sign at the foot of [Beacon Hill Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beacon_Hill_Park), with a nearby statue of runner [Terry Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_Fox) to commemorate his cross-country [marathon](49132.md) that was planned to end at the monument.[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3] The highway travels north on Douglas Street and forms the boundary between the residential [James Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Bay,_Greater_Victoria) neighbourhood to the west and Beacon Hill Park to the east. At the northwest edge of the park, Blanshard Street, which later carries [Highway 17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Columbia_Highway_17), splits off from the highway to run a block east, staying parallel to Douglas Street.[^cite_note-googlemaps-4] Highway 1 passes the [Royal BC Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_BC_Museum) and intersects Belleville Street, a short connector that carries a section of Highway 17 from the [Black Ball Ferries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Ball_Ferries) terminal (which is used by the [MV *Coho*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Coho) to [Port Angeles, Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Angeles,_Washington)) and passes the [British Columbia Parliament Buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Columbia_Parliament_Buildings).[^cite_note-MOT-Official-1][^cite_note-5]

The highway travels through [Downtown Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downtown_Victoria) and passes several city landmarks, including the [Fairmont Empress Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Empress_\(hotel\)), the [Bay Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_Centre), [Chinatown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinatown,_Victoria), and Mayfair Shopping Centre.[^cite_note-6] It follows Douglas Street, a six-lane urban thoroughfare with [bus lanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus_lane) during [peak periods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_period), and continues north into the suburban municipality of [Saanich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saanich,_British_Columbia).[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-CapitalMap-8] Near the Uptown [shopping centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shopping_centre), Highway 1 turns west and becomes a [limited-access road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited-access_road) that travels alongside the [Galloping Goose Regional Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galloping_Goose_Regional_Trail) through residential areas and along the north side of [Portage Inlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portage_Inlet). The highway becomes a full freeway with four-to-six lanes as it enters the town of [View Royal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/View_Royal,_British_Columbia) and travels around the north side of [Mill Hill Regional Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mill_Hill_Regional_Park?action=edit\&redlink=1). It then intersects [Highway 14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Columbia_Highway_14) in [Langford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langford,_British_Columbia) and reverts to a limited-access road with a median divider.[^cite_note-CRD-Map-9][^cite_note-10] Highway 1 (part of the Island Highway) then travels around [Bear Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bear_Mountain_\(resort\)) and turns north to follow the [Goldstream River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldstream_River) into [Goldstream Provincial Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldstream_Provincial_Park), where it meets several [trailheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trailhead).[^cite_note-googlemaps-4][^cite_note-11]

The Island Highway continues along the west side of the [Saanich Inlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saanich_Inlet) and enters the [Cowichan Valley Regional District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cowichan_Valley_Regional_District) near [Malahat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malahat,_British_Columbia). It descends from Malahat Summit, at 352 metres (1,155 ft) [above sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Above_sea_level), on a highway with passing lanes and a median barrier added in the late 2010s in response to a high rate of collisions.[^cite_note-12] The section also has occasional closures, relying on the limited-capacity [Mill Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mill_Bay,_British_Columbia)-Brentwood Bay Ferry or the longer [Pacific Marine Circle Route](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Marine_Circle_Route) as alternate connections between [Greater Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Victoria) and other Vancouver Island communities.[^cite_note-13] Highway 1 passes the Malahat SkyWalk, an observation built by the [Malahat First Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malahat_First_Nation),[^cite_note-14] and through farmland surrounding Mill Bay. The highway travels around central [Duncan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duncan,_British_Columbia) and through [North Cowichan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Cowichan,_British_Columbia) and [Ladysmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ladysmith,_British_Columbia) as it continues north as a divided highway with limited access at signalized intersections.[^cite_note-googlemaps-4] In southern [Nanaimo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanaimo,_British_Columbia), it has a short [concurrency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concurrency_\(road\)) with [Highway 19](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Columbia_Highway_19), which continues east to the [Duke Point ferry terminal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_Point_ferry_terminal) and northwest along the [Strait of Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strait_of_Georgia). Highway 1 travels through central Nanaimo on Nicol Street and Stewart Avenue to the [Departure Bay ferry terminal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Departure_Bay_ferry_terminal), where the Vancouver Island section of the highway ends.[^cite_note-MOT-Official-1] [BC Ferries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BC_Ferries) operates an automobile ferry service from Departure Bay to [Horseshoe Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horseshoe_Bay_ferry_terminal) that carries Highway 1 to [Metro Vancouver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metro_Vancouver_Regional_District), the [Lower Mainland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Mainland) region of British Columbia, and the Canadian mainland. A typical vessel assigned to the route can carry 1,460 to 1,571 passengers and 310 to 322 vehicles.[^cite_note-15]

### History

The Vancouver Island section of Highway 1 was designated in the initial numbering scheme announced by the provincial government in March 1940, along with Highway 1A.[^cite_note-16] It originally connected Victoria to [Kelsey Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelsey_Bay), a small coastal community north of [Campbell River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campbell_River,_British_Columbia). The Vancouver Island section was truncated to downtown Nanaimo in 1953, with the section north of Nanaimo being re-numbered to [Highway 19](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Columbia_Highway_19). When [BC Ferries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BC_Ferries) took over the ferry route between Departure Bay in Nanaimo and Horseshoe Bay in West Vancouver in 1961, Highway 1 was extended to the Departure Bay ferry terminal.

The Malahat Highway was completed in 1911 as a gravel road with a single lane and was later upgraded to two paved lanes.[^cite_note-17] A bridge across the [Finlayson Arm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finlayson_Arm) to bypass the section was among 19 options studied in 2007, but were discarded in favour of other solutions that would cost less.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19] In 2019, the provincial government studied the construction of a permanent detour for the Goldstream–Malahat section of Highway 1 and identified several potential routes, but instead decided to move forward with safety improvements to the existing highway.[^cite_note-20] The section was severely damaged by several [floods in November 2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/November_2021_Pacific_Northwest_floods), which closed the road for several days and required \$15 million in repairs the following year.[^cite_note-21]

### Route details

Sections of Highway 1 from Grandview Highway in Vancouver to 216 Street in Langley vary from being 3 to 4 lanes in each direction, with one of these lanes being a [high-occupancy vehicle (HOV) lane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-occupancy_vehicle_lane). These HOV lanes were constructed in 1998 as part of the BC MOT's "Go Green" project to promote the use of HOV vehicles, and cost \$62 million.[^cite_note-frontiertofreeway-22] The highway shortens to two lanes per direction after leaving Langley (Metro Vancouver), and enters Abbotsford (Fraser Valley).

### History

#### North Shore

The Upper Levels Highway opened between Horseshoe Bay and Taylor Way in West Vancouver on September 14, 1957, replacing a section of Marine Drive that had carried Highway 1.[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24] Construction on a new, high-level Second Narrows Bridge began two months later and was planned to be incorporated into the Trans-Canada Highway upon completion.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26] On June 17, 1958, several spans of the unfinished bridge collapsed during work on the main arch; 18 workers died and one diver also died during a later search at the site.[^cite_note-27] The Second Narrows Bridge was dedicated to the accident's victims and opened to traffic on August 25, 1960; it cost \$23 million to construct and was the second-longest bridge in Canada at the time of its completion.[^cite_note-28] The Upper Levels Highway was extended 9.3 kilometres (5.76 mi) east to the Second Narrows Bridge on March 4, 1961; the limited-access highway across North Vancouver cost \$50 million to construct.[^cite_note-29][^cite_note-30]

#### Vancouver to Chilliwack

Prior to the opening of the freeway (and prior to the 1980s and 1990s, expressway) segments of the present Trans-Canada, traffic used the [Pattullo Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pattullo_Bridge), [Kingsway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingsway_\(Vancouver\)), and [Fraser Highway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraser_Highway) as the Trans-Canada Highway. These roads were a part of the Highway 1 from its designation in 1940[^cite_note-vsun29march1940-31][^cite_note-1948shellmap-32] until the redesignation of the B.C.'s [400 series highways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/400-series_highways_\(British_Columbia\)) in 1972/73.[^cite_note-G22/72-33][^cite_note-34]

By 1932 a new cutoff across northern parts of the drained [Sumas Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumas_Lake) was mostly built.[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36] The cutoff bypassed the [Yale Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Yale_Road) which avoided the historical lake by running on its southern flank and along the base of [Vedder Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedder_Mountain). The highway was initially partly gravel,[^cite_note-progress16june1932-37] but it was fully paved within a few years of its opening.[^cite_note-progress31dec1935-38]

From 1960 to 1964, the province opened several expressway and freeway segments as a part of a continuous express route between [Bridal Falls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridal_Falls,_British_Columbia) and Taylor Way in [West Vancouver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Vancouver).

On August 1, 1960, the Chilliwack Bypass was officially opened by Highways Minister [Phil Gaglardi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phil_Gaglardi), MLA for Chillwack [William Kenneth Kiernan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Kenneth_Kiernan) and a six-year-old girl who cut the blue ribbon.[^cite_note-progress2aug1960-39] About 6.4 km (4 mi) of the road had been opened before Gaglardi officially opened the bypass.[^cite_note-progress22july1960-40] Work on the bypass started on December 12, 1956, with two men clearing bushes.[^cite_note-progress29july1960-41]

Around the time of opening of the Chilliwack Bypass, a bypass of [Abbotsford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbotsford,_British_Columbia) was also being constructed.[^cite_note-progress23aug1960-42] That section of freeway was officially opened by Phil Gaglardi on April 19, 1962.[^cite_note-vsun21april1962-43][^cite_note-progress18april1962-44]

On May 1, 1964, the section of Freeway between what is now north of the 1st Avenue interchange to the [Cape Horn Interchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Horn_Interchange) opened.[^cite_note-vsun30april1964-45][^cite_note-vsun2may1964-46] This was followed on June 12 by the opening of the Port Mann bridge, and the official opening of the freeway-expressway system from Bridal Falls to Taylor Way. A 90-year-old man and 11-year-old girl assisted Premier [W.A.C. Bennett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W.A.C._Bennett) and Phil Gaglardi in opening the bridge.[^cite_note-vsun13june1964-47][^cite_note-province13june1964-48] At the time of the bridge's opening, various speed limits were in effect. The section from Bridal Falls to the [Port Mann Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Mann_Bridge) had a 70 mph (110 km/h) limit. Through Burnaby, 65 mph (105 km/h) was the limit. Speeds dropped on approach to Cassiar Street with a 50 mph (80 km/h) limit west of Boundary Road, with a drop to 30 mph (48 km/h) for Cassiar Street.[^cite_note-province13june1964-48]

##### New interchanges and upgrades

Over the years, various interchanges have been built and rebuilt.

On July 31, 1969, the interchange with Lickman Road in [Chilliwack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilliwack) opened.[^cite_note-progress6august1969-49] The Prest Road overpass followed in the early 1970s.[^cite_note-progress13sept1972-50]

The two at-grade intersections of Highway 1 with Westview Drive and Lonsdale Avenue were replaced with interchanges. Lonsdale was replaced in 1991 and Westview was replaced in 1997.[^cite_note-51]

In January 1992 the [Cassiar Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassiar_Tunnel) opened. The project replaced a surface street section of Cassiar Street which was used by traffic to get from the Burnaby Freeway to the [Ironworkers Memorial Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironworkers_Memorial_Second_Narrows_Crossing).[^cite_note-vsun10jan1992-52]

Through the 2000s and 2010s multiple interchanges were upgraded and rebuilt along the highway. The Gateway program saw the rebuilding of several interchanges from Willingdon Avenue to [176 Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Columbia_Highway_15). Through Abbotsford the Mount Lehman/Fraser Highway, Clearbrook Road, and McCallum Road interchanges were rebuilt.[^cite_note-mcelhanneymtlehmaninterchange-53][^cite_note-mccallumInterchange-54][^cite_note-clearbrookinterchange-55]

On June 9, 2011, Highway 1 between 152 Street in Surrey and [Highway 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Columbia_Highway_11) in Abbotsford was designated as the *Highway of Heroes*.[^cite_note-56]

In 2013, construction reversing the [dead end overpass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridge_to_nowhere) anomaly along with two sectioned off on/off ramps of the Gaglardi Way interchange in Burnaby was completed.

On September 4, 2020, a new interchange with 216 Street was opened.[^cite_note-govt216interchange-57]

On November 10, 2022, it was announced that major construction of a new overpass at Glover Road (which will be built first), a revised interchange with [Highway 10/232 St.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Columbia_Highway_10) and widening to three lanes between 216 Street and [Highway 13/264 St.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Columbia_Highway_13) had started.[^cite_note-bcgovnews10nov2022-58] This work is part of a plan to eventually widen the highway to Whatcom Road in Abbotsford.[^cite_note-bcgovhighway1corridor-59]

## Interior section



Highway 1 just east of Kamloops heading westbound (2006)

Several sections of Highway 1 between Revelstoke and the Alberta border are under the jurisdiction of [Parks Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parks_Canada).[^cite_note-MOT-FourLaning-60]

### History

The first section of the highway to be twinned was the 12-kilometre stretch in 1984–1985 through Malakwa starting 8 kilometres east of Sicamous until Oxbow Road. This remained the only four-lane stretch east of Kamloops until the 2000s.

Since the 2000s, 25 kilometres of road in the [Kicking Horse Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kicking_Horse_Pass) near [Golden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden,_British_Columbia) have been rebuilt in phases to modern standards, with four lanes and the removal of sharp corners.[^cite_note-61][^cite_note-\:0-62][^cite_note-63] The final phase is due for completion in 2024.[^cite_note-\:0-62]

During [major floods in November 2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/November_2021_Pacific_Northwest_floods), sections of Highway 1 between Hope and Spences Bridge were washed away into the Thompson River. Other sections of the highway on Vancouver Island and a railroad underpass near Lytton were also damaged in the same event. As a result of the floods, which also damaged other highways in the Fraser Valley, road connections from Metro Vancouver to the rest of Canada were cut off.[^cite_note-64]

### Future

The Interior section of Highway 1 is considered sub-standard when compared to other highways with similar traffic volume in the U.S. or other parts of Canada. The majority of the route is a dangerous, undivided two-lane highway with sharp corners, prone to frequent closures and accidents.[^cite_note-65] To address this, the Ministry of Transportation and Infrastructure has undertaken an effort to twin the highway to four-lane 100 km/h standards between Kamloops and Alberta, with a targeted completion date of 2050. Several stretches of four-lane divided highway, including the Monte Creek to Pritchard section; the four-lane portions of the Kicking Horse Canyon, the 13 km-long passing lanes near Blind Bay, and many smaller four-lane divided fragments typically 2–4 km in length, are the results of this effort. As of 2020, about 25 percent of the highway between Alberta and Kamloops has been upgraded to a divided four-lane cross-section. Several new projects have been funded and are expected to the constructed by 2023, including:[^cite_note-MOT-FourLaning-60]

- A new 4.9 km-long four-lane divided section around Chase, involving the construction of an interchange at the town;

- A new interchange and twinned highway at the West entrance of Salmon Arm;

- A new four-lane bridge across the Shuswap River in Sicamous;

- A new rest area and a 2 km-long four-lane section in the Illecillewaet Valley;

- A 4.4 km-long twinning near Quartz Creek east of Golden;

- And upgrading the remaining 4 km-long stretch of two-lane highway in the Kicking Horse Canyon.
