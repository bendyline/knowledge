The **Amazon rainforest**,[^cite_note-2] also called the **Amazon jungle**, **Amazonia**, or simply the **Amazon**, is a [moist broadleaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_and_subtropical_moist_broadleaf_forests) [tropical rainforest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_rainforest) in the [Amazon biome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_biome) that covers most of the [Amazon basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_basin) of [South America](26769.md). This basin encompasses 7 million km<sup>2</sup> (2.7 million sq mi),[^cite_note-3] of which 6 million km<sup>2</sup> (2.3 million sq mi) are covered by the [rainforest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainforest).[^cite_note-4] This region includes territory belonging to nine nations and 3,344 [indigenous territories](31287705.md).

The majority of the forest, 60%, is in [Brazil](1305102.md), followed by [Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peruvian_Amazonia) with 13%, [Colombia](9299263.md) with 10%, and with minor amounts in [Bolivia](3462.md), [Ecuador](9334.md), [French Guiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Guiana), [Guyana](17238691.md), [Suriname](26828.md), and [Venezuela](32374.md). Four nations have "[Amazonas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazonas_\(disambiguation\))" as the name of one of their first-level administrative [regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regions), and [France](5843419.md) uses the name "[Guiana Amazonian Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guiana_Amazonian_Park)" for French Guiana's protected rainforest area. The Amazon represents over half of the total area of remaining rainforests on [Earth](9228.md),[^cite_note-5] and comprises the largest and most [biodiverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiverse) tract of [tropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropics) rainforest in the world, with an estimated 390 billion individual trees in about 16,000 species.[^cite_note-eurekalert.org-6]

More than 30 million people of 350 different ethnic groups live in the Amazon, which are subdivided into 9 different national political systems and 3,344 formally acknowledged [indigenous territories](31287705.md). Indigenous peoples make up 9% of the total population, and 60 groups remain largely isolated.[^cite_note-7]

[Large scale deforestation is occurring in the forest](19732090.md), creating different harmful effects. Economic losses due to deforestation in Brazil could be approximately 7 times higher in comparison to the cost of all commodities produced through deforestation. In 2023, the [World Bank](36752.md) published a report proposing a non-deforestation based economic program in the region.[^cite_note-TheWorldBank-8][^cite_note-Balancing-9] Deforestation hurts agriculture so severely that it can lead to "agro-suicide."[^cite_note-regnskog.no-10]

## Etymology

The name *Amazon* is said to arise from a war [Francisco de Orellana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_de_Orellana) fought with the [Tapuyas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapuyas) and other tribes. The women of the tribe fought alongside the men, as was their custom.[^cite_note-taylor1898-11] Orellana derived the name *Amazonas* from the [Amazons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazons) of [Greek mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mythology), described by [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus) and [Diodorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diodorus_Siculus).[^cite_note-taylor1898-11]

Based on [archaeological](18951655.md) evidence from an excavation at [Caverna da Pedra Pintada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caverna_da_Pedra_Pintada), human inhabitants first settled in the Amazon region at least 11,200 years ago.[^cite_note-14] Subsequent development led to late-prehistoric settlements along the periphery of the forest by AD 1250, which induced alterations in the [forest cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_cover).[^cite_note-Amazonia1492-15]

For a long time, it was thought that the Amazon rainforest was never more than sparsely populated, as it was impossible to sustain a large population through [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture) given the poor soil. Archeologist [Betty Meggers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betty_Meggers) was a prominent proponent of this idea, as described in her book *Amazonia: Man and Culture in a Counterfeit Paradise*. She claimed that a population density of 0.2 inhabitants per square kilometre (0.52/sq mi) is the maximum that can be sustained in the rainforest through hunting, with agriculture needed to host a larger population.[^cite_note-16] However, recent [anthropological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology) findings have suggested that the region was actually densely populated.[^cite_note-17] The [Upano Valley sites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upano_Valley_sites) in present-day eastern Ecuador predate all known complex Amazonian societies.[^cite_note-18]

Some 5 million people may have lived in the Amazon region in AD 1500, divided between dense coastal settlements, such as that at [Marajó](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marajoara_culture), and inland dwellers.[^cite_note-park-19] Based on projections of food production, one estimate suggests over 8 million people living in the Amazon in 1492.[^cite_note-Clement_Denevan_Heckenberger_Junqueira_2015_p._20150813-20] By 1900, the native indigenous population had fallen to 1 million and by the early 1980s it was less than 200,000.[^cite_note-park-19]

The first European to travel the length of the [Amazon River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_River) was [Francisco de Orellana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_de_Orellana) in 1542.[^cite_note-21] The BBC's *[Unnatural Histories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unnatural_Histories_\(TV_series\))* presents evidence that Orellana, rather than exaggerating his claims as previously thought, was correct in his observations that a complex civilization was flourishing along the Amazon in the 1540s. The [Pre-Columbian agriculture in the Amazon Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_agriculture_in_the_Amazon_Basin) was sufficiently advanced to support prosperous and populous societies. It is believed that civilization was later devastated by the spread of diseases from Europe, such as [smallpox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallpox).[^cite_note-bbc-22] This civilization was investigated by the British explorer [Percy Fawcett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_Fawcett) in the early twentieth century. The results of his expeditions were inconclusive, and he disappeared mysteriously on his last trip. His name for this lost civilization was the [City of Z](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Z).

Since the 1970s, numerous [geoglyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoglyph) have been discovered on deforested land dating between AD 1–1250, furthering claims about [Pre-Columbian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_era) civilizations.[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24] Ondemar Dias is accredited with first discovering the geoglyphs in 1977, and Alceu Ranzi is credited with furthering their discovery after flying over [Acre](229497.md).[^cite_note-bbc-22][^cite_note-25] The BBC's *Unnatural Histories* presented evidence that the Amazon rainforest, rather than being a pristine [wilderness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilderness), has been shaped by man for at least 11,000 years through practices such as [forest gardening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_gardening) and *[terra preta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_preta)*.[^cite_note-bbc-22] Terra preta is found over large areas in the Amazon forest; and is now widely accepted as a product of indigenous [soil management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_management). The development of this fertile soil allowed agriculture and [silviculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silviculture) in the previously hostile environment; meaning that large portions of the Amazon rainforest are probably the result of centuries of human management, rather than naturally occurring as has previously been supposed.[^cite_note-26] In the region of the [Xingu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xingu_peoples) tribe, remains of some of these large settlements in the middle of the Amazon forest were found in 2003 by Michael Heckenberger and colleagues of the [University of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Florida). Among those were evidence of roads, bridges and large plazas.[^cite_note-Amazonia1492-15]

In the Amazonas, there has been fighting and wars between the neighboring tribes of the [Jivaro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jivaroan_peoples). Several tribes of the Jivaroan group, including the [Shuar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuar_people), practised [headhunting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Headhunting) for trophies and [headshrinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrunken_head).[^cite_note-27] The accounts of missionaries to the area in the borderlands between Brazil and Venezuela have recounted constant infighting in the [Yanomami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yanomami) tribes. More than a third of the Yanomamo males, on average, died from warfare.[^cite_note-28]

The [Munduruku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munduruku) were a [warlike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warrior) tribe that expanded along the [Tapajós](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapaj%C3%B3s) river and its tributaries and were feared by neighboring tribes. In the early 19th century, the Munduruku were [pacified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacification_\(military_action\)) and subjugated by the Brazilians.[^cite_note-29] It is documented that large war parties of the [Bororo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bororo), [Kayapo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kayapo), Munduruku, [Guaraní](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guaran%C3%AD_people), and [Tupi people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupi_people) carried out long-distance [raids](6873191.md). Most Bororo groups were continually at war with their neighbors. In contrast, the [Xingu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xingu_peoples) have been described by ethnographers as a "peaceful" society, resorting to violence only in defense against their warlike neighbors.[^cite_note-30] In the early 20th century, thirty indigenous tribes in the Amazon basin were listed as "peaceful" and eighty-three were specifically described as "warlike".[^cite_note-31]

During the [Amazon rubber boom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_rubber_boom) it is estimated that diseases brought by immigrants, such as [typhus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhus) and [malaria](20423.md), killed 40,000 native Amazonians.[^cite_note-32]

In the 1950s, Brazilian explorer and defender of indigenous people, [Cândido Rondon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C3%A2ndido_Rondon), supported the [Villas-Bôas brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villas-B%C3%B4as_brothers)' campaign, which faced strong opposition from the government and the ranchers of [Mato Grosso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mato_Grosso) and led to the establishment of the [first Brazilian National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xingu_National_Park) for indigenous people along the [Xingu River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xingu_River) in 1961.[^cite_note-33]

In 1961, British explorer [Richard Mason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Mason_\(explorer\)) was killed by an uncontacted Amazon [tribe](55510.md) known as the [Panará](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panar%C3%A1_people).[^cite_note-34]

The [Matsés](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mats%C3%A9s) made their first permanent contact with the outside world in 1969. Before that date, they were effectively at-war with the Peruvian government.[^cite_note-35]

## Geography

### Location

Nine countries share the Amazon basin—most of the rainforest, 58.4%, is contained within the borders of Brazil. The other eight countries are Peru with 12.8%, Bolivia with 7.7%, Colombia with 7.1%, Venezuela with 6.1%, Guyana with 3.1%, Suriname with 2.5%, French Guiana with 1.4% and Ecuador with 1%.[^cite_note-Segamazonia_Coca-Castro_201301-36]

### Natural



Amazon rainforest in Colombia



Aerial view of the Amazon rainforest, near [Manaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manaus)

The rainforest likely formed during the [Eocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eocene) era (from 56 million years to 33.9 million years ago). It appeared following a global reduction of tropical temperatures when the [Atlantic Ocean](698.md) had widened sufficiently to provide a warm, moist climate to the Amazon basin. The rainforest has been in existence for at least 55 million years, and most of the region remained free of [savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savanna)-type [biomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biome) at least until the [current ice age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_ice_age) when the climate was drier and savanna more widespread.[^cite_note-morley-37][^cite_note-ptors359-38]

Following the [Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretaceous%E2%80%93Paleogene_extinction_event), the extinction of the [dinosaurs](8311.md) and the wetter climate may have allowed the tropical rainforest to spread out across the continent. From 66 to 34 [Mya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mya_\(unit\)), the rainforest extended as far south as [45°](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/45th_parallel_south). Climate fluctuations during the last 34 million years have allowed savanna regions to expand into the tropics. During the [Oligocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oligocene), for example, the rainforest spanned a relatively narrow band. It expanded again during the [Middle Miocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Miocene), then retracted to a mostly inland formation at the [last glacial maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_glacial_period).[^cite_note-39] However, the rainforest still managed to thrive during these [glacial periods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glacial_periods), allowing for the survival and evolution of a broad diversity of species.[^cite_note-yadvinder-40]



Aerial view of the Amazon rainforest

During the [mid-Eocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-Eocene), it is believed that the [drainage basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin) of the Amazon was split along the middle of the continent by the [Purus Arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purus_Arch). Water on the eastern side flowed toward the [Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic), while to the west water flowed toward the Pacific across the [Amazonas Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazonas_Basin). As the [Andes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andes) Mountains rose, however, a large basin was created that enclosed a lake; now known as the [Solimões Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solim%C3%B5es_River). Within the last 5–10 million years, this accumulating water broke through the Purus Arch, joining the easterly flow toward the Atlantic.[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42]



Aerial view of the Amazon rainforest near [Manaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manaus)

There is evidence that there have been significant changes in the Amazon rainforest [vegetation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation) over the last 21,000 years through the [last glacial maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_glacial_maximum) (LGM) and subsequent deglaciation. Analyses of sediment deposits from Amazon basin paleolakes and the Amazon Fan indicate that rainfall in the basin during the LGM was lower than for the present, and this was almost certainly associated with reduced moist tropical vegetation cover in the basin.[^cite_note-Colinvaux1-43] In present day, the Amazon receives approximately 9 feet of rainfall annually. There is a debate, however, over how extensive this reduction was. Some scientists argue that the rainforest was reduced to small, isolated [refugia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refugium_\(population_biology\)) separated by open forest and grassland;[^cite_note-VanDerHammen-44] other scientists argue that the rainforest remained largely intact but extended less far to the north, south, and east than is seen today.[^cite_note-Colinvaux2-45] This debate has proved difficult to resolve because the practical limitations of working in the rainforest mean that data sampling is biased away from the center of the Amazon basin, and both explanations are reasonably well supported by the available data.

#### Sahara Desert dust

More than 56% of the dust fertilizing the Amazon rainforest is blown by the wind to the Amazon from the [Bodélé depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bod%C3%A9l%C3%A9_depression) in Northern Chad in the [Sahara](325363.md) desert. The dust contains [phosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus), important for plant growth. The yearly Sahara dust replaces the equivalent amount of phosphorus washed away yearly in Amazon soil from rains and floods.[^cite_note-46]

NASA's [CALIPSO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CALIPSO) satellite has measured the amount of dust transported by wind from the Sahara to the Amazon: an average of 182 million tons of dust are windblown out of the Sahara each year (some dust falls into the Atlantic), 15% of which of falls over the Amazon basin (22 million tons of it consisting of phosphorus).[^cite_note-47]

CALIPSO uses a laser range finder to scan the Earth's atmosphere for the vertical distribution of dust and other aerosols. and regularly tracks the Sahara-Amazon dust plume. CALIPSO has measured variations in the dust amounts transported – an 86 percent drop between the highest amount of dust transported in 2007 and the lowest in 2011. This is possibly caused by rainfall variations in the [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel), a strip of semi-arid land on the southern border of the Sahara.[^cite_note-48]

Amazon phosphorus also comes as smoke due to biomass burning in Africa.[^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50]

## Biodiversity

See also: [List of plants of the Amazon rainforest of Brazil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_plants_of_the_Amazon_rainforest_of_Brazil), [Fauna of the Amazon rainforest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fauna_of_the_Amazon_rainforest), and [Amazonian manatee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazonian_manatee)



[Deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation) in the Amazon rainforest threatens many species of tree frogs, which are very sensitive to environmental changes (pictured: [giant leaf frog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phyllomedusa_bicolor))



A giant, bundled [liana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liana) in western Brazil

Wet tropical forests are the most species-rich [biome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biome), and tropical forests in the Americas are consistently more species rich than the wet forests in Africa and Asia.[^cite_note-Turner_2001-51] As the largest tract of tropical rainforest in the Americas, the Amazonian rainforests have unparalleled [biodiversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity). One in ten known species in the world lives in the Amazon rainforest.[^cite_note-52] This constitutes the largest collection of living [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant) and [animal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal) [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species) in the world.[^cite_note-53]

The region is home to about 2.5 million insect [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species),[^cite_note-54] tens of thousands of plants, and some 2,000 birds and [mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammal). To date, at least 40,000 plant species,[^cite_note-55] 2,200 [fishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neotropical_fish),[^cite_note-AlbertReis2011-56] 1,294 birds, 427 mammals, 428 amphibians, and 378 reptiles have been scientifically classified in the region.[^cite_note-Da_Silva_2005-57] One in five of all bird species are found in the Amazon rainforest, and one in five of the fish species live in Amazonian rivers and streams. Scientists have described between 96,660 and 128,843 [invertebrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertebrate) species in [Brazil](3383.md) alone.[^cite_note-Lewinsohn_2005-58]

The biodiversity of plant species is the highest on Earth with one 2001 study finding a quarter square kilometer (62 acres) of Ecuadorian rainforest supports more than 1,100 tree species.[^cite_note-59] A study in 1999 found one square kilometer (247 acres) of Amazon rainforest can contain about 90,790 tonnes of living plants. The average plant biomass is estimated at 356 ± 47 tonnes per hectare.[^cite_note-60] To date, an estimated 438,000 species of plants of economic and social interest have been registered in the region with many more remaining to be discovered or catalogued.[^cite_note-61] The total number of [tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree) species in the region is estimated at 16,000.[^cite_note-eurekalert.org-6]

The green leaf area of plants and trees in the rainforest varies by about 25% as a result of seasonal changes. Leaves expand during the dry season when sunlight is at a maximum, then undergo [abscission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abscission) in the cloudy wet season. These changes provide a balance of carbon between photosynthesis and respiration.[^cite_note-62]

Each hectare of the Amazon rainforest contains around 1 billion [invertebrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertebrate). The number of species per hectare in the Amazon rainforest is presented in the following table:[^cite_note-63]

| [Reptile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reptile) | 22 |

The rainforest contains several species that can pose a hazard. Among the largest predatory creatures are the [black caiman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_caiman), [jaguar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaguar), [cougar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cougar), and [anaconda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaconda). In the river, [electric eels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_eel) can produce an electric shock that can stun or kill, while [piranha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piranha) are known to bite and injure humans.[^cite_note-64] Various species of [poison dart frogs](812186.md) secrete [lipophilic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipophilic) [alkaloid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkaloid) toxins through their flesh. There are also numerous parasites and disease vectors. [Vampire bats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vampire_bat) dwell in the rainforest and can spread the [rabies](16946852.md) virus.[^cite_note-65] [Malaria](20423.md), [yellow fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_fever) and [dengue fever](39669.md) can also be contracted in the Amazon region.

The biodiversity in the Amazon is becoming increasingly threatened, primarily by habitat loss from deforestation as well as increased frequency of fires. Over 90% of Amazonian plant and vertebrate species (13,000–14,000 in total) may have been impacted to some degree by fires.[^cite_note-Feng-2021-66]

## Threats

### Deforestation

Main article: [Deforestation of the Amazon rainforest](19732090.md)

See also: [Trans-Amazonian Highway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Amazonian_Highway) and [Trans-Amazonian Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Amazonian_Railway)

&#x20;Home to much of the Amazon rainforest, Brazil's tropical [primary (old-growth)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old-growth_forest) [forest loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_loss) greatly exceeds that of other countries.

[^cite_note-WRI_20240404-67]



Overall, 20% of the Amazon rainforest has been "transformed" (deforested) and another 6% has been "highly degraded", causing Amazon Watch to warn that the Amazonia is in the midst of a tipping point crisis.

[^cite_note-AmazonWatch_202209-68]

[Deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation) is the conversion of forested areas to non-forested areas. The main sources of deforestation in the Amazon are human settlement and the development of the land.[^cite_note-69] In 2022, about 20% of the Amazon rainforest has already been deforested and a further 6% was "highly degraded".[^cite_note-Tandon-2023-70] Research suggests that upon reaching about 20–25% (hence 0–5% more), the [tipping point](16817594.md) to flip it into a non-forest ecosystem – degraded [savannah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savannah) – (in eastern, southern and central Amazonia) will be reached.[^cite_note-71][^cite_note-AmazonTippingPoint-72][^cite_note-73] This process of savanisation would take decades to take full effect.[^cite_note-Tandon-2023-70]

Prior to the early 1960s, access to the forest's interior was highly restricted, and the forest remained basically intact.[^cite_note-Kirbyetal-2006-74] Farms established during the 1960s were based on crop cultivation and the [slash and burn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash_and_burn) method. However, the colonists were unable to manage their fields and the crops because of the loss of [soil fertility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_fertility) and weed invasion.[^cite_note-watkins_griffiths-75] The soils in the Amazon are productive for just a short period of time, so farmers are constantly moving to new areas and clearing more land.[^cite_note-watkins_griffiths-75] These farming practices led to deforestation and caused extensive environmental damage.[^cite_note-76] Deforestation is considerable, and areas cleared of forest are visible to the naked eye from outer space.

In the 1970s, construction began on the [Trans-Amazonian highway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Amazonian_highway). This highway represented a major threat to the Amazon rainforest.[^cite_note-77] The highway still has not been completed, limiting the environmental damage.

Between 1991 and 2000, the total area of forest lost in the Amazon rose from 415,000 to 587,000 km<sup>2</sup> (160,000 to 227,000 sq mi), with most of the lost forest becoming pasture for cattle.[^cite_note-CIFOR2004-78] Seventy percent of formerly forested land in the Amazon, and 91% of land deforested since 1970, have been used for livestock [pasture](30865437.md).[^cite_note-79][^cite_note-80] Currently, Brazil is the largest global producer of [soybeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soybean). New research however, conducted by Leydimere Oliveira et al., has shown that the more rainforest is logged in the Amazon, the less precipitation reaches the area and so the lower the yield per hectare becomes. So despite the popular perception, there has been no economical advantage for Brazil from logging rainforest zones and converting these to pastoral fields.[^cite_note-81]



Indigenous protesters from [Vale do Javari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vale_do_Javari)

The needs of soy farmers have been used to justify many of the controversial transportation projects that are currently developing in the Amazon. The first two highways successfully opened up the rainforest and led to increased settlement and deforestation. The mean annual deforestation rate from 2000 to 2005 (22,392 km<sup>2</sup> or 8,646 sq mi per year) was 18% higher than in the previous five years (19,018 km<sup>2</sup> or 7,343 sq mi per year).[^cite_note-82] Although deforestation declined significantly in the Brazilian Amazon between 2004 and 2014, there has been an increase to the present day.[^cite_note-83]



Brazilian mining operation in the Amazon Rainforest

Brazil's former President, [Jair Bolsonaro](46603496.md), supported the relaxation of regulations placed on agricultural land. In 2019 Brazil's protections of the Amazon rainforest were slashed, resulting in a severe loss of trees.[^cite_note-84] According to Brazil's [National Institute for Space Research](30875637.md) (INPE), deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon rose more than 50% in the first three months of 2020 compared to the same three-month period in 2019.[^cite_note-85] Deforestation reached a 15 year high in 2021.[^cite_note-86]

Since the discovery of [fossil fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel) reservoirs in the Amazon rainforest, oil drilling activity has steadily increased, peaking in the Western Amazon in the 1970s and ushering another drilling boom in the 2000s.[^cite_note-87] Oil companies have to set up their operations by opening new roads through the forests, which often contributes to deforestation in the region.[^cite_note-88] 9.4% of the territory of the Amazon is affected by oil fields.[^cite_note-Mongabay-89]

Mining is also a major driver of deforestation. 17% of the area of the Amazon Rainforest is affected by mining.[^cite_note-Mongabay-89]

The transition to solar and wind energy, digitalization, raised the demand for [cassiterite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassiterite) (the main ore of [tin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin) used also for financing [gold mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_mining_in_Brazil)), [manganese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese) and [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper), which attracted many illegal miners to the Amazon. This led to deforestation, different environmental and social problems. Hydropower also creates significant problems in the Amazon. Such activities are defined by the [World Rainforest Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Rainforest_Movement) as "Green extractivism".[^cite_note-90][^cite_note-91]

The [European Union–Mercosur free trade agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union%E2%80%93Mercosur_free_trade_agreement), which would form one of the world's largest free trade areas, has been denounced by environmental activists and indigenous rights campaigners.[^cite_note-92] The fear is that the deal could lead to more deforestation of the Amazon rainforest as it expands market access to Brazilian beef.[^cite_note-93]

According to a November 2021 report by Brazil's [INPE](30875637.md), based on [satellite data](46597.md#Environmental_monitoring), deforestation has increased by 22% over 2020 and is at its highest level since 2006.[^cite_note-94][^cite_note-95]

### 2019 fires

Main article: [2019 Amazon rainforest wildfires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Amazon_rainforest_wildfires)

There were 72,843 fires in Brazil in 2019, with more than half within the Amazon region.[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-97][^cite_note-98] In August 2019 there were a record number of fires.[^cite_note-99] [Deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation_in_Brazil) rose more than 88% in June 2019 compared with the same month in 2018.[^cite_note-100]

The increased area of fire-impacted forest coincided with a relaxation of environmental regulations from the Brazilian government. Notably, before those regulations were put in place in 2008 the fire-impacted area was also larger compared to the regulation period of 2009–2018. As these fire continue to move closer to the heart of the Amazon basin, their impact on biodiversity will only increase in scale, as the cumulative fire-impacted area is correlated with the number of species impacted.[^cite_note-Feng-2021-66]

In 2020, a 17 percent rise was noted in the [Amazon wildfires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Amazon_rainforest_wildfires), marking the worst start to the fire season in a decade. The first 10 days of August 2020 witnessed 10,136 fires. An analysis of the government figures reflected 81 per cent increase in fires in federal reserves, in comparison with the same period in 2019.[^cite_note-101] However, President [Jair Bolsonaro](46603496.md) turned down the existence of fires, calling it a "lie", despite the data produced by his own government.[^cite_note-102] Satellites in September recorded 32,017 hotspots in the world's largest rainforest, a 61% rise from the same month in 2019.[^cite_note-103] In addition, October saw a huge surge in the number of hotspots in the forest (more than 17,000 fires are burning in the Amazon's rainforest) – with more than double the amount detected in the same month last year.[^cite_note-104]

### Impact of early 21st-century Amazon droughts

In 2005, parts of the Amazon basin experienced the worst drought in one hundred years,[^cite_note-105] and there were indications that 2006 may have been a second successive year of drought.[^cite_note-106] A 2006 article in the UK newspaper *[The Independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Independent)* reported the [Woods Hole Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woods_Hole_Research_Center) results, showing that the forest in its present form could survive only three years of drought.[^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108] Scientists at the Brazilian [National Institute of Amazonian Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Amazonian_Research) argued in the article that this drought response, coupled with the effects of deforestation on regional climate, are pushing the rainforest towards a "[tipping point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tipping_point_\(climatology\))" where it would irreversibly start to die.[^cite_note-AmazonTippingPoint-72] It concluded that the forest is on the brink of being turned into [savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savanna) or desert, with catastrophic consequences for the world's climate. A study published in *Nature Communications* in October 2020 found that about 40% of the Amazon rainforest is at risk of becoming a savanna-like ecosystem due to reduced rainfall.[^cite_note-109] A study published in *[Nature climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_climate_change)* provided direct empirical evidence that more than three-quarters of the Amazon rainforest has been losing resilience since the early 2000s, risking dieback with profound implications for biodiversity, carbon storage and climate change at a global scale.[^cite_note-110] Research from 2025 using hundreds of climate-model simulations says even passing 1.5C of global warming temporarily would trigger a significant risk of Amazon forest dieback.[^cite_note-111][^cite_note-112]

&#x20;According to the [World Wide Fund for Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Fund_for_Nature), the combination of climate change and deforestation increases the drying effect of dead trees that fuels [forest fires](56106.md).[^cite_note-113]



[Guiana Amazonian Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guiana_Amazonian_Park) in [French Guiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Guiana)

In 2010, the Amazon rainforest experienced another severe drought, in some ways more extreme than the 2005 drought. The affected region was approximately 3,000,000 km<sup>2</sup> (1,160,000 sq mi) of rainforest, compared with 1,900,000 km<sup>2</sup> (734,000 sq mi) in 2005. The 2010 drought had three epicenters where vegetation died off, whereas in 2005, the drought was focused on the southwestern part. The findings were published in the journal *Science*. In a typical year, the Amazon absorbs 1.5 gigatons of carbon dioxide; during 2005 instead 5 gigatons were released and in 2010 8 gigatons were released.[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115] Additional severe droughts occurred in 2010, 2015, and 2016.[^cite_note-116]

### Climate change

&#x20;Environmentalists are concerned about [loss of biodiversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loss_of_biodiversity) that will result from [destruction of the forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_destruction), and also about the [release of the carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide_in_Earth's_atmosphere) contained within the vegetation, which could accelerate [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming). Amazonian evergreen forests account for about 10% of the world's terrestrial primary productivity and 10% of the [carbon stores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_sink) in ecosystems[^cite_note-Melillo-117] – of the order of 1.1 × 10<sup>11</sup> metric tonnes of carbon.[^cite_note-Tian-118] Amazonian forests are estimated to have accumulated 0.62 ± 0.37 tons of carbon per hectare per year between 1975 and 1996.[^cite_note-Tian-118] In 2021 it was reported that the Amazon emitted more greenhouse gases than it absorbed for the first time.[^cite_note-RainforestNetEmitsGreenhouseGases-119] Though often referenced as producing more than a quarter of the Earth's oxygen, this often stated, but misused statistic actually refers to oxygen turnover. The net contribution of the ecosystem is approximately zero.[^cite_note-120]



Tipping cascades in the Amazon rainforest, according to the 2023 Global Tipping Points report. Potential tipping points for the Amazon include a 3–4°C rise in global temperature and deforestation levels over 40%.

[^cite_note-121]

[Deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation) in the Amazon rainforest region has a negative impact on local climate.[^cite_note-122] It was one of the main causes of the severe [drought](37335.md) of 2014–2015 in Brazil.[^cite_note-123][^cite_note-124] This is because the moisture from the forests is important to the rainfall in [Brazil](3383.md), [Paraguay](23420.md) and [Argentina](18951905.md). Half of the rainfall in the Amazon area is produced by the forests.[^cite_note-125]

A 2009 study found that a 4 °C rise (above pre-industrial levels) in global temperatures by 2100 would kill 85% of the Amazon rainforest while a temperature rise of 3 °C would kill some 75% of the Amazon.[^cite_note-126]

Results of a 2021 [scientific synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_review) indicate that, in terms of global warming, the [Amazon basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_basin) with the Amazon rainforest is currently emitting more [greenhouse gases](21350772.md) than [it absorbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_sink) overall. Climate change impacts and human activities in the area – mainly wildfires, current land-use and [deforestation](19732090.md) – are causing a release of forcing agents that likely result in a net warming effect.[^cite_note-RainforestNetEmitsGreenhouseGases-119][^cite_note-10.3389/ffgc.2021.618401-127][^cite_note-128]

&#x20;One [computer model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_climate_model) of future [climate change](5042951.md) caused by [greenhouse gas emissions](9528025.md) shows that the Amazon rainforest could become unsustainable under conditions of severely reduced rainfall and increased temperatures, leading to an almost complete loss of rainforest cover in the basin by 2100.,[^cite_note-129][^cite_note-Radford-130] and severe economic, [natural capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_capital) and [ecosystem services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem_service) impacts of not averting the tipping point.[^cite_note-131] However, simulations of Amazon basin climate change across many different models are not consistent in their estimation of any rainfall response, ranging from weak increases to strong decreases.[^cite_note-IPCC-132] The result indicates that the rainforest could be threatened through the 21st century by climate change in addition to deforestation.

## Conservation

See also: [Deforestation and climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation_and_climate_change), [Gaviotas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaviotas), and [Amazon Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Fund)



Peruvian researcher [Tatiana Espinosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tatiana_Espinosa?action=edit\&redlink=1) with a *[Dipteryx micrantha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipteryx_micrantha)* tree in the [Peruvian Amazonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peruvian_Amazonia)

In 1989, environmentalist C.M. Peters and two colleagues stated there is economic as well as biological incentive to protecting the rainforest. One hectare in the [Peruvian Amazon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peruvian_Amazon) has been calculated to have a value of \$6820 if [intact forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intact_forest_landscape) is sustainably harvested for fruits, latex, and timber; \$1000 if clear-cut for commercial timber (not sustainably harvested); or \$148 if used as cattle pasture.[^cite_note-Peters-133]



A map of [uncontacted tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncontacted_peoples), around the start of the 21st century

As indigenous territories continue to be destroyed by deforestation and [ecocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecocide) (such as in the [Peruvian Amazon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peruvian_Amazon)),[^cite_note-Dean-134] [indigenous peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples)' rainforest communities continue to disappear, while others, like the [Urarina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urarina) continue to struggle to fight for their cultural survival and the fate of their forested territories. Meanwhile, the relationship between non-human primates in the subsistence and symbolism of indigenous lowland South American peoples has gained increased attention, as have ethno-biology and [community-based conservation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community-based_conservation) efforts.

A 2020 study in the Brazilian Amazon shows that protection of freshwater biodiversity can be increased by up to 600% through integrated freshwater-terrestrial planning.[^cite_note-135]

From 2002 to 2006, the conserved land in the Amazon rainforest almost tripled and deforestation rates dropped up to 60%. About 1,000,000 km<sup>2</sup> (250,000,000 acres) have been put onto some sort of conservation, which adds up to a current amount of 1,730,000 km<sup>2</sup> (430,000,000 acres).[^cite_note-136]

In April 2019, the [Ecuadorian](9334.md) court stopped [oil exploration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_exploration) activities in 180,000 hectares (440,000 acres) of the Amazon rainforest.[^cite_note-137] In July 2019, the Ecuadorian court forbade the government to sell territory with forests to oil companies.[^cite_note-138]

In September 2019, the US and Brazil agreed to promote private-sector development in the Amazon. They also pledged a \$100m biodiversity conservation fund for the Amazon led by the private sector. Brazil's foreign minister stated that opening the rainforest to economic development was the only way to protect it.[^cite_note-139]

&#x20;In 2022 the supreme court of Ecuador decided that ""under no circumstances can a project be carried out that generates excessive sacrifices to the collective rights of communities and nature." It also required the government to respect the opinion of [Indigenous peoples of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas) about different industrial projects on their land. Advocates of the decision argue that it will have consequences far beyond Ecuador. In general, ecosystems are in better shape when [indigenous peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples) own or manage the land.[^cite_note-140]



Amazon rainforest

Due to the conservation policies of [Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva](99328.md) in the first 10 months of 2023 deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon decreased by around 50% compared to the same period in 2022. This was despite a severe drought, one of the worst on record, that exacerbated the situation. Climate change, El Nino, deforestation increases the likelihood of drought condition in the Amazon.[^cite_note-141]

According to Amazon Conservation's MAAP forest monitoring program, the deforestation rate in the Amazon from the January 1 to November 8, 2023, decreased by 56% in comparison to the same period in 2022. The main cause is the decline in deforestation rate in Brazil, due to the government's policies, while Columbia, Peru and Bolivia also reduced deforestation.[^cite_note-142]

In January 2024 published data showed a 50% decline in deforestation rate in the Amazon rainforest and 43% rise in vegetation loss in the neighbor [Cerrado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerrado) during the year of 2023 in comparison to 2022. Both biomes together lose 12,980 km², 18% less than in 2022.[^cite_note-143]

[Conservation International Brazil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_International_Brazil) is an implementing partner in large-scale conservation programmes in the Brazilian Amazon, including the Global Environment Facility (GEF)-financed *Amazon Sustainable Landscapes Project* and restoration actions funded through the Amazon Fund initiative *Restaura Amazônia* in [Pará](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Par%C3%A1) and [Maranhão](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maranh%C3%A3o).[^cite_note-144][^cite_note-145]

### Remote sensing

See also: [Environmental monitoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_monitoring), [Environmental management system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_management_system), and [Unmanned aerial vehicle](58900.md)



This image reveals how the forest and the [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere) interact to create a uniform layer of "popcorn-shaped" [cumulus clouds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumulus_humilis_cloud).

The use of [remotely sensed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_sensing) data is dramatically improving conservationists' knowledge of the Amazon basin. Given the objectivity and lowered costs of [satellite-based](46597.md) land cover and -change analysis, it appears likely that remote sensing technology will be an integral part of assessing the extents, locations and damage of deforestation in the basin.[^cite_note-146] Furthermore, remote sensing is the best and perhaps only possible way to study the Amazon on a large scale.[^cite_note-147]

The use of remote sensing for the conservation of the Amazon is also being used by the indigenous tribes of the basin to protect their tribal lands from commercial interests. Using handheld [GPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Positioning_System) devices and programs like [Google Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Earth), members of the Trio Tribe, who live in the rainforests of southern Suriname, map out their ancestral lands to help strengthen their territorial claims.[^cite_note-148] Currently, most tribes in the Amazon do not have clearly defined boundaries, making it easier for commercial ventures to target their territories.

To accurately map the Amazon's biomass and subsequent carbon-related emissions, the classification of tree growth stages within different parts of the forest is crucial. In 2006, Tatiana Kuplich organized the trees of the Amazon into four categories: mature forest, regenerating forest \[less than three years], regenerating forest \[between three and five years of regrowth], and regenerating forest \[eleven to eighteen years of continued development].[^cite_note-149] The researcher used a combination of [synthetic aperture radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_aperture_radar) (SAR) and [Thematic Mapper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thematic_Mapper) (TM) to accurately place the different portions of the Amazon into one of the four classifications.

### Possibility of forest-friendly economy

Experts consider a forest-friendly economy the best method to sustain the Brazilian agricultural sector, because deforestation disrupts rainfall patterns and increases temperatures, hurting agriculture. This may lead to "agro-suicide." Even irrigation can not solve the problem, because for irrigation farmers need water, which is already in short supply.[^cite_note-regnskog.no-10]

In 2023 the [World Bank](36752.md), published a report named: "A Balancing Act for Brazil's Amazonian States: An Economic Memorandum". The report stating that economic losses due to deforestation in Brazil could reach around 317 billion dollars per year, approximately 7 times higher in comparison to the cost of all commodities produced through deforestation, proposed non-deforestation based economic program in the region of the Amazon rainforest.[^cite_note-TheWorldBank-8][^cite_note-Balancing-9]



Silvopasture integrates livestock, forage, and trees. (Photo: USDA NAC)

[Silvopasture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silvopasture) (integrating trees, forage and grazing) can help to stop deforestation in the region.[^cite_note-150]

According to WWF, [ecotourism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecotourism) could help the Amazon to reduce deforestation and climate change. Ecotourism is currently still little practiced in the Amazon, partly due to lack of information about places where implementation is possible. Ecotourism is a sector that can also be taken up by the Indigenous community in the Amazon as a source of income and revenue. An ecotourism project in the Brazilian section of the rainforest had been under consideration by Brazil's State Secretary for the Environment and Sustainable Development in 2009, along the [Aripuanã River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aripuan%C3%A3_River), in the Aripuanã Sustainable Development Reserve.[^cite_note-151] Also, some community-based ecotourism exists in the [Mamirauá Sustainable Development Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamirau%C3%A1_Sustainable_Development_Reserve).[^cite_note-152] [Ecotourism is also practiced in the Peruvian section of the rainforest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecotourism_in_Peru). A few ecolodges are for instance present between Cusco and Madre de Dios.[^cite_note-153]

In May 2023 Brazil's bank federation decided to implement a new sustainability standard demanding from meatpackers to ensure their meat is not coming from illegally deforested area. Credits will not be given to those who will not meet the new standards. The decision came after the European Union decides to implement regulations to stop deforestation. Brazil beef exporters, said the standard is not just because it is not applied to land owners.[^cite_note-154] 21 banks representing 81% of the credit market in Brazil agree to follow those rules.[^cite_note-155]

According to a statement of the Colombian government deforestation rates in [the Colombian Amazon](9299263.md) fell by 70% in the first 9 months of 2023 compared to the same period in the previous year, what can be attributed to the conservation policies of the government. One of them is paying local residents for conserving the forest.[^cite_note-156]

On June 4, Colombia passed the Sustainable and Deforestation-Free Cattle Ranching law, which tries to ensure that beef sold in supermarkets is not linked to deforestation. According to the Environmental Investigation Agency, the country has accumulated 3.3 million hectares of forest loss that is equal to the size of Belgium. "Colombia is setting an example for the region and the rest of the world. This law is a victory for forests, for the communities that protect them, and for consumers who demand that their beef does not contribute to deforestation and illicit economies," said Susanne Breitkopf, Director of Forest Campaigns at EIA-US.[^cite_note-157]
