# Bipartisanship in United States politics



Republican president [Ronald Reagan](25433.md) discusses the 1984 budget with Democratic speaker [Tip O'Neill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tip_O'Neill).

[American politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_politics) has often settled into a [two-party system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-party_system), which as well as involving conflict between the two parties has also involved long periods of [bipartisanship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bipartisanship).

## Background

[James Madison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Madison) argued in *[The Federalist Papers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Federalist_Papers)* that [factionalism](40362512.md) was a danger to democracy, as it involved groups pushing their interests to the detriment of the [national interest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_interest). The [Founders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Founders_of_the_United_States) were largely [nonpartisan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonpartisanship), and did not think that political parties would play a role in American politics.&#x20;

However, political parties have long been a major force in [US politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_politics), and in [its history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_United_States), the nation has alternated between periods of intense party rivalry and partisanship, as well as periods of bipartisanship.

## Periods of bipartisanship

There have been periods of bipartisanship in American politics, such as when [Republican](32070.md) [Abraham Lincoln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln) selected [Democrat](5043544.md) [Andrew Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Johnson) as his running mate for [vice president](32759.md).[^cite_note-1] Republicans supported legislation by Democratic president [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson) in the early 1960s, and when Democrats worked with Republican president [Ronald Reagan](25433.md) in the 1980s.[^cite_note-2] It is claimed that the non-partisanship in foreign policy was a precursor to the concept of modern bipartisanship in US politics. This was articulated in 1912 by President [William Howard Taft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Howard_Taft), who stated that the fundamental foreign policies of the United States should be raised above party differences.[^cite_note-3]

More recently, this was also shown in the case of President [George H. W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_H._W._Bush)'s administration, which began with an atmosphere of bipartisanship on foreign policy in Washington. During this period, the concept of bipartisanship implied a consensus not only between the two parties but also the executive and legislative branches of the government to implement foreign policy. This was seen in the article *Bipartisan Objectives for American Foreign Policy*, authored by [Henry Kissinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Kissinger), President [Richard Nixon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nixon)'s Secretary of State, and [Cyrus Vance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_Vance), who was secretary during President [Jimmy Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Carter)'s administration.[^cite_note-4]

## Oppositional politics since 2010s

In the 2010s, there was wide disagreement between the Republicans and Democrats, because the minority party had been voting as a bloc against major legislation, according to [James Fallows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Fallows). In 2010 ([Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) had begun [his presidency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_Barack_Obama) on [January 20, 2009](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_inauguration_of_Barack_Obama)), the minority party (then the Republicans) had the ability to "discipline its ranks" so that none join the majority, and this situation in the [Congress](31756.md) is unprecedented, according to Fallows. He saw this inability to have bipartisanship as evidence of a "structural failure of the American government."[^cite_note-twsNovA11aa-5] Adviser to Obama, [Rahm Emanuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahm_Emanuel), said the period from 2008–2010 was marked by extreme partisanship.[^cite_note-6] After the [U.S. elections of 2010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._elections_of_2010), with sizeable gains by Republicans in the [House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_States_House_elections) and [Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_States_Senate_elections), analyst Charles Babington of the [Associated Press](18935732.md) suggested that both parties remained far apart on major issues such as [immigration](15051.md) and [Medicare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicare_\(United_States\)), and there may be chances for agreement about lesser issues such as [electric cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_car), [nuclear power](22153.md), and tax breaks for businesses; Babington was not optimistic about chances for bipartisanship on major issues in the next few years.[^cite_note-7]

In 2010, analyst [Benedict Carey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedict_Carey) (*[The New York Times](30680.md)*) agreed that political analysts tend to agree that the government will continue to be divided and marked by paralysis and feuding, there was research suggesting that humans have a "profound capacity through which vicious adversaries can form alliances," according to [Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California) professor [Dacher Keltner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dacher_Keltner).[^cite_note-8] According to [Robert Siegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Siegel) ([National Public Radio](29697232.md) (NPR)), there was virtually no cooperation between Democrats and Republicans in the U.S. during the few years before 2010.[^cite_note-twsNovA13-9]

An analysis in March 2010 suggested that the then state of American politics was marked by *oppositional politics* which left the voters [cynical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynical) about the process.[^cite_note-twsNovA16-10] Bipartisanship requires "hard work", is "sometimes dull", and entails trying to find "common ground" but enables "serious problem solving", according to editorial writers at *[The Christian Science Monitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Christian_Science_Monitor)* in 2010.[^cite_note-11]

## Impact

According to political analyst [James Fallows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Fallows) in *[The Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Atlantic)* (based on a "note from someone with many decades' experience in national politics"), bipartisanship is a phenomenon belonging to a [two-party system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-party_system) such as the political system of the [United States](3434750.md) and does not apply to a [parliamentary system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_system) (such as [Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain)) since the minority party is not involved in helping write legislation or voting for it. Fallows argues that in a two-party system, the minority party can be obstructionist and thwart the actions of the majority party.[^cite_note-twsNovA11aa-5] Although, [Anne Applebaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Applebaum) has argued that the [United Kingdom](31717.md) often has a bipartisan approach to politics despite appearances.[^cite_note-12]

A call for bipartisanship is often made by presidents who "can't get their way in Congress," according to one view.[^cite_note-13] Military policies of the [Cold War](325329.md) and actions like the [Iraq War](5043324.md) were promoted and supported, through the [mass media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_media), as bipartisan acts.[^cite_note-14]
