# Exile

For other uses, see [Exile (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exile_\(disambiguation\)).

"Banish" and "Banishment" redirect here. For other uses, see [Banish (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banish_\(disambiguation\)).



Painting of [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon) in exile on [Saint Helena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Helena)



*The First Night in Exile* – This painting comes from a series illustrating the *[Ramayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramayana)*, a [Hindu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism) [epic poem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_poetry). It depicts prince Rama, who is wrongly exiled from his father's kingdom, accompanied only by his wife and brother.



*[Dante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante) in Exile* by Domenico Petarlini

**Exile** or **banishment** is primarily penal expulsion from one's native [country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country), and secondarily [expatriation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expatriation) or prolonged absence from one's homeland under either the compulsion of circumstance or the rigors of some high purpose. Usually persons and peoples suffer exile, but sometimes social entities like institutions (e.g. the [papacy](23056.md) or a [government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government-in-exile)) are forced from their homeland.

In [Roman law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_law), *exsilium* denoted both voluntary exile and banishment as a [capital punishment](5902.md) alternative to death. [Deportation](412990.md) was forced exile, and entailed the lifelong loss of citizenship and property. [Relegation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relegatio) was a milder form of deportation, which preserved the subject's citizenship and property.[^cite_note-\:0-1]

The term [diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diaspora) describes group exile, both voluntary and forced. "[Government in exile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_in_exile)" describes a government of a country that has relocated and argues its legitimacy from outside that country. Voluntary exile is often depicted as a form of protest by the person who claims it, to avoid persecution and prosecution (such as tax or criminal allegations), an act of shame or repentance, or isolating oneself to be able to devote time to a particular pursuit.

Article 9 of the [Universal Declaration of Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights) states that "No one shall be subjected to arbitrary arrest, detention or exile.

## For individuals

### History

See also: [Penal transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penal_transportation) and [Penal colony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penal_colony)

Banishment was used as a punishment in ancient societies such as [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia), [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) and [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome).[^cite_note-2] It is stipulated as the punishment for [incest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incest) with one's daughter in the [Code of Hammurabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Hammurabi),[^cite_note-3] for [manslaughter](7397035.md) in [Mosaic law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosaic_Law), for murder in [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Athens) according to [Draco's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draco_\(legislator\)) homicide law,[^cite_note-\:1-4] and for rape according to the ancient Indian text *[Manusmriti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manusmriti)*.[^cite_note-\:2-5] A special form of banishment known as [ostracism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostracism) was practiced in Athens, in which citizens could vote for the expulsion of any citizen for ten years. Ostracism did not entail the loss of property or citizenship, and the ostracized person could return after ten years without disgrace or further penalty.[^cite_note-6] Banishment was the punishment for a variety of offenses in Ancient Rome.[^cite_note-\:1-4] In [Roman law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_law), *exsilium* denoted both voluntary exile and banishment as an alternative to [capital punishment](5902.md).[^cite_note-\:0-1] When allowed as an alternative to death, it was accompanied by an administrative decree called *interdictio aquae et ignis* ("interdiction of water and fire"), which declared the offender an [outlaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outlaw), allowing any person who encountered him or her within the borders of the country to kill him or her.[^cite_note-7] During the [Late Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Republic), there was consensus among the public and legislators to reduce the incidence of capital punishment by allowing condemned persons to go into voluntary exile.[^cite_note-8] [Deportation](412990.md) was forced exile, and entailed the lifelong loss of citizenship and property. [Relegation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relegatio) was a milder form of deportation, which preserved the subject's citizenship and property.[^cite_note-\:0-1] The [Tang Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_Code) of 7th-century China lists a number of crimes punishable by banishment.[^cite_note-\:2-5]

Exile was also imposed as a punishment in many societies in medieval Europe. Germanic peoples such as the Franks and Danes are known to have used it.[^cite_note-\:2-5] In England, it can be traced back to the 12th century. At that time, a criminal could be allowed to claim [sanctuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanctuary) in a church; if they confessed to their crimes within forty days and took an oath to leave the country and not return without royal permission, they would be allowed to safely go into exile. This practice was abolished by King [James I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_VI_and_I) in 1623.[^cite_note-\:1-4] Banishment was also a common punishment in the Netherlands: from 1650 to 1750, at least 97 percent of non-capital sentences passed in Amsterdam included some form of banishment.[^cite_note-\:1-4] In 1597, the English parliament empowered magistrates to deport "rogues and vagabonds 'beyond the seas' ", and in 1615 James I permitted the pardoning of felons on condition of banishment to the Americas. However, it was only after the passing of the [Transportation Act of 1718](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_Act_1717) that banishment to British colonies overseas, termed "[transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penal_transportation)", began to be applied systematically as a punishment for serious crimes. Hundreds of convicts were transported annually to the colonies and sold as servants. About 50,000 people were subjected to the punishment throughout the 18th century, including more than two-thirds of all felons convicted at London's main criminal court, [Old Bailey](229046.md). The practice of transportation in Britain continued well into the 19th century.[^cite_note-9] France also employed banishment to colonies as a punishment, but on a smaller scale than Britain; it was in use to a limited extent until the mid-20th century. The [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEYung2007111-10] and [Qing China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qing_China) used exile as a means to populate frontier territories.[^cite_note-11] Prison colonies became obsolete as the amount of habitable unsettled territory in the world decreased, and prisons became the normal method for dealing with convicts.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEYung2007111–112-12]

Banishment was frequently used as a punishment in the [Thirteen Colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies), but it fell into disfavor under the United States. There is no federal law in the United States controlling banishment as a punishment. In the case *Cooper v. Telfair* (1800), the US Supreme Court established that legislatures have the right to confiscate the property of and banish individuals who take up arms against the United States. At least one legal scholar has argued that this Supreme Court ruling "offered a definitive ruling on the legality of banishment." However, another scholar has argued that this reasoning cannot be easily used in the case of ordinary offenses, "where the security of the country is not at stake." Some US states allow intrastate banishment, although the practice is rare and its legality and constitutionality in many US jurisdictions has been described as "still an open question" by one scholar. Interstate banishment (i.e., expulsion from one US state to another) has been allowed in the United States only in isolated instances.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEYung2007113–116-13] The state of Georgia and Native American tribes are the two main users of banishment in the US, and often banish criminals from their tribal lands or judicial circuits.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18]

### International law and contemporary use

Main article: [Denaturalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denaturalization)

[Denaturalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denaturalization), or depriving a person of their citizenship, can be viewed as a modern form of banishment.[^cite_note-19] Denaturalization does not necessarily result in an individual losing the right of [legal residence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_residence) in the country that revokes their citizenship, but it often does.[^cite_note-20] Modern [international law](8195726.md) severely limits the circumstances under which a person can be deprived of their citizenship. Countries which permit the deprivation of citizenship after a criminal conviction for a serious offense include the Netherlands, Belgium, and France. Most states only allow the deprivation of citizenship if it will not cause the person to become [stateless](789786.md).[^cite_note-21]

### Exiled heads of state

Main article: [List of heads of state and government who have been in exile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_heads_of_state_and_government_who_have_been_in_exile)

In some cases the [deposed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_deposed_politicians) [head of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_of_state) is allowed to go into exile following a [coup](25914154.md) or other change of government, allowing a more peaceful transition to take place or to escape justice.[^cite_note-22]

### Avoiding tax or legal matters

Main articles: [Tax exile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_exile) and [Fugitive](406792.md)

A wealthy citizen who moves to a jurisdiction with lower taxes is termed a *tax exile*. Creative people such as authors and musicians who achieve sudden wealth sometimes choose this. Examples include the British-Canadian writer [Arthur Hailey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Hailey), who moved to the Bahamas to avoid taxes following the runaway success of his novels *Hotel* and *Airport*,[^cite_note-23] and the English rock band the [Rolling Stones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_Stones) who, in the spring of 1971, owed more in taxes than they could pay and left Britain before the government could seize their assets. Members of the band all moved to France for a period of time where they recorded music for the album that came to be called *Exile on Main Street*, the Main Street of the title referring to the French Riviera.[^cite_note-24] In 2012, [Eduardo Saverin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eduardo_Saverin), one of the founders of Facebook, made headlines by renouncing his U.S. citizenship before his company's [IPO](63879.md).[^cite_note-25] The dual Brazilian/U.S. citizen's decision to move to Singapore and renounce his citizenship spurred a bill in the U.S. Senate, the [Ex-PATRIOT Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ex-PATRIOT_Act), which would have forced such wealthy *tax exiles* to pay a special tax in order to re-enter the United States.[^cite_note-26]

In some cases a person voluntarily lives in exile to avoid legal issues, such as [litigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litigation) or [criminal prosecution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_prosecution). An example of this is [Asil Nadir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asil_Nadir), who fled to the [Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_Republic_of_Northern_Cyprus) for 17 years rather than face [prosecution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosecution) in connection with the failed £1.7 bn company [Polly Peck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polly_Peck) in the [United Kingdom](31717.md).

### Euphemism for convict

*Exile*, *government man* and *assigned servant* were all [euphemisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphemism) used in the 19th century for [convicts under sentence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convicts_in_Australia) who had been [transported](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penal_transportation) from Britain to [Australia](4689264.md).[^cite_note-29]

## For groups, nations, and governments

### Nation in exile

Main articles: [Diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diaspora) and [Refugee](45547.md)

When a large group, or occasionally a whole people or [nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nation) is exiled, it can be said that this nation is in exile, or "diaspora". Nations that have been in exile for substantial periods include the [Israelites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Israel_\(Samaria\)) by the Assyrian king [Sargon II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_II) in 720 BCE, the [Judeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Judah) who were deported by [Babylonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon) king [Nebuchadnezzar II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebuchadnezzar_II) in 586 BC, and the [Jews](25955086.md) following the destruction of the second [Temple in Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_in_Jerusalem) in AD 70. Jewish prayers include a yearning to return to [Jerusalem](16043.md) and the [Land of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_of_Israel),[^cite_note-30] such as "[Next Year in Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L'Shana_Haba'ah)".

After the [Partitions of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partitions_of_Poland) in the late 18th century, and following the uprisings (like [Kościuszko Uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko%C5%9Bciuszko_Uprising), [November Uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/November_Uprising) and [January Uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/January_Uprising)) against the partitioning powers ([Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire), [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Prussia) and [Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburg_monarchy)), many Poles have chosen – or been forced – to go into exile, forming large diasporas (known as [Polonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_diaspora)), especially [in France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poles_in_France) and [the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Americans).[^cite_note-31] The entire population of [Crimean Tatars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_Tatars) (numbering 200,000 in all) that remained in their homeland of [Crimea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_Peninsula) was exiled on 18 May 1944 to Central Asia as a form of [ethnic cleansing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_cleansing) and [collective punishment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_punishment) on false accusations.[^cite_note-changonrevison-32]

Since the [Cuban Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Revolution), over a million [Cubans](1288758.md) have left [Cuba](5042481.md). Most of these self-identified as exiles as their motivation for leaving the island is political in nature. At the time of the Cuban Revolution, Cuba only had a population of 6.5 million, and was not a country that had a history of significant emigration, it being the sixth largest recipient of immigrants in the world as of 1958. Most of the exiles' children also consider themselves to be [Cuban exiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_exile). Under Cuban law, children of Cubans born abroad are considered Cuban citizens.[^cite_note-NorthAmerica-33] An extension of colonial practices, Latin America saw widespread exile, of a political variety, during the 19th and 20th century.[^cite_note-34] Exiled political groups often develop complex media strategies, including diaspora engagement and investigative reporting, to maintain visibility, mobilise support, and address challenges of operating outside their home country.[^cite_note-35]

### Government in exile

Main article: [Government in exile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_in_exile)

During a foreign [occupation](816214.md) or after a [coup d'état](25914154.md), a *government in exile* of a such afflicted country may be established abroad. One of the most well-known instances of this is the [Polish government-in-exile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_government-in-exile), a government in exile that commanded [Polish armed forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_contribution_to_World_War_II) operating outside Poland, and the African-based [Free French Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_French_Forces) government of [Charles de Gaulle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_de_Gaulle) during the [German Occupation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German-occupied_Europe) of Poland and France in [WWII](32927.md). Other post-war examples include the client [All Palestine Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-Palestine_Government) established by the [Egyptian Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Egypt), and the [Central Tibetan Administration](54751.md), commonly known as the Tibetan government-in-exile, and headed by the [14th Dalai Lama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/14th_Dalai_Lama).

### For inanimate objects

[Ivan the Terrible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_the_Terrible) once exiled to Siberia an inanimate object: a bell.[^cite_note-36] "When the inhabitants of the town of [Uglich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uglich) rang their bell to rally a demonstration against Ivan the Terrible, the cruel Czar executed two hundred (nobles), and exiled the Uglich bell to Siberia, where it remained for two hundred years."[^cite_note-37]

## In popular culture

### Drama



*[Jason and Medea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medea#Jason_and_Medea)*, by [John William Waterhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_William_Waterhouse), 1907

Exile is an early motif in [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) [tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy). In the ancient Greek world, this was seen as a fate worse than death. The motif reaches its peak on the play *[Medea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medea_\(play\))*, written by [Euripides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euripides) in the fifth century BC, and rooted in the very old oral traditions of Greek mythology. Euripides' *Medea* has remained the most frequently performed Greek tragedy through the 20th century.[^cite_note-38]

### Art



Exiled [Klaus Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaus_Mann) as Staff Sergeant of the 5th US Army, Italy 1944



Cover of [Anna Seghers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Seghers)' *[Das siebte Kreuz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Seventh_Cross)*

After [Medea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medea) was abandoned by [Jason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jason) and had become a murderess out of revenge, she fled to Athens and married king [Aigeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aigeus) there, and became the stepmother of the hero [Theseus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theseus). Due to a conflict with him, she must leave the [Polis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polis) and go away into exile. [John William Waterhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_William_Waterhouse) (1849–1917), the English [Pre-Raphaelite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Raphaelite) painter's famous picture *[Jason and Medea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jason_and_Medea)* shows a key moment before, when Medea tries to poison Theseus.[^cite_note-39]

### Literature

In ancient Rome, the Roman Senate had the power to declare the exile to individuals, families or even entire regions. One of the Roman victims was the poet [Ovid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovid), who lived during the reign of [Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus). He was forced to leave Rome and move away to the city of Tomis on the Black Sea, now [Constanța](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constan%C8%9Ba). There he wrote his famous work *Tristia* (Sorrows) about his bitter feelings in exile.[^cite_note-40] Another, at least in a temporary exile, was [Dante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante).

The German-language writer [Franz Kafka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Kafka) described the exile of Karl Rossmann in the posthumously published novel *[Amerika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amerika_\(novel\))*.[^cite_note-41]

During the [period of National Socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Period_of_National_Socialism) in the first few years after 1933, many Jews, as well as a significant number of German artists and intellectuals fled into exile; for instance, the authors [Klaus Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaus_Mann) and [Anna Seghers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Seghers). So Germany's own exile literature emerged and received worldwide credit.[^cite_note-42] Klaus Mann finished his novel *Der Vulkan* (*The Volcano: A Novel Among Emigrants*) in 1939[^cite_note-43] describing the German exile scene, "to bring the rich, scattered and murky experience of exile into epic form",[^cite_note-44] as he wrote in his literary balance sheet. At the same place and in the same year, Anna Seghers published her famous novel *Das siebte Kreuz* (*[The Seventh Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Seventh_Cross)*, published in the United States in 1942).

Important exile literature in recent years include that of the Caribbean, many of whose artists emigrated to Europe or the United States for political or economic reasons. These writers include Nobel Prize winners [V. S. Naipaul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V._S._Naipaul) and [Derek Walcott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek_Walcott) as well as the novelists [Edwidge Danticat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwidge_Danticat) and [Sam Selvon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Selvon).[^cite_note-45]
