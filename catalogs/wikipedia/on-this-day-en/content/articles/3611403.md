# List of Category 5 Pacific hurricanes



Tracks of all Category 5 Pacific hurricanes northeast of the [International Date Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Date_Line) between 1959 and 2024.

A **Category 5 Pacific hurricane** is a [tropical cyclone](8282374.md) in the Pacific Ocean that reaches Category 5 intensity on the [Saffir–Simpson hurricane scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saffir%E2%80%93Simpson_hurricane_scale), the highest possible classification on the scale. Hurricanes of this intensity are infrequent in the northeastern Pacific Ocean; only 24 have formed since 1959, and they generally develop in clusters during the same year. Landfalls by such storms are rare due to the generally westward path of tropical cyclones in the Northern Hemisphere. The term "hurricane" is used for tropical cyclones in the Pacific Ocean, north of the equator and east of the [International Date Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Date_Line). A Category 5 [Pacific hurricane](3092184.md) is therefore a tropical cyclone in the north Pacific Ocean that reached Category 5 intensity east of the International Date Line. Identical phenomena in the north Pacific Ocean west of the dateline are called [typhoons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoon) or super typhoons. Category 5 super typhoons generally happen several times per season, so cyclones of that intensity are not exceptional for that region. This difference in terminology therefore excludes storms such as [Typhoon Paka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoon_Paka) and [Typhoon Oliwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoon_Oliwa_\(1997\)) of [1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Pacific_typhoon_season), and [Typhoon Genevieve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Genevieve_\(2014\)) of [2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Pacific_hurricane_season), which formed east of the dateline but did not reach Category 5 intensity until after crossing the dateline.

## Climatology and statistics

The majority of tropical cyclones form and organize in areas of warm [sea surface temperatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_surface_temperature), usually of at least 26.5 [°C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celsius) (79.7 [°F](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fahrenheit)) and low vertical [wind shear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_shear); however, there are outliers to this general rule, such as storms that manage to intensify despite high amounts of vertical wind shear. When a pre-existing tropical disturbance – usually a [tropical wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_wave) or a disturbance originating in the [Intertropical Convergence Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intertropical_Convergence_Zone) – enters an area where the aforementioned conditions are present, the disturbance can develop into a tropical cyclone, provided it is far enough from the equator to experience a sufficiently strong [Coriolis force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriolis_force), which causes the counterclockwise rotation of hurricanes in the Northern Hemisphere.[^cite_note-cyclogenesis-1]

Between the months of December and April, sea surface temperatures in the tropics, where most Northeast Pacific tropical cyclones develop, are usually too low to support significant development. Also, the presence of a [semi-permanent high-pressure area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ridge_\(meteorology\)) known as the [North Pacific High](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pacific_High) in the eastern Pacific greatly reduces tropical cyclone development in the winter months, as the North Pacific High results in vertical wind shear that causes environmental conditions to be unconducive to tropical cyclone formation. Another factor preventing tropical cyclones from forming during the winter is the presence of a semi-permanent [low-pressure area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-pressure_area) called the [Aleutian Low](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleutian_Low) between January and April. Its effects in the central Pacific near the [160th meridian west](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/160th_meridian_west) cause tropical waves that form in the area to move northward into the [Gulf of Alaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Alaska). As the disturbances travel northward, they dissipate or transition into an [extratropical cyclone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extratropical_cyclone). The Aleutian Low's retreat in late-April allows the warmth of the Pacific High to meander in, bringing its powerful clockwise wind circulation with it. During the month of May, the Intertropical Convergence Zone migrates southward while vertical shear over the tropics decreases. As a result, the earliest tropical waves begin to form,[^cite_note-longshore-2] coinciding with the start of the eastern Pacific hurricane season on May 15.[^cite_note-SeasonDates-3] During summer and early autumn, sea surface temperatures rise further, reaching 29 °C (84 °F) in July and August, well above the 26.5 °C (79.7 °F) threshold for the formation and intensification of tropical cyclones. This allows for tropical cyclones developing during that time to strengthen significantly, perhaps even [rapidly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapid_intensification).[^cite_note-longshore-2]

The [El Niño–Southern Oscillation](317311.md) also influences the frequency and intensity of hurricanes in the Northeast Pacific basin. During [El Niño](317311.md) events, sea surface temperatures increase in the Northeast Pacific and vertical wind shear decreases. Because of this, an increase in tropical cyclone activity occurs; the opposite happens in the [Atlantic basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_cyclone_basins#Northern_Atlantic_Ocean) during El Niño, where increased wind shear creates an unfavorable environment for tropical cyclone formation.[^cite_note-4] Contrary to El Niño, [La Niña](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Ni%C3%B1a) events increase wind shear and decrease sea surface temperatures over the eastern Pacific, while reducing wind shear and increasing sea surface temperatures over the Atlantic.[^cite_note-longshore-2]

A Category 5 hurricane is defined as having sustained windspeeds of at least 157 mph (253 km/h) over a one-minute period 10 m (32 ft 10 in) above the ground.[^cite_note-SSHS-5][^cite_note-SustainedWinds-6] As a tropical cyclone is moving, its wind field is asymmetric. In the northern hemisphere, the strongest winds are on the right side of the storm (relative to the direction of motion). The highest winds given in advisories are those from the right side.[^cite_note-Semicircles-7]

Since the [1959](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1959_Pacific_hurricane_season) season, only 24 hurricanes are known to have reached Category 5 intensity. There are no known Category 5 storms occurring before 1959. It is possible that some earlier storms reached Category 5 over open waters, but they were never recognized because they never affected land and remained at sea.[^cite_note-EPAC_HURDAT-8]

## List



A collage of all Category 5 Pacific hurricanes between 1994 and 2026



[Hurricane Patricia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Patricia) shortly after its record peak intensity on October 23, 2015.

This lists all of the Category 5 hurricanes in the order in which they formed according to the central and eastern Pacific [HURDAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HURDAT) database, which dates back to 1949. Before the advent of reliable geostationary [satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather_satellite) coverage in 1966, the number of eastern Pacific tropical cyclones was significantly underestimated.[^cite_note-SatelliteCoverage-9] It is therefore very possible that there are additional Category 5 hurricanes other than those listed, but they were not reported and therefore not recognized. However, the lack of Pacific Category 5 hurricanes during the late 1970s, 1980s, and early 1990s is considered certain.[^cite_note-EPAC_HURDAT-8]

The minimum [central pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_pressure) of these storms is, for the most part, estimated from satellite imagery, typically by using the [Dvorak technique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dvorak_technique) to estimate maximum sustained winds and then applying a [pressure–wind relationship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure-wind_relationship_calculations_for_tropical_cyclones). The reason for estimating the pressure (in lieu of direct measurements) is that most of these storms did not threaten land.[^cite_note-Recon-10] In the case of Kenna,[^cite_note-Kenna-11] Ava,[^cite_note-AvaRecon-12] Patricia, Lane, Lowell, and Polo, the central pressure was measured by [hurricane hunter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_hunter) aircraft flying into the storm; all of these but Ava were threatening land, and Ava was flown into by hurricane hunters to test equipment and conduct research.[^cite_note-AvaRecon-12] Because of the estimation of central pressures, it is possible that other storms more intense than those listed here have formed.[^cite_note-Guillermo-13]

Older storms have incomplete pressure records, since there were no satellite-based estimates; the only observations were taken by ships, land-based observations, or reconnaissance aircraft when available. Ava's minimum known pressure was measured when it was a Category 4 hurricane, for example.[^cite_note-EPAC_HURDAT-8] John and Gilma have incomplete pressures because the [Central Pacific Hurricane Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Pacific_Hurricane_Center), in general, did not publish pressure on systems in the central Pacific (140°W to the dateline) at the time.[^cite_note-Dalilia-14] This list is not identical to the list of most intense Pacific hurricanes; for example, 2014's [Hurricane Odile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Odile), the most intense known Category 4 Pacific hurricane that did not reach Category 5, had a lowest pressure of 918 mbar (27.1 inHg), lower than that of some Category 5 hurricanes, such as Guillermo.[^cite_note-EPAC_HURDAT-8]

Hurricanes have reached Category 5 intensity during each month from June to October. The earliest to do so was [1973's Hurricane Ava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Ava), on June 7. The latest to intensify to Category 5 was 2023's [Hurricane Otis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Otis) on October 25. Hurricanes Ava, [Gilma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Gilma_\(1994\)), [Ioke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Ioke), [Polo](84283796.md), and [Patricia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Patricia) are the most intense storms to form in their respective months. There have been no May, November, or off-season Category 5 hurricanes.[^cite_note-EPAC_HURDAT-8]

Four Pacific hurricanes are known to have reached Category 5 intensity multiple times: [Emilia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Emilia_\(1994\)), [Ioke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Ioke), [Lowell](84128118.md), and [Polo](84283796.md). Polo reached Category 5 intensity three times, while the others reached it twice. Ioke also reached Category 5 status a third time, but the third instance occurred as a typhoon while in the western Pacific.[^cite_note-EPAC_HURDAT-8] Polo holds the record for longest total duration as a Category 5 hurricane, with 65 hours and 20 minutes as a Category 5 hurricane. Hurricanes Ioke, John, and Linda are tied for the second-longest-lasting Category 5 hurricane recorded, spending 42 hours at that strength, but as Ioke's and Polo's time at Category 5 intensity were in multiple distinct time intervals,[^cite_note-Ioke-15] John and Linda had the longest time spent *consecutively* at that intensity.[^cite_note-EPAC_HURDAT-8]

## Landfalls



[Hurricane Otis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Otis), the only Pacific hurricane to make landfall at Category 5 intensity.

Of all of the Category 5 Pacific hurricanes, only six made [landfall](5016402.md), all of which occurred during the late-season month of October (except [Polo](84283796.md), which made both of its landfalls in late September), and all of which impacted Mexico. Only [Hurricane Otis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Otis) of [2023](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_in_Mexico) was a Category 5 hurricane at landfall. Originally, a [hurricane in 1959](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1959_Mexico_hurricane) was thought to have struck [Manzanillo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manzanillo,_Colima) at Category 5 intensity, but a reanalysis in 2016 determined that the storm had peaked as a Category 4 hurricane, and made landfall with the same sustained wind speed as Kenna.[^cite_note-21] Likewise, Patricia was originally determined to have made landfall as a Category 5 hurricane, but was downgraded to a Category 4 hurricane at landfall in post-season reanalysis.[^cite_note-Patricia-22] This list is not identical to the list of strongest landfalling Pacific hurricanes, as [1976's Hurricane Madeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Madeline_\(1976\)) did not reach Category 5 strength, but made landfall as a stronger Category 4 storm than Kenna.[^cite_note-Kenna-11] Polo was the only landfalling Category 5 Pacific hurricane to make landfall twice.

The reason for the lack of landfalls is that tropical cyclones in the northern hemisphere usually travel to the west[^cite_note-G8-23] due to easterly [trade winds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_winds). This means Pacific hurricanes generally move westward out into the open Pacific Ocean. Only rarely do tropical cyclones forming during the peak months of the season make landfall. Closer to the end of the season, the [subtropical ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtropical_ridge) steers some storms northwards or northeastwards. Storms influenced by this ridge may bring impacts to the western coasts of Mexico and occasionally even Central America. In the central Pacific basin, the North Pacific High keeps tropical cyclones away from the Hawaiian Islands by forcing them southwards.[^cite_note-longshore-2] Combined with cooler waters around the Hawaiian Islands that tend to weaken tropical cyclones that approach them, this makes direct impacts on the Hawaiian Islands by tropical cyclones rare.[^cite_note-24]

### Other land impacts

In addition to these six systems, hurricanes [John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_John_\(1994\)), [Linda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Linda_\(1997\)), [Ioke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Ioke), [Lane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Lane_\(2018\)), [Walaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Walaka), and [Lowell](84128118.md) all threatened land at some point during their existence. John, Ioke and Walaka had minimal impacts on [Johnston Atoll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnston_Atoll), John caused heavy surf in [Hawaii](13270.md), and Walaka passed close to [East Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Island_\(Hawaii\)) in the French Frigate Shoals.[^cite_note-EmiliaJohn-16] Linda was briefly forecast to [approach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_California_tropical_storms) [southern California](62520.md), and it passed close to [Socorro Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socorro_Island) near peak intensity.[^cite_note-Ioke-15][^cite_note-Linda-29][^cite_note-30] Lane threatened Hawai'i as a major hurricane and dropped more than 40 in (1,000 mm) of rain across many areas.[^cite_note-31] Out of the six aforementioned hurricanes, Lowell had the most significant impact on land, making a direct hit on [Kaua'i](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaua'i) and [Ni'ihau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ni'ihau), causing three deaths and over \$500 million of damage.&#x20;
