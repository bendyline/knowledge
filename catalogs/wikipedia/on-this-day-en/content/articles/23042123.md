**Air France Flight 447** was a scheduled international transatlantic passenger flight from [Rio de Janeiro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rio_de_Janeiro/Gale%C3%A3o_International_Airport), Brazil, to [Paris Charles de Gaulle Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_de_Gaulle_Airport), France, that crashed into the mid-[Atlantic Ocean](698.md) on 1 June 2009, killing all 228 passengers and crew on board. The incident, which occurred on an [Airbus A330](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A330), happened because inconsistent airspeed indications and resulting miscommunication between the pilots led to an unrecoverable [stall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stall_\(fluid_dynamics\)).[^cite_note-bea.aero-3] The [Brazilian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazilian_Navy) recovered the first major wreckage and two bodies from the sea within five days of the crash. The aircraft's [flight recorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_recorder) were not recovered from the [ocean floor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_floor) until May 2011, nearly two years after the accident.[^cite_note-4]

France's [Bureau of Enquiry and Analysis for Civil Aviation Safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Enquiry_and_Analysis_for_Civil_Aviation_Safety) final report, released at a [press conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Press_conference) on 5 July 2012, concluded that the aircraft suffered temporary inconsistencies between the [airspeed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airspeed) measurements—likely resulting from ice crystals obstructing the aircraft's [pitot tubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitot_tube)—which caused the [autopilot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autopilot) to disconnect. The crew reacted incorrectly to this, causing the aircraft to enter an aerodynamic stall which the pilots failed to correct.[^cite_note-bea.aero-3]<sup>: 79 </sup>[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_third2011-5]<sup>: 7 </sup>[^cite_note-NYToninterim3-6] The accident is the deadliest in the history of [Air France](14998012.md), as well as the deadliest aviation accident involving the Airbus A330.[^cite_note-ASN-7]

On 21 May 2026, [Airbus](26220236.md) and [Air France](14998012.md) were both found guilty of [corporate manslaughter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_manslaughter). Each company was sentenced to pay the maximum fine of €225,000 (\$261,112).

## Aircraft

The aircraft involved was a four-year-old [Airbus A330-203](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A330),[^cite_note-A330-203-2] with [serial number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_number) 0660, [registered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_registration) as F-GZCP. It was delivered to the airline in April 2005.[^cite_note-bea.aero-3][^cite_note-8] The aircraft was powered by two [General Electric CF6-80E1A3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric_CF6#Applications) engines with a maximum thrust of 304.8 or 268.7 kN (68,530 or 60,400 lbf) (take-off/max continuous),[^cite_note-9] with a cruising speed of Mach 0.82 to Mach 0.86 (470 to 500 kn; 880 to 920 km/h; 540 to 570 mph), at 35,000 feet (11,000 m) of altitude and a range of 12,500 km (6,700 nmi; 7,800 mi). The aircraft underwent a major overhaul on 16 April 2009, and at the time of the accident it had accumulated about 18,870 flying hours.[^cite_note-JACDEC-10] It was the last Airbus A330 delivered to the airline.

The aircraft departed [Rio de Janeiro–Galeão International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rio_de_Janeiro%E2%80%93Gale%C3%A3o_International_Airport) on 31 May 2009 at 19\:29 Brazilian Standard Time (22\:29 UTC),[^cite_note-bea.aero-3]<sup>: 21 </sup> with a scheduled arrival at [Paris-Charles de Gaulle Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris-Charles_de_Gaulle_Airport) of 11\:03 Central European Summer Time (09\:03 UTC) the following day (estimated flight time of 10\:34).[^cite_note-Summary-65] Voice contact with the aircraft was lost around 01\:35 UTC, 3 hours and 6 minutes after departure. The last message reported that the aircraft had passed [waypoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waypoint) INTOL ([1°21′39″S 32°49′53″W  /  1.36083°S 32.83139°W  / -1.36083; -32.83139](https://geohack.toolforge.org/geohack.php?pagename=Air_France_Flight_447\&params=1_21_39_S_32_49_53_W_type\:event)), located 565 km (351 mi; 305 nmi) from [Natal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natal,_Rio_Grande_do_Norte), on [Brazil's northeastern coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastline_of_Brazil).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first200913-66] The aircraft left Brazilian Atlantic radar surveillance at 01\:49 UTC,[^cite_note-bea.aero-3]<sup>: 49 </sup>[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_final201249-67] and entered a communication dead zone.[^cite_note-VF201410-44][^cite_note-bea.aero-3]

The Airbus A330 is designed to be flown by two pilots, but the 13-hour "duty time" (the total flight duration, as well as preflight and post flight duties) exceeded the 10 hours permitted before a pilot had to take a rest, as dictated by Air France's procedures. To comply with these procedures, Flight 447 was crewed by three pilots: a captain and two first officers.[^cite_note-68] With three pilots on board, each pilot could take a break in the A330's [crew rest compartment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_rest_compartment), located behind the cockpit.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first2009§1.17.2.2-69]

In accordance with common practice, Captain Dubois sent First Officer Robert for the first rest period with the intention of taking the second break himself.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_final2012§2.1.1.3.1-70] At 01\:55 UTC, he woke up First Officer Robert and said, "... he's going to take my place". After attending the briefing between the two co-pilots, the captain left the cockpit to rest at 02\:01\:46 UTC. At 02\:06 UTC, the pilot warned the cabin crew that they were about to enter an area of turbulence. About two to three minutes later, the aircraft encountered [icing conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icing_conditions). The [cockpit voice recorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cockpit_voice_recorder) (CVR) recorded sounds akin to hail or [graupel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graupel) on the outside of the aircraft and [ice crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_crystals) began to accumulate in the [pitot tubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitot_tube), which measure airspeed.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_third201173-71] Bonin, the pilot flying, turned the aircraft slightly to the left and decreased its speed from [Mach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mach_number) 0.82 to 0.80, which was the recommended speed to penetrate turbulence. The [engine anti-ice system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_protection_system) was also turned on.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmer20134,_39-72]



The aircraft's [vertical stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_stabilizer) after its recovery from the ocean

At 02\:10\:05 UTC, the autopilot disengaged, most likely because the pitot tubes had iced over, and the aircraft transitioned from "[normal law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_control_modes#Normal_law)" to "[alternate law 2 (ALT2)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternate_law)" due to the lack of reliable flight data.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmer20134–5-73] The engines' [autothrust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autothrottle) systems disengaged three seconds later. The autopilot disconnect warning was heard on the CVR.[^cite_note-Final-CVR-English2-74]

Bonin took manual control of the aircraft. Without the autopilot, turbulence caused the aircraft to start to roll to the right, and Bonin reacted by deflecting his [side-stick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Side-stick) to the left. One consequence of the change to ALT2 was the removal of both the dampers to control inputs, as well as maximum limitations to the A330's safe pitch, yaw and bank angles. Due to this increase in the aircraft's sensitivity to roll, the pilot overcorrected. During the next 30 seconds, the aircraft rolled alternately left and right as he adjusted to the altered handling characteristics of the aircraft.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmer201386-75] At the same time, he abruptly pulled back on his side-stick, raising the nose. This action was unnecessary and excessive under the circumstances.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_final2012§2.1.2.3-76] The aircraft's [stall warning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stall_warning) briefly sounded twice because the [angle of attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_attack) tolerance was exceeded, and the aircraft's indicated airspeed dropped sharply from 274 to 52 knots (507 to 96 km/h; 315 to 60 mph). The aircraft's angle of attack increased, and the aircraft subsequently began to climb above its cruising altitude of 35,000 ft (FL350). During this ascent, the aircraft attained vertical speeds well in excess of the typical rate of climb for the Airbus A330, which usually ascends at rates no greater than 2,000 feet per minute (10 m/s; 37 km/h). The aircraft experienced a peak vertical speed close to 7,000 feet per minute (36 m/s; 130 km/h),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmer201386-75] which occurred as Bonin brought the rolling movements under control.

At 02\:10\:34 UTC, after displaying incorrectly for half a minute, the left-side instruments recorded a sharp rise in airspeed to 223 knots (413 km/h; 257 mph), as did the [integrated standby instrument system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_standby_instrument_system) (ISIS) 33 seconds later.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_final_appx22012-77] The right-side instruments were not recorded by the flight data recorder. The icing event had lasted for just over a minute,[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-79][^cite_note-bea.aero-3]<sup>: 198 </sup>[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_final2012198-80] yet Bonin continued to make nose-up inputs. The trimmable [horizontal stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_stabilizer) (THS) moved from 3 to 13° nose-up in about one minute, and remained in the latter position until the end of the flight.

At 02\:11\:10 UTC, the aircraft had climbed to its maximum altitude around 38,000 feet (11,600 metres). At this point, the aircraft's angle of attack was 16°, and the engine thrust levers were in the fully forward [takeoff/go-around (TOGA) detent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takeoff/go-around_switch). As the aircraft began to descend, the angle of attack rapidly increased toward 30°. A second consequence of the reconfiguration into ALT2 was that the stall protection no longer operated, whereas in normal law, the aircraft's flight-management computers would have acted to prevent such a high angle of attack.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmer201378–80-81] The wings lost [lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lift_\(force\)) and the aircraft began to [stall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stall_\(flight\)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_third2011-5]

Confused, Bonin exclaimed, "I don't have control of the aircraft any more now", and two seconds later, "I don't have control of the aircraft at all!"[^cite_note-understandingaf447.com-43] Robert responded to this by saying, "controls to the left", and took over control of the aircraft.[^cite_note-Final-CVR-English-82][^cite_note-popularmechanics.com-83] He pushed his side-stick forward to lower the nose and recover from the stall; however, Bonin was still pulling his side-stick back. The inputs cancelled each other out and triggered an audible "dual input" warning.

At 02\:11\:40 UTC, Dubois re-entered the cockpit after being summoned by Robert.[^cite_note-popularmechanics.com-83] The angle of attack had then reached 40°, and the aircraft had descended to 35,000 feet (11,000 m) with the engines running at almost 100% [N<sub>1</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbreathing_jet_engine#Terminology) (the rotational speed of the front intake fan, which delivers most of a [turbofan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbofan) engine's thrust). The stall warnings stopped, as all airspeed indications were now considered invalid by the aircraft's computer because of the high angle of attack.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmer201357-84] The aircraft had its nose above the horizon, but was descending steeply.

Roughly 20 seconds later, at 02\:12 UTC, Bonin decreased the aircraft's [pitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_\(aviation\)) slightly. Airspeed indications became valid, and the stall warning sounded again; it then sounded intermittently for the remaining duration of the flight, stopping only when the pilots increased the aircraft's nose-up pitch. From there until the end of the flight, the angle of attack never dropped below 35°. From the time the aircraft stalled until its impact with the ocean, the engines were primarily developing either 100% N<sub>1</sub> or TOGA thrust, though they were briefly spooled down to about 50% N<sub>1</sub> on two occasions. The engines always responded to commands and were developing in excess of 100% N<sub>1</sub> when the flight ended. Robert responded to Dubois by saying, "We've lost all control of the aeroplane, we don't understand anything, we've tried everything".[^cite_note-popularmechanics.com-83] Soon after this, Robert said to himself, "climb" four consecutive times. Bonin heard this and replied, "But I've been at maximum nose-up for a while!" When Dubois heard this, he realized Bonin was causing the stall, and shouted, "No no no, don't climb! No No No!"[^cite_note-youtube.com-85][^cite_note-popularmechanics.com-83] When Robert heard this, he told Bonin to give him control of the aircraft,[^cite_note-bea.aero-3] and Bonin initially obliged.[^cite_note-popularmechanics.com-83][^cite_note-youtube.com-85][^cite_note-bea.aero-3]

The aircraft was now too low to recover from the stall. Robert pushed his side-stick forward to try to regain lift in order to get out of the stall; however, shortly thereafter, the [ground proximity warning system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_proximity_warning_system) sounded a "sink rate" alarm followed by "pull up" alarms.[^cite_note-bea.aero-3] In response, Bonin (without informing his colleagues) pulled his side-stick all the way back again,[^cite_note-popularmechanics.com-83][^cite_note-bea.aero-3] and said, "We're going to crash! This can't be true. But what's happening?"[^cite_note-youtube.com-85][^cite_note-popularmechanics.com-83][^cite_note-bea.aero-3][^cite_note-books.google.ca-86][^cite_note-understandingaf447.com-43] The last recording on the CVR was Dubois saying, "(ten)[^cite_note-87] degrees pitch attitude."[^cite_note-Final-CVR-English2-74]

Both flight recorders stopped recording at 02\:14\:28 UTC, 3 hours and 45 minutes after takeoff. At that point, the aircraft's ground speed was recorded as 107 knots (198 km/h; 123 mph), and the aircraft was descending at 10,912 feet per minute (55.43 m/s), a vertical speed of 108 knots (200 km/h; 124 mph). Its pitch was 16.2° nose-up, with a roll angle of 5.3° to the left. During its descent, the aircraft had turned more than 180° to the right to a compass heading of 270°. The aircraft remained stalled during its entire 3-minute-30-second descent from 38,000 feet (12,000 m).[^cite_note-bea2011may27-88] The aircraft struck the ocean belly-first at a speed of 152 knots (282 km/h; 175 mph), comprising vertical and horizontal components of 108 knots (200 km/h; 124 mph) and 107 knots (198 km/h; 123 mph), respectively. All 228 passengers and crew on board died on impact from extreme trauma and the aircraft was destroyed.[^cite_note-spiegel_2011_05_23-89][^cite_note-bea.aero-3][^cite_note-books.google.ca-86]

### Automated messages

Air France's A330s are equipped with a communications system, [Aircraft Communications Addressing and Reporting System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACARS) (ACARS), which enables them to transmit data messages via VHF or satellite.[^cite_note-90] ACARS can be used by the aircraft's on-board computers to send messages automatically, and F-GZCP transmitted a position report about every 10 minutes. Its final position report at 02\:10\:34 gave the aircraft's coordinates as [2°59′N 30°35′W  /  2.98°N 30.59°W  / 2.98; -30.59](https://geohack.toolforge.org/geohack.php?pagename=Air_France_Flight_447\&params=2.98_N_30.59_W_type\:event).[^cite_note-91]

In addition to the routine position reports, F-GZCP's centralized maintenance system sent a series of messages via ACARS in the minutes immediately prior to its disappearance.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first200948–57-92][^cite_note-AvWK-93][^cite_note-AvWK2-94] These messages, sent to prepare maintenance workers on the ground prior to arrival, were transmitted between 02\:10 UTC and 02\:15 UTC,[^cite_note-France2_news-95] and consisted of 5 failure reports and 19 warnings.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first200949-96][^cite_note-97][^cite_note-A330_ms-98][^cite_note-JASC_codes-99]



[Recife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recife), 8 June 2009; Brazilian Navy Commander Giucemar Tabosa Cardoso shows the map with the location of the remains of the Airbus A330-203.

Among the ACARS transmissions at 02\:10 is one message that indicates a fault in the [pitot-static system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitot-static_system).[^cite_note-France2_news-95][^cite_note-JASC_codes-99] Bruno Sinatti, president of Alter, Air France's third-biggest pilots' union, stated, "Piloting becomes very difficult, near impossible, without reliable speed data."[^cite_note-Alter-100] The 12 warning messages with the same time code indicate that the autopilot and autothrust system had disengaged, that the [traffic collision avoidance system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_collision_avoidance_system) was in fault mode, and that the flight mode went from ["normal law"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_control_modes#Normal_law) to ["alternate law (ALT)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_control_modes#Alternate_law).[^cite_note-A330_fc-101][^cite_note-Crash:_Air_France_A332_over_Atlantic_on_1_June_2009,_aircraft_impacted_ocean-102]

The remainder of the messages occurred from 02\:11 UTC to 02\:14 UTC, containing a fault message for an [air data inertial reference unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_data_inertial_reference_unit) and ISIS.[^cite_note-Crash:_Air_France_A332_over_Atlantic_on_1_June_2009,_aircraft_impacted_ocean-102][^cite_note-A320_isis-103] At 02\:12 UTC, a warning message *NAV ADR DISAGREE* indicated that a disagreement existed between the three independent [air data systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_data_inertial_reference_unit#Air_data_reference).[^cite_note-104] At 02\:13 UTC, a fault message for the flight management guidance and envelope computer was sent.[^cite_note-A330_comm-105] One of the two final messages transmitted at 02\:14 UTC was a warning referring to the air data reference system, the other *ADVISORY* was a "cabin vertical speed warning", indicating that the aircraft was descending at a high rate.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first200948–57-92][^cite_note-AvHer090602-106][^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108]

### Weather conditions

Weather conditions in the mid-Atlantic were normal for the time of year, and included a broad band of thunderstorms along the [Intertropical Convergence Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intertropical_Convergence_Zone) (ITCZ).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_final2012§1.7.1_p._46-111] A meteorological analysis of the area surrounding the flight path showed a [mesoscale convective system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoscale_convective_system) extending to an altitude of around 50,000 feet (15,000 m) above the Atlantic Ocean before Flight 447 disappeared.[^cite_note-tim_af447-112][^cite_note-cimss_wisc_2601-113][^cite_note-BBC_Meteosat-9-114][^cite_note-115] During its final hour, Flight 447 encountered areas of light turbulence.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_final2012§1.11.2_p._58-116]

Commercial air transport crews routinely encounter this type of storm in this area.[^cite_note-117] With the aircraft under the control of its automated systems, one of the main tasks occupying the cockpit crew was that of monitoring the progress of the flight through the ITCZ, using the on-board weather radar to avoid areas of significant turbulence.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_final2012§2.1.1.1_p._167-118] Twelve other flights had recently shared more or less the same route that Flight 447 was using at the time of the accident.[^cite_note-119][^cite_note-120]

## Search and recovery



Brigadier Ramon Borges Cardoso speaks to the media about the search for the crashed aircraft.

### Surface search

Flight 447 was due to pass from Brazilian airspace into [Senegalese](27278.md) airspace around 02\:20 (UTC) on 1 June, and then into [Cape Verdean](18962637.md) airspace at roughly 03\:45. Shortly after 04\:00, when the flight had failed to contact air traffic control in either Senegal or Cape Verde, the controller in Senegal attempted to contact the aircraft. When he received no response, he asked the crew of another Air France flight (AF459) to try to contact AF447; this was also met with no success.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first2009§1.9.2_"Coordination_between_the_control_centres"-121]

After further attempts to contact Flight 447 were unsuccessful, an aerial search for the missing Airbus commenced from both sides of the Atlantic. [Brazilian Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazilian_Air_Force) aircraft from the [archipelago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archipelago) of [Fernando de Noronha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernando_de_Noronha) and French reconnaissance aircraft based in [Dakar](44251.md), Senegal, led the search.[^cite_note-BBC1-41] They were assisted by a [Casa 235](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CASA/IPTN_CN-235) maritime patrol aircraft from Spain[^cite_note-122] and a [United States Navy](20518076.md) Lockheed Martin [P-3 Orion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-3_Orion) anti-submarine warfare and maritime patrol aircraft.[^cite_note-123][^cite_note-124]

By early afternoon on 1 June, officials with Air France and the [French government](3398091.md) had already presumed the aircraft had been lost with no survivors. An Air France spokesperson told *[L'Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L'Express_\(France\))* that "no hope for survivors" remained,[^cite_note-125][^cite_note-126] and [French president](24899.md) [Nicolas Sarkozy](557007.md) announced almost no chance existed for anyone to have survived.[^cite_note-WRAL-127] On 2 June at 15\:20 (UTC), a Brazilian Air Force [Embraer R-99](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embraer_R-99)A spotted wreckage and signs of oil, possibly [jet fuel](1205435.md), strewn along a 5 km (3 mi; 3 nmi) band 650 km (400 mi; 350 nmi) north-east of Fernando de Noronha Island, near the [Saint Peter and Saint Paul Archipelago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Peter_and_Saint_Paul_Archipelago). The sighted wreckage included an aircraft seat, an orange buoy, a barrel, and "white pieces and electrical conductors".[^cite_note-fab_3103-128][^cite_note-NYT0602-129] Later that day, after meeting with relatives of the Brazilians on the aircraft, [Brazilian defence minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Defence_\(Brazil\)) [Nelson Jobim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nelson_Jobim) announced that the Air Force believed the wreckage was from Flight 447.[^cite_note-CNNconfirm-130][^cite_note-BBCConfirm-131] Brazilian vice-president [José Alencar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Alencar) (acting as president since [Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva](99328.md) was out of the country) declared three days of [official mourning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_mourning).[^cite_note-BBCConfirm-131][^cite_note-132]



Lt. Col. Henry Munhoz describes the recovery of Airbus A330 wreckage from the ocean.

Also on 2 June, two [French Navy](383256.md) vessels, the frigate [*Ventôse*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_frigate_Vent%C3%B4se) and helicopter-carrier [*Mistral*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mistral_class_amphibious_assault_ship), were *en route* to the suspected crash site. Other ships sent to the site included the French research vessel [*Pourquoi Pas?*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pourquoi_Pas%3F_\(2005\)), equipped with two minisubmarines able to descend to 6,000 m (20,000 ft),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first2009§1.16.1.3_"Resources_deployed_by_France"-133][^cite_note-134] since the area of the Atlantic in which the aircraft went down was thought to be as deep as 4,700 m (15,400 ft).[^cite_note-135][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first2009§1.16.1.1_"Context_of_the_searches"-136]

On 3 June, the first Brazilian Navy (the "Marinha do Brasil" or MB) ship, the [patrol boat](536773.md) [*Grajaú*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graja%C3%BA-class_patrol_boat), reached the area in which the first debris was spotted. The Brazilian Navy sent a total of five ships to the debris site; the [frigate](11464.md) [*Constituição*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazilian_frigate_Constitui%C3%A7%C3%A3o_\(F42\)) and the [corvette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corvette) [*Caboclo*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazilian_corvette_Caboclo_\(V19\)) were scheduled to reach the area on 4 June, the frigate [*Bosísio*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F_Bos%C3%ADsio_\(F48\)) on 6 June and the [replenishment oiler](1580755.md) [*Almirante Gastão Motta*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazilian_tanker_Almirante_Gast%C3%A3o_Motta_\(G23\)) on 7 June.[^cite_note-137][^cite_note-138]

Early on 6 June 2009, five days after Flight 447 disappeared, two male bodies, the first to be recovered from the crashed aircraft, were brought on board the *Caboclo*[^cite_note-139] along with a seat, a nylon backpack containing a computer and vaccination card, and a leather briefcase containing a boarding pass for the Air France flight. Initially, media (including *[The Boston Globe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Boston_Globe)*, the *[Los Angeles Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles_Times)*, and the *[Chicago Tribune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Tribune)*) cited unnamed investigators in their reporting that the recovered bodies were naked, which implied the plane had broken up at high altitude.[^cite_note-140] However, the notion that the aircraft fragmented while airborne ultimately was refuted by investigators.[^cite_note-141] At this point, on the evidence of the recovered bodies and materials, investigators confirmed the plane had crashed, killing everyone on board.[^cite_note-bodies_found-142][^cite_note-143] The following day, 7 June, search crews recovered the Airbus's [vertical stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_stabilizer), the first major piece of wreckage to be discovered. Pictures of this part being lifted onto the *Constituição* became a poignant symbol of the loss of the Air France craft.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first2009-1][^cite_note-BBCtail-144]



The bodies found in the ocean were transferred to the morgue in Brazil for autopsy and identification.



The vertical stabilizer being recovered

The search and recovery effort reached its peak over the next week or so, as the number of personnel mobilized by the Brazilian military exceeded 1100.[^cite_note-145][^cite_note-nota39-146] Fifteen aircraft (including two helicopters) were devoted to the search mission.[^cite_note-nota37-147] The Brazilian Air Force Embraer R99 flew for more than 100 hours, and electronically scanned more than one million square kilometres (390,000 square miles; 290,000 square nautical miles) of ocean.[^cite_note-nota31-148] Other aircraft involved in the search scanned, visually, 320,000 square kilometres (120,000 sq mi; 93,000 sq nmi) of ocean and were used to direct Navy vessels involved in the recovery effort.[^cite_note-nota39-146]



Seat map showing locations of the recovered bodies during the 2009 search operations.

By 16 June 2009, 50 bodies had been recovered from a wide area of the ocean.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first2009§1.12.1_"Localisation_of_the_bodies_and_aircraft_parts"-149][^cite_note-150][^cite_note-BRMOD_Nota33-151] They were transported to shore, first by the frigates *Constituição* and *Bosísio* to Fernando de Noronha, and thereafter by air to [Recife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recife) for identification.[^cite_note-BRMOD_Nota33-151][^cite_note-BRMOD_Nota31-152][^cite_note-153][^cite_note-BRMOD_Nota27-154] Pathologists identified all 50 bodies recovered from the crash site, including that of the captain, by using dental records and fingerprints.[^cite_note-155][^cite_note-156][^cite_note-157] The search teams logged the time and location of every find in a database which, by the time the search ended on 26 June, catalogued 640 items of debris from the aircraft.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first2009§1.12.1_"Localisation_of_the_bodies_and_aircraft_parts"-149]

The BEA documented the timeline of discoveries in its first interim report.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first2009Appx_A_pp._101–107_"Chronology_of_recovery_of_bodies_and_airplane_parts"-158][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_second2009§1.13-159][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first2009§1.12.1_p._37-160]

### Underwater search

On 5 June 2009, the French [nuclear submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_submarine) *[Émeraude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_submarine_%C3%89meraude_\(S604\))* was dispatched to the crash zone, arriving in the area on the 10th. Its mission was to assist in the search for the missing [flight recorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_recorder) or "black boxes" that might be located at great depth.[^cite_note-cbc_nuclear-161] The submarine would use its [sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_sonar) to listen for the ultrasonic signal emitted by the black boxes' "[pingers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underwater_locator_beacon)",[^cite_note-162] covering 34 square kilometres (13 sq mi; 9.8 sq nmi) per day. The *Émeraude* was to work with the [mini-sub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midget_submarine) *[Nautile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nautile)*, which can descend to the [ocean floor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seabed). The French submarines would be aided by two U.S. [underwater audio devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underwater_acoustic_communication) capable of picking up signals at a depth of 6,000 metres (20,000 ft).[^cite_note-163]



Colour [bathymetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathymetry) relief map of the part of Atlantic Ocean into which Air France Flight 447 crashed. Image shows two different data sets with different resolution.

[^cite_note-164]

Following the end of the search for bodies, the search continued for the Airbus's "black boxes"—the [Cockpit Voice Recorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_recorder#Cockpit_voice_recorder) (CVR) and the [Flight Data Recorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_recorder#Flight_data_recorder) (FDR). French [Bureau of Enquiry and Analysis for Civil Aviation Safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Enquiry_and_Analysis_for_Civil_Aviation_Safety) (BEA) chief [Paul-Louis Arslanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul-Louis_Arslanian) said that he was not optimistic about finding them since they might have been under as much as 3,000 metres (10,000 ft) of water, and the terrain under this portion of the ocean was very rugged.[^cite_note-BBC3-165] Investigators were hoping to find the aircraft's lower aft section, for that was where the recorders were located.[^cite_note-166] Although France had never recovered a flight recorder from such depths,[^cite_note-BBC3-165] there was precedent for such an operation: in 1988, an independent contractor recovered the CVR of [South African Airways Flight 295](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_African_Airways_Flight_295) from a depth of 4,900 metres (16,100 ft) in a search area of between 270 and 860 square kilometres (110 and 330 mi<sup>2</sup>; 80 and 250 sq nmi).[^cite_note-167][^cite_note-168] The Air France flight recorders were fitted with water-activated acoustic underwater locator beacons or "pingers", which should have remained active for at least 30 days, giving searchers that much time to locate the origin of the signals.[^cite_note-169]

France requested two "towed pinger locator hydrophones" from the United States Navy to help find the aircraft.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first2009§1.16.1.3_"Resources_deployed_by_France"-133] The French nuclear submarine and two French-contracted ships (the *Fairmount Expedition* and the *Fairmount Glacier*, towing the [U.S. Navy](20518076.md) listening devices) trawled a search area with a radius of 80 kilometres (50 mi), centred on the aircraft's last known position.[^cite_note-170][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first200946-171] By mid-July, recovery of the black boxes still had not been announced. The finite beacon battery life meant that, as the time since the crash elapsed, the likelihood of location diminished.[^cite_note-France24230609-172] In late July, the search for the black boxes entered its second phase, with a French research vessel resuming the search using a towed sonar array.[^cite_note-csmonitor-173] The second phase of the search ended on 20 August without finding wreckage within a 75 kilometres (47 mi; 40 nmi) radius of the last position, as reported at 02\:10.[^cite_note-174]



East-west cross-section of Atlantic Ocean portion in which Air France Flight 447 crashed, showing depth of the sea floor. The vertical scale is exaggerated by a factor of 100 relative to the horizontal.

The third phase of the search for the recorders lasted from 2 April until 24 May 2010,[^cite_note-BEA_SeaSearchPhase3-175][^cite_note-airfrance_search3-176][^cite_note-177] and was conducted by two ships, the *Anne Candies* and the *Seabed Worker*. The *Anne Candies* towed a U.S. Navy sonar array, while the *Seabed Worker* operated three [AUV ABYSS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AUV_ABYSS) (a [REMUS AUV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/REMUS_\(AUV\)) type) [robot submarines](1885043.md).[^cite_note-BEA_SeaSearchPhase3-175][^cite_note-178][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_final2012§1.16.1.1-179][^cite_note-180] Air France and Airbus jointly funded the third phase of the search.[^cite_note-third_1-181][^cite_note-182] The search covered an area of 6,300 square kilometres (2,400 sq mi; 1,800 sq nmi), mostly to the north and north-west of the aircraft's last known position.[^cite_note-BEA_SeaSearchPhase3-175][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_final2012§1.16.1.1-179][^cite_note-third_2-183] The search area had been drawn up by oceanographers from France, Russia, Great Britain and the United States combining data on the location of floating bodies and wreckage, and currents in the mid-Atlantic in the days immediately after the crash.[^cite_note-third_3-184][^cite_note-185][^cite_note-third_4-186] A smaller area to the south-west was also searched, based on a re-analysis of sonar recordings made by *Émeraude* the previous year.[^cite_note-187][^cite_note-Suggestion1-188][^cite_note-189] The third phase of the search ended on 24 May 2010 without any success, though the BEA says that the search 'nearly' covered the whole area drawn up by investigators.[^cite_note-BEA_SeaSearchPhase3-175]

### 2011 search and recovery

In July 2010, the U.S.-based search consultancy Metron, Inc., had been engaged to draw up a probability map of where to focus the search, based on prior probabilities from flight data and local condition reports, combined with the results from the previous searches. The Metron team used what it described as "classic" [Bayesian search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_search_theory) methods, an approach that had previously been successful in the search for the submarine [USS *Scorpion*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Scorpion_\(SSN-589\)) and [SS *Central America*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Central_America). Phase 4 of the search operation started close to the aircraft's last known position, which was identified by the Metron study as being the most likely resting place of Flight 447.[^cite_note-stone-190][^cite_note-191]



[Cable ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_layer) [*Île de Sein*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_%C3%8Ele_de_Sein?action=edit\&redlink=1) was assigned to assist in the recovery of materials from the ocean floor.

Within a week of resuming the search operation, on 3 April 2011, a team led by the [Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woods_Hole_Oceanographic_Institution) operating full ocean depth [autonomous underwater vehicles](1885043.md) (AUVs) owned by the Waitt Institute[^cite_note-192] discovered, by means of [sidescan sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidescan_sonar), a large portion of the debris field from flight AF447.[^cite_note-stone-190] Further debris and bodies, still trapped in the partly intact remains of the aircraft's [fuselage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuselage), were at a depth of 3,980 metres (2,180 fathoms; 13,060 ft).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_third2011§1.12.1-193] The debris was found lying in a relatively flat and silty area of the ocean floor (as opposed to the extremely mountainous topography originally believed to be AF447's final resting place).[^cite_note-194] Other items found were engines, wing parts and the [landing gear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_gear).[^cite_note-195]

The debris field was described as "quite compact", measuring 200 by 600 metres (700 by 2,000 ft) and a short distance north of where pieces of wreckage had been recovered previously, suggesting the aircraft hit the water largely intact.[^cite_note-Willsher-196] The French Ecology and Transportation Minister [Nathalie Kosciusko-Morizet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathalie_Kosciusko-Morizet) stated the bodies and wreckage would be brought to the surface and taken to France for examination and identification.[^cite_note-197] The French government chartered the *Île de Sein* to recover the flight recorders from the wreckage.[^cite_note-198][^cite_note-CBS20050223-199] An American Remora 6000 [remotely operated vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remotely_operated_vehicle) (ROV)[^cite_note-200] and operations crew from [Phoenix International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_International_Holdings) experienced in the recovery of aircraft for the [United States Navy](20518076.md) were on board the *Île de Sein*.[^cite_note-Remora-201][^cite_note-202]

*Île de Sein* arrived at the crash site on 26 April, and during its first dive, the Remora 6000 found the flight data recorder chassis, although without the crash-survivable memory unit.[^cite_note-203][^cite_note-204] On 1 May the memory unit was found and lifted on board the *Île de Sein* by the ROV.[^cite_note-205] The aircraft's cockpit voice recorder was found on 2 May 2011, and was raised and brought on board the *Île de Sein* the following day.[^cite_note-206]

On 7 May, the flight recorders, under judicial seal, were taken aboard the French Navy patrol boat *La Capricieuse* for transfer to the port of [Cayenne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cayenne). From there they were transported by air to the BEA's office in [Le Bourget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Bourget) near Paris for data download and analysis. One engine and the [avionics bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avionics_bay), containing onboard computers, had also been raised.[^cite_note-207]

By 15 May, all the data from both the flight data recorder and the cockpit voice recorder had been downloaded.[^cite_note-bea.aero-3]<sup>: 20 </sup>[^cite_note-Data_downloaded-208] The data was analysed over the following weeks, and the findings published in the third interim report at the end of July.[^cite_note-BEA-16May-209] The entire download was filmed and recorded.[^cite_note-BEA-16May-209]

Between 5 May and 3 June 2011, 104 bodies were recovered from the wreckage, bringing the total number of bodies found to 154. Fifty bodies had been previously recovered from the sea.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_second2009§1.13-159][^cite_note-hylton_nytmag-210][^cite_note-AFP_bodies_recovery_started-211][^cite_note-Bloomberg_bodies_recovery_started-212] The search ended with the remaining 74 bodies still not recovered.[^cite_note-213]

## Investigation and safety improvements

The French authorities opened two investigations. A criminal investigation for manslaughter began on 5 June 2009, under the supervision of Investigating Magistrate Sylvie Zimmerman from the Paris High Court ([French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language): *Tribunal de Grande Instance*).[^cite_note-214] The judge gave the investigation to the [National Gendarmerie](6330819.md) ([French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language): *Gendarmerie nationale*), which would conduct it through its aerial transportation division (Air transport police, [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language): *Gendarmerie des transports aériens* or GTA) and its forensic research institute (the [National Gendarmerie Institute for Criminal Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institut_de_recherche_criminelle_de_la_gendarmerie_nationale), [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language): *Institut de recherche criminelle de la gendarmerie nationale*).[^cite_note-215] As part of the criminal investigation, the [DGSE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DGSE) (the external French intelligence agency) examined the names of passengers on board for any possible links to terrorist groups.[^cite_note-216] In March 2011, a French judge filed preliminary manslaughter charges against Air France and Airbus over the crash.[^cite_note-217] The cases against Air France and Airbus were dropped in 2019 and 2021 respectively.[^cite_note-\:0-218][^cite_note-\:1-219][^cite_note-\:2-220]

A technical investigation was started, the goal of which was to enhance the safety of future flights. In accordance with the provisions of ICAO Annex 13, the BEA participated in the investigation as representative for the state (country) of manufacture of the Airbus.[^cite_note-221] The Brazilian Air Force's [Aeronautical Accidents Investigation and Prevention Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeronautical_Accidents_Investigation_and_Prevention_Center) (CENIPA), the [German Federal Bureau of Aircraft Accident Investigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Federal_Bureau_of_Aircraft_Accident_Investigation) (BFU), the UK's [Air Accidents Investigation Branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Accidents_Investigation_Branch) (AAIB), and the U.S. [National Transportation Safety Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Transportation_Safety_Board) (NTSB) also became involved in accordance with these provisions; the NTSB became involved as the representative of the state of manufacture of the General Electric turbine engines installed on the aircraft, and the other representatives could supply important information. The People's Republic of China, Croatia, Hungary, Republic of Ireland, Italy, Lebanon, Morocco, Norway, South Korea, Russia, South Africa, and Switzerland appointed observers, since citizens of those countries were on board.[^cite_note-222]

On 5 June 2009, the BEA cautioned against premature speculation as to the cause of the crash. At that time, the investigation had established only two facts—the weather near the aircraft's planned route included significant convective cells typical of the equatorial regions, and the speeds measured by the three pitot tubes differed from each other during the last few minutes of the flight.[^cite_note-BEA090605-223]

On 2 July 2009, the BEA released an intermediate report, which described all known facts, and a summary of the visual examination of the rudder and the other parts of the aircraft that had been recovered at that time.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first200937–40-224] According to the BEA, this examination showed:

- The airliner was likely to have struck the surface of the sea in a normal flight attitude, with a high rate of descent;

  [^cite_note-225]

  [^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first200938–40-226]

  [^cite_note-227]

- No signs of any fires or explosions were found.

- The airliner did not break up in flight.

  [^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first200971-228]

  &#x20;The report also stresses that the BEA had not had access to the *post mortem* reports at the time of its writing.

  [^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first200940-229]

  [^cite_note-www\.bea.aero_vol-230]

On 16 May 2011, *[Le Figaro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Figaro)* reported that the BEA investigators had ruled out an aircraft malfunction as the cause of the crash, according to preliminary information extracted from the FDR.[^cite_note-FigaroFirstBlackBoxInfo-231] The following day, the BEA issued a press release explicitly describing the *Le Figaro* report as a "sensationalist publication of non-validated information". The BEA stated that no conclusions had been made, investigations were continuing, and no interim report was expected before the summer.[^cite_note-232] On 18 May, the head of the investigation further stated no major malfunction of the aircraft had been found so far in the data from the flight data recorder, but that minor malfunctions had not been ruled out.[^cite_note-AlainBouillard-233]

### Airspeed inconsistency

In the minutes before its disappearance, the aircraft's onboard systems sent a number of messages via the ACARS indicating disagreement in the [indicated airspeed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indicated_airspeed) readings. A spokesperson for the BEA stated, "the airspeed of the aircraft was unclear" to the pilots[^cite_note-cbc_nuclear-161] and, on 4 June 2009, Airbus issued an Accident Information [Telex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telex) to operators of all its aircraft reminding pilots of the recommended abnormal and emergency procedures to be taken in the case of unreliable airspeed indication.[^cite_note-234] French transport minister [Dominique Bussereau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominique_Bussereau) said, "Obviously, the pilots \[of Flight 447] did not have the \[correct] speed showing, which can lead to two bad consequences for the life of the aircraft: under-speed, which can lead to a [stall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stall_\(flight\)), and over-speed, which can lead to the aircraft breaking up because it is approaching the [speed of sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_sound) and the structure of the plane is not made for resisting such speeds".[^cite_note-timesonline-235]

#### Pitot tubes

Between May 2008 and March 2009, nine incidents involving the temporary loss of airspeed indication appeared in the [air safety reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_Safety_Reporting_System) (ASRs) for Air France's A330/A340 fleet. All occurred in cruise between [flight levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_level) FL310 and FL380. Further, after the Flight 447 accident, Air France identified six additional incidents that had not been reported on ASRs. These were intended for maintenance aircraft technical logs drawn up by the pilots to describe these incidents only partially, to indicate the characteristic symptoms of the incidents associated with unreliable airspeed readings.[^cite_note-bea.aero-3]<sup>: 122 </sup>[^cite_note-BBC8086860-236] The problems primarily occurred in 2007 on the A320, but awaiting a recommendation from Airbus, Air France delayed installing new pitot tubes on A330/A340 and increased inspection frequencies in these aircraft.[^cite_note-NC_06-06-237][^cite_note-sensors-238]

When it was introduced in 1994, the Airbus A330 was equipped with pitot tubes, part number 0851GR, manufactured by [Goodrich Sensors and Integrated Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodrich_Corporation). A 2001 [Airworthiness Directive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airworthiness_Directive) (AD) required these to be replaced with either a later Goodrich design, part number 0851HL, or with pitot tubes made by [Thales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales_Group), part number C16195AA.[^cite_note-239] Air France chose to equip its fleet with the Thales pitot tubes. In September 2007, Airbus recommended that Thales C16195AA pitot tubes should be replaced by Thales model C16195BA to address the problem of water ingress that had been observed.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_second2009§1.18.7_pp._65–67-240] Since it was not an AD, the guidelines allowed the operator to apply the recommendations at its discretion. Air France implemented the change on its A320 fleet, on which the incidents of water ingress were observed, and decided to do so in its A330/340 fleet only when failures started to occur in May 2008.[^cite_note-241][^cite_note-AF_PR_12-242]

After discussing these issues with the manufacturer, Air France sought a means of reducing these incidents, and Airbus indicated that the new pitot probe designed for the A320 was not designed to prevent cruise-level ice-over. In 2009, tests suggested that the new probe could improve its reliability, prompting Air France to accelerate the replacement program,[^cite_note-AF_PR_12-242] which started on 29 May. F-GZCP was scheduled to have its pitot tubes replaced as soon as it returned to Paris.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmer201353-243] By 17 June 2009, Air France had replaced all pitot probes on its A330 type aircraft.[^cite_note-AF_corporate-244]

In July 2009, Airbus issued new advice to A330 and A340 operators to exchange Thales pitot tubes for tubes from Goodrich.[^cite_note-245][^cite_note-WPost_on_Airbus,_EASA,_Thales,_Goodrich-246][^cite_note-247] This was followed on 12 August by three mandatory service bulletins applying to all A330 and A340 aircraft. These required that two of the three tubes be Goodrich 0851HL with the third being either the same or Thales C16195BA. Thales C16195AA tubes were no longer to be used.[^cite_note-EASA_2009-0195-248][^cite_note-bea.aero-3]<sup>: 216 </sup> This was incorporated into ADs issued by the [EASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union_Aviation_Safety_Agency) on 31 August[^cite_note-EASA_2009-0195-248] and the [FAA](11186.md) on 3 September[^cite_note-FAA_E9-21368-249]<sup>: ¶23 </sup> with the FAA requiring that replacement be completed within 120 days of the effective date.[^cite_note-FAA_E9-21368-249]<sup>: ¶27 </sup> The ADs stated that reports of airspeed indication discrepancies while flying at high altitudes in inclement weather conditions were attributable to the model of tube being replaced having a greater susceptibility to adverse environmental conditions, and further noted that the new Thales model had not yet demonstrated the same level of robustness.[^cite_note-FAA_E9-21368-249]<sup>: ¶14 </sup>

On 20 December 2010, Airbus issued a warning to roughly 100 operators of A330, A340-200, and A340-300 aircraft regarding pitot tubes, advising pilots not to re-engage the autopilot following failure of the airspeed indicators.[^cite_note-Airbus_pitot_tubes-250][^cite_note-251][^cite_note-252] Safety recommendations issued by BEA for pitot probes design, recommended, "they must be fitted with a heating system designed to prevent any malfunctioning due to icing. Appropriate means must be provided (visual warning directly visible to the crew) to inform the crew of any nonfunctioning of the heating system".[^cite_note-bea.aero-3]<sup>: 137 </sup>

### Findings from the flight data recorder

On 27 May 2011, the BEA released an update on its investigation describing the history of the flight as recorded by the FDR. This confirmed what had previously been concluded from *post mortem&#x20;*&#x65;xamination of the bodies and debris recovered from the ocean surface; the aircraft had not broken up at altitude, but had fallen into the ocean intact.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_first200971-228][^cite_note-www\.bea.aero_vol-230] The FDRs also revealed that the aircraft's descent into the sea was not due to mechanical failure or the aircraft being overwhelmed by the weather, but because the flight crew had raised the aircraft's nose, reducing its speed until it entered an aerodynamic stall.[^cite_note-bea2011may27-88][^cite_note-PM-253]

While the inconsistent airspeed data caused the disengagement of the autopilot, the reason the pilots lost control of the aircraft had remained a mystery, in particular because pilots would normally try to lower the nose in the event of a stall.[^cite_note-Newscientist-28May-254][^cite_note-255][^cite_note-256] Multiple sensors provide the [pitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_principal_axes#Transverse_axis_\(pitch\)) information and no indication was given that any of them were malfunctioning.[^cite_note-257] One factor may be that since the A330 does not normally accept control inputs that would cause a stall, the pilots were unaware that a stall could happen when the aircraft switched to an alternative mode because of failure of the airspeed indication.[^cite_note-PM-253][^cite_note-262]

In October 2011, a transcript of the CVR was leaked and published in the book *Erreurs de Pilotage* (*Pilot Errors*) by Jean Pierre Otelli.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOtelli2011-263] The BEA and Air France both condemned the release of this information, with Air France calling it "sensationalized and unverifiable information" that "impairs the memory of the crew and passengers who lost their lives."[^cite_note-264] The BEA subsequently released its final report on the accident, and Appendix 1 contained an official CVR transcript that did not include groups of words deemed to have no bearing on flight.[^cite_note-Final-CVR-English-82]

### Third interim report

On 29 July 2011, the BEA released a third interim report on safety issues it found in the wake of the crash.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_third2011-5] It was accompanied by two shorter documents summarizing the interim report[^cite_note-synth3-266] and addressing safety recommendations.[^cite_note-recommendation3-267]

The third interim report stated that some new facts had been established. In particular:

- The pilots had not applied the unreliable-airspeed procedure.

- The pilot-in-control pulled back on the stick, increasing the angle of attack and causing the aircraft to [climb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climb_\(aeronautics\)) rapidly.

- The pilots apparently did not notice that the aircraft had reached its [maximum permissible altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_envelope#Service_ceiling).

- The pilots did not read out the available data (vertical velocity, altitude, etc.).

- The stall warning sounded continuously for 54 seconds.

- The pilots did not comment on the stall warnings and apparently did not realize that the aircraft was stalled.

- There was some [buffeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buffeting) associated with the stall.

- The stall warning deactivates by design when the angle of attack measurements are considered invalid, as is the case when the measured speed drops below a certain value.

  [^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_third201178-268]

- In consequence, the stall warning came on whenever the pilot pushed forward on the stick and then stopped when he pulled back; this happened several times during the stall, and may have confused the pilots.

- Although they knew that altitude was declining rapidly, the pilots were unable to determine which instruments to trust; all values may have appeared to them to be incoherent.

  [^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_third20114-269]

The BEA assembled a [human factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_factors) working group to analyze the crew's actions and reactions during the final stages of the flight.[^cite_note-270]

### Final report

On 5 July 2012, the BEA released its final report on the accident. This confirmed the findings of the preliminary reports and provided additional details and recommendations to improve safety. According to the final report,[^cite_note-bea.aero-3] the accident resulted from this succession of major events:

- Temporary inconsistency between the measured speeds, likely as a result of the obstruction of the pitot tubes by ice crystals, caused autopilot disconnection and \[flight control mode] reconfiguration to "[alternate law (ALT)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternate_law#Alternate_law)".

- The crew made inappropriate control inputs that destabilized the flight path.

- The crew failed to follow appropriate procedure for loss of displayed airspeed information.

- The crew were late in identifying and correcting the deviation from the flight path.

- The crew lacked understanding of the approach to stall.

- The crew failed to recognize the aircraft had stalled, and consequently did not make inputs that would have made recovering from the stall possible.

  [^cite_note-bea.aero-3]

  : 200&#x20;

These events resulted from these major factors in combination:[^cite_note-bea.aero-3]

- Feedback mechanisms between all those involved (the report identifies manufacturers, operators, flight crews, and regulatory agencies), which made it impossible to identify repeated non-application of the loss of airspeed information procedure, and to ensure that crews were trained in icing of the pitot probes and its consequences.

- The crew's lack of practical training in manually handling the aircraft both at high altitude and in the event of anomalies of speed indication.

- The weakening of the two co-pilots' task sharing, both by incomprehension of the situation at the time of autopilot disconnection and by poor management of the "[startle effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Startle_response)", leaving them in an emotionally charged state.

- The cockpit's lack of a clear display of the inconsistencies in airspeed readings identified by the flight computers.

- The crew's lack of response to the stall warning, whether due to a failure to identify the aural warning, to the transience of the stall warnings that could have been considered spurious, to the absence of any visual information that could confirm that the aircraft was approaching stall after losing the characteristic speeds, to confusing stall-related [buffet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buffeting) for overspeed-related buffet, to the indications by the flight director that might have confirmed the crew's mistaken view of their actions, or to difficulty in identifying and understanding the implications of the switch to alternate law, which does not protect the angle of attack.

## Independent analyses

Before and after the publication of the final report by the BEA in July 2012, many independent analyses and expert opinions were published in the media about the cause of the accident.

### Significance of the accident

In May 2011, [Wil S. Hylton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wil_S._Hylton) of *[The New York Times](30680.md)* commented that the crash "was easy to bend into myth" because "no other passenger jet in modern history had disappeared so completely—without a Mayday call or a witness or even a trace on radar." Hylton explained that the A330 "was considered to be among the safest" of the passenger aircraft. Hylton added that when "Flight 447 seemed to disappear from the sky, it was tempting to deliver a tidy narrative about the hubris of building a self-flying aircraft, Icarus falling from the sky. Or maybe Flight 447 was the *Titanic*, an uncrashable ship at the bottom of the sea."[^cite_note-hylton_nytmag-210] Guy Gratton, an aviation expert from the Flight Safety Laboratory at [Brunel University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunel_University), said, "This is an air accident the likes of which we haven't seen before. Half the accident investigators in the Western world—and in Russia too—are waiting for these results. This has been the biggest investigation since [Lockerbie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_Am_Flight_103). Put bluntly, big passenger planes do not just fall out of the sky."[^cite_note-day_telegraph-273]

### Angle-of-attack indication

[Chesley "Sully" Sullenberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sully_Sullenberger) suggested that pilots would be able to better handle upsets of this type if they had an indication of the wing's angle of attack (AoA).[^cite_note-274] By contrast, aviation author Captain Bill Palmer has expressed doubts that an AoA indicator would have saved AF447, writing: "as the [pilot flying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilot_flying) (PF) seemed to be ignoring the more fundamental indicators of pitch and attitude, along with numerous stall warnings, one could question what difference a rarely used AoA gauge would have made".[^cite_note-275]

Following its investigation, the BEA recommended that the European Aviation Safety Agency and the FAA should consider making an AoA indicator on the instrument panel mandatory.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_final2012§4.2.2_p._205-276] In 2014, the FAA streamlined requirements for AoA indicators for [general aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_aviation)[^cite_note-aopa-2014-277][^cite_note-faa-feb-2014-278] without affecting requirements for commercial aviation.

### Human factors and computer interaction

On 6 December 2011, *[Popular Mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_Mechanics)* published an English translation of the analysis of the transcript of the CVR controversially leaked in the book *Erreurs de Pilotage*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOtelli2011-263] It highlighted the role of the co-pilot in stalling the aircraft, while the flight computer was under alternate law at high altitude. This "simple but persistent" human error was given as the most direct cause of this accident.[^cite_note-PM-253] In the commentary accompanying the article, they also noted that the failure to follow principles of [crew resource management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_resource_management) was a contributory factor.

The final BEA report points to the human-computer interface (HCI) of the Airbus as a possible factor contributing to the crash. It provides an explanation for most of the pitch-up inputs by the pilot flying, left unexplained in the *Popular Mechanics* piece: namely that the flight director display was misleading.[^cite_note-279] The pitch-up input at the beginning of the fatal sequence of events appears to be the consequence of an altimeter error. The investigators also pointed to the lack of a clear display of the airspeed inconsistencies, though the computers had identified them. Some systems generated failure messages only about the consequences, but never mentioned the origin of the problem. The investigators recommended a blocked pitot tube should be clearly indicated as such to the crew on the flight displays. *[The Daily Telegraph](23797577.md)* pointed out the absence of AoA information, which is important in identifying and preventing a stall.[^cite_note-Ross-280] The paper stated, "though angle of attack readings are sent to onboard computers, there are no displays in modern jets to convey this critical information to the crews." *[Der Spiegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_Spiegel)* indicated the difficulty the pilots faced in diagnosing the problem: "One alarm after another lit up the cockpit monitors. One after another, the autopilot, the automatic engine control system, and the flight computers shut themselves off."[^cite_note-281] Against this backdrop of confusing information, difficulty with aural cognition (due to heavy buffeting from the storm, as well as the stall) and zero external visibility, the pilots had less than three minutes to identify the problem and take corrective action. *Der Spiegel* concluded that such a crash "could happen again".

In an article in *[Vanity Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanity_Fair_\(magazine\))*, [William Langewiesche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Langewiesche) noted that once the AoA was so extreme, the system rejected the data as invalid, and temporarily stopped the stall warnings, but "this led to a perverse reversal that lasted nearly to the impact; each time Bonin happened to lower the nose, rendering the angle of attack marginally less severe, the stall warning sounded again—a negative reinforcement that may have locked him into his pattern of pitching up", which increased the angle of attack and thus aggravated the stall.[^cite_note-VF201410-44]

#### Side-stick control issue

See also: [Side-stick § Handling of dual input situations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Side-stick#Handling_of_dual_input_situations)



Right-hand [side-stick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Side-stick) control on an [Airbus A380](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A380) [flight deck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cockpit) (similar to the one installed on A330s)

In April 2012 in *The Daily Telegraph*, British journalist [Nick Ross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Ross) published a comparison of Airbus and [Boeing](18933266.md) flight controls; unlike the [control yoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoke_\(aircraft\)) used on Boeing flight decks, the Airbus [side-stick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Side-stick) controls give little visual feedback and no sensory or tactile feedback to the second pilot. The cockpit synthetic voice, however, does give an aural 'Dual Input' warning, but only in the absence of higher priority warnings,[^cite_note-282] whenever both pilots move their side-sticks.[^cite_note-synth3-266] Ross reasoned that the lack of the dual control warning might in part explain why the PF's \[pilot flying] fatal nose-up inputs were not countermanded by his two colleagues.[^cite_note-Ross-280][^cite_note-283]

In a July 2012 CBS report, Sullenberger suggested the design of the Airbus cockpit might have been a factor in the accident. The flight controls are not mechanically linked between the two pilot seats, and Robert, the left-seat pilot who believed he had taken over control of the aircraft, was not aware that Bonin continued to hold the stick back, which overrode Robert's own control.[^cite_note-284][^cite_note-285][^cite_note-286]

The BEA final report acknowledged the difficulty for one pilot to observe the side-stick input of the other,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_final2012p._174-287] but did not identify it as a cause of the accident[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_final2012§3.2-288] and made no recommendation related to the side-stick input design.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_final2012§4-289]

### Fatigue

Getting enough sleep is a constant concern for pilots of long-haul flights.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmer201319-290] Although the BEA could find no "objective" indications that the pilots of Flight 447 were suffering from fatigue,[^cite_note-bea.aero-3]<sup>: 100 </sup>[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_final2012§1.16.7_"Aspects_relating_to_fatigue"_p._100-291] some exchanges recorded on the CVR, including a remark made by Captain Dubois that he had only slept an hour,[^cite_note-292] could indicate the crew were not well rested before the flight.[^cite_note-293] The co-pilots had spent three nights in Rio de Janeiro, but the BEA was unable to retrieve data regarding their rest and could not determine their activities during the stopover.[^cite_note-bea.aero-3]<sup>: 24 </sup>[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBEA_final201224§1.5-294][^cite_note-295]

## Aftermath



Memorial to the victims of Flight 447 in Rio. An identical monument was erected in Paris at [Père Lachaise Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C3%A8re_Lachaise_Cemetery).

Shortly after the crash, Air France changed the number of the regular Rio de Janeiro-Paris flight from AF447 to AF445;[^cite_note-296] and, as of December 2023, the number for the route had been changed to AF485. Initially the Airbus A330 continued to operate the route, but the airline later switched to a [Boeing 777-300ER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_777-300ER).[^cite_note-297]

Following the crash of Air France 447, other Airbus A330 operators studied their internal flight records to seek patterns. [Delta Air Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Air_Lines) analyzed the data of Northwest Airlines flights that occurred before the two companies merged and found a dozen incidents in which at least one of an A330's pitot tubes had briefly stopped working when the aircraft was flying through the ITCZ, the same location where Air France 447 crashed.[^cite_note-298][^cite_note-299]

### Legal cases

Air France and Airbus have been investigated for manslaughter since 2011, but in 2019, prosecutors recommended dropping the case against Airbus and charging Air France with manslaughter and negligence, arguing, "the airline was aware of technical problems with a key airspeed monitoring instrument on its planes but failed to train pilots to resolve them".[^cite_note-\:0-218] The case against Airbus was dropped on 22 July 2019.[^cite_note-\:1-219] The case against Air France was dropped in September 2019 when magistrates said, "there were not enough grounds to prosecute".[^cite_note-\:2-220] However, in 2021, a public prosecutor in Paris requested to have Airbus and Air France tried in a court of law.[^cite_note-300] In April, it was announced that both companies would be prosecuted over the crash. Lawyers for Airbus stated they would lodge an immediate appeal against the decision.[^cite_note-France24-20210512-301] The trial opened on 10 October 2022, with Airbus and Air France both being charged with involuntary manslaughter.[^cite_note-\:3-302] Both companies pleaded not guilty to the charges.[^cite_note-\:3-302][^cite_note-303][^cite_note-304]

On 7 December, prosecutors announced that they would not seek conviction of either company for manslaughter as they were unable to prove them guilty, and recommended acquitting both companies. Families and friends of the victims were outraged by the decision.[^cite_note-not_seeking_conviction-305][^cite_note-dismay-306][^cite_note-307] On 17 April 2023, Airbus and Air France were both acquitted of manslaughter.[^cite_note-308][^cite_note-309][^cite_note-310] A French prosecutor lodged an appeal against the verdict.[^cite_note-311]

The second trial opened on 29 September 2025. Airbus and Air France both pleaded not guilty to involuntary manslaughter a second time.[^cite_note-312] On 21 May 2026 both companies were found guilty of [corporate manslaughter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_manslaughter). Each company was sentenced to pay the maximum fine of €225,000 (\$261,112). Both companies plan to appeal the verdict.[^cite_note-313][^cite_note-314][^cite_note-315]

## In popular culture

A one-hour documentary entitled *Lost: The Mystery of Flight 447* detailing an early independent hypothesis about the crash was produced by Darlow Smithson in 2010 for *[Nova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nova_\(American_TV_series\))* and the [BBC](19344654.md). Without data from the black boxes, an independent panel of experts analyzed [ACARS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACARS) messages among weather patterns and limited debris data to postulate that [supercooled water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercooled_water) blocked pitot tubes, causing most automatic systems to shut down, before the information overload combined with auto-thrust not adjusting the indicated thrust level of the thrust levers caused crew to neglect increasing power, with automatic systems decreasing the capability of pilots to handle the stall.[^cite_note-BBC_documentary-316][^cite_note-317][^cite_note-Nova1-318][^cite_note-Nova2-319]

On 16 September 2012, [Channel 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_4) in the UK presented *Fatal Flight 447: Chaos in the Cockpit*, which showed data from the black boxes including an in-depth re-enactment. It was produced by Minnow Films.[^cite_note-320] Similar presentation was made by [*60 Minutes* Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/60_Minutes_\(Australian_TV_program\)) in 2014.[^cite_note-321]

The aviation-disaster documentary television series *[Mayday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayday_\(Canadian_TV_series\))* (also known as *Air Crash Investigation* and *Air Emergency*) produced a 45-minute episode titled "[Air France 447: Vanished](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Mayday_episodes#Season_12_\(2012\))", which aired on 15 April 2013 in Great Britain and 17 May 2013 in the U.S.[^cite_note-322]

An article about the crash by American author and pilot [William Langewiesche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Langewiesche), entitled "Should Airplanes Be Flying Themselves?", was published by *Vanity Fair* in October 2014.[^cite_note-VF201410-44]

A *[99% Invisible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/99%25_Invisible)* podcast episode about the flight, entitled "Children of the Magenta (Automation Paradox, pt. 1)", was released on 23 June 2015 as the first of a two-part story about [automation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automation).[^cite_note-323]

In November 2015, [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](18879.md) professor David Mindell discussed the Air France Flight 447 tragedy in the opening segment of an *[EconTalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EconTalk)* podcast dedicated to the ideas in Mindell's 2015 book *Our Robots, Ourselves: Robotics and the Myths of Autonomy*.[^cite_note-324] Mindell said the crash illustrated a "failed handoff", with insufficient warning, from the aircraft's autopilot to the human pilots.[^cite_note-325]

The [Rooster Teeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rooster_Teeth)–produced podcast *Black Box Down* covered the flight in an episode titled "Stalling 38,000 Feet Over The Atlantic" on 30 July 2020.[^cite_note-326]

On 9 September 2021, the Science Channel Documentary *Deadly Engineering* covered the crash in Season 3 Episode 1: "Catastrophes in the Sky".[^cite_note-327]
