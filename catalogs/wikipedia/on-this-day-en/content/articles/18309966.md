***Billboard*** (stylized in [lowercase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_case) since 2013) is a [Manhattan](45470.md)-based [multinational](214491.md) music and entertainment [magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magazine) published weekly by [Penske Media Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penske_Media_Corporation). The publication group and miscellaneous media corporation provides music charts, news, video, opinion, reviews, events and styles related to the [music industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_industry). Its [music charts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboard_charts) include the [Hot 100](423161.md), [*Billboard* 200](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboard_200), and [Global 200](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboard_Global_200), which rank the most popular singles and albums across a wide range of genres based on sales, streaming, and radio airplay. It also hosts events, owns a publishing firm and operates several television shows.

*Billboard* was founded in 1894 by William Donaldson and James Hennegan as a [trade publication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_publication) for bill posters. Donaldson acquired Hennegan's interest in 1900 for \$500. In the early years of the 20th century, it covered the entertainment industry, such as circuses, fairs and [burlesque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burlesque) shows, and also created a mail service for travelling entertainers. *Billboard* began focusing more on the music industry as the [jukebox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jukebox), [phonograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonograph), and [radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio) became commonplace. Many topics that it covered became the subjects of new magazines, including *Amusement Business* in 1961 to cover outdoor entertainment, so that *Billboard* could focus on music. After Donaldson died in 1925, *Billboard* was inherited by his and Hennegan's children, who retained ownership until selling it to private investors in 1985. The magazine has since been owned by various parties.

## History

### Early history



First issue of *Billboard* (1894)

The first issue of *Billboard* was published in [Cincinnati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cincinnati), Ohio, by William Donaldson and James Hennegan on November 1, 1894.[^cite_note-LampelLant-2][^cite_note-Broven_2009_p._187-3] Initially it covered the advertising and [bill-posting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flyposting) industry[^cite_note-4] and was known as ***Billboard Advertising***.[^cite_note-Gussow\._2015-5][^cite_note-godfrey-6][^cite_note-7] At the time, billboards, posters, and paper advertisements placed in public spaces were the primary means of advertising.[^cite_note-godfrey-6] Donaldson handled editorial and advertising, while Hennegan, who owned Hennegan Printing Co., managed magazine production. The first issues were just eight pages long.[^cite_note-HighBeam_Business:_Arrive_Prepared_1994-8] The paper had columns such as The Bill Room Gossip and The Indefatigable and Tireless Industry of the Bill Poster.[^cite_note-LampelLant-2] A department for agricultural fairs was established in 1896.[^cite_note-on_Project_1943_p._184-9] The *Billboard Advertising* publication was renamed ***The Billboard*** in 1897.[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10]

After a brief departure over editorial differences, Donaldson purchased Hennegan's interest in the business in 1900 for \$500 (equal to \$15,500 today) to save it from bankruptcy.[^cite_note-HighBeam_Business:_Arrive_Prepared_1994-8][^cite_note-Hoffmann_2004_p._212-11] On May 5, Donaldson changed the publication from a monthly to a weekly paper with a greater emphasis on breaking news. He improved editorial quality and opened new offices in New York, Chicago, San Francisco, London, and Paris,[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10][^cite_note-Hoffmann_2004_p._212-11] and also refocused the magazine on outdoor entertainment such as fairs, carnivals, circuses, [vaudeville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaudeville), and burlesque shows.[^cite_note-LampelLant-2][^cite_note-booksdirectory-10] A section devoted to circuses was introduced in 1900, followed by more prominent coverage of outdoor events in 1901.[^cite_note-on_Project_1943_p._184-9] *Billboard* also covered topics including regulation, professionalism, economics and new shows. It had a "stage gossip" column covering the private lives of entertainers, a "tent show" section covering traveling shows and a subsection called "Freaks to Order".[^cite_note-LampelLant-2] Donaldson also published news articles opposing censorship, supporting productions exhibiting good taste and decrying [yellow journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_journalism)."[^cite_note-Radel_1994-12]

As railroads became more developed, *Billboard* enabled a mail-forwarding system for traveling entertainers. The location of an entertainer was tracked in the paper's Routes Ahead column, and then *Billboard* would receive mail on the star's behalf and publish a notice in its Letter-Box column that it had mail for him or her.[^cite_note-LampelLant-2] This service was first introduced in 1904 and became one of *Billboard*'s largest sources of profit[^cite_note-Radel_1994-12] and celebrity connections.[^cite_note-LampelLant-2] By 1914, 42,000 people were using the service.[^cite_note-HighBeam_Business:_Arrive_Prepared_1994-8] It was also used as the official address of traveling entertainers for draft letters during [World War I](4764461.md).[^cite_note-newsweek-13] In the 1960s, when the service was discontinued, *Billboard* was still processing 1,500 letters per week.[^cite_note-Radel_1994-12]

In 1920, Donaldson controversially hired black journalist [James Albert Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Albert_Jackson) to write a weekly column devoted to black performers.[^cite_note-LampelLant-2] According to *The Business of Culture: Strategic Perspectives on Entertainment and Media*, the column identified discrimination against black performers and helped validate their careers.[^cite_note-LampelLant-2] Jackson was the first black critic at a national magazine with a predominantly white audience. According to his grandson, Donaldson also established a policy against identifying performers by their race.[^cite_note-Radel_1994-12] Donaldson died in 1925.[^cite_note-LampelLant-2]

### Focus on music

*Billboard*'s editorial content changed focus as technology in recording and playback developed, covering "marvels of modern technology" such as the [phonograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonograph) and wireless radios.[^cite_note-LampelLant-2] The magazine began covering coin-operated entertainment machines in 1899 and created a dedicated section called Amusement Machines in March 1932.[^cite_note-Hoffmann_2004_p._212-11] *Billboard* began covering the motion-picture industry in 1907[^cite_note-on_Project_1943_p._184-9] but, facing strong competition from *[Variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variety_\(magazine\))*, centered its focus on music.[^cite_note-Bloom_2013_p._83-14] It created a radio-broadcasting station in the 1920s.[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10]

The jukebox industry continued to grow through the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression) and was advertised heavily in *Billboard*,[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10]<sup>: 262 </sup> which led to even more editorial focus on music.[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10] The proliferation of the phonograph and radio also contributed to its growing music emphasis.[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10] *Billboard* published the first [music hit parade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboard_charts) on January 4, 1936[^cite_note-15] and introduced a Record Buying Guide in January 1939.[^cite_note-Hoffmann_2004_p._212-11] In 1940, it introduced Chart Line, which tracked the best-selling records, and was followed by a chart for jukebox records in 1944 called Music Box Machine.[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10][^cite_note-Hoffmann_2004_p._212-11] By the 1940s, *Billboard* was more of a music-industry specialist publication.[^cite_note-Gussow\._2015-5] The number of charts that it published grew after [World War II](32927.md), as new music interests and genres became popular. It had eight charts by 1987, covering different genres and formats,[^cite_note-Hoffmann_2004_p._212-11] and 28 charts by 1994.[^cite_note-Radel_1994-12]

By 1943, *Billboard* had about 100 employees.[^cite_note-on_Project_1943_p._184-9] The magazine's offices moved to Brighton, Ohio in 1946, then to New York City in 1948.[^cite_note-Radel_1994-12] A five-column tabloid format was adopted in November 1950 and coated paper was first used in *Billboard*'s print issues in January 1963, allowing for photojournalism.[^cite_note-Hoffmann_2004_p._212-11]

*Billboard Publications Inc.* acquired a monthly trade magazine for candy and cigarette machine vendors called *Vend*, and in the 1950s it acquired an advertising trade publication called *Tide*.[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10] By 1969, Billboard Publications Inc. owned 11 trade and consumer publications, [Watson-Guptill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watson-Guptill) Publications, a set of self-study cassette tapes and four television franchises. It also acquired *Photo Weekly* that year.[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10]

Over time, subjects that *Billboard* covered outside of the music world formed the basis of separate publications: *Funspot* magazine was created in 1957 to cover amusement parks and *Amusement Business* was created in 1961 to cover outdoor entertainment. In January 1961, *The Billboard* was renamed ***Billboard Music Week***[^cite_note-godfrey-6][^cite_note-booksdirectory-10] to emphasize its newly exclusive interest in music.[^cite_note-Bloom_2013_p._83-14] Two years later, it was renamed to simply *Billboard*.[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10][^cite_note-Hoffmann_2004_p._212-11] According to *The New Business Journalism*, by 1984, Billboard Publications was a "prosperous" conglomerate of trade magazines, and *Billboard* had become the "undisputed leader" in music-industry news.[^cite_note-Gussow\._2015-5] In the early 1990s, *Billboard* introduced *Billboard Airplay Monitors*, a publication for disc jockeys and music programmers.[^cite_note-godfrey-6] By the end of the 1990s, *Billboard* dubbed itself the "bible" of the recording industry.[^cite_note-godfrey-6]

### Changes in ownership

*Billboard* struggled after its founder William Donaldson died in 1925, and within three years, was once again heading towards [bankruptcy](4695.md).[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10] Donaldson's son-in-law Roger Littleford took command in 1928 and "nursed the publication back to health."[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10][^cite_note-newsweek-13] His sons Bill and Roger became co-publishers in 1946[^cite_note-newsweek-13] and inherited the magazine in the late 1970s after Littleford's death.[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10] They sold it to private investors in 1985 for an estimated \$40 million.[^cite_note-Jackson_Keller_Flood_2010_p._638-16] The investors cut costs and acquired a trade publication for the [Broadway](725252.md) theatre industry called *Backstage*.[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10]

In 1987, *Billboard* was sold again to Affiliated Publications for \$100 million.[^cite_note-Jackson_Keller_Flood_2010_p._638-16] Billboard Publications Inc. became a subsidiary of Affiliated Publications called BPI Communications.[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10] As BPI Communications, it acquired *[The Hollywood Reporter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hollywood_Reporter)*, *[Adweek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adweek)*, *[Marketing Week](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketing_Week)* and *[Mediaweek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediaweek_\(American_magazine\))*, and also purchased [Broadcast Data Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nielsen_Broadcast_Data_Systems), a high-tech firm for tracking music airtime.[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10] Private investors from Boston Ventures and BPI executives repurchased a two-thirds interest in Billboard Publications for \$100 million, and more acquisitions followed. In 1993, it created a division known as Billboard Music Group for music-related publications.[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10]

In 1994, Billboard Publications was sold to Dutch media conglomerate [Verenigde Nederlandse Uitgeverijen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verenigde_Nederlandse_Uitgeverijen) (VNU) for \$220 million.[^cite_note-The_New_York_Times_1994-17][^cite_note-19] VNU acquired the [Clio Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clio_Awards) in advertising and the National Research Group in 1997, as well as *[Editor & Publisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Editor_&_Publisher)* in 1999. In July 2000, it paid \$650 million to the publisher [Miller Freeman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_Freeman,_Inc.). BPI was combined with other entities in VNU in 2000 to form Bill Communications Inc. By the time CEO Gerald Hobbs retired in 2003, VNU had grown substantially larger, but had a great deal of debt from the acquisitions. An attempted \$7 billion acquisition of [IMS Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IMS_Health) in 2005 prompted protests from shareholders that halted the deal; it eventually agreed to an \$11 billion takeover bid from investors in 2006.[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10]

VNU changed its name to [Nielsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nielsen_N.V.) in 2007, the namesake of a company that it had acquired for \$2.5 billion in 1999.[^cite_note-WSJ_1999-20][^cite_note-Deliso_2007-21] New CEO Robert Krakoff divested some of the previously owned publications, restructured the organization and planned some acquisitions before dying suddenly in 2007. He was subsequently replaced by Greg Farrar.[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10]

Nielsen owned *Billboard* until 2009, when it was one of eight publications sold to e5 Global Media Holdings. e5 was formed by investment firms Pluribus Capital Management and [Guggenheim Partners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guggenheim_Partners) for the purpose of the acquisition.[^cite_note-Ives_2009-22][^cite_note-Los_Angeles_Times_2009-23] The following year, the new parent company was renamed [Prometheus Global Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prometheus_Global_Media).[^cite_note-Folio:_2010-24] Three years later, Guggenheim Partners acquired Pluribus' share of Prometheus and became the sole owner of *Billboard*.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-Numbers_2013-26]

In December 2015, Guggenheim Digital Media spun out several media brands, including *Billboard*, to its own executive [Todd Boehly](35675191.md).[^cite_note-deadline-guggensell-27][^cite_note-thewrap-todd-28] The assets operate under the Hollywood Reporter-Billboard Media Group, a unit of the holding company [Eldridge Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eldridge_Industries).[^cite_note-bloomberg-draftkingsfund-29]

### 1990s–present

[Timothy White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_White_\(editor\)) was appointed editor-in-chief in 1991, a position that he held until his unexpected death in 2002. White wrote a weekly column promoting music with "artistic merit" while criticizing music with violent or misogynistic themes,[^cite_note-latimes_2002-30] and also reworked the publication's music charts.[^cite_note-latimes_2002-30] Rather than relying on data from music retailers, new charts used data from store checkout scanners obtained by [Nielsen SoundScan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nielsen_SoundScan).[^cite_note-booksdirectory-10] White also wrote in-depth profiles on musicians,[^cite_note-Pareles_2002-31] but was replaced by Keith Girard, who was subsequently fired in May 2004. Girard and a female employee filed a \$29 million lawsuit alleging that *Billboard* fired them unfairly with an intent to damage their reputations[^cite_note-Jurkowitz_2004-32] and that they experienced sexual harassment, a hostile work environment and a financially motivated lack of editorial integrity.[^cite_note-Jurkowitz_2004-32][^cite_note-Grinberg_2005-33] Email evidence suggested that human resources were given special instructions to watch minority employees.[^cite_note-Grinberg_2005-33] The case was settled out of court in 2006 for an undisclosed sum.[^cite_note-Tsioulcas_2015-34]

In the 2000s, economic decline in the music industry dramatically reduced readership and advertising from *Billboard*'s traditional audience.[^cite_note-Jurkowitz_2004-32][^cite_note-Sisario_2014-35] Circulation declined from 40,000 in the 1990s to less than 17,000 by 2014.[^cite_note-Tsioulcas_2015-34] The publication's staff and ownership were also undergoing frequent changes.[^cite_note-Grinberg_2005-33]

In 2004, Tamara Conniff became the first female and youngest-ever executive editor at *Billboard* and led its first major redesign since the 1960s, designed by Daniel Stark and Stark Design. During Conniff's tenure, *Billboard*'s newsstand sales jumped 10%, ad pages climbed 22% and conference registrations rose 76%.[^cite_note-36] In 2005, *Billboard* expanded its editorial outside the music industry into other areas of digital and mobile entertainment. In 2006, after leading *Billboard*'s radio publication, former ABC News and CNN journalist Scott McKenzie was named editorial director across all *Billboard* properties.[^cite_note-37] Conniff launched the *Billboard* Women in Music event in 2007.[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41]

Bill Werde was named editorial director in 2008,[^cite_note-one-42] and was followed by [Janice Min](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janice_Min) in January 2014, also responsible for editorial content at *The Hollywood Reporter*.[^cite_note-one-42] The magazine became more of a general-interest music-news source rather than solely an industry trade, covering more celebrity and fashion news.[^cite_note-Tsioulcas_2015-34][^cite_note-Sisario_2014-35][^cite_note-Sisario_20142-43] Min hired Tony Gervino as editor although he did not have a background in the music industry.[^cite_note-Sisario_20142-43] Gervino was appointed editor-in-chief in April 2014.[^cite_note-Steigrad_2014-44] An [NPR](29697232.md) item covered a leaked version of *Billboard*'s annual survey, which it said had more gossip and focused on less professional topics than had prior surveys. For example, the magazine polled readers on a lawsuit that singer [Kesha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kesha) filed against her producer, alleging sexual abuse.[^cite_note-Tsioulcas_2015-34]

Gervino was fired in May 2016. A note from Min to the editorial staff indicated that senior vice president of digital content Mike Bruno would head the editorial department.[^cite_note-45] On June 15, 2016, [BillboardPH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboard_Philippines), the first *Billboard* chart company in Southeast Asia, mainly in the Philippines, was announced.[^cite_note-46] On September 12, 2016, *Billboard* expanded into China by launching *[Billboard China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboard_China)* in partnership with Vision Music Ltd.[^cite_note-47]

On September 23, 2020, it was announced that [Penske Media Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penske_Media_Corporation) would assume operations of the MRC Media & Info publications under a joint venture with MRC known as PMRC. The joint venture includes the management of *Billboard*.[^cite_note-48]

On January 13, 2024, *Billboard* shared the intent to expand further in Asia by announcing the launch of *[Billboard Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboard_Korea)*.[^cite_note-49]

## News publishing

*Billboard* publishes a news website and weekly [trade magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_magazine) that covers music, video and home entertainment. Most of the articles are written by staff writers, while some are written by industry experts.[^cite_note-Hoffmann_2004_p._212-11] It covers news, gossip, opinion,[^cite_note-LampelLant-2] and music reviews, but its "most enduring and influential creation" is the [*Billboard* charts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboard_charts).[^cite_note-godfrey-6] The charts track music sales, radio airtime and other data about the most popular songs and albums.[^cite_note-godfrey-6] The [*Billboard* Hot 100](423161.md) chart of the top-selling songs was introduced in 1958. Since then, the [*Billboard* 200](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboard_200), which tracks the top-selling albums, has become more popular as an indicator of commercial success.[^cite_note-LampelLant-2] *Billboard* has also published books in collaboration with [Watson-Guptill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watson-Guptill) and a radio and television series called American Top 40, based on Billboard charts.[^cite_note-Hoffmann_2004_p._212-11] A daily *Billboard Bulletin* was introduced in February 1997[^cite_note-godfrey-6] and *Billboard* hosts about 20 industry events each year.[^cite_note-primary-1]

*Billboard* is considered one of the most reputable sources of music industry news.[^cite_note-Radel_1994-12][^cite_note-Sisario_2014-35] The website includes the Billboard Charts, news separated by music genre, videos and a separate website. It also compiles lists, hosts a fashion website called Pret-a-Reporter and publishes eight different newsletters. The print magazine's regular sections include:[^cite_note-primary-1]

- Hot 100: A chart of the top 100 most popular songs of the week

- Topline: News from the week

- The Beat: Hitmaker interviews, gossip and trends in the music industry

- Style: Fashion and accessories

- Features: In-depth interviews, profiles and photography

- Reviews: Reviews of new albums and songs

- Backstage pass: information about events and concerts

- Charts and CODA: More information about current and historical Billboard Charts

## Award shows

In 1990, *Billboard* established the [*Billboard* Music Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboard_Music_Awards), an awards ceremony which honors the top albums, artists and singles in a number of different [music genres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_genres), which achieved the highest results during the year from sales, streaming, radio airplay, touring, and social engagement. The data are taken from *Billboard* and its data partners, including [MRC Data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MRC_Data) and [Next Big Sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Next_Big_Sound). Through the years, *Billboard* has established several other awards and polls to honor different music genres, live performances, and artists.

- [*Billboard* Latin Music Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboard_Latin_Music_Awards) (1994–present)

- [*Billboard* R\&B/Hip-Hop Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboard_R\&B/Hip-Hop_Awards?action=edit\&redlink=1) (2004)

- [*Billboard* Digital Entertainment Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboard_Digital_Entertainment_Awards) (2004)

- [*Billboard* Live Music Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboard_Live_Music_Awards) (2004–2019, 2024–present)

- [*Billboard* Women in Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboard_Women_in_Music) (2007–present)

- [*Billboard* Japan Music Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billboard_Japan_Music_Awards) (2009–2020)

- *Billboard* Mid-Year Awards (2011–2015) are a fan-voted poll conducted by the publication to recognize the most popular artists and music based on fan votes, reflecting their preferences during the first half of the year.
