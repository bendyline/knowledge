The **South Korean won**, sometimes known as the **Republic of Korea won**[^cite_note-6] ([symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Won_sign): **₩**; [code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_4217): **KRW**; [Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language): 대한민국 원), is the official currency of [South Korea](27019.md).

Each *won* (the [base unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_account)) is technically divided into 100 *jeon*, but the *jeon* is no longer used for everyday transactions and appears only in foreign exchange rates. The currency is issued by the [Bank of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_Korea), based in the capital city of [Seoul](19159283.md). The South Korean won was first issued in 1949, then was replaced by the [South Korean hwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Korean_hwan) between 1953 and 1962, before the adoption of the current South Korean won in 1962.[^cite_note-7]

## Etymology

Main article: [Etymology of the Korean currencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etymology_of_the_Korean_currencies)

See also: [Korean won (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_won_\(disambiguation\))

The old "won" was a [cognate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognate) of the Chinese [yuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuan_\(currency\)), which was derived from the [Spanish-American silver dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_dollar). It is derived from the [hanja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanja) [圓](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%9C%93) (원, *won*), meaning 'round', which describes the shape of the silver dollar.

The won was subdivided into 100 *jeon* ([Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language): 전; [Hanja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanja): 錢; [MR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCune%E2%80%93Reischauer): *chŏn*), itself a cognate of the East Asian unit of weight [mace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mace_\(unit\)) and synonymous with money in general. The current won (1962 to present) is written in hangul only and does not officially have any hanja associated with it.[^cite_note-hangulonly1-4][^cite_note-hangulonly2-5]

## First South Korean won

Main article: [South Korean won (1945–1953)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Korean_won_\(1945%E2%80%931953\))

### History

The [Korean won](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_Empire_won), [Chinese yuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuan_\(currency\)) and [Japanese yen](34392.md) were all derived from the [Spanish-American silver dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_dollar), a coin widely used for international trade between Asia and the Americas from the 16th to 19th centuries.

During the [colonial era under the Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea_under_Japanese_rule) (1910–45), the won was replaced by the [Korean yen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_yen) which was [at par](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/At_par#Currency) with the Japanese yen.

After [World War II](32927.md) ended in 1945, [Korea was divided](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_of_Korea), resulting in two separate currencies, both called won, for the South and the [North](21255.md). Both the [Southern won](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Korean_won_\(1945\)) and the [Northern won](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Korean_won) replaced the yen at par. The first South Korean won was subdivided into 100 *jeon*.

The South Korean won initially had [a fixed exchange rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_exchange-rate_system) to the [U.S. dollar](18717338.md) at a rate of 15 won to 1 dollar. A series of [devaluations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devaluation) followed, the later ones, in part, due to the [Korean War](16772.md) (1950–53). The pegs were:

| Date introduced | Value of U.S. dollar in won |
| --- | --- |
| October 1945 | 15 |
| July 15, 1947 | 50 |
| October 1, 1948 | 450 |
| June 14, 1949 | 900 (non-government transactions only) |
| May 1, 1950 | 1,800 |
| November 1, 1950 | 2,500 |
| April 1, 1951 | 6,000 |

The first South Korean won was replaced by the *[hwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Korean_hwan)* on February 15, 1953, at a rate of 1 hwan = 100 won.[^cite_note-8]

### Banknotes

In 1946, the [Bank of Joseon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_Joseon) introduced 10 and 100 won notes. These were followed in 1949 by 5 and 1,000 won notes.

A new central bank, the [Bank of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_Korea), was established on June 12, 1950,[^cite_note-9] and assumed the duties of Bank of Joseon. Notes were introduced (some dated 1949) in denominations of 5, 10 and 50 jeon, and 100 and 1,000 won. The 500 won notes were introduced in 1952. In 1953, a series of banknotes was issued which, although it gave the denominations in [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) in won, were, in fact, the first issues of the [hwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Korean_hwan).

## Second South Korean won

### History

The won was reintroduced on June 10, 1962, at a rate of 1 won = 10 hwan. It became the sole legal tender on March 22, 1975, with the withdrawal of the last circulating hwan coins. Its [ISO 4217](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_4217) code is KRW. At the reintroduction of the won in 1962, its value was pegged at 125 won = US\$1. The following pegs operated between 1962 and 1980:

| Date introduced | Value of U.S. dollar in won |
| --- | --- |
| June 10, 1962 | 125 |
| May 3, 1964 | 255 |
| August 3, 1972 | 400 |
| December 7, 1974 | 480 |
| January 12, 1980 | 580 |

On February 27, 1980, efforts were initiated to lead to a [floating exchange rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_exchange_rate). The won was allowed to float on December 24, 1997, when an agreement was signed with the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md).[^cite_note-10] Shortly after, the won was devalued by almost half, as part of the [1997 Asian financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Asian_financial_crisis).

### Coins

Until 1966, 10 and 50 hwan coins, revalued as 1 and 5 won, were the only coins in circulation. New coins, denominated in won, were introduced by the Bank of Korea on August 16, 1966, in denominations of 1, 5 and 10 won, with the 1 won struck in [brass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brass) and the 5 and 10 won in [bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze). These were the first South Korean coins to display the date in the [Gregorian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_calendar), earlier coins having used the [Korean calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_calendar). The 10 and 50 hwan coins were demonetized on March 22, 1975.[^cite_note-issue-11]

In 1968, as the intrinsic value of the brass 1 won coin far surpassed its face value, new [aluminium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium) 1 won coins were issued to replace them. As an attempt to further reduce currency production costs, new 5 and 10 won coins were issued in 1970, struck in brass. [Cupronickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupronickel) 100 won coins were also introduced that year, followed by cupronickel 50 won coins in 1972.[^cite_note-issue-11]

The Bank of Korea announced in early 2006 its intention to redesign the 10 won coin by the end of that year. With the increasing cost of production, then at 38 won per 10 won coin, and rumors that some people had been melting the coins to make jewelry, the redesign was needed to make the coin more cost-effective to produce.[^cite_note-16] The new coin is made of copper-coated aluminium with a reduced diameter of 18 millimetres (0.71 in), and a weight of 1.22 grams (0.043 oz). Its visual design is the same as the old coin.[^cite_note-17] The new coin was issued on December 18, 2006.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19]

The 1 and 5 won coins are [rarely in circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Withdrawal_of_low-denomination_coins) since 1992, and prices of consumer goods are rounded to the nearest 10 won. However, they are still in production, minting limited amounts of these two coins every year, for the Bank of Korea's annual mint sets.[^cite_note-20] In 1998, the production costs per coin were: 10 won coins each cost 35 won to produce, 100 won coins cost 58 won, and 500 won coins cost 77 won.[^cite_note-21]

### Banknotes

The [Bank of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_Korea) designates banknote and coin series in a unique way. Instead of putting those of similar design and issue dates in the same series, it assigns series number X to the Xth design of a given denomination. The series numbers are expressed with [Korean letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangul) used in alphabetical order, e.g. 가, 나, 다, 라, 마, 바, 사. Therefore, 1,000 won issued in 1983 is series II (나) because it is the second design of all 1,000 won designs since the introduction of the South Korean won in 1962.

In 1962, 10 and 50 jeon, 1, 5, 10, 50, 100 and 500 won notes were introduced by the Bank of Korea. The first issue of 1, 5, 10, 50, 100 and 500 won notes was printed in the [UK](31717.md) by [Thomas De La Rue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_La_Rue). The jeon notes together with a second issue of 10 and 100 won notes were printed domestically by the [Korea Minting and Security Printing Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea_Minting_and_Security_Printing_Corporation).

In 1965, 100 won notes (series III) were printed using [intaglio printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intaglio_\(printmaking\)) techniques, for the first time on domestically printed notes, to reduce counterfeiting. Replacements for the British 500 won notes followed in 1966, also using intaglio printing, and for the 50 won notes in 1969 using [lithoprinting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithography).[^cite_note-issue-11]

With the economic development from the 1960s, the value of the 500 won notes fell, resulting in a greater use of cashier's checks with higher fixed denominations as means of payment, as well as an increased use of counterfeited ones.[^cite_note-issue-11] In 1970, the 100 won notes were replaced by coins, with the same happening to the 50 won notes in 1972.

Higher-denomination notes of 5,000 and 10,000 won were introduced in 1972 and 1973, respectively. The notes incorporated new security features, including [watermark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermark), [security thread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_thread), and ultraviolet response fibres, and were intaglio printed. The release of 10,000 won notes was planned to be at the same time as the 5,000 won notes, but problems with the main theme delayed it by a year.[^cite_note-10,000wonhis-22] Newly designed 500 won notes were also released in 1973, and the need for a medium denomination resulted in the introduction of 1,000 won notes in 1975.

In 1982, the 500 won note was replaced by a coin. The following year, as part of its policy of rationalizing the currency system, the Bank of Korea issued a new set of notes, as well as a new set of coins. Some of the notes' most notable features were distinguishable marks for the blind under the watermark and the addition of machine-readable language in preparation for mechanization of cash handling. They were also printed on better-quality cotton pulp to reduce the production costs by extending their circulation life.[^cite_note-issue-11]

To cope with the deregulation of imports of color printers and the increasing use of computers and scanners, modified 5,000 and 10,000 won notes were released between 1994 and 2002 with various new security features, which included color-shifting ink, microprint, segmented metal thread, [moiré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moir%C3%A9_pattern), and [EURion constellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EURion_constellation). The latest version of the 5,000 and 10,000 won notes are easily identifiable by the copyright information inscribed under the watermark: "© 한국은행" and year of issue on the obverse, "© The Bank of Korea" and year of issue on the reverse.

The plates for the 5,000 won notes were produced in Japan, while the ones for the 1,000 and 10,000 won notes were produced by the Korea Minting and Security Printing Corporation. They were all printed in intaglio.[^cite_note-10,000wonhis-22][^cite_note-5,000wonhis-23][^cite_note-1000wonhis-24]

## New security features

In 2006, it became a major concern that the South Korean won banknotes were being [counterfeited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterfeit_money). This led the government to issue a new series of banknotes, with the 5,000 won note being the first one to be redesigned. Later in 2007, the 1,000 and 10,000 won notes were introduced.

On June 23, 2009, the Bank of Korea released the 50,000 won note. The obverse bears a portrait of [Shin Saimdang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shin_Saimdang), a prominent 16th-century artist, calligrapher, and mother of Korean scholar [Yulgok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yulgok), also known as Yi I, who is on the 5,000 won note. This note is the first Korean banknote to feature the portrait of a woman.[^cite_note-26] The release of the 50,000 won note stirred some controversy among shop owners and those with visual impairments due to its similarity in color and numerical denomination with the 5,000 won note.[^cite_note-27]

New 100,000 won notes were also announced, but their release was later cancelled due to the controversy over the banknote's planned image, featuring the [Daedongyeojido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daedongyeojido) map, and not including the disputed [Dokdo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dokdo) islands.[^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29][^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31] Also of controversy was the appearance of [Kim Ku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Ku) on the note, who is controversial among the South Korean right.[^cite_note-32]

The banknotes include over 10 security features in each denomination. The 50,000 won note has 22 security features, the 10,000 won note 21, the 5,000 won note 17, the 2,000 won note 10 and the 1,000 won note 19. Many modern security features that can be also found in [euros](9472.md), [pounds](270673.md), [Canadian dollars](101846.md), and [Japanese yen](34392.md) are included in the banknotes. Some security features inserted in won notes are:

- [Holograms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holograms) with three-dimensional images that change colors within the metallic foil on the obverse side of the notes (except ₩1,000)

- [Watermark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermark) portraits of the effigy of the note are visible when held to the light in the white section of the note.

- Intaglio printing on words and the effigy give off a raised feeling, different from ordinary paper

- [Security thread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_thread) in the right side of the obverse side of the note with small lettering "한국은행 Bank of Korea" and its corresponding denomination

- [Color-shifting ink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optically_variable_ink) on the value number at the back of the note:

For the first time in the world, [KOMSCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KOMSCO), the Korean mint, inserted a new substance in the notes to detect counterfeits. This technique is being exported, such as to Europe and North America.[^cite_note-33]

## Future

### Coinless trials

As the South Korean economy is evolving through the use of electronic payments, coins of the South Korean won are becoming less used by consumers. The Bank of Korea began a trial which would result in the total cessation of the production of coins by depositing change into prepaid cards.[^cite_note-36] As of 2019, however, public participation in this program has decreased.[^cite_note-37]

### Redenomination proposals

There have been recurring proposals in the [South Korean National Assembly](601423.md) to redenominate the won by introducing a new won or new unit, equal to 1,000 old won, and worth nearly one [U.S. dollar](18717338.md). While proponents cite a more valuable currency unit better projects the strength of the nation's economy, a majority remain opposed to the idea. Reasons cited are: economic harm if done immediately, no issues on public confidence in the won and its inflation rate, limited cost savings, and the presence of more urgent economic issues.[^cite_note-38]

## Currency production

The Bank of Korea is the only institution in South Korea with the right to print banknotes and mint coins. The banknotes and coins are printed at the KOMSCO, a government-owned corporation, under the guidance of the Bank of Korea. After the new banknotes and coins are [minted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mint_\(facility\)), they are bundled or rolled and shipped to the headquarters of the Bank of Korea. When delivered, they are deposited inside the bank's vault, ready to be distributed to commercial banks when requested. Every year, around [Seollal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seollal) and [Chuseok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuseok), two major Korean holidays, the Bank of Korea distributes large amounts of its currency to most of the commercial banks in South Korea, which are then given to their customers upon request.
