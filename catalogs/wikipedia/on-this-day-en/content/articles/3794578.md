**László Krasznahorkai**[^cite_note-1] (born 5 January 1954) is a Hungarian [writer](45353.md), [novelist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novelist) and [screenwriter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screenwriter). Krasznahorkai is known for his difficult and demanding novels, often labeled [postmodern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodern_literature), which explore [dystopian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dystopian) and melancholic themes.[^cite_note-2] Several of his works, including his novels *[Satantango](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satantango_\(novel\))* (1985) and *[The Melancholy of Resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Melancholy_of_Resistance)* (1989), have been adapted into feature films by the director [Béla Tarr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%A9la_Tarr).

In 2025, he was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature) "for his compelling and visionary oeuvre that, in the midst of apocalyptic terror, reaffirms the power of art".[^cite_note-bbc-3][^cite_note-4]

## Early life and education

László Krasznahorkai was born into a middle-class family on 5 January 1954 in [Gyula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyula,_Hungary), eastern Hungary.[^cite_note-UniWienKrasz-5][^cite_note-New_York_Times-6] His father, György Krasznahorkai, was a lawyer, and his mother, Júlia Pálinkás, was a social security administrator.[^cite_note-Krasz-CA-7][^cite_note-New_York_Times-6]

Krasznahorkai has stated in interviews he had Jewish heritage on his father's side,[^cite_note-fridpod-8][^cite_note-paristhirlwell-9] explaining that his father initially concealed their Jewish roots from him, revealing it only when he was around 11 years old.[^cite_note-paristhirlwell-9] He has also said his maternal ancestors include Hungarian [hajduks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hajduk#Kingdom_of_Hungary) from [Transylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transylvania).[^cite_note-fridpod-8][^cite_note-szombat1-10] His paternal grandfather changed the family name from Korim (or Korin) to Krasznahorkai in 1931.[^cite_note-fridpod-8][^cite_note-paristhirlwell-9] His older brother Géza is a former director of the Mogyoróssy János Municipal Library in Gyula.[^cite_note-bekesmarai-11]

From 1968 to 1972, he attended [Erkel Ferenc High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erkel_Ferenc_High_School?action=edit\&redlink=1) in Gyula, specializing in Latin.[^cite_note-diabio-12][^cite_note-13] During his teenage years, he performed as a pianist in various [jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz) and [beat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beat_music) ensembles.[^cite_note-dobszerda-14] After completing one year of compulsory military service,[^cite_note-paristhirlwell-9] he began studying law in 1973 at József Attila University (now the [University of Szeged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Szeged)),[^cite_note-diabio-12][^cite_note-sztenobel-15] but he suspended his studies after three weeks as he did not feel a sense of belonging to the legal profession.[^cite_note-dobszerda-14] Afterwards, he spent a period changing location every few months in an effort to avoid a second year of military duty. During this time, he took on various jobs as a stable boy, a cultural educator and a miner.[^cite_note-paristhirlwell-9][^cite_note-dobszerda-14] In 1976, he transferred to the [Faculty of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ELTE_Faculty_of_Law) of [Eötvös Loránd University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E%C3%B6tv%C3%B6s_Lor%C3%A1nd_University) in Budapest, where he continued his legal studies until 1978.[^cite_note-diabio-12]

His first published work, a short story titled "Tebenned hittem" ("I Believed in You"), appeared in *[Mozgó Világ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozg%C3%B3_Vil%C3%A1g?action=edit\&redlink=1)* in 1977.[^cite_note-mtanobel-16] From 1978, he studied Hungarian and cultural education at the [Faculty of Humanities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ELTE_Faculty_of_Humanities) of Eötvös Loránd University, graduating in 1983.[^cite_note-diabio-12][^cite_note-mtanobel-16] His [thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thesis) focused on the work of writer [Sándor Márai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A1ndor_M%C3%A1rai) following his emigration from Hungary.[^cite_note-diabio-12][^cite_note-17] During his studies, he worked at the [Gondolat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gondolat?action=edit\&redlink=1) publishing house as a documenter from 1977 to 1982.[^cite_note-UniWienKrasz-5][^cite_note-diabio-12]

## Career as writer

Since completing his studies, Krasznahorkai has worked as a freelance writer. His debut novel, *[Sátántangó](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satantango_\(novel\))* (1985), was an immediate success that established him as a leading figure in Hungarian literature.[^cite_note-everything-18] An English translation of the novel later received the [Best Translated Book Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Best_Translated_Book_Award) in 2013.[^cite_note-everything-18]

### International travel and influence

Krasznahorkai first travelled outside [Communist Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_People's_Republic) in 1987–1988,[^cite_note-everything-18] when he spent one year in [West Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Berlin) as a fellow of the [DAAD Artists-in-Berlin Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DAAD_Artists-in-Berlin_Program).[^cite_note-mtanobel-16][^cite_note-daadnobel-19] After the [fall of the Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Eastern_Bloc), he began living reclusively in various locations worldwide. His first extended trip to [East Asia](19605700.md) in 1990 profoundly influenced his work; his experiences in [Mongolia](19271.md) and [China](5405.md) informed the books *The Prisoner of Urga* and *[Destruction and Sorrow Beneath the Heavens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destruction_and_Sorrow_Beneath_the_Heavens)*.[^cite_note-review-20] He later spent six months in [Kyoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto) in 1996, 2000, and 2005, with the aesthetics and literary theory of the Far East causing significant shifts in his style and themes.[^cite_note-21] While writing *[War and War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_and_War)*, he traveled extensively across Europe and received assistance from the American poet [Allen Ginsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_Ginsberg), whom he credited with providing valuable advice while staying at his New York apartment.[^cite_note-22] Krasznahorkai was the Spring 2014 Harriman Writer in Residence at [Harriman Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harriman_Institute) of [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University) where he taught the seminar “Artistic Collaboration in East Central Europe,” centered on his work with famed Hungarian film director Béla Tarr (Satantango, Werckmeister Harmonies) and German painter Max Neumann (Animalinside).[^cite_note-23]

### Critical recognition and collaborations

His novel *[The Melancholy of Resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Melancholy_of_Resistance)* (1989) received the German [Bestenliste-Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preis_der_SWR-Bestenliste) for the best literary work of the year in 1993.[^cite_note-everything-18][^cite_note-visionary-24] In 1996, he was a fellow at the [Wissenschaftskolleg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Advanced_Study,_Berlin) in Berlin.[^cite_note-review-20]

Beginning with *[Sátántangó](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A1t%C3%A1ntang%C3%B3)* (1994), filmmaker [Béla Tarr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%A9la_Tarr), a close friend, adapted several of Krasznahorkai's works into films, including *[Werckmeister Harmonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werckmeister_Harmonies)* (2000) which adapts *The Melancholy of Resistance*.[^cite_note-review-20] Krasznahorkai stated that their 2011 film, *[The Turin Horse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Turin_Horse)*, would be their final collaboration.[^cite_note-25] He has also collaborated with artist Max Neumann on illustrated works such as the novella *[Chasing Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chasing_Homer)* (2021), which was released with an original score by jazz musician Szilveszter Miklós.[^cite_note-26]

His novel *[Seiobo There Below](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seiobo_There_Below)* won the [Best Translated Book Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Best_Translated_Book_Award) in 2014.[^cite_note-27] In 2015, he became the first Hungarian author to receive the [Man Booker International Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man_Booker_International_Prize).[^cite_note-visionary-24]

Krasznahorkai's work has received significant critical acclaim. [Susan Sontag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Sontag) described him as "the contemporary Hungarian master of apocalypse who inspires comparison with [Gogol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Gogol) and [Melville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Melville)",[^cite_note-everything-18] while [W. G. Sebald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._G._Sebald) wrote that "the universality of Krasznahorkai's vision rivals that of Gogol's *[Dead Souls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Souls)* and far surpasses all the lesser concerns of contemporary writing".[^cite_note-28]

In 2024, the Austrian National Library in [Vienna](55866.md) acquired Krasznahorkai’s literary archive (Vorlass), which is now preserved in its Literature Archives.[^cite_note-29]

## Views

### Russian invasion of Ukraine

Krasznahorkai expressed strong condemnation of the Hungarian government's policy on the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine). He described Prime Minister [Viktor Orbán](750582.md)'s framing of the conflict as an "internal Slavic affair" as morally indefensible and historically incoherent, given Hungary's own past invasions by Russia. Krasznahorkai argued that neutrality in the face of aggression amounts to complicity and likened the Orbán government's reasoning to a form of psychological denial. He characterized the regime as "a psychiatric case," motivated by a fatalistic and self-destructive logic that, in his words, accepts the death of one's own child to spare one's mother, only to result in the death of both.[^cite_note-30]

## Personal life

Krasznahorkai was married to his first wife, Anikó Pelyhe, from 1990 until their divorce. In 1997, he married Dóra Kopcsányi, a [sinologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinology) and graphic designer.[^cite_note-Krasz-CA-7] He has three daughters,[^cite_note-Krasz-CA-7] including Ágnes, an actress who starred in the 2023 film *[Without Air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Without_Air?action=edit\&redlink=1)*.[^cite_note-31]

After living for several years in [Berlin](3354.md), where he held the S. Fischer Guest Professorship at the [Free University of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_University_of_Berlin) for one semester, he returned to [Hungary](13275.md).[^cite_note-Krasz-CA-7][^cite_note-NDBooksAuthorPage-32]
