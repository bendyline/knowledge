The **Senate**, or by its former English translation name, the **House of Councillors**, ([Japanese](15606.md): 参議院, [Hepburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepburn_romanization): *Sangiin*; Japanese pronunciation: [\[saŋʲ.ɡʲiꜜ.iɴ, -ŋʲiꜜ-\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Japanese)[^cite_note-4]) is the [upper house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_house) of the [National Diet](163847.md) of [Japan](15573.md). The [House of Representatives](217778.md) is the [lower house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_house). The Senate is the successor to the pre-war [House of Peers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Peers_\(Japan\)). If the two houses disagree on matters of the budget, treaties, or the nomination of the prime minister, the House of Representatives can insist on its decision. In other decisions, the House of Representatives can override a vote of the Senate only by a two-thirds majority of senators present.

The Senate has 248 members who each serve six-year terms, two years longer than those of the House of Representatives. Senators must be at least 30 years old, compared with 25 years old in the House of Representatives. The Senate cannot be dissolved, and terms are [staggered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staggered_elections) so that only half of its membership is up for election every three years. Of the 124 senators subject to election each time, 74 are elected from [45 districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_districts_of_the_House_of_Councillors_of_Japan) by [single non-transferable vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_non-transferable_vote) (SNTV) and 50 are elected from a nationwide list by [proportional representation](23298.md) (PR) with [open lists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_list).[^cite_note-5]

The English translation of the institution's name was established in 1946.[^cite_note-yomiuri_rename-6] In 2026 the official translation is planned to be changed to 'Senate',[^cite_note-7] a name which already enjoyed some informal use before,[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11] to prevent international confusion, as the term "Councillor" is rarely used for national-level upper houses and more frequently associated with local government.[^cite_note-yomiuri_rename-6]

## Roles and responsibilities



Emperor [Akihito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akihito) and [Empress Michiko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empress_Michiko) seated in the Chamber of the Senate, with [Prince](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fumihito,_Crown_Prince_of_Japan) and [Princess Akishino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiko,_Crown_Princess_of_Japan), the cabinet, and Prime Minister [Naoto Kan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naoto_Kan) giving the government's speech in front of the assembled members of parliament at a ceremony commemorating the 120th anniversary of the founding of the National Diet. (2010)

The power of the Senate is very similar to the [Canadian Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_of_Canada) or the [Irish Seanad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seanad_%C3%89ireann).[^cite_note-tokyoreview-12] In central issues, there is a "supremacy of the House of Representatives" ([衆議院の優越](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%A1%86%E8%AD%B0%E9%99%A2%E3%81%AE%E5%84%AA%E8%B6%8A), *Shūgiin no yūetsu*). In the [election of the prime minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_of_the_Prime_Minister_of_Japan), the ratification of international treaties, and on passing the budget, a decision by the House of Representatives always overrides dissent from the Senate. Only the lower house can pass votes of no-confidence against the cabinet. All other legislation requires either the approval by majorities in both houses, an agreement in the conference committee of both houses or an additional override vote by two-thirds majority in the House of Representatives.[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14] (For context, no single party achieved a two-thirds majority in the House of Representatives until the [Liberal Democratic Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Democratic_Party_of_Japan) did in the [February 2026 election](77996830.md).) Usually, controlling a majority in the Senate and one third of the House of Representatives is enough for a united opposition to be able to block the passage of legislation. For certain important administrative nominations by the cabinet, the approval of both houses is required (although the laws containing this requirement could be changed by two-thirds lower house override as a "nuclear option"); and constitutional amendment proposals need two-thirds majorities in both houses of the Diet to be submitted to the people in a national [referendum](51582.md).[^cite_note-tokyoreview-12]

One additional constitutional role of the Senate is to serve as functioning fully elected emergency legislature on its own during lower house election campaigns: While the House of Representatives is dissolved, the National Diet can not be convened, and therefore no law can be passed in regular procedure; but in urgent cases requiring parliamentary action (e.g. election management, provisional budgets, disaster response), an emergency session (緊急集会, *kinkyū shūkai*) of the Senate can still be invoked to take provisional decisions for the whole Diet. Such decisions will become invalid unless confirmed by the House of Representatives as soon as the whole Diet convenes again.

The basic stipulations on the role of the Senate are subject of chapter IV of the [constitution](62699.md).[^cite_note-15] Laws and rules containing more detailed provisions on parliamentary procedures and the relations between the two houses include the National Diet Law (国会法, *Kokkai-hō*),[^cite_note-16] the conference committee regulations (両院協議会規程, *ryōin-kyōgikai kitei*),[^cite_note-17] and the rules of each house (衆議院/参議院規則, *Shūgiin/Sangiin kisoku*).[^cite_note-18]

### Constitutional practice

In practice, governments often tried to ensure legislative majorities, either by forming [coalition governments](6036.md) with safe legislative majorities in the first place or by negotiating with part of the opposition, or avoided to submit bills with no prospects of passage,[^cite_note-19] so the Senate rarely voted against the decisions reached by the lower house for much of postwar history: As the [Liberal Democratic Party](164231.md) (LDP), founded in 1955, often held majorities in both houses or was sufficiently close to control both houses together with independents and micro-parties for a long period, inter-chamber disagreement was rare during most of the [1955 System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1955_System).

After the opposition victory in the 1989 election, the relative importance of the Senate initially increased, as the LDP continued to govern alone and did not hold a two-thirds majority in the House of Representatives. Crucial legislation had to be negotiated with parts of the opposition. The most prominent example was the so-called "PKO Diet" ([PKO国会](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/PKO%E5%9B%BD%E4%BC%9A), *PKO Kokkai*) of 1992 when the LDP negotiated and passed the peace-keeping operations bill with centre-left/right-of-JSP opposition parties ([DSP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Socialist_Party_\(Japan\)) and [Kōmeitō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%8Dmeit%C5%8D)) against fierce opposition from [JSP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Socialist_Party) and [JCP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Communist_Party); the Peace-Keeping Operations law (PKO law) became the base for the Self-Defense Forces' first ground deployment abroad as part of the [UN mission in Cambodia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Transitional_Authority_in_Cambodia). After the 1993 House of Representatives election, with the exception of a [brief minority government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hata_Cabinet) in 1994, coalition governments or the [confidence and supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_and_supply) arrangement during the [restored LDP single-party government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Hashimoto_Cabinet) ensured legislative government majorities until the opposition victory in the 1998 Senate election which led to the formation of another coalition government by 1999.

The legislative two-thirds override power of the House of Representatives was never used between 1950s and 2008 when the LDP-Kōmeitō coalition government had lost the Senate majority in the 2007 election, but did control a two-thirds majority in the House of Representatives since 2005. After that, it has been used somewhat more frequently (see [衆議院の再議決](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%A1%86%E8%AD%B0%E9%99%A2%E3%81%AE%E5%86%8D%E8%AD%B0%E6%B1%BA), *Shūgin no saikaketsu*, "Override decisions by the House of Representatives" for a list). If a government controls a two-thirds majority in the House of Representatives and is willing to use it, the Senate can only delay a bill, but not prevent passage.

Opposition control of the Senate is often summarized by the term *nejire Kokkai* ([ねじれ国会](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%81%AD%E3%81%98%E3%82%8C%E5%9B%BD%E4%BC%9A), "twisted" or "skewed" Diet). Setting aside the immediate postwar years, when many governments were in the minority in the upper house, but the strongest force, the centrist [Ryokufūkai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryokuf%C5%ABkai_\(1947%E2%80%931960\)), was not in all-out opposition to either centre-left or centre-right governments and willing to cooperate, the Diet was "twisted" from 1989 to 1993, 1998–1999, 2007–2009, and most recently 2010–2013.

### "Gridlock" and reform proposals

In recent years, many constitutional revision advocates call for reforming the role of the Senate ("carbon copy" of the House of Representatives or "recalcitrant naysayer") or abolishing it altogether to "prevent political paralysis", after the recently more frequent twisted Diets have seen an increase in inter-chamber friction/"political nightmare"s.[^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21] Examples of high-stakes, internationally noted conflicts in recent twisted Diets:

- In 2008, two nominees for [BoJ](188155.md) governor by the [Fukuda Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasuo_Fukuda_Cabinet) ([Toshirō Mutō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toshir%C5%8D_Mut%C5%8D), [Kōji Tanami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%8Dji_Tanami)) were rejected by the DPJ-led opposition in the Senate, and the [SDF naval support mission for NATO/OEF in the Indian Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JSDF_Overseas_Dispatches) had to be interrupted for one month while the extension of the anti-terrorism law was delayed by the extended legislative proceedings necessary to override the Senate rejection.

- In 2011, the [Kan Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kan_Cabinet) struggled to pass a renewable energy bill and a bond ceiling increase (unlike the budget itself subject to the normal legislative procedure) against the LDP-led opposition majority in the Senate until it negotiated a deal with the LDP in exchange for child allowance reform and the cabinet's resignation which Kan had already announced, but conditioned on the passage of the bills.

## Membership and elections

Article 102 of the [Japanese Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Constitution) provided that half of the councillors elected in the first Senate election in [1947](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1947_Japanese_House_of_Councillors_election) would be up for re-election [three years later](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1950_Japanese_House_of_Councillors_election) in order to introduce staggered six-year terms.

The House initially had 250 seats. Two seats were added to the House in 1970 after the agreement on the repatriation of [Okinawa](22477.md), increasing the House to a total of 252.[^cite_note-history-24] Legislation aimed at addressing [malapportionment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malapportionment) that favoured less populated prefectures was introduced in 2000; this resulted in ten seats being removed (five each at the 2001 and 2004 elections), bringing the total number of seats to 242.[^cite_note-history-24] Further reforms to address malapportionment took effect in 2007 and 2016, but did not change the total number of members in the house.[^cite_note-history-24]

From 1947 to 1983, the House had 100 seats allocated to a [nationwide district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Councillors_national_district), of which fifty seats were allocated in each election, elected by [single non-transferable vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_non-transferable_vote).[^cite_note-history-24] It was originally intended to give nationally prominent figures a route to the House without going through local electioneering processes. Some national political figures, such as feminists [Shidzue Katō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shidzue_Kat%C5%8D) and [Fusae Ichikawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fusae_Ichikawa) and former Imperial Army general [Kazushige Ugaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazushige_Ugaki), were elected through the nationwide district, along with a number of celebrities such as comedian [Yukio Aoshima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukio_Aoshima) (later Governor of Tokyo), journalist [Hideo Den](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hideo_Den) and actress [Yūko Mochizuki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y%C5%ABko_Mochizuki). [Shintaro Ishihara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shintaro_Ishihara) won a record 3 million votes in the nationwide district in the [1968 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Japanese_House_of_Councillors_election). The last 50-seat SNTV election took place in [1980](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_Japanese_House_of_Councillors_election). For the [1983 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1983_Japanese_House_of_Councillors_election) the electoral system was changed to closed-list proportional representation; to avoid ambiguity, the nationwide district was now called the [proportional district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_House_of_Councillors_national_proportional_representation_block).[^cite_note-history-24] The national proportional representation district was reduced to 96 senators in the 2000 reforms.[^cite_note-history-24]
