**Yosemite National Park** ([/joʊˈsɛmɪti/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*yoh-SEM-ih-tee*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key)[^cite_note-5]) is a [national park of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_national_parks_of_the_United_States) in [California](5407.md).[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-VisitCal-7] It is bordered on the southeast by the [Sierra National Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_National_Forest) and on the northwest by [Stanislaus National Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanislaus_National_Forest). The park is managed by the [National Park Service](161535.md) and covers 1,187 mi<sup>2</sup> (3,070 km<sup>2</sup>)[^cite_note-statistics-3] in four [counties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County) – centered in [Tuolumne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuolumne_County,_California) and [Mariposa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariposa_County,_California), extending north and east to [Mono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mono_County,_California) and south to [Madera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madera_County,_California). Designated a [World Heritage Site](44940.md) in 1984, Yosemite is internationally recognized for its granite cliffs, waterfalls, clear streams, groves of [giant sequoia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequoiadendron_giganteum), lakes, mountains, meadows, glaciers, and [biological diversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity).[^cite_note-naturehistory-8] Almost 95 percent of the park is designated [wilderness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Wilderness_Preservation_System).[^cite_note-9] Yosemite is one of the largest and least fragmented habitat blocks in the [Sierra Nevada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada) mountain range.

Its [geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology_of_the_Yosemite_area) is characterized by [granite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granite) and remnants of older rock. About 10 million years ago, the [Sierra Nevada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada) was [uplifted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_formation) and tilted to form its unique slopes, which increased the steepness of stream and river beds, forming deep, narrow canyons. About one million years ago, [glaciers](12463.md) formed at higher elevations. They moved downslope, cutting and sculpting the U-shaped [Yosemite Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yosemite_Valley).[^cite_note-naturehistory-8]

Humans may have first entered the area 10,000 to 8,000 years ago, with [Native Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States) having inhabited the region for nearly 4,000 years.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-\:1-11] European Americans entered the area by 1833. The best documented first entrance of European Americans into [Yosemite Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yosemite_Valley) occurred when the Mariposa Battalion, commissioned by [Governor McDougal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_McDougal) and led by [James D. Savage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Savage), attacked the Ahwahneechee there in 1851.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13][^cite_note-\:7-14][^cite_note-15]

Yosemite was critical to the development of the concept of national parks. [Galen Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galen_Clark) and others lobbied to protect Yosemite Valley from development, ultimately leading to President [Abraham Lincoln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln)'s signing of the Yosemite Grant of 1864 that declared Yosemite as federally preserved land.[^cite_note-historyculture-16] In 1890, environmentalist [John Muir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Muir) led a successful movement to motivate [Congress](31756.md) to establish Yosemite Valley and its surrounding areas as a National Park. This helped pave the way for the [National Park System](161535.md#National_Park_System).[^cite_note-historyculture-16] Yosemite draws about four million visitors annually.[^cite_note-visits-17] Most visitors spend the majority of their time in the valley's seven square miles (18 km<sup>2</sup>).[^cite_note-naturehistory-8] The park set a visitation record in 2016, surpassing five million visitors for the first time.[^cite_note-18] In 2024, the park received over four million visitors.[^cite_note-19]

## Toponym

The word *Yosemite* (derived from *yohhe'meti*, 'they are killers' in [Miwok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miwok_languages)) historically referred to the name that the [Miwok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miwok) gave to the [Ahwahneechee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahwahneechee), the resident indigenous tribe.[^cite_note-Anderson-20][^cite_note-21][^cite_note-\:0-22] Previously, the region had been called *Ahwahnee* ('big mouth') by the Ahwahneechee.[^cite_note-Anderson-20] The term *Yosemite* in Miwok is easily confused with a similar term for '[grizzly bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grizzly_bear)', and is still a common misconception.[^cite_note-Anderson-20][^cite_note-Beeler-23]

## History

Main article: [History of the Yosemite area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Yosemite_area)

### Ahwahneechee and the Mariposa Wars



[Paiute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Paiute_people) ceremony (1872)

engraving of Dr Lafayette Bunnell, showing him as an older man with a craggy face, short bristly hair, and a cropped grey beard.

[Lafayette Bunnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lafayette_Bunnell) gave Yosemite Valley its name.

The first humans of Yosemite called themselves the [Ahwahneechee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahwahnechee), meaning "dwellers" in [Ahwahnee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahwahnee).[^cite_note-24] The Ahwahneechee People were the only tribe that lived within the park boundaries, but other tribes lived in surrounding areas. Together they formed a larger Indigenous population in California, called the [Southern Sierra Miwok.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plains_and_Sierra_Miwok)[^cite_note-Spence-25] They are related to the Northern [Paiute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Paiute) and [Mono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mono_people) tribes. Other tribes like the Central [Sierra Miwoks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Miwoks) and the [Yokuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yokuts), who both lived in the [San Joaquin Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Joaquin_Valley) and central California, visited Yosemite to trade and intermarry.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreene198778-26] This resulted in a blending of culture that helped preserve their presence in Yosemite after early American settlements and urban development threatened their survival.[^cite_note-Spence-25] Vegetation and game in the region were similar to modern times; acorns were a dietary staple, as well as other seeds and plants, salmon, and deer.[^cite_note-Spence-25]

The 1848–1855 [California Gold Rush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Gold_Rush) was a major event impacting the native population. It drew more than 90,000 European Americans to the area in 1849, causing competition for resources between gold miners and residents.[^cite_note-27] About 70 years before the Gold Rush, the indigenous population was estimated to be 300,000, quickly dropping to 150,000, and just ten years later, only about 50,000 remained.[^cite_note-\:1-11] The reasons for such a decline included disease, birth rate decreases, starvation, and conflict. The conflict in Yosemite, which is known as the [Mariposa War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariposa_War), was part of the [California genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_genocide), which was the systemic killing of indigenous peoples throughout the State between the 1840s and 1870s.[^cite_note-Adhikari-28] It started in December 1850 when California funded a state militia to drive Native people from contested territory to suppress Native American resistance to the European American influx.[^cite_note-miwuk-29]

Yosemite tribes often stole from settlers and miners, sometimes killing them, in retribution for the extermination/domestication of their people, and loss of their lands and resources.[^cite_note-\:1-11] The War and formation of the [Mariposa Battalion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariposa_Battalion) was partially the result of a single incident involving [James Savage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Savage), a [Fresno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresno) trader whose trading post was attacked in December 1850. After the incident, Savage rallied other miners and gained the support of local officials to pursue a war against the Natives. He was appointed [United States Army](32087.md) Major and leader of the Mariposa Battalion at the beginning of 1851.[^cite_note-\:1-11] He and Captain John Boling were responsible for pursuing the Ahwahneechee people, led by [Chief Tenaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Tenaya) and driving them west, and out of Yosemite.[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-\:1-11] In March 1851 under Savage's command, the Mariposa Battalion captured about 70 Ahwahneechee and planned to take them to a reservation in Fresno, but they escaped. Later in May, under the command of Boling, the battalion captured 35 Ahwahneechee, including Chief Tenaya, and marched them to the reservation. Most were allowed to eventually leave, and the rest escaped.[^cite_note-\:1-11] Tenaya and others fled across the [Sierra Nevada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada) and settled with the [Mono Lake Paiutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kucadikadi). Tenaya and some of his companions were ultimately killed in 1853, either over stealing horses or a gambling conflict. The survivors of Tenaya's group and other [Ahwahneechee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahwahnechee) were absorbed into the Mono Lake Paiute tribe.[^cite_note-\:1-11][^cite_note-\:2-31][^cite_note-\:3-32]



Sculpture of Chief Tenaya made by Sal Maccarone for the Tenaya Lodge in Yosemite National Park

Accounts from this battalion were the first well-documented reports of European Americans entering Yosemite Valley. Attached to Savage's unit was Doctor [Lafayette Bunnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lafayette_Bunnell), who later wrote about his awestruck impressions of the valley in *The Discovery of the Yosemite*. Bunnell is credited with naming Yosemite Valley, based on his interviews with Chief Tenaya. Bunnell wrote that Chief Tenaya was the founder of the Ahwahnee colony.[^cite_note-Bunnell17-33] Bunnell falsely believed that the word "Yosemite" meant "full-grown grizzly bear".[^cite_note-\:7-14]

### Indigenous peoples' continuing presence



Basket woven by [Lucy Telles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucy_Telles) (1885–1955), a [Mono Lake Paiute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kucadikadi) and [Southern Sierra Miwok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miwok#Southern_Sierra_Miwok) Native American artist from the Yosemite region

After the [Mariposa War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariposa_War), Native Americans continued to live in the Yosemite area in reduced numbers. The remaining Yosemite [Ahwahneechee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahwahneechee) tribe members there were forced to relocate to a village constructed in 1851 by the state government.[^cite_note-\:1-11] They learned to live within this camp and their limited rights, adapting to the changed environment by entering the tourism industry through employment and small businesses, manufacturing and selling goods, and providing services.[^cite_note-Spence-25] By the late 19th century, the population of all native inhabitants in Yosemite was difficult to determine; estimates ranged from thirty to several hundred. The Ahwahneechee people and their descendants were hard to identify.[^cite_note-\:1-11] The last full-blooded Ahwahneechee died in 1931. Her name was Totuya, or Maria Lebrado. She was the granddaughter of Chief [Tenaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenaya) and one of many forced out of her ancestral homelands.[^cite_note-\:1-11][^cite_note-miwuk-29] The Ahwahneechee live through the memory of their descendants, their fellow Yosemite Natives, and through the Yosemite exhibit in the [Smithsonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smithsonian_Museum_of_American_History) and the Yosemite Museum.[^cite_note-\:1-11] As a method of self-preservation and resilience, the Indigenous people of California proposed treaties in 1851 and 1852 that would have established land reservations for them, but Congress refused to ratify them.[^cite_note-\:1-11] The Southern Sierra Miwuk Nation is still seeking tribal sovereignty and federal recognition.[^cite_note-miwuk-29][^cite_note-\:4-34]

In 1953, the National Park Service banned all non-employee Natives from residing in the Park and evicted the non-employees who had residence. In 1969, with only a few families left in the Park, the National Park Service evicted the remaining Native people living within the Park (all Park employees and their families) to a government housing area for park employees and destroyed the village as part of a fire-fighting exercise.[^cite_note-SpenceBookChapter8-35][^cite_note-miwuk-29] A reconstructed "Indian Village of Ahwahnee" sits behind the [Yosemite Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yosemite_Museum), located next to the Yosemite Valley Visitor Center.[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38] The National Park Service has created policies to protect sacred sites and allow Native People to return to their homelands and use National Park resources.[^cite_note-\:4-34][^cite_note-39]

In 1855, entrepreneur [James Mason Hutchings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Mason_Hutchings), artist [Thomas Ayres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Ayres_\(artist\)), and two others were the first tourists to visit.[^cite_note-GeologyNP326-40] Hutchings and Ayres were responsible for much of Yosemite's earliest publicity, writing articles and [special issues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutchings'_Illustrated_California_Magazine) about the valley.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWuerthner199420-41] Ayres' style was detailed with exaggerated angularity. His works and written accounts were distributed nationally, and an exhibition of his drawings was held in New York City. Hutchings' publicity efforts between 1855 and 1860 increased tourism to Yosemite.[^cite_note-42] Natives supported the growing tourism industry by working as laborers or maids. Later, they performed dances for tourists, acted as guides, and sold handcrafted goods, notably woven baskets.[^cite_note-Spence-25] The Indian village and its peoples fascinated visitors, especially James Hutchings, who advocated for Yosemite tourism. He and others considered the indigenous presence to be one of Yosemite's greatest attractions.[^cite_note-Spence-25]

[Wawona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wawona,_California) was an early Indian encampment for Nuchu and Ahwahneechee people who were captured and relocated to a reservation on the Fresno River by Savage and the Mariposa Battalion in March 1851.[^cite_note-43] [Galen Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galen_Clark) discovered the [Mariposa Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariposa_Grove) of [giant sequoia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_sequoia) in [Wawona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wawona,_California) in 1857. He had simple lodgings and roads built. In 1879, the [Wawona Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wawona_Hotel) was built to serve tourists visiting Mariposa Grove.[^cite_note-44] As tourism increased, so did the number of trails and hotels to build on it.[^cite_note-45]

The [Wawona Tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wawona_Tree), also known as the [Tunnel Tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunnel_tree), was a [giant sequoia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequoiadendron_giganteum) that grew in the [Mariposa Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariposa_Grove). It was 234 feet (71 m) tall, and was 90 ft (27 m) in circumference. When a carriage-wide tunnel was cut through the tree in 1881, it became even more popular as a tourist photo attraction. Carriages and automobiles traversed the road that passed through the tree. The tree was permanently weakened by the tunnel, and it fell in 1969 under a heavy load of snow. It was estimated to have been 2,100 years old.[^cite_note-46]

Yosemite's first concession was established in 1884 when John Degnan and his wife established a bakery and store.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENPS198958-47] In 1916, the National Park Service granted a 20-year concession to the Desmond Park Service Company. It bought out or built hotels, stores, camps, a dairy, a garage, and other park facilities.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreene1987360-48] The [Hotel Del Portal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotel_Del_Portal) was completed in 1908 by a subsidiary of the [Yosemite Valley Railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yosemite_Valley_Railroad). It was located at [El Portal, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Portal,_California) just outside of Yosemite.[^cite_note-49][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreene1987362,_364-50]

The Curry Company started in 1899, led by David and Jennie Curry, to provide concessions. They founded Camp Curry, now [Curry Village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curry_Village,_California).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWuerthner199440-51]

Park service administrators felt that limiting the number of concessionaires in the park would be more financially sound. The Curry Company and its rival, the Yosemite National Park Company, were forced to merge in 1925 to form the [Yosemite Park & Curry Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yosemite_Park_and_Curry_Company) (YP\&CC).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreene1987387-52] The company built the [Ahwahnee Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahwahnee_Hotel) in 1926–27.[^cite_note-53]

### Yosemite Grant





[Eadweard Muybridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eadweard_Muybridge), [Vernal Fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernal_Fall), 1872; [Willard Worden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willard_Worden), [Yosemite Falls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yosemite_Falls), c. 1910.

Concerned by the impact of commercial interests, citizens, including [Galen Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galen_Clark) and Senator [John Conness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Conness), advocated protection for the area.[^cite_note-54] The [38th United States Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/38th_United_States_Congress) passed legislation that was signed by [President](24113.md) [Abraham Lincoln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln) on June 30, 1864, creating the Yosemite Grant.[^cite_note-Schaffer48-55][^cite_note-56] This is the first time land was set aside specifically for preservation and public use by the U.S. government, and set a precedent for the 1872 creation of [Yellowstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellowstone_National_Park) national park, the nation's first.[^cite_note-historyculture-16] According to Rolf Diamant and Ethan Carr, "the Yosemite Grant was a direct consequence of the [war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War) ... an embodiment of the ongoing process of remaking government ... an intentional assertion of a steadfast belief in the eventual [Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_\(American_Civil_War\)) victory."[^cite_note-57] Yosemite Valley and the [Mariposa Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariposa_Grove) were ceded to [California](5407.md) as a [state park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_park), and a board of commissioners was established two years later.[^cite_note-58]

Galen Clark was appointed by the commission as Grant's first guardian, but neither Clark nor the commissioners had the authority to evict [homesteaders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestead_Act) (which included Hutchings).[^cite_note-Schaffer48-55] The issue was not settled until 1872, when the homesteader land holdings were invalidated by the U.S. Supreme Court.[^cite_note-59] Clark and the commissioners were ousted in a dispute that reached the [Supreme Court](31737.md) in 1880.[^cite_note-60] The two Supreme Court decisions affecting management of the Yosemite Grant are considered precedents in land management law.[^cite_note-61] Hutchings became the new park guardian.[^cite_note-Schaffer49-62]

Tourist access to the park improved, and conditions in the Valley became more hospitable. Tourism significantly increased after the [first transcontinental railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_transcontinental_railroad) was completed in 1869, while the long horseback ride to reach the area was a deterrent.[^cite_note-Schaffer48-55] Three [stagecoach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagecoach) roads were built in the mid-1870s to provide better access for the growing number of visitors.[^cite_note-63]

[John Muir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Muir) was a Scottish-born American naturalist and explorer. Muir's leadership ensured that many National Parks were left untouched, including Yosemite.[^cite_note-64]

Muir wrote articles popularizing the area and increasing scientific interest in it. Muir was one of the first to theorize that the major landforms in Yosemite Valley were created by alpine glaciers, bucking established scientists such as [Josiah Whitney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josiah_Whitney).[^cite_note-Schaffer49-62] Muir wrote scientific papers on the area's biology. Landscape architect [Frederick Law Olmsted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Law_Olmsted) emphasized the importance of conservation of Yosemite Valley.[^cite_note-65]

### Increased protection efforts



Early settler, [Galen Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galen_Clark)



[Theodore Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Roosevelt) and [John Muir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Muir) on Glacier Point

[Overgrazing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overgrazing) of meadows (especially by sheep), logging of giant sequoia, and other damage led Muir to become an advocate for further protection. Muir convinced prominent guests of the importance of putting the area under federal protection. One such guest was [Robert Underwood Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Underwood_Johnson), editor of *[Century Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Century_Magazine)*. Muir and Johnson lobbied Congress for the Act that created Yosemite National Park on October 1, 1890.[^cite_note-Schaffer50-66] The State of California, however, retained control of Yosemite Valley and [Mariposa Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariposa_Grove). Muir's writings raised awareness about the damage caused by sheep grazing, and he actively campaigned to virtually eliminate grazing from Yosemite's high country.[^cite_note-67]

The newly created national park came under the jurisdiction of the United States Army's Troop I of the [4th Cavalry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4th_Cavalry_Regiment_\(United_States\)) on May 19, 1891, which set up camp in Wawona with Captain [Abram Epperson Wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abram_Wood) as acting superintendent.[^cite_note-Schaffer50-66] By the late 1890s, sheep grazing was no longer a problem, and the Army made other improvements. However, the cavalry could not intervene to ease the worsening conditions. From 1899 to 1913, cavalry regiments of the Western Department, including the all Black [9th Cavalry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/9th_Cavalry_Regiment_\(United_States\)) (known as the "Buffalo Soldiers") and the [1st Cavalry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1st_Cavalry_Regiment_\(United_States\)), stationed two troops at Yosemite.



[Bridalveil Fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridalveil_Fall) and [El Capitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Capitan), by [Carleton Watkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carleton_Watkins) (c. 1880)

Muir and his [Sierra Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Club) continued to lobby the government and influential people for the creation of a unified Yosemite National Park. In May 1903, President [Theodore Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Roosevelt) camped with Muir near [Glacier Point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glacier_Point) for three days. On that trip, Muir convinced Roosevelt to take control of Yosemite Valley and Mariposa Grove away from California and return it to the federal government. In 1906, Roosevelt signed a bill that shifted control.[^cite_note-68]

### National Park Service

The [National Park Service](161535.md) (NPS) was formed in 1916, and Yosemite was transferred to that agency's jurisdiction. Tuolumne Meadows Lodge, [Tioga Pass Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tioga_Pass_Road), and campgrounds at Tenaya and Merced lakes were also completed in 1916.[^cite_note-Schaffer52-69] Automobiles started to enter the park in ever-increasing numbers following the opening of all-weather highways to the park. The Yosemite Museum was founded in 1926 through the efforts of [Ansel Franklin Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ansel_Franklin_Hall).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENPS1989117-70] In the 1920s, the museum featured Native Americans practicing traditional crafts, and many Southern Sierra Miwok continued to live in Yosemite Valley until they were evicted from the park in the 1960s.[^cite_note-71] Although the NPS helped create a museum that included Native American culture, its early actions and organizational values were dismissive of Yosemite Natives and the Ahwahneechee.[^cite_note-\:1-11] NPS in the early 20th century criticized and restricted the expression of indigenous culture and behavior. For example, park officials penalized Natives for playing games and drinking during the Indian Field Days of 1924.[^cite_note-Spence-25] In 1929, Park Superintendent Charles G. Thomson concluded that the Indian village was aesthetically unpleasant and was limiting white settler development and ordered the camp to be burned down.[^cite_note-\:1-11] In 1969, many Native residents left in search of work as a result of the decline in tourism. NPS demolished their empty houses, evicted the remaining residents, and destroyed the entire village.[^cite_note-\:1-11] This was the last Indigenous settlement within the park.[^cite_note-\:1-11][^cite_note-miwuk-29]

In 1903, a dam in [Hetch Hetchy Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hetch_Hetchy_Valley) in the northwestern region of the park was proposed. Its purpose was to provide water and [hydroelectric power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroelectric_power) to [San Francisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco,_California). Muir and the Sierra Club opposed the project, while others, including [Gifford Pinchot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gifford_Pinchot), supported it.[^cite_note-72] In 1913, the [O'Shaughnessy Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O'Shaughnessy_Dam_\(California\)) was approved via passage of the [Raker Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raker_Act).[^cite_note-Schaffer51-73]

In 1918, [Clare Marie Hodges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clare_Marie_Hodges) was hired as the first female Park Ranger in Yosemite.[^cite_note-\:6-74] Following Hodges in 1921, [Enid Michael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enid_Michael) was hired as a seasonal Park Ranger[^cite_note-\:6-74] and continued to serve in that position for 20 years.[^cite_note-\:6-74]



[O'Shaughnessy Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O'Shaughnessy_Dam_\(California\)) in [Hetch Hetchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hetch_Hetchy) Valley

In 1937, conservationist [Rosalie Edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosalie_Edge), head of the Emergency Conservation Committee (ECC), successfully lobbied Congress to purchase about 8,000 acres (3,200 ha) of old-growth sugar pines on the perimeter of Yosemite National Park that were to be logged.[^cite_note-75]

By 1968, [traffic congestion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_congestion) and parking in Yosemite Valley during the summer months had become a concern.[^cite_note-76] NPS reduced artificial inducements to visit the park, such as the *[Firefall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yosemite_Firefall)*, in which red-hot embers were pushed off a cliff near Glacier Point at night.[^cite_note-77]

In 1984, preservationists persuaded Congress to designate 677,600 acres (274,200 ha), or about 89 percent of the park, as the Yosemite Wilderness. As a [wilderness area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilderness_area), it would be preserved in its natural state with humans being only temporary visitors.[^cite_note-78]

In 2025, laid-off National Park Service workers displayed a giant [upside down American flag](11447.md#Flying_a_U.S._flag_upside_down) as a "distress signal" at El Capitan to protest layoffs recently made by the [Trump administration](78298108.md).[^cite_note-79]

## Geography

Yosemite National Park is located in the central Sierra Nevada. Three wilderness areas are adjacent to Yosemite: the [Ansel Adams Wilderness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ansel_Adams_Wilderness) to the southeast, the [Hoover Wilderness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoover_Wilderness) to the northeast, and the [Emigrant Wilderness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emigrant_Wilderness) to the north.

The 1,189 mi<sup>2</sup> (3,080 km<sup>2</sup>) park contains thousands of lakes and ponds, 1,600 miles (2,600 km) of streams, 800 miles (1,300 km) of hiking trails, and 350 miles (560 km) of roads.[^cite_note-nature-80] Two federally designated [Wild and Scenic Rivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_and_Scenic_River), the [Merced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merced_River) and the [Tuolumne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuolumne_River), begin within Yosemite's borders and flow westward through the Sierra foothills into the [Central Valley of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Central_Valley).

### Rocks and erosion



[El Capitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Capitan), a granite monolith on Yosemite Valley's northern escarpment

Almost all of the landforms are cut from the granitic rock of the [Sierra Nevada Batholith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_Batholith) (a [batholith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batholith) is a large mass of intrusive [igneous rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igneous_rock) that formed deep below the surface).[^cite_note-GeologyNP329-81] About five percent of the park's landforms (mostly in its eastern margin near [Mount Dana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Dana)) are [metamorphosed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamorphic_rock) [volcanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_rock) and [sedimentary rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary_rock).[^cite_note-landforms-82] These [roof pendants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roof_pendants) once formed the roof over the underlying granitic magma.[^cite_note-83]

[Erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erosion) acting upon different types of uplift-generated joint and fracture systems is responsible for producing the valleys, canyons, [domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dome_\(geology\)), and other features. These joints and fracture systems do not move, and are therefore not [faults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_\(geology\)).[^cite_note-GeologyNP331-84] Spacing between joints is controlled by the amount of [silica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silica) in the granite and [granodiorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granodiorite) rocks; more silica tends to form a more resistant rock, resulting in larger spaces between joints and fractures.[^cite_note-GeologyUSP220-85]

Pillars and columns, such as [Washington Column](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Column_\(Yosemite\)) and [Lost Arrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Arrow_Spire), are generated by cross joints. Erosion acting on master joints is responsible for shaping valleys and later canyons.[^cite_note-GeologyUSP220-85] The single most erosive force over the last few million years has been large alpine [glaciers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaciers), which turned the previously V-shaped river-cut valleys into U-shaped glacial-cut canyons (such as Yosemite Valley and Hetch Hetchy Valley). [Exfoliation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exfoliation_\(geology\)) (caused by the tendency of [crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal) in [plutonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluton) rocks to expand at the surface) acting on granitic rock with widely spaced joints is responsible for producing domes such as [Half Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_Dome) and [North Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Dome) and inset arches like Royal Arches.[^cite_note-GeologyNP332-86]

### Popular features



[Cathedral Peak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_Peak_\(California\))

Yosemite Valley represents only one percent of the park area. The [Tunnel View](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunnel_View) gives a view of the valley. [El Capitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Capitan) is a prominent granite cliff that looms over the valley, and is a rock climbing favorite because of its sheer size, diverse climbing routes, and year-round accessibility. The park contains dozens of [granite domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granite_Domes_of_Yosemite_National_Park), including [Sentinel Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentinel_Dome) and [Half Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_Dome) which rise 3,000 and 4,800 feet (910 and 1,460 m), respectively, above the valley floor.[^cite_note-87] [Taft Point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taft_Point) provides notable views of the valley floor, 3,500 feet (1,100 m) below.[^cite_note-88]

The high country of Yosemite contains other important features such as [Tuolumne Meadows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuolumne_Meadows), [Dana Meadows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dana_Meadows_\(California\)), the [Clark Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clark_Range_\(California\)), the [Cathedral Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_Range), and the [Kuna Crest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuna_Crest). The [Sierra Crest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Crest) and the [Pacific Crest Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Crest_Trail) run through Yosemite. Mount Dana and [Mount Gibbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Gibbs) are peaks of red metamorphic rock. Granite peaks include [Mount Conness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Conness), [Cathedral Peak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_Peak), and [Matterhorn Peak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matterhorn_Peak). [Mount Lyell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Lyell_\(California\)) is the highest point in the park, standing at 13,120 feet (4,000 m). The [Lyell Glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyell_Glacier) is the largest glacier in the park and one of the few remaining in the Sierra.[^cite_note-89]

The park has three groves of ancient giant sequoia (*[Sequoiadendron giganteum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequoiadendron_giganteum)*) trees; the [Mariposa Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariposa_Grove) (200 trees), the [Tuolumne Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuolumne_Grove) (25 trees), and the [Merced Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merced_Grove) (20 trees).[^cite_note-GeologyNP340-90] This species grows larger in volume than any other and is one of the tallest and longest-lived.[^cite_note-Kiver227-91]

### Water and ice



The [Merced River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merced_River) flowing through [Yosemite Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yosemite_Valley), a [U-shaped valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-shaped_valley)

The [Tuolumne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuolumne_County,_California) and [Merced River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merced_River) systems originate along the crest of the Sierra in the park and have carved river canyons 3,000 to 4,000 feet (910 to 1,220 m) deep. The Tuolumne River drains the entire northern portion of the park, an area of approximately 680 square miles (1,800 km<sup>2</sup>). The Merced River begins in the park's southern peaks, primarily the Cathedral and Clark Ranges, and drains an area of approximately 511 square miles (1,320 km<sup>2</sup>).[^cite_note-water_overview-92]

Hydrologic processes, including [glaciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaciation), flooding, and fluvial geomorphic response, have been fundamental in creating park landforms.[^cite_note-water_overview-92] The park contains approximately 3,200 lakes (greater than 100 m<sup>2</sup>), two [reservoirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reservoir_\(water\)), and 1,700 miles (2,700 km) of streams.[^cite_note-hydrology-93] [Wetlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wetland) flourish in valley bottoms throughout the park, and are often hydrologically linked to nearby lakes and rivers through seasonal flooding and groundwater. Meadow habitats, distributed at elevations from 3,000 to 11,000 feet (910 to 3,350 m) in the park, are generally wetlands, as are the [riparian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riparian) habitats found on the banks of Yosemite's watercourses.[^cite_note-wetland_vegetation-94]



[Bridalveil Fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridalveil_Fall) flows from a U-shaped hanging valley that was created by a tributary glacier.

Yosemite is famous for its [high concentration of waterfalls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_waterfalls_in_Yosemite_National_Park) in a small area. Numerous sheer drops, glacial steps, and [hanging valleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanging_valley) in the park feature spectacular cascades, especially during April, May, and June (as the snow melts). Located in Yosemite Valley, [Yosemite Falls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yosemite_Falls) is the fourth tallest waterfall in North America at 2,425 feet (739 m) according to the World Waterfall Database.[^cite_note-95] Also in the valley is the much lower volume [Ribbon Falls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribbon_Fall), which has the highest single vertical drop, 1,612 feet (491 m).[^cite_note-Kiver227-91] Perhaps the most prominent of the valley waterfalls is [Bridalveil Fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridalveil_Fall_\(Yosemite\)). [Horsetail Fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horsetail_Fall_\(Yosemite\)) flows over the eastern edge of El Capitan. This small waterfall usually flows only during winter and is easy to miss. On rare occasions during mid- to late February, it can glow orange when backlit by sunset. This unique lighting effect happens only on evenings with a clear sky. Minor haze or cloudiness can spoil the effect. Although entirely natural, the phenomenon is reminiscent of the human-caused Firefall that historically occurred from Glacier Point. Wapama Falls in [Hetch Hetchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hetch_Hetchy) Valley is another notable waterfall. Hundreds of [ephemeral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephemeral) waterfalls become active in the park after heavy rains or melting snowpack.[^cite_note-96]

Park [glaciers](12463.md) are relatively small and occupy areas that are in almost permanent shade, such as north- and northeast-facing [cirques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirque_\(landform\)). [Lyell Glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyell_Glacier) is the largest glacier in Yosemite (the Palisades Glaciers are the largest in the Sierra Nevada) and covers 160 acres (65 ha).[^cite_note-GeologyUSP228-97] None of the Yosemite glaciers are a remnant of the [Ice Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Glacial_Period) alpine glaciers responsible for sculpting the Yosemite landscape. Instead, they were formed during one of the [neoglacial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoglacial) episodes that have occurred since the thawing of the Ice Age (such as the [Little Ice Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age)).[^cite_note-GeologyNP340-90] Many Yosemite glaciers have disappeared, such as the Black Mountain Glacier that was marked in 1871 and had gone by the mid-1980s.[^cite_note-98] Yosemite's final two glaciers – the Lyell and Maclure glaciers – have receded over the last 100 years and are expected to disappear as a result of climate change.[^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100]

### Climate



Yosemite in [autumn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autumn)

Yosemite has a [Mediterranean climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_climate) ([Köppen climate classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) *Csa*), meaning most precipitation falls during the mild winter, and the other seasons are nearly dry (less than three percent of precipitation falls during the long, hot summers). Because of [orographic lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orographic_lift), precipitation increases with elevation up to 8,000 feet (2,400 m) where it slowly decreases to the crest. Precipitation amounts vary from 36 inches (910 mm) at 4,000 feet (1,200 m) elevation to 50 inches (1,300 mm) at 8,600 feet (2,600 m). Snow does not typically accumulate until November in the high country. It deepens into March or early April.[^cite_note-climate-101]

Mean daily temperatures range from 25 °F (−4 °C) to 53 °F (12 °C) at Tuolumne Meadows at 8,600 feet (2,600 m). At the Wawona Entrance (elevation 5,130 feet or 1,560 metres), mean daily temperature ranges from 36 to 67 °F (2 to 19 °C). At the lower elevations below 5,000 feet (1,500 m), temperatures are hotter; the mean daily high temperature at Yosemite Valley (elevation 3,966 feet or 1,209 metres) varies from 46 to 90 °F (8 to 32 °C). At elevations above 8,000 feet (2,400 m), the hot, dry summer temperatures are moderated by frequent summer thunderstorms, along with snow that can persist into July. The combination of dry vegetation, low relative humidity, and thunderstorms results in frequent lightning-caused [fires](56106.md) as well.[^cite_note-climate-101]

At park headquarters (elevation 4,018 ft or 1,225 m), January averages 38.0 °F (3.3 °C), while July averages 73.3 °F (22.9 °C). In summer, the nights are much cooler than the days. An average of 45.5 days have highs of 90 °F (32 °C) or higher and an average of 105.6 days have freezing temperatures, which have been recorded in every month of the year. The record high temperature was 112 °F (44 °C) on July 22 and July 24, 1915, while the record low temperature was −7 °F (−22 °C) on January 1, 2009. Average annual precipitation is nearly 37 inches (940 mm), falling on 67 days. The wettest "rain year" was from July 1937 to June 1938 with 58.64 inches (1,489.5 mm) and the driest from July 1976 to June 1977 with 14.81 inches (376 mm). The most precipitation in one month was 29.61 inches (752 mm) in December 1955, and the most in one day was 6.92 inches (176 mm) on December 23, 1955. Average annual snowfall is 39.4 inches or 100 centimeters. The snowiest winter was 1948–1949 with 176.5 inches (448 cm). The most snow in one month was 175.0 inches (444 cm) in January 1993.

In addition to the typical weather patterns, Yosemite is also impacted by the [Mono winds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mono_winds), which are strong, dry winds that blow down the Sierra Nevada slopes, especially during the late fall through spring.[^cite_note-nps-jan21-102]

## Geology

Main article: [Geology of the Yosemite area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology_of_the_Yosemite_area)



Generalized geologic map of the Yosemite area (based on a [USGS](23814944.md) image)

### Tectonic and volcanic activity

See also: [Tioga Pass caldera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tioga_Pass_caldera)

The location of the park was a [passive continental margin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_continental_margin) during the [Precambrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precambrian) and early [Paleozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleozoic).[^cite_note-GeologyNP328-107] Sediment was derived from continental sources and was deposited in shallow water. These rocks became deformed and metamorphosed.[^cite_note-108][^cite_note-109]

Heat generated from the [Farallon Plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farallon_Plate) [subducting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subduction) below the [North American Plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Plate) led to the creation of an [island arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island_arc) of volcanoes on the west coast of proto-North America between the late [Devonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devonian) and [Permian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permian) periods.[^cite_note-GeologyNP328-107] Material accreted onto the western edge of North America, and mountains were raised to the east in Nevada.[^cite_note-110]

The first phase of regional [plutonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intrusion_\(geology\)) started 210 million years ago in the late Triassic and continued throughout the Jurassic to about 150 million years before present ([BP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Before_Present)), which led to the creation of the [Sierra Nevada Batholith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_Batholith).[^cite_note-GeologyNP329-81] The resulting rocks were mostly granitic in composition and lay about 6 miles (9.7 km) below the surface.[^cite_note-GeologyNP337-111] Around the same time, the [Nevadan orogeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevadan_orogeny) built the Nevadan mountain range (also called the Ancestral Sierra Nevada) to a height of 15,000 feet (4,600 m).

The second major pluton emplacement phase lasted from about 120 million to 80 million years ago during the [Cretaceous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretaceous).[^cite_note-GeologyNP329-81] This was part of the [Sevier orogeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sevier_orogeny).[^cite_note-112]

Starting 20 million years ago (in the [Cenozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cenozoic)) and lasting until 5 million years ago, a now-extinct extension of [Cascade Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cascade_Range) volcanoes erupted, bringing large amounts of igneous material to the area. These igneous deposits blanketed the region north of the Yosemite region. Volcanic activity persisted past 5 million years BP east of the current park borders in the Mono Lake and [Long Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Valley_Caldera) areas.[^cite_note-113]

### Uplift and erosion



[Exfoliation joints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exfoliation_joint) cause erosion in granitic rocks, creating many [domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granite_dome) including [Half Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_Dome).

Starting 10 million years ago, vertical movement along the Sierra fault started to uplift the Sierra Nevada. Subsequent tilting of the Sierra block and the resulting accelerated uplift of the Sierra Nevada increased the [gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradient) of western-flowing streams.[^cite_note-GeologyNP339-114] The streams consequently ran faster and thus cut their valleys more quickly. Additional uplift occurred when major faults developed to the east, forming [Owens Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owens_Valley) from [Basin and Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basin_and_Range_Province)-associated extensional forces. Sierra uplift accelerated again about two million years ago during the [Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene).[^cite_note-115]

The uplifting and increased erosion exposed granitic rocks to surface pressures, resulting in exfoliation (responsible for the rounded shape of the many domes in the park) and mass wasting following the numerous fracture joint planes (cracks, especially vertical ones) in the now solidified plutons.[^cite_note-GeologyNP332-86] [Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene) glaciers further accelerated this process, while glacial meltwater transported the resulting [talus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scree) and [till](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Till) from valley floors.[^cite_note-Huber-116]

Numerous vertical joint planes controlled where and how fast erosion took place. Most of these long, linear, and very deep cracks trend northeast or northwest and form parallel, often regularly spaced sets.[^cite_note-Huber-116]

### Glacial sculpting



Glacially polished granite [cirque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirque) in upper [Tenaya Canyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenaya_Canyon)

A series of glaciations further modified the region starting about 2 to 3 million years ago and ending sometime around 10,000 BP. At least four major glaciations occurred in the Sierra, locally called the Sherwin (also called the pre-Tahoe), Tahoe, Tenaya, and Tioga.[^cite_note-GeologyNP339-114] The Sherwin glaciers were the largest, filling Yosemite and other valleys, while later stages produced much smaller glaciers. A Sherwin-age glacier was almost surely responsible for the major excavation and shaping of Yosemite Valley and other canyons in the area.[^cite_note-117]

Glacial systems reached depths of up to 4,000 feet (1,200 m) and left their marks. The longest glacier ran down the Grand Canyon of the Tuolumne River for 60 miles (97 km), passing well beyond Hetch Hetchy Valley. Merced Glacier flowed out of Yosemite Valley and into the Merced River Gorge. Lee Vining Glacier carved Lee Vining Canyon and emptied into Lake Russel (the much-enlarged ice age version of Mono Lake). Only the highest peaks, such as Mount Dana and Mount Conness, were not covered by glaciers. Retreating glaciers often left recessional [moraines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moraine) that impounded lakes such as the 5.5 miles (9 km) long Lake Yosemite (a shallow lake that periodically covered much of the floor of Yosemite Valley).[^cite_note-GeologyNP333-118]

## Ecology and environment

Further information: [Ecology of the Sierra Nevada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecology_of_the_Sierra_Nevada) and [List of plants of the Sierra Nevada (U.S.)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_plants_of_the_Sierra_Nevada_\(U.S.\))

### Habitats



[Mule deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mule_deer) in Yosemite Valley

The park has an elevation range from 2,127 to 13,114 feet (648 to 3,997 m) and contains five major [vegetation zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_zone): [chaparral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaparral) and [oak](39696.md) woodland, [lower montane forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_lower_montane_forest), upper [montane forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montane_ecosystems#Montane_forests), [subalpine zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_subalpine_zone), and [alpine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_tundra). Of California's 7,000 plant species, approximately 50% occur in the Sierra Nevada, and more than 20% within the park. The park contains suitable habitat for more than 160 rare plants, with rare local [geologic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology) formations and unique [soils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil) characterizing the restricted ranges many of these plants occupy.[^cite_note-naturehistory-8]

With its scrubby sun-baked [chaparral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaparral), stately groves of pine, fir, and sequoia, and expanses of alpine woodlands and meadows, Yosemite National Park preserves a Sierra landscape as it prevailed before Euro-American settlement.[^cite_note-snepLateSuccessional-119] In contrast to surrounding lands, which have been significantly altered by logging, the park contains some 352.36 mi<sup>2</sup> (912.6 km<sup>2</sup>) of [old-growth forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old-growth_forest).[^cite_note-1993OldGrowthEstimates-120] Taken together, the park's varied [habitats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitat_\(ecology\)) support over 250 species of [vertebrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertebrate), which include fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals.[^cite_note-wildlife-121]

Yosemite's western boundary has habitats dominated by [mixed coniferous forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_coniferous_forest) of [ponderosa pine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponderosa_pine), [sugar pine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_pine), [incense cedar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incense_cedar), [white fir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_fir), [Douglas fir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_fir), and a few stands of giant sequoia, interspersed by areas of [black oak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_black_oak) and [canyon live oak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_oak). These habitats support relatively high wildlife diversity. Wildlife include [black bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_black_bear), [coyote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coyote), [raccoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raccoon), [mountain kingsnake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingsnake), [Gilbert's skink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert's_skink), [white-headed woodpecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-headed_woodpecker), [bobcat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobcat), [river otter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_river_otter), [gray fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gray_fox), [red fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_fox), [brown creeper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_creeper), two species of skunk, [cougar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_cougar), [spotted owl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spotted_owl), and bats.[^cite_note-wildlife-121]

At higher elevations, the coniferous forests become purer stands of [red fir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fir), [western white pine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_white_pine), [Jeffrey pine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_pine), [lodgepole pine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lodgepole_pine), and the occasional [foxtail pine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foxtail_pine). Fewer wildlife species tend to be found in these habitats because of their higher elevation and lower complexity. Animals include [golden-mantled ground squirrel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden-mantled_ground_squirrel), [chickaree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chickaree), [fisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_\(animal\)), [Steller's jay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steller's_jay), water ouzel, [hermit thrush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermit_thrush), and [American goshawk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_goshawk). Reptiles are not common, but include [rubber boa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubber_boa), [western fence lizard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_fence_lizard), and [northern alligator lizard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_alligator_lizard).[^cite_note-wildlife-121]



[Marmot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow-bellied_marmot) in [Tuolumne Meadows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuolumne_Meadows)

As the landscape rises, trees become smaller and more sparse, with stands broken by areas of exposed granite. These include lodgepole pine, [whitebark pine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitebark_pine), and [mountain hemlock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_hemlock) that, at highest elevations, give way to vast expanses of granite as the treeline is reached. The climate in these habitats is harsh, and the growing season is short, but species such as [pika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_pika), [yellow-bellied marmot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow-bellied_marmot), white-tailed [jackrabbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackrabbit), [Clark's nutcracker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clark's_nutcracker), and [black rosy finch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_rosy_finch) are adapted to these conditions. Treeless alpine habitats are favored by [Sierra Nevada bighorn sheep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_bighorn_sheep). This species is found in the Yosemite area only around Tioga Pass, where a small, reintroduced population exists.[^cite_note-wildlife-121]

At a variety of elevations, meadows provide important habitat. Animals come to feed on the green [grasses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poaceae) and use the flowing and standing water found in many meadows. Predators follow these animals. The interface between meadow and forest is favored by many animal species because of the proximity of open areas for foraging and cover for protection. Species that are highly dependent upon meadow habitat include [great grey owl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_grey_owl), [willow flycatcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willow_flycatcher), [Yosemite toad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yosemite_toad), and [mountain beaver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_beaver).[^cite_note-wildlife-121]

### Management issues



A [black bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_black_bear) with an ear tag in Yosemite Valley

The black bears of Yosemite were once famous for breaking into parked cars to steal food. They were an encouraged tourist sight for many years at the park's garbage dumps, where they congregated to eat garbage, and tourists gathered to photograph them. Increasing bear/human encounters and increasing property damage led to an aggressive campaign to discourage bears from interacting with people and their stuff. The open-air dumps were closed; trash receptacles were replaced with [bear-proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bear-resistant_food_storage_container) receptacles; campgrounds were equipped with bear-proof food lockers so that people would not leave food in their vehicles. Because bears who show aggression towards people usually are destroyed, park personnel have come up with innovative ways to lead bears to associate humans and their property with experiences such as getting hit with a [rubber bullet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubber_bullet). As of 2001, about 30 bears a year were captured and [ear-tagged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ear_tag) and their [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA) sampled so that, when bear damage occurs, rangers can ascertain which bear was causing the problem.[^cite_note-122]

Despite the richness of high-quality habitats in Yosemite, the [brown bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_bear), [California condor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_condor), and [least Bell's vireo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_Bell's_vireo) have become extinct in the park within historical time,[^cite_note-snepTerrestrialVertebrates-123] and another 37 species currently have special status under either California or federal [endangered species](20110668.md) legislation. The most serious current threats include loss of a natural fire regime, [exotic species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exotic_species), air pollution, [habitat fragmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitat_fragmentation), and climate change. On a more local basis, factors such as [road kill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road_kill) and the availability of human food have affected some wildlife species.[^cite_note-124][^cite_note-125]



The [yellow star thistle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centaurea_solstitialis) competes with Yosemite's native plants.

[^cite_note-exotic-126]

Yosemite National Park has documented the presence of more than 130 non-native plant species within park boundaries. They were introduced into Yosemite following the migration of early [Euro-American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_colonization_of_the_Americas) settlers in the late 1850s. Natural and human-caused disturbances, such as wildland fires and construction activities, have contributed to a rapid increase in the spread of non-native plants. Some of these species invade and displace the native plant communities, impacting park resources. Non-native plants can bring about significant ecosystem changes by altering native plant communities and the processes that support them. Some non-native species may cause an increase in fire frequency or increase the available soil nitrogen that allows other non-native plants to establish. Many non-native species, such as [yellow star thistle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centaurea_solstitialis) (*Centaurea solstitialis*), can produce a long [tap root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tap_root) that allows them to out-compete the native plants for available water.[^cite_note-exotic-126]

Bull thistle (*[Cirsium vulgare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirsium_vulgare)*), common mullein (*[Verbascum thapsus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verbascum_thapsus)*), and Klamath weed (*[Hypericum perforatum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypericum_perforatum)*) have been identified as noxious [pests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pest_\(organism\)) in Yosemite since the 1940s. More recently recognized species are yellow star thistle (*Centaurea solstitialis*), sweet clover (*[Melilot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melilot)* spp.), Himalayan blackberry (*[Rubus armeniacus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubus_armeniacus)*), cut-leaved blackberry (*[Rubus laciniatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubus_laciniatus)*) and large periwinkle (*[Vinca major](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinca_major)*).[^cite_note-exotic-126]

Increasing [ozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone) pollution causes tissue damage to sequoia trees, making them more vulnerable to insect infestation and disease. Since the [cones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conifer_cone) of these trees require fire-touched soil to [germinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germination), historic [fire suppression](56106.md#Suppression) has reduced their ability to reproduce. Planned [prescribed fires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prescribed_fire) may help the germination issue.[^cite_note-127]

### Restoration projects



NPS employees and volunteers planting vegetation at Ackerson Meadow

In 2016, [The Trust for Public Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Trust_for_Public_Land) (TPL) purchased [Ackerson Meadow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ackerson_Meadow), a 400-acre tract (160 ha) on the western edge of the park, for \$2.3 million. Ackerson Meadow was originally included in the proposed 1890 park boundary but never acquired by the federal government. The purchase and donation of the meadow was made possible through a cooperative effort by TPL, NPS, and Yosemite Conservancy. On September 7, 2016, NPS accepted the donation of the land, making the meadow the largest addition to Yosemite since 1949.[^cite_note-\:12-128] The meadow was purchased and donated because it faced several issues, including extensive gully erosion from years of cattle ranching, timber harvest, and domestic water diversion which have over the decades lowered the water table drying out vegetation.[^cite_note-\:02-129] Additionally, the use of roads over the years had compacted soils, and invasive species have taken hold across the wetland. To address these issues, the National Park Service, in conjunction with the Forest Service, restored the meadow at a cost of \$17.7 million with funding coming from a variety of nonprofit organizations.[^cite_note-\:12-128] Restoration work would begin in the fall of 2023 and conclude in the spring of 2025.[^cite_note-\:42-130] Today the wetland has been restored as a critical [wildlife corridor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildlife_corridor) and serves important functions like flood attenuation, [carbon sequestration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_sequestration), and improving water quality.[^cite_note-\:52-131]

### Wildfires



The [Meadow Fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meadow_Fire) burns in Little Yosemite Valley, 2014.

Indigenous residents intentionally set small fires in the early 1860s and before to clear the ground of brush as part of their farming practices.[^cite_note-Spence-25] These fires are comparable to contemporary practices such as [controlled burns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_burn) that are done by the U.S. Forest Service and others. Although it was not their primary reason, Yosemite Natives helped preserve biodiversity and resilience by lighting these small fires. Native Americans used fire as an early [wildlife management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildlife_management) tool to keep certain lands clear, resulting in more food for large animals and decreasing the chance of large forest fires, which now devastate forest ecosystems.[^cite_note-\:5-132] Some early uncontrolled forest fires were set accidentally by the militia group led by Major John Savage when the group burned down the Ahwahneechee camp in an attempt to expel them. The house fires eventually spread to a large section of the forest, and the militia group ended up having to abandon their raid to save their own camp from the conflagration.[^cite_note-\:5-132]

Forest fires clear the park of dead vegetation, making way for new growth.[^cite_note-Evergreen_Magazine-133] Small fires damage the income generated by tourism. During late July and early August 2018, the Valley and other sections of the park, temporarily closed due to the [Ferguson Fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferguson_Fire) at its western boundary.[^cite_note-134] The closing was the largest in almost thirty years.[^cite_note-135]

### Climate change

Yosemite National Park has been showing signs of [climate change](5042951.md) over the past few decades. Temperatures have gone up and caused the Sierra Nevada snowpack to shrink compared to what it used to be. Snow melts earlier now, more winter precipitation is falling as rain,[^cite_note-Halofsky-136] and the timing of river flow throughout the park has shifted.[^cite_note-ccsnowpack-137]

These changes affect the plants and animals that live in the park.[^cite_note-ccecosystems-138] For example, climate change can decrease both [atmospheric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humidity) and [soil moisture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_moisture) in the Sierra Nevada, which will harm [drought](37335.md)-sensitive plant species.[^cite_note-Halofsky-136] Wildlife can be harmed by climate change either due to direct temperature effects, or changes in habitat.[^cite_note-Halofsky-136] Researchers have noticed shifts in where certain species live inside the park. Some plants and animals are moving higher up because the [temperatures there are lower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapse_rate). Others are struggling to adjust at all because the environment around them is changing faster than they can adapt.[^cite_note-ccecosystems-138]

Climate change has also played a role in the rise of large intense [wildfires](56106.md) in and around Yosemite. With hotter and drier weather, the fire season lasts longer than it used to, which leads to bigger fire and more damage overall.[^cite_note-139] Climate change is making it more difficult for [coniferous forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coniferous_forest) to return after wildfire in the Western United States.[^cite_note-140] [Smoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoke) from wildfires in the Sierra Nevada can drift for many miles, causing human illness and excess death far away from the park.[^cite_note-141]

## Activities



Tunnel tree at Yosemite National Park in May 2022



Yosemite Valley Shuttle System bus, a free [bus service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_transport_bus_service)

### Public access

Yosemite National Park is open year-round, though certain roads close during snowy months, usually from November through May or June.[^cite_note-142] Certain trails also close during winter, including The 4-Mile Trail and part of The Mist Trail.[^cite_note-143]

Traffic congestion in the valley is heavy during the peak summer season (June to August), and a free [shuttle bus system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_transport) operates in the valley. Parking in the valley during the summer is often full.[^cite_note-144] [Amtrak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amtrak) and the [Yosemite Area Regional Transportation System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yosemite_Area_Regional_Transportation_System) (YARTS) provide public transit into Yosemite from four gateway communities: Merced, Fresno, Sonora, and Mammoth Lakes/Lee Vining. The Merced route provides service year-round.[^cite_note-145]

The natural and cultural history of Yosemite Valley is presented at the Yosemite Valley Visitor Center, the adjoining Yosemite Museum, and the Nature Center at Happy Isles. The parks' two [National Historic Landmarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Historic_Landmark) are the [Sierra Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Club)'s [LeConte Memorial Lodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LeConte_Memorial_Lodge) (Yosemite's first public visitor center), and the Ahwahnee Hotel. Camp 4 is on the [National Register of Historic Places](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Register_of_Historic_Places).[^cite_note-146]

### Hiking



Hikers line the [Half Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_Dome) cables on a busy summer day in 2008

Over 800 miles (1,300 km) of trails are available to hikers[^cite_note-naturehistory-8]—everything from an easy stroll to a challenging mountain hike, or [backpacking trips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backpacking_\(wilderness\)). The popular Half Dome hike to the summit of [Half Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_Dome) requires a permit whenever the cables are up (usually from [Memorial Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memorial_Day) weekend to [Columbus Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbus_Day)).[^cite_note-147] A maximum of 300 hikers, selected by lottery, are permitted to advance beyond the base of the sub dome each day, including 225 day hikers and 75 backpackers.[^cite_note-148]

The park can be divided into five sections—Yosemite Valley, Wawona/Mariposa Grove/Glacier Point, Tuolumne Meadows, Hetch Hetchy, and [Crane Flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crane_Flat_Campground)/White Wolf.[^cite_note-149] Numerous books describe park trails, while the National Park Service provides free information.

Between late spring and early fall, much of the park can be accessed for backpacking trips. All overnight trips into the back country require a wilderness permit[^cite_note-permits-150] and most require approved bear-resistant food storage.[^cite_note-food_storage-151]

### Driving

Further information: [List of Yosemite destinations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Yosemite_destinations)

While some locations in Yosemite are accessible only on foot, other locations can be reached via road. The most famous road is [Tioga Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_State_Route_120).[^cite_note-auto_touring-152]

Bicycles are allowed on the roads, but only 12 miles (19 km) of paved off-road trails are available in Yosemite Valley itself; mountain biking is not allowed.[^cite_note-bike-153]

### Climbing



Climbing the Narrows in [Sentinel Rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentinel_Rock)

Rock climbing is an important part of Yosemite.[^cite_note-climbing-154] In particular the valley is surrounded by summits such as Half Dome and El Capitan. [Camp 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_4_\(Yosemite\)) is a walk-in campground in the Valley that was instrumental in the development of rock climbing as a sport, and is listed on the [National Register of Historic Places](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Register_of_Historic_Places).[^cite_note-camp4-155] Climbers can generally be spotted in the snow-free months on anything from ten-foot-high (3 m) boulders to the 3,300-foot (1.0 km) face of El Capitan. Classes on rock climbing are offered there.

[Tuolumne Meadows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuolumne_Meadows) is well known for [rock and mountain climbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiking,_rock_climbing,_and_mountain_climbing_around_Tuolumne_Meadows).

### Winter activities



A ranger-guided [snowshoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowshoe) walk in the park

Away from the Valley, the park is snowed in during the winter months, with many roads closed. [Downhill skiing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downhill_skiing) is available at the [Badger Pass Ski Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badger_Pass_Ski_Area)—the oldest downhill skiing area in California, operating from mid-December through early April.[^cite_note-skiing-156] Much of the park is open to [cross-country skiing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-country_skiing) and [snowshoeing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowshoe), and backcountry ski huts are available.[^cite_note-TuolumneWinter-157][^cite_note-winter-158] Wilderness permits are required for backcountry overnight ski trips.[^cite_note-permits-150]

The [Bracebridge dinner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahwahnee_Hotel#Bracebridge_tradition) is an annual holiday event, held since 1927 at the Ahwahnee Hotel, inspired by [Washington Irving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Irving)'s descriptions of Squire Bracebridge and English [Christmas](6237.md) traditions of the 18th century in his *Sketch Book*. Between 1929 and 1973, the show was organized by [Ansel Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ansel_Adams).[^cite_note-Bracebridge-159]

### Other

Yosemite has 13 official campgrounds.[^cite_note-160]

Bicycle rentals are available from spring through fall. Over 12 miles (19 km) of paved bike paths are available in Yosemite Valley. In addition, bicyclists can ride on roads. Helmets are required for children under 18 years of age. Off-trail riding and [mountain biking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_biking) are not permitted in the park.[^cite_note-161]

Water activities are plentiful during warmer months. Rafting can be done through the valley on the Merced River from late May to July.[^cite_note-162] Swimming pools are available at Yosemite Lodge and Curry Village.
