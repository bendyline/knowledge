The **European Space Agency**[^cite_note-7] (**ESA**; pronounced [/ˈiːsə/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*EE-sə*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key))[^cite_note-8] is a 23-member [international organisation](18201224.md) devoted to [space exploration](28431.md).[^cite_note-ESA-9] It has its headquarters in Paris and a staff of around 3,000 people globally as of 2025.[^cite_note-10] ESA was founded in 1975 in the context of [European integration](683704.md). Its 2026 annual budget was around €8.3 billion.[^cite_note-budget2024-4]

The ESA [human spaceflight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_spaceflight) programme includes participation in the [International Space Station](15043.md) (ISS) and collaboration with [NASA](18426568.md) on the [Artemis](60758751.md) programme, especially manufacturing of the [Orion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orion_\(spacecraft\)) spacecraft's [European Service Module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Service_Module) (ESM). ESA launches and operates uncrewed missions to the Moon, Mars, Jupiter, Venus, Mercury, the Sun, and various comets and asteroids. Other activities include [space telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_telescope), [Earth observation satellites](46597.md), [asteroid impact avoidance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid_impact_avoidance), [telecommunication](45207.md) and [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_navigation) satellites, designing [launch vehicles](1306157.md) (*e.g.* [Ariane 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_6) is operated through [Arianespace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arianespace) with ESA sharing in the costs), and maintaining Europe's [spaceport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceport) (the [Guiana Space Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guiana_Space_Centre) in [Kourou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kourou), French Guiana), as well as [space safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Safety_Programme)\[^cite_note-[www.esa.int-2025e-11](http://www.esa.int-2025e-11/)] and commercialisation.[^cite_note-12]

## Mission

The treaty establishing the European Space Agency reads:[^cite_note-European_Space_Agency-2007-13]

> "The purpose of the Agency shall be to provide for and to promote, for exclusively peaceful purposes, cooperation among European States in space research and technology and their space applications, with a view to their being used for scientific purposes and for operational space applications systems."

ESA is responsible for setting a unified space and related industrial policy, recommending space objectives to the member states, and integrating national programmes like satellite development, into the European programme as much as possible.[^cite_note-European_Space_Agency-2007-13]

[Jean-Jacques Dordain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Dordain)—ESA's Director General (2003–2015)—outlined the European Space Agency's mission in a 2003 interview:[^cite_note-14]

> "Today space activities have pursued the benefit of citizens, and citizens are asking for a better quality of life on Earth. They want greater security and economic wealth, but they also want to pursue their dreams, to increase their knowledge, and they want younger people to be attracted to the pursuit of science and technology.\
> \
> &#x20;I think that space can do all of this: it can produce a higher quality of life, better security, more economic wealth, and also fulfill our citizens' dreams and thirst for knowledge, and attract the young generation. This is the reason space exploration is an integral part of overall space activities. It has always been so, and it will be even more important in the future."

At the ministerial council of 2025, ESA member states widened the agency's mandate to include defence.[^cite_note-Hitchens-2025-15] The resolution reads:[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17]

> "ESA's intergovernmental framework provides the credentials and tools for developing space technologies and systems \[...] for security and defence and to implement corresponding activities."

After [World War II](32927.md), many European scientists left Western Europe to work with the United States. Although the 1950s boom made it possible for Western European countries to invest in research and specifically in space-related activities, Western European scientists realised solely national projects would not be able to compete with the two superpowers. In 1958, only months after the [Sputnik shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sputnik_crisis), [Edoardo Amaldi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edoardo_Amaldi) (Italy) and [Pierre Auger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Victor_Auger) (France), two prominent members of the Western European scientific community, met to discuss the foundation of a common Western European space agency. The meeting was attended by scientific representatives from eight countries.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20]

The Western European nations decided to have two agencies: one concerned with developing a launch system, [ELDO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Launcher_Development_Organisation) (European Launcher Development Organisation), of which Renzo Carrobio di Carrobio became the first Secretary General from 1964 to 1971,[^cite_note-21] and the other the precursor of the European Space Agency, [ESRO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Space_Research_Organisation) (European Space Research Organisation), led by [Pierre Auger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Victor_Auger). The latter was established on 20 March 1964 by an agreement signed on 14 June 1962. From 1968 to 1972, ESRO launched seven research satellites, but ELDO was not able to deliver a launch vehicle. Both agencies struggled with the underfunding and diverging interests of their participants.[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23]

### Foundation

The ESA in its current form was founded with the ESA Convention in 1975, when ESRO was merged with ELDO. ESA had ten founding member states: Belgium, Denmark, France, West Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom.[^cite_note-24] These signed the ESA Convention in 1975 and deposited the instruments of ratification by 1980, when the convention came into force.[^cite_note-25] During this interval the agency functioned in a *de facto* fashion.[^cite_note-26]

### First science missions

ESA launched its first major scientific mission in 1975, *[Cos-B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cos-B)*, a satellite monitoring [gamma-ray emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma-ray_astronomy) in the universe, which was first worked on by ESRO.[^cite_note-27] ESA collaborated with [NASA](18426568.md) on the [International Ultraviolet Explorer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Ultraviolet_Explorer) (IUE), the world's first high-orbit telescope, which was launched in 1978 and operated successfully for 18 years.[^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29] A number of successful Earth-orbit projects followed, and in 1986 ESA began [*Giotto*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giotto_mission), its first deep-space mission, to study the comets [Halley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet_Halley) and [Grigg–Skjellerup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/26P/Grigg%E2%80%93Skjellerup).[^cite_note-30] *[Hipparcos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hipparcos)*, a star-mapping mission, was launched in 1989[^cite_note-31] and in the 1990s [SOHO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_and_Heliospheric_Observatory), *[Ulysses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_\(spacecraft\))*, and the [Hubble Space Telescope](40203.md) were all jointly carried out with NASA.[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35] Later scientific missions in cooperation with NASA include the *[Cassini–Huygens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassini%E2%80%93Huygens)* space probe, to which the ESA contributed by building the [Titan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titan_\(moon\)) landing module *[Huygens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huygens_\(spacecraft\))*.[^cite_note-36]

### First launch vehicles

As the successor of [ELDO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Launcher_Development_Organisation), the ESA has also constructed rockets for scientific and commercial payloads. [Ariane 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_1), launched in 1979, carried mostly commercial payloads into orbit from 1984 onward. The next two versions of the [Ariane rocket family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_\(rocket_family\)) were intermediate stages in the development of a more advanced launch system, the [Ariane 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_4), which operated between 1988 and 2003 and established the ESA as the world leader in commercial space launches in the 1990s.[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38] Although the succeeding [Ariane 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_5) experienced a failure on its first flight in 1996,[^cite_note-39] it has since firmly established itself within the heavily competitive commercial space launch market with 112 successful launches until 2023.[^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41] In 1998, ESA started developing the [small-lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small-lift_launch_vehicle) launch vehicle [Vega](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vega_\(rocket\)).[^cite_note-Phys-2025-42]

### First astronauts

Astronauts from the future ESA member states have been joining Soviet space missions since 1978.[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44] The first [ESA astronaut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Astronaut_Corps) to fly to space was [Ulf Merbold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulf_Merbold) who joined the [STS-9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STS-9) mission of the American [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle) in 1983, that carried the first European-built [Spacelab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacelab) laboratory module.[^cite_note-45] The experience gained during the Spacelab programme was later instrumental in developing the [International Space Station](15043.md) (ISS).[^cite_note-46][^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48] Since then, ESA astronauts have been joining Space Shuttle flights, as well as Russian [Soyuz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz_\(spacecraft\)) flights to the [Mir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mir) space station.[^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50][^cite_note-51] During the 1980s and 1990s, ESA was considering developing its own crewed spacecraft [Hermes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes_\(spacecraft\))[^cite_note-52] and a small space station [Columbus MTFF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbus_Man-Tended_Free_Flyer).[^cite_note-53] These plans were later abandoned and instead, ESA joined the ISS programme with *[Columbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbus_\(ISS_module\))* being repurposed as one of the station's laboratory modules and ESA astronauts flying to the station on American and Russian spacecraft.[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55]

### ESA in the 2000s

The beginning of the new millennium saw the ESA become, along with agencies like [NASA](18426568.md) and [JAXA](716184.md), one of the major participants in [space research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outline_of_space_science).[^cite_note-56] Although ESA had relied on co-operation with NASA in previous decades, changed circumstances, such as tough legal restrictions on information sharing under [ITAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Traffic_in_Arms_Regulations),[^cite_note-57] led to decisions to rely more on itself and on cooperation with [Roscosmos](406573.md).[^cite_note-international-coop-58]

The agency continued its contribution to the International Space Station (ISS) programme with [European astronauts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Astronaut_Corps) joining assembly flights as well as [long-term missions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISS_expeditions) to the station. In 2008, ESA added its laboratory module *[Columbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbus_\(ISS_module\))* to ISS and started launching the [ATV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automated_Transfer_Vehicle) cargo spacecraft.[^cite_note-59] During the 2000s, ESA was considering cooperation with Russia on the proposed [Kliper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kliper) and [CSTS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSTS) crewed vehicles, but neither of these was developed.[^cite_note-60][^cite_note-61][^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64]

Notable deep space missions during the 2000s included the agency's first Moon, Mars, and Venus orbiters: *[SMART-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMART-1)*,[^cite_note-65] *[Mars Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Express)*, and *[Venus Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus_Express)*.[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67] ESA's *[Huygens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huygens_\(spacecraft\))* probe, launched together with the NASA's *[Cassini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassini%E2%80%93Huygens)* mission in 1997, reached its destination in 2005 when it successfully landed on [Titan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titan_\(moon\)), marking the farthest landing from Earth a spacecraft has ever made.[^cite_note-68] The comet orbiter *[Rosetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosetta_\(spacecraft\))* launched in 2004 and performed multiple deep space flybys and observations during the decade, but wouldn't reach its destination until 2014.[^cite_note-69]

ESA has launched multiple major astronomy missions in the 2000s: the [gamma ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_ray) observatory *[INTEGRAL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INTEGRAL)*,[^cite_note-70] the [infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared) observatory [*Herschel*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herschel_Space_Observatory), the [cosmic microwave background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_microwave_background) mapper [*Planck*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_\(spacecraft\)),[^cite_note-71] and [*Corot*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corot_\(space_mission\)), a milestone in the search for [exoplanets](9763.md).[^cite_note-72] Notable [Earth observation](46597.md) missions launched during the decade included *[Envisat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envisat),* *[Double Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_Star_\(satellite\)),* *[GOCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GOCE),* [*SMOS*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_Moisture_and_Ocean_Salinity), and the experimental [PROBA series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PROBA_satellite_series). ESA also contributed to the meteorological constellations [Meteosat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteosat#Second_generation) and [MetOp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MetOp) and tested technologies for the future [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_\(satellite_navigation\)) satellite navigation system with two [GIOVE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GIOVE) satellites.

During the 2000s, ESA pursued the [Aurora programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurora_programme) which planned to launch a series of increasingly ambitious missions culminating in a crewed landing on Mars.[^cite_note-73] The programme was eventually abandoned with only the [ExoMars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ExoMars) series of [astrobiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrobiology) space probes to [Mars](14640471.md) remaining active. ExoMars, as well as the plans for human spaceflight to [LEO](47568.md), the [Moon](19331.md), and Mars, were later incorporated into the new [Terrae Novae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrae_Novae) programme established in 2016.[^cite_note-74]

### ESA in the 2010s

In 2010, ESA added the *[Cupola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupola_\(ISS_module\))* observation module to [ISS](15043.md)[^cite_note-75][^cite_note-76] and [European astronauts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Astronaut_Corps) continued joining [long-term missions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISS_expeditions) to the station. The [ATV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automated_Transfer_Vehicle) cargo spacecraft continued resupplying ISS until its last flight in 2015.[^cite_note-77][^cite_note-78] In 2012, ESA committed to providing the ATV-derived [European Service Module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Service_Module) for NASA's crewed lunar spacecraft [Orion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orion_\(spacecraft\)).[^cite_note-79]

In 2014, ESA's *[Rosetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosetta_\(spacecraft\))* probe arrived at its destinatination, the Jupiter-family comet [67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/67P/Churyumov%E2%80%93Gerasimenko). It became the first spacecraft ever to orbit a comet[^cite_note-80] and its lander *[Philae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philae_\(spacecraft\))* performed the first ever landing on a comet.[^cite_note-81][^cite_note-82] In 2016, ESA launched its second Mars orbiter mission, the ExoMars [Trace Gas Orbiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace_Gas_Orbiter) (TGO), as the first [ExoMars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ExoMars) mission within the newly established [Terrae Novae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrae_Novae) programme. When the spacecraft arrived at Mars later the same year, it released the *[Schiaparelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schiaparelli_EDM)* lander, which failed on landing.[^cite_note-83] TGO, however, entered the Martian orbit and after 11 months of [aerobraking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerobraking) began its scientific observations, focused mostly on the [atmosphere of Mars](4548379.md).[^cite_note-84] In 2018, ESA and [JAXA](716184.md) launched the joint mission *[BepiColombo](551228.md)*, which is expected to arrive at Mercury in 2026.[^cite_note-85][^cite_note-86]

Notable astronomy missions launched in 2010s were the [astrometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrometry) telescope *[Gaia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaia_\(spacecraft\))*, which produced the largest and most precise 3D catalogue of astronomical objects ever made,[^cite_note-87] and the [exoplanets](9763.md)-characterising telescope *[CHEOPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CHEOPS)*.[^cite_note-88] ESA also launched *[LISA Pathfinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LISA_Pathfinder)*, a technology demonstrator for the future [gravitational wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_wave) observatory [LISA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_Interferometer_Space_Antenna).[^cite_note-89] In 2010, ESA launched the [cryosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryosphere)-monitoring satellite *[CryoSat-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CryoSat-2)*, a replacement for *[CryoSat-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CryoSat-1)* which had been destroyed in 2005 due to a failure of its Russian launch vehicle.[^cite_note-90] Another major [Earth observation satellite](46597.md) mission of the decade was the [magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_magnetic_field)-observing *[Swarm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swarm_\(spacecraft\))*, launched in 2013.[^cite_note-91]

The 2010s saw the first launches of two major European [satellite constellations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_constellation), to which ESA contributed alongside other European institutions, the [satellite navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_navigation) system [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_\(satellite_navigation\)) and the [Earth observation](46597.md) programme [Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicus_Programme) with its [Sentinel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentinel_satellite) satellites. The first operational pair of [Galileo satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Galileo_satellites) was launched in 2011.[^cite_note-92] The radar satellite [Sentinel-1A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentinel-1A), first dedicated mission of the Copernicus Programme, was launched in 2014,[^cite_note-93] followed by the optical imaging satellite [Sentinel-2A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentinel-2A) in 2015,[^cite_note-94] the [oceanography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanography) satellite [Sentinel-3A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentinel-3A) in 2016,[^cite_note-95] and the air pollution-monitoring [Sentinel-5p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentinel-5_Precursor) in 2017.[^cite_note-96]

The [small-lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small-lift_launch_vehicle) launch vehicle [Vega](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vega_\(rocket\)), developed by the Italian company [Avio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avio), had its first flight in 2012 and then flew 22 times until 2024 when it was replaced by the more powerful [Vega-C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vega_C).[^cite_note-97][^cite_note-Phys-2025-42] In 2016, ESA started supporting the Spanish company [PLD Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PLD_Space) with [FLPP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_Launchers_Preparatory_Programme) funding for the development of their commercial [reusable launch vehicle](336014.md) [Miura 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miura_5).[^cite_note-98][^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100]

### ESA in the 2020s

After the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md), the cooperation between ESA and [Roscosmos](406573.md) was mostly severed.[^cite_note-101][^cite_note-102][^cite_note-103][^cite_note-104][^cite_note-105] This led to a delay in the [ExoMars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ExoMars) programme[^cite_note-106] and ending of [Soyuz launches from the Guiana Space Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz_at_the_Guiana_Space_Centre).[^cite_note-107] Furthermore, the [space policy of the second Trump administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_policy_of_the_second_Trump_administration) brought uncertainty into many joint ESA-NASA programmes including major science missions[^cite_note-108][^cite_note-109] and human spaceflight.[^cite_note-110][^cite_note-111][^cite_note-112] The ministerial council held in November 2025 reacted to the changed international situation by including security and defence in ESA's mandate,[^cite_note-Hitchens-2025-15] funding development of new European launch vehicles,[^cite_note-113] as well as confirming many priorities of the long term, autonomy-focused "Strategy 2040" proposed earlier in 2025[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115] and approving a record budget of €22.1 billion.[^cite_note-116][^cite_note-117] In early 2026, ESA established the new Resilience, Navigation, and Connectivity Directorate focused on security and defence technologies.[^cite_note-118][^cite_note-119][^cite_note-120]

In early 2020s, ESA has added two major components to the ISS. The [Bartolomeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbus_External_Payload_Facility#Bartolomeo) platform was connected to the *[Columbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbus_\(ISS_module\))* module in 2020 to increase its capacity for external payloads.[^cite_note-121] The [European Robotic Arm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Robotic_Arm) was launched in 2021 together with the *[Nauka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nauka_\(ISS_module\))* module.[^cite_note-122] ESA continued contributing [European astronauts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Astronaut_Corps) to regular [ISS expeditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISS_expeditions),[^cite_note-123][^cite_note-124] and also started paying private companies for short-term astronaut flights to the station.[^cite_note-125][^cite_note-126] ESA also started supporting European companies in developing uncrewed [space capsules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_capsule) for resupplying the ISS and [future space stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Space_Stations) via the [LCRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LEO_Cargo_Return_Service) initiative,[^cite_note-127] as well as the Indian [ISRO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISRO) in developing their crewed [Gaganyaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaganyaan) spacecraft.[^cite_note-128][^cite_note-129] The ESA-built [European Service Module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Service_Module) of the [Orion spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orion_\(spacecraft\)) flew on its [first uncrewed test flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_I) in 2022[^cite_note-130] and in 2026, it brought [Artemis II](41391511.md), the first beyond-[low Earth orbit](47568.md) crewed spaceflight since 1972, around the Moon.[^cite_note-131][^cite_note-132][^cite_note-133]

The *[BepiColombo](551228.md)* mission, launched in 2018, has completed all its nine [gravity assist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_assist) manoeuvres at Earth, Venus, and Mercury, in preparation for the planned 2026 insertion into Mercury orbit.[^cite_note-134] The [heliophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliophysics) mission *[Solar Orbiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_Orbiter)*, launched in 2020, continues to perform periodic Venus flybys which gradually increase its [orbital inclination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_inclination), allowing it to observe the Sun from outside the [Solar system plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic_plane).[^cite_note-135] The *[Juice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter_Icy_Moons_Explorer)* mission, launched in 2023, has so far completed two gravity assist manoeuvres at Earth and Venus on its way to enter an orbit around Jupiter in 2031.[^cite_note-136] Two European orbiters, *[Mars Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Express)* and [TGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace_Gas_Orbiter), continued their operations around Mars, providing scientific insights into the planet's surface and atmosphere. In 2025, *Mars Express* received a software update, which could allow it to stay operational until 2030s.[^cite_note-137]

The [*Hera*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hera_\(space_mission\)) mission, launched in 2024 as the first space mission of the optional [Space Safety Programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Safety_Programme), flew by Mars in 2025 on its way to perform a post-impact survey of the asteroid [Dimorphos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimorphos) which had been impacted by NASA's [Double Asteroid Redirection Test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_Asteroid_Redirection_Test) mission.[^cite_note-138] The Space Safety Programme was established in 2019 by substantially expanding the former Space Situational Awareness (SSA) programme. Since then, it prepared an extensive series of space missions and ground-based projects focused on mitigating the dangers of [near-Earth asteroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near-Earth_object), [space weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_weather), and [space debris](266344.md),[^cite_note-139] which was fully endorsed by ESA member states.[^cite_note-140]

The long-awaited [NASA](18426568.md)-ESA-[CSA](328618.md) space telescope, the [James Webb Space Telescope](434221.md), finally launched in 2021[^cite_note-141] and started operating in its [halo orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halo_orbit) around the Sun–Earth [L<sub>2</sub> point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange_point) in 2022.[^cite_note-142] The first half of 2023 saw the launch the [*Euclid*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid_\(spacecraft\)) space telescope designed to better understand [dark energy](19604228.md) and [dark matter](8651.md) by accurately measuring the [accelerating expansion of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accelerating_expansion_of_the_universe).[^cite_note-NYT-20230701-143] The experimental *[PROBA-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PROBA-3)* mission, launched in 2024, successfully demonstrated high-precision [formation flying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_formation_flying) for [coronagraphy](889722.md) observation of the Sun.[^cite_note-144] The [space weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_weather) mission [*SMILE*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMILE_\(spacecraft\)), developed in cooperation with [CAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Academy_of_Sciences), was launched in May 2026.[^cite_note-\:0-145][^cite_note-\:1-146]

The successor to the [Vega](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vega_\(rocket\)) launch vehicle, [Vega C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vega_C), launched successfully for the first time in July 2022, delivering the *[LARES 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LARES_2)* satellite and six [CubeSats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CubeSat) to Earth orbit.[^cite_note-147][^cite_note-148] The successor to the [Ariane 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_5) launch vehicle, [Ariane 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_6), had its maiden flight in July 2024, followed by the first commercial launch in March 2025[^cite_note-149] and the first launch of the 4-booster variant Ariane 64 in February 2026.[^cite_note-150][^cite_note-151] In the 2020s, ESA started supporting aspiring European [commercial launch providers](752732.md) with substantial development funding and launch contracts through the Boost! programme,[^cite_note-152] the Flight Ticket Initiative,[^cite_note-153] and the [European Launcher Challenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Launcher_Challenge) (ELC).[^cite_note-154][^cite_note-155][^cite_note-156][^cite_note-157] This resulted in the first successful orbital launch from Western European soil performed on 5 September 2026 by [Isar Aerospace](65261460.md)'s [Spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_\(rocket\)) rocket from [Andøya Spaceport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/And%C3%B8ya_Spaceport).[^cite_note-158][^cite_note-159][^cite_note-160]

### Membership and contribution to the ESA

Member states participate to varying degrees with both mandatory space programmes and those that are optional. As of 2008, the mandatory programmes made up 25% of total expenditures while optional space programmes were the other 75%.[^cite_note-czechspace.cz-175] The ESA has traditionally implemented a policy of "georeturn", where funds that ESA member states provide to the ESA "are returned in the form of contracts to companies in those countries."[^cite_note-sn20240619-176]

By 2015, the ESA was an intergovernmental organisation of 22 member states.[^cite_note-ESA-9] The 2008 ESA budget amounted to €3.0 billion whilst the 2009 budget amounted to €3.6 billion.[^cite_note-177] The total budget amounted to about €3.7 billion in 2010, €3.99 billion in 2011, €4.02 billion in 2012, €4.28 billion in 2013, €4.10 billion in 2014, €4.43 billion in 2015, €5.25 billion in 2016, €5.75 billion in 2017, €5.60 billion in 2018, €5.72 billion in 2019, €6.68 billion in 2020, €6.49 billion in 2021, €7.15 billion in 2022, €7.46 billion in 2023, €7.79 billion in 2024 and €8.26 billion in 2026[^cite_note-Budgets-178][^cite_note-179]

English and French are the two official languages of the ESA.[^cite_note-180] Additionally, official documents are also provided in German and documents regarding the [Spacelab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacelab) have been also provided in Italian. If found appropriate, the agency may conduct its correspondence in any language of a member state.[^cite_note-181]

### Non-full member states

Previously associated members were Austria, Norway, Finland and Slovenia, all of which later joined the ESA as full members. Since March 2026 there have been five associate members: Latvia, Lithuania, Slovakia, Cyprus and Canada. The four European members have shown interest in full membership and may eventually apply within the next years.

#### Latvia

Latvia became the second current associated member on 30 June 2020, when the Association Agreement was signed by ESA Director Jan Wörner and the Minister of Education and Science of Latvia, [Ilga Šuplinska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilga_%C5%A0uplinska) in [Riga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riga). The [Saeima](499765.md) ratified it on 27 July.\[^cite_note-[www.esa.int-2020-193](http://www.esa.int-2020-193/)]

#### Lithuania

In May 2021, Lithuania became the third current associated member.[^cite_note-198] As a consequence its citizens became eligible to apply to the [2022 ESA Astronaut group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_European_Space_Agency_Astronaut_Group), applications for which were scheduled to close one week later. The deadline was therefore extended by three weeks to allow Lithuanians a fair chance to apply.[^cite_note-199]

#### Slovakia

Slovakia's Associate membership came into effect on 13 October 2022, for an initial duration of seven years. The Association Agreement supersedes the European Cooperating State (ECS) Agreement, which entered into force upon Slovakia's subscription to the Plan for European Cooperating States Charter on 4 February 2016, a scheme introduced at ESA in 2001. The ECS Agreement was subsequently extended until 3 August 2022.\[^cite_note-[www.esa.int-195](http://www.esa.int-195/)]

#### Cyprus

Cyprus has signed an Associate Agreement in October 2025, with expectations of becoming an associate member by early 2026.[^cite_note-ESA-2025f-200] On 19 March 2026 Cyprus officially became an Associate member of ESA.[^cite_note-Cyprus_Associate_2026-192]

#### Canada

Since 1 January 1979, Canada has had the special status of a Cooperating State within the ESA. By virtue of this accord, the [Canadian Space Agency](328618.md) takes part in the ESA's deliberative bodies and decision-making and also in the ESA's programmes and activities. Canadian firms can bid for and receive contracts to work on programmes. The accord has a provision ensuring a fair industrial return to Canada.[^cite_note-201] The most recent Cooperation Agreement was signed on 12 February 2019 with a term extending to 2030.[^cite_note-202]

For 2014, Canada's annual assessed contribution to the ESA general budget was €6,059,449 ([CAD\$](101846.md)8,559,050).[^cite_note-203] This annual contribution increased in 2017 to €21,600,000 ([CAD\$](101846.md)30,000,000).[^cite_note-204] In 2025, the Government of Canada announced a plan to increase contributions to the ESA by €326,000,000 ([CAD\$](101846.md)528,500,000) over the following three to five-year period.[^cite_note-205][^cite_note-206]

On 14 April 2026 ESA and Canada signed a General Security of Information Agreement (GSOIA), which establishes a legal framework for the exchange of [classified information](252857.md). This was intended to facilitate collaboration in strategic domains such as surveillance, disaster response, and security and to support the development of [dual-use technologies](1003982.md).[^cite_note-207][^cite_note-208]

### Enlargement

See also: [Enlargement of the European Space Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enlargement_of_the_European_Space_Agency)

After the decision of the ESA Council of 21/22 March 2001, the procedure for accession of the European states was detailed as described the document titled "The Plan for European Co-operating States (PECS)".[^cite_note-212] Nations that want to become a full member of the ESA do so in 3 stages. First a Cooperation Agreement is signed between the country and ESA. In this stage, the country has very limited financial responsibilities. If a country wants to co-operate more fully with ESA, it signs a European Cooperating State (ECS) Agreement, albeit to be a candidate for said agreement, a country must be European. The ECS Agreement makes companies based in the country eligible for participation in ESA procurements. The country can also participate in all ESA programmes, except for the Basic Technology Research Programme. While the financial contribution of the country concerned increases, it is still much lower than that of a full member state. The agreement is normally followed by a Plan For European Cooperating State (or PECS Charter). This is a 5-year programme of basic [research and development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_and_development) activities aimed at improving the nation's [space industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_industry) capacity. At the end of the 5-year period, the country can either begin negotiations to become a full member state or an associated state or sign a new PECS Charter.[^cite_note-213] Many countries, most of which joined the EU in both 2004 and 2007, have started to co-operate with the ESA on various levels:

## Launch vehicles



Mock-up of the [Ariane 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_1)

The ESA currently has two operational launch vehicles [Vega C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vega_C) and [Ariane 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_6).[^cite_note-240] Rocket launches are carried out by [Arianespace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arianespace), which has 23 shareholders representing the industry that manufactures the Ariane 5 as well as [CNES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNES), at the ESA's [Guiana Space Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guiana_Space_Centre). Because many communication satellites have equatorial orbits, launches from [French Guiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Guiana) are able to take larger payloads into space than from spaceports at higher latitudes. In addition, equatorial launches give spacecraft an extra 'push' of nearly 500 m/s due to the higher [rotational velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_velocity) of the Earth at the equator compared to near the Earth's poles where rotational velocity approaches zero. In 2014, ESA proposed changes to the [Ariane family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_\(rocket_family\)) development scheme by moving to competitive bids for the development of Ariane 6.[^cite_note-aw20140310-241]

### Ariane 6

Main article: [Ariane 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_6)

Ariane 6 is a [heavy lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy-lift_launch_vehicle) [expendable launch vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expendable_launch_system) developed by Arianespace. The Ariane 6 entered into its inaugural flight campaign on 26 April 2024 with the flight conducted on 9 July 2024.[^cite_note-242]

### Vega-C

Main article: [Vega-C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vega-C)



[Vega](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vega_\(rocket\)) rocket

Vega is the ESA's carrier for small satellites. Developed by seven ESA members led by [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Space_Agency). It is capable of carrying a payload with a mass of between 300 and 1500 kg to an altitude of 700 km, for low [polar orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_orbit). Its maiden launch from [Kourou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guiana_Space_Centre) was on 13 February 2012.[^cite_note-243] Vega began full commercial exploitation in December 2015.[^cite_note-244]

The rocket has three solid propulsion stages and a [liquid propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_rocket) [upper stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_stage) (the [AVUM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AVUM)) for accurate orbital insertion and the ability to place multiple [payloads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payload_\(air_and_space_craft\)) into different orbits.[^cite_note-245][^cite_note-246]

A larger version of the Vega launcher, Vega-C had its first flight in July 2022.[^cite_note-247] The new evolution of the rocket incorporates a larger first stage booster, the [P120C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P120_\(rocket_stage\)) replacing the [P80](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P80_\(rocket_stage\)), an upgraded [Zefiro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zefiro_\(rocket_stage\)) second stage, and the AVUM+ upper stage. This new variant enables larger single payloads, dual payloads, return missions, and orbital transfer capabilities.[^cite_note-248]

### Future rocket development

Future projects under development within the [Future Launchers Preparatory Programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_Launchers_Preparatory_Programme) (FLPP) include the [Prometheus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prometheus_\(rocket_engine\)) reusable engine technology demonstrator, Phoebus (an upgraded second stage for [Ariane 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_6)), and [Themis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Themis_programme) (a reusable first stage).[^cite_note-249][^cite_note-250]

At the time the ESA was formed, its main goals did not encompass human space flight; rather it considered itself to be primarily a scientific research organisation for uncrewed space exploration in contrast to its American and Soviet counterparts. It is therefore not surprising that the first non-Soviet European in space was not an ESA astronaut on a European space craft; it was Czechoslovak [Vladimír Remek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladim%C3%ADr_Remek) who in 1978 became the first non-Soviet or American in space (the first man in space being [Yuri Gagarin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuri_Gagarin) of the Soviet Union) – on a [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_space_programme) [Soyuz spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz_spacecraft), followed by the Pole [Mirosław Hermaszewski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miros%C5%82aw_Hermaszewski) and East German [Sigmund Jähn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_J%C3%A4hn) in the same year. This Soviet co-operation programme, known as [Intercosmos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercosmos), primarily involved the participation of [Eastern bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_bloc) countries. In 1982, however, [Jean-Loup Chrétien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Loup_Chr%C3%A9tien) became the first [non-Communist Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Bloc) astronaut on a flight to the Soviet [Salyut 7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salyut_7) space station.

Because Chrétien did not officially fly into space as an ESA astronaut, but rather as a member of the French [CNES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNES) astronaut corps, the German [Ulf Merbold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulf_Merbold) is considered the first ESA astronaut to fly into space. He participated in the [STS-9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STS-9) [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle) mission that included the first use of the European-built [Spacelab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacelab) in 1983. STS-9 marked the beginning of an extensive ESA/NASA joint partnership that included dozens of space flights of ESA astronauts in the following years. Some of these missions with Spacelab were fully funded and organisationally and scientifically controlled by the ESA (such as two missions by Germany and one by Japan) with European astronauts as full crew members rather than guests on board. Beside paying for Spacelab flights and seats on the shuttles, the ESA continued its human space flight co-operation with the Soviet Union and later Russia, including numerous visits to [Mir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mir).

During the latter half of the 1980s, European human space flights changed from being the exception to routine and therefore, in 1990, the [European Astronaut Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Astronaut_Centre) in [Cologne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne), Germany was established. It selects and trains prospective astronauts and is responsible for the co-ordination with international partners, especially with regard to the [International Space Station](15043.md). As of 2006, the ESA astronaut corps officially included twelve members, including nationals from most large European countries except the United Kingdom.

In 2008, the ESA started to recruit new astronauts so that final selection would be due in spring 2009. Almost 10,000 people registered as astronaut candidates before registration ended in June 2008. 8,413 fulfilled the initial application criteria. Of the applicants, 918 were chosen to take part in the first stage of psychological testing, which narrowed down the field to 192. After two-stage psychological tests and medical evaluation in early 2009, as well as formal interviews, six new members of the [European Astronaut Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Astronaut_Corps) were selected – five men and one woman.[^cite_note-251]

### Crew vehicles

In the 1980s, France pressed for an independent European crew launch vehicle. Around 1978, it was decided to pursue a reusable spacecraft model and starting in November 1987 a project to create a mini-shuttle by the name of [Hermes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes_\(shuttle\)) was introduced. The craft was comparable to early proposals for the [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle) and consisted of a small reusable spaceship that would carry 3 to 5 astronauts and 3 to 4 metric tons of payload for scientific experiments. With a total maximum weight of 21 metric tons it would have been launched on the [Ariane 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_5) rocket, which was being developed at that time. It was planned solely for use in [low Earth orbit](47568.md) space flights. The planning and pre-development phase concluded in 1991; the production phase was never fully implemented because at that time the political landscape had changed significantly. With the fall of the Soviet Union, the ESA looked forward to co-operation with Russia to build a next-generation space vehicle. Thus the Hermes programme was cancelled in 1995 after about 3 billion dollars had been spent. The [Columbus space station programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbus_Man-Tended_Free_Flyer) had a similar fate.

In the 21st century, ESA started new programmes to create its own crew vehicles, most notable among its various projects and proposals is [Hopper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopper_\(spacecraft\)), whose prototype by [EADS](26220236.md), called [Phoenix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EADS_Phoenix), has already been tested. While projects such as Hopper are neither concrete nor to be realised within the next decade, other possibilities for human spaceflight in co-operation with [Roscosmos](406573.md) (the Russian space agency) have emerged. Following talks with Roscosmos in 2004 and June 2005,[^cite_note-252] a co-operation between the ESA and Roscosmos was announced to jointly work on the Russian-designed [Kliper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kliper), a reusable spacecraft that would be available for space travel beyond LEO (e.g. the moon or even Mars). It was speculated that Europe would finance part of it. A€50 million participation study for Kliper, which was expected to be approved in December 2005, was finally not approved by ESA member states. The Russian state tender for the project was subsequently cancelled in 2006.

In June 2006, ESA member states granted 15 million to the [Crew Space Transportation System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSTS) (CSTS) study, a two-year study to design a spacecraft capable of going beyond Low-Earth orbit based on the current [Soyuz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz_spacecraft) design. This project was pursued with [Roskosmos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roskosmos) instead of the cancelled Kliper proposal. A decision on the actual implementation and construction of the CSTS spacecraft was contemplated for 2008. In mid-2009 EADS Astrium was awarded a €21 million study into designing a crew vehicle based on the European ATV which was believed to be the basis of the Advanced Crew Transportation System design.[^cite_note-253] Neither of these projects was pursued further in the 2010s.

In November 2012, ESA decided to join NASA's [Orion programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orion_\(spacecraft\)). The ATV would form the basis of the [European Service Module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Service_Module) (ESM) for NASA's new crewed spacecraft. ESA may also seek to work with NASA on Orion's launch system as well to secure a seat on the spacecraft for its own astronauts.[^cite_note-254] The complete [Orion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orion_\(spacecraft\)) with ESM flew on its first mission to Lunar orbit in 2022.[^cite_note-255]

In September 2014, the ESA signed an agreement with [Sierra Nevada Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_Corporation) for co-operation in [Dream Chaser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser) project. Further studies on the Dream Chaser for European Use or [DC4EU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DC4EU) project were funded, including the feasibility of launching a Europeanised Dream Chaser onboard Ariane 5.[^cite_note-ESA_signs_agreement_2014-256][^cite_note-IBDM-257]

### International Space Station (ISS)

See also: [European contribution to the International Space Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_contribution_to_the_International_Space_Station)

With regard to the [International Space Station](15043.md) (ISS), the ESA is not represented by all of its member states:[^cite_note-258] 11 of the 22 ESA member states currently participate in the project: Belgium, Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, Netherlands, Norway, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom. Austria, Finland and Ireland chose not to participate, because of lack of interest or concerns about the expense of the project. Portugal, Luxembourg, Greece, the Czech Republic, Romania, Poland, Estonia, and Hungary joined ESA after the agreement had been signed.

ESA takes part in the construction and operation of the ISS, with contributions such as [Columbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbus_\(ISS_module\)), a science laboratory module that was brought into orbit by NASA's [STS-122](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STS-122) [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle) mission, and the [Cupola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupola_\(ISS_module\)) observatory module that was completed in July 2005 by [Alenia Spazio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alenia_Aeronautica) for the ESA. The current estimates for the ISS are approaching €100 billion in total (development, construction and 10 years of maintaining the station) of which the ESA has committed to paying €8 billion.[^cite_note-259] About 90% of the costs of the ESA's ISS share will be contributed by Germany (41%), France (28%) and Italy (20%). German ESA astronaut [Thomas Reiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Reiter) was the first long-term ISS crew member.

ESA has developed the [Automated Transfer Vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automated_Transfer_Vehicle) for ISS resupply. Each ATV has a cargo capacity of 7,667 kilograms (16,903 lb).[^cite_note-atvspecs-260] The first ATV, *[Jules Verne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jules_Verne_ATV)*, was launched on 9 March 2008 and on 3 April 2008 successfully docked with the ISS. This manoeuvre, considered a major technical feat, involved using automated systems to allow the ATV to track the ISS, moving at 27,000 km/h, and attach itself with an accuracy of 2 cm. Five vehicles were launched before the programme ended with the launch of the fifth ATV, *[Georges Lemaître](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Lema%C3%AEtre_ATV)*, in 2014.[^cite_note-spaceflightnow-261]

European Life and Physical Sciences research on board the International Space Station (ISS) is mainly based on the [European Programme for Life and Physical Sciences in Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Programme_for_Life_and_Physical_Sciences_in_Space) programme that was initiated in 2001.

## Cooperation with other countries and organisations

The ESA has signed co-operation agreements with the following states that currently neither plan to integrate as tightly with ESA institutions as Canada, nor envision future membership of the ESA: Argentina,[^cite_note-314] Brazil,[^cite_note-315] China,[^cite_note-316] India,[^cite_note-317] Russia,[^cite_note-AR2003-318] and Turkey.[^cite_note-esa-20040906-233] Additionally, the ESA has joint projects with the [EUSPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union_Agency_for_the_Space_Programme) of the European Union, [NASA](18426568.md) of the United States and is participating in the [International Space Station](15043.md) together with the United States (NASA), Russia ([Roscosmos](406573.md)), Japan ([JAXA](716184.md)), and Canada ([CSA](328618.md)).

### National space organisations of member states

- The *[Centre National d'Études Spatiales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNES)* (CNES) (National Centre for Space Study) is the French government space agency (administratively, a "public establishment of industrial and commercial character"). Its headquarters are in central Paris. CNES is the main participant on the Ariane project. Indeed, CNES designed and tested all Ariane family rockets (mainly from its centre in [Évry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89vry,_Essonne) near Paris)

- The [UK Space Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_Space_Agency) is a partnership of the UK government departments which are active in space. Through the UK Space Agency, the partners provide delegates to represent the UK on the various ESA governing bodies. Each partner funds its own programme.

- The [Italian Space Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Space_Agency) (*Agenzia Spaziale Italiana* or ASI) was founded in 1988 to promote, co-ordinate and conduct space activities in Italy. Operating under the Ministry of the Universities and of Scientific and Technological Research, the agency cooperates with numerous entities active in space technology and with the president of the Council of Ministers. Internationally, the ASI provides Italy's delegation to the Council of the European Space Agency and to its subordinate bodies.

- The [German Aerospace Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Aerospace_Center) (DLR) (German: *Deutsches Zentrum für Luft- und Raumfahrt e. V.*) is the national research centre for aviation and space flight of the Federal Republic of Germany and of other member states in the [Helmholtz Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmholtz_Association_of_German_Research_Centres). Its extensive research and development projects are included in national and international cooperative programmes. In addition to its research projects, the centre is the assigned space agency of Germany bestowing headquarters of German space flight activities and its associates.

- The [Instituto Nacional de Técnica Aeroespacial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instituto_Nacional_de_T%C3%A9cnica_Aeroespacial) (INTA) (National Institute for Aerospace Technique) is a Public Research Organisation specialised in aerospace research and technology development in Spain. Among other functions, it serves as a platform for space research and acts as a significant testing facility for the aeronautic and space sector in the country.

### NASA

The ESA has a long history of collaboration with NASA. Since ESA's astronaut corps was formed, the [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle) has been the primary launch vehicle used by the ESA's astronauts to get into space through partnership programmes with NASA. In the 1980s and 1990s, the [Spacelab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacelab) programme was an ESA-NASA joint research programme that had the ESA develop and manufacture orbital labs for the Space Shuttle for several flights in which the ESA participates with astronauts in experiments.

In October 2020, ESA entered into a memorandum of understanding (MOU) with NASA to collaborate on the [Artemis program](60758751.md), which would provide an orbiting [Lunar Gateway](53648310.md) and also accomplish the first crewed lunar landing in 50 years.[^cite_note-319] Gateway was later cancelled by NASA.[^cite_note-320][^cite_note-321]

ESA purchases seats on the NASA operated [Commercial Crew Program](64072812.md). The first ESA astronaut to be on a Commercial Crew Program mission was [Thomas Pesquet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Pesquet) who launched into space aboard [Crew Dragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_2) [Endeavour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_Dragon_Endeavour) on the [Crew-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Crew-2) mission. Following ESA astronauts on [Crew Dragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_2) missions were [Matthias Maurer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthias_Maurer) ([Crew-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Crew-3)), [Samantha Cristoforetti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samantha_Cristoforetti) ([Crew-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Crew-4)), and others.

In robotic science mission and exploration missions, NASA has been the ESA's main partner. *[Cassini–Huygens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassini%E2%80%93Huygens)* was a joint NASA-ESA mission, along with the [Infrared Space Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_Space_Observatory), [INTEGRAL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INTEGRAL), [SOHO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_and_Heliospheric_Observatory), and others. The [Hubble Space Telescope](40203.md) and [James Webb Space Telescope](434221.md) are joint projects of NASA and ESA. Possible future ESA-NASA joint projects include the [Laser Interferometer Space Antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_Interferometer_Space_Antenna), [Mars sample-return mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_sample-return_mission), and others.[^cite_note-322][^cite_note-323]

### JAXA

ESA and the [Japanese Space Agency](716184.md) (JAXA) have engaged in long-standing collaboration in various domains of space exploration, satellite technology, space science, and human spaceflight. Their partnership has evolved over the decades, focusing on joint missions, technology sharing, and international cooperation in space exploration.[^cite_note-324] Some of the programmes include [BepiColombo Mission](551228.md), [EarthCARE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EarthCARE), [Hera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hera_\(space_mission\)), and [Comet Interceptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet_Interceptor).[^cite_note-325][^cite_note-326] In 2025, ESA opened its first Asian office in Tokyo, Japan.[^cite_note-327]

### Roscosmos

In the 2000s and 2010s, ESA collaborated with [Roscosmos](406573.md) (the Russian space agency) on the development of the crewed vehicle [CSTS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSTS) (later cancelled), the use of [French Guiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Guiana)'s [Guiana Space Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guiana_Space_Centre) spaceport for launches of [Soyuz-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz-2_\(rocket\)) rockets, and the [ExoMars programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ExoMars) including the [*Schiaparelli*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schiaparelli_EDM) lander and the [Trace Gas Orbiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace_Gas_Orbiter).[^cite_note-328][^cite_note-NYT-20161019-329] In 2022, most collaboration with the Roscosmos was terminated after the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md).[^cite_note-330]

### CNSA

ESA and the [Chinese Space Agency](344618.md) (CNSA) cooperated on development of the [Double Star Mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_Star_\(satellite\)).[^cite_note-331] In 2017, ESA sent two astronauts to China for two weeks sea survival training with Chinese astronauts in [Yantai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yantai), Shandong.[^cite_note-332] In 2024, the joint [Einstein Probe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_Probe) was launched.[^cite_note-333] The joint ESA-[CAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Academy_of_Sciences) mission [SMILE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMILE_\(spacecraft\)) was launched in 2026 to image for the first time the magnetosphere of the Sun in soft X-rays and UV.[^cite_note-\:0-145][^cite_note-\:1-146]

### ISRO

ESA provided instruments for the [Indian Space Research Organisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Space_Research_Organisation) (ISRO)'s lunar mission [Chandrayaan-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandrayaan-1) in 2008.[^cite_note-334] Since 2024, ESA has been supporting ISRO's [human spaceflight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_spaceflight) programme [Gaganyaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaganyaan).[^cite_note-335][^cite_note-336][^cite_note-337][^cite_note-338]

## Link between ESA and EU

Main article: [European Union Space Programme § EU/ESA Space Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union_Space_Programme#EU/ESA_Space_Council)

The ESA is an independent space agency and not under the jurisdiction of the European Union, although they have common goals, share funding, and work together often.[^cite_note-m.esa.int-342] The initial aim of the European Union (EU) was to make the European Space Agency an [agency of the EU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agency_of_the_European_Union) by 2014.[^cite_note-343] While the EU and its member states fund together 86% of the budget of the ESA, it is not an EU agency. Furthermore, the ESA has several non-EU members, most notably the United Kingdom which [left the EU](41688778.md) while remaining a full member of the ESA. The ESA is partnered with the EU on two space programmes, the [Copernicus series of Earth observation satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicus_Programme) and the [Galileo satellite navigation system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_\(satellite_navigation\)). The ESA provided technical oversight for both as well as funding for Copernicus. The EU has shown interest in expanding into space exploration with a proposal to rename and further develop its satellite navigation agency (the [European GNSS Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_GNSS_Agency)) into the new EU Agency for the Space Programme. The ESA strongly criticized the proposal, as it was seen as competition or replacement of the ESA.

In January 2021, after years of acrimonious relations, EU and ESA officials mended their relationship, with the EU Internal Market commissioner [Thierry Breton](1545085.md) saying "The European space policy will continue to rely on the ESA and its unique technical, engineering and science expertise," and that the "ESA will continue to be the European agency for space matters. If we are to be successful in our European strategy for space, and we will be, I will need the ESA by my side." ESA director Aschbacher reciprocated, saying "I would really like to make the ESA the main agency, the go-to agency of the European Commission for all its flagship programmes." The ESA and [EUSPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union_Agency_for_the_Space_Programme) are now seen to have distinct roles and competencies, which will be officialised in the Financial Framework Partnership Agreement (FFPA). The ESA's focus will be on the technical elements of the EU space programmes, the EUSPA will handle the operational elements of those programmes.[^cite_note-Space_News-2021-344]

## Employment

As of 2023, Many other facilities are operated by national space agencies in close collaboration with the ESA. The ESA employs around 2,547 people, and thousands of contractors. Initially, new employees are contracted for an expandable four-year term, which is until the organisation's retirement age of 63. According to the ESA's documents, the staff can receive myriad of perks, such as financial childcare support, retirement plans, and financial help when migrating. The ESA also prevents employees from disclosing any private documents or correspondences to outside parties. *[Ars Technica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ars_Technica)'*&#x73; 2023 report, which contained testimonies of 18 people, suggested that there is a widespread harassment between management and its employees, especially with its contractors. Since the ESA is an international organisation, unaffiliated with any single nation, any form of legal action is difficult to raise against the organisation.[^cite_note-345]

## Security incidents

On 3 August 1984, the ESA's Paris headquarters were severely damaged and six people were hurt when a bomb exploded. It was planted by the far-left armed [Action Directe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_directe_\(armed_group\)) group.[^cite_note-346]

On 14 December 2015, hackers from [Anonymous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anonymous_\(group\)) breached the ESA's subdomains and leaked thousands of login credentials.[^cite_note-347]

In late December 2025, ESA servers suffered a data breach, the extent and severity of which was disputed.[^cite_note-348][^cite_note-349]
