# Volcanic eruption

"Eruption" redirects here. For other uses, see [Eruption (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eruption_\(disambiguation\)).



Some of the eruptive structures formed during volcanic activity (counterclockwise): a [Plinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plinian_eruption) [eruption column](1533051.md), [Hawaiian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_eruption) [pahoehoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava#P%C4%81hoehoe) flows, and a lava arc from a [Strombolian eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strombolian_eruption)

A **volcanic eruption** occurs when material is expelled from a [volcanic vent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_vent) or [fissure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fissure_vent). Several types of volcanic eruptions have been distinguished by [volcanologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanologists). These are often named after famous [volcanoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcano) where that type of behavior has been observed. Some volcanoes may exhibit only one characteristic type of eruption during a period of activity, while others may display an entire sequence of types all in one eruptive series.

There are three main types of volcanic eruptions. [Magmatic eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magmatic_eruption) involve the decompression of gas within [magma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma) that propels it forward. [Phreatic eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phreatic_eruption) are driven by the [superheating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superheating) of [steam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam) due to the close proximity of [magma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma). This type exhibits no magmatic release, instead causing the [granulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granulation_\(process\)) of existing rock. [Phreatomagmatic eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phreatomagmatic_eruption) are driven by the direct interaction of magma and water, as opposed to phreatic eruptions, where no fresh magma reaches the surface.

Within these broad eruptive types are several subtypes. The weakest are [Hawaiian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_eruption) and [submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_eruption), then [Strombolian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strombolian_eruption), followed by [Vulcanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulcanian_eruption) and [Surtseyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surtseyan_eruption). The stronger eruptive types are [Pelean eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelean_eruption), followed by [Plinian eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plinian_eruption); the strongest eruptions are called [ultra-Plinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultra-Plinian). [Subglacial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subglacial_eruption) and phreatic eruptions are defined by their eruptive mechanism, and vary in strength. An important measure of eruptive strength is the [Volcanic Explosivity Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_Explosivity_Index), an [order-of-magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_magnitude) scale, ranging from 0 to 8, that often correlates to eruptive types.

## Mechanisms



Diagram showing the scale of [VEI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_Explosivity_Index) correlation with total [ejecta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ejecta) volume

Volcanic eruptions arise through three main mechanisms:[^cite_note-vol_cash-1]

- Gas release under decompression, causing magmatic eruptions

- Ejection of entrained particles during steam eruptions, causing phreatic eruptions

- Thermal contraction from chilling on contact with water, causing phreatomagmatic eruptions

In terms of activity, there are [explosive eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explosive_eruption) and [effusive eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effusive_eruption). The former are characterized by gas-driven explosions that propel magma and tephra.[^cite_note-vol_cash-1] The latter pour out lava without significant explosion.[^cite_note-usgs-effusive-2]

## Impact

Volcanic eruptions vary widely in strength. On the one extreme there are effusive Hawaiian eruptions, which are characterized by [lava fountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava_fountain) and [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscosity) [lava flows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava), which are typically not very dangerous. On the other extreme, Plinian eruptions are large, violent, and highly dangerous explosive events. Volcanoes are not bound to one eruptive style, and frequently display many different types, both passive and explosive, even in the span of a single eruptive cycle.[^cite_note-gsc-class-3] Volcanoes do not always erupt vertically from a single crater near their peak, either. Some volcanoes exhibit [lateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateral_eruption) and [fissure eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fissure_eruption). Notably, many Hawaiian eruptions start from [rift zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rift_zone).[^cite_note-hvw-haw-4] Scientists believed that pulses of magma mixed together in the [magma chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma_chamber) before climbing upward—a process estimated to take several thousands of years. [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University) volcanologists found that the eruption of Costa Rica's [Irazú Volcano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraz%C3%BA_Volcano) in 1963 was likely triggered by magma that took a nonstop route from the [mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_mantle) over just a few months.[^cite_note-5]

> It is important when studying the products of explosive eruptions to distinguish between...:
>
> 1. *magnitude* - the total volume;
>
> 2. *intensity* - the emission rate;
>
> 3. *dispersive power* - the extent of dispersal;
>
> 4. *violence* - the importance of momentum;
>
> 5. *destructive potential* - the extent of destruction of life or property (real or potential);

### Volcanic explosivity index

Main article: [Volcanic explosivity index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_explosivity_index)

See also: [List of largest volcanic eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_volcanic_eruptions)

The volcanic explosivity index (commonly shortened to VEI) is a scale, from 0 to 8, for measuring the strength of eruptions but does not capture all of the properties that may be perceived to be important. It is used by the [Smithsonian Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smithsonian_Institution)'s [Global Volcanism Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Volcanism_Program) in assessing the impact of historic and prehistoric lava flows. It operates in a way similar to the [Richter scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richter_magnitude_scale) for [earthquakes](10106.md), in that each interval in value represents a tenfold increasing in magnitude (it is [logarithmic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithm)).[^cite_note-hvw-vei-7] The vast majority of volcanic eruptions are of VEIs between 0 and 2.[^cite_note-gsc-class-3]

## Magmatic

[Magmatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma) eruptions produce [juvenile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ejecta) [clasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clastic_rock) during [explosive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explosive_eruption) [decompression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compression_\(physical\)) from gas release. They range in intensity from the relatively small [lava fountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava_fountain) on [Hawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_eruption) to catastrophic [Ultra-Plinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultra-Plinian) [eruption columns](1533051.md) more than 30 km (19 mi) high, bigger than the [eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eruption_of_Mount_Vesuvius_in_79_AD) that buried [Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompeii).[^cite_note-vol_cash-1]

### Hawaiian

Hawaiian eruptions are a type of volcanic eruption named after the [Hawaiian volcanoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii_hotspot), such as [Mauna Loa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauna_Loa), where this eruptive type is a hallmark. Hawaiian eruptions are calm volcanic events, characterized by the [effusive eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effusive_eruption) of very [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscosity) [basalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basalt)-type [lavas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava) with low [gaseous content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_gas). The volume of ejected material from Hawaiian eruptions is less than half of that found in other eruptive types. Steady production of small amounts of lava builds up the large, broad form of a [shield volcano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shield_volcano). Eruptions are not centralized at the main summit as with other volcanic types, and often occur at vents around the summit and from [fissure vents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fissure_vent) radiating out of the center.[^cite_note-hvw-haw-4]

Hawaiian eruptions often begin as a line of vent eruptions along a [fissure vent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fissure_vent), a so-called "curtain of fire." These die down as the lava begins to concentrate at a few of the vents. Central-vent eruptions, meanwhile, often take the form of large [lava fountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava_fountain) (both continuous and sporadic), which can reach heights of hundreds of meters or more. The particles from lava fountains usually cool in the air before hitting the ground, resulting in the accumulation of cindery [scoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scoria) fragments; when the air is especially thick with [clasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clast), they cannot cool off fast enough due to the surrounding heat, and hit the ground still hot, the accumulation of which forms [spatter cones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_cone#Spatter_cone). If eruptive rates are high enough, they may even form splatter-fed lava flows. Hawaiian eruptions are often extremely long lived; [Puʻu ʻŌʻō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pu%CA%BBu_%CA%BB%C5%8C%CA%BB%C5%8D), a volcanic cone on [Kilauea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilauea), erupted continuously for over 35 years. Another Hawaiian volcanic feature is the formation of active [lava lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava_lake), self-maintaining pools of raw lava with a thin crust of semi-cooled rock.[^cite_note-hvw-haw-4]



Ropey [pahoehoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pahoehoe) lava from [Kilauea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilauea), [Hawaiʻi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii_\(island\))

Flows from Hawaiian eruptions are basaltic, and can be divided into two types by their structural characteristics. [Pahoehoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pahoehoe) lava is a relatively smooth lava flow that can be billowy or ropey. They can move as one sheet, by the advancement of "toes", or as a snaking lava column.[^cite_note-10] [A'a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A'a) lava flows are denser and more viscous than pahoehoe, and tend to move slower. Flows can measure 2 to 20 m (7 to 66 ft) thick. A'a flows are so thick that the outside layers cools into a rubble-like mass, insulating the still-hot interior and preventing it from cooling. A'a lava moves in a peculiar way—the front of the flow steepens due to pressure from behind until it breaks off, after which the general mass behind it moves forward. Pahoehoe lava can sometimes become A'a lava due to increasing [viscosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscosity) or increasing rate of [shear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_\(geology\)), but A'a lava never turns into pahoehoe flow.[^cite_note-hvw-basaltic-11]

Hawaiian eruptions are responsible for several unique volcanological objects. Small volcanic particles are carried and formed by the wind, chilling quickly into teardrop-shaped [glassy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_glass) fragments known as [Pele's tears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pele's_tears) (after [Pele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pele_\(deity\)), the Hawaiian volcano deity). During especially high winds these chunks may even take the form of long drawn-out strands, known as [Pele's hair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pele's_hair). Sometimes basalt aerates into [reticulite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reticulite), the lowest density rock type on earth.[^cite_note-hvw-haw-4]

Although Hawaiian eruptions are named after the volcanoes of Hawaii, they are not necessarily restricted to them; the highest lava fountain recorded was during the 23 November 2013 eruption of [Mount Etna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Etna) in Italy, which reached a stable height of around 2,500 m (8,200 ft) for 18 minutes, briefly peaking at a height of 3,400 m (11,000 ft).[^cite_note-12]

Volcanoes known to have Hawaiian activity include:

- Puʻu ʻŌʻō, a [parasitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitic_cone) [cinder cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinder_cone) located on [Kilauea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilauea) on the [island of Hawaiʻi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii_\(island\)) which erupted continuously from 1983 to 2018. The eruptions began with a 6 km (4 mi)-long [fissure-based](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fissure_vent) "curtain of fire" on 3 January 1983. These gave way to centralized eruptions on the site of Kilauea's east rift, eventually building up the cone.

### Strombolian

Strombolian eruptions are a type of volcanic eruption named after the volcano [Stromboli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stromboli), which has been erupting nearly continuously for centuries.[^cite_note-hvw-strom-13] Strombolian eruptions are driven by the bursting of gas bubbles within the [magma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma). These gas bubbles within the magma accumulate and coalesce into large bubbles, called [gas slugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_slug). These grow large enough to rise through the lava column.[^cite_note-sci-strom-14] Upon reaching the surface, the difference in [air pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_pressure) causes the bubble to burst with a loud pop,[^cite_note-hvw-strom-13] throwing magma in the air in a way similar to a [soap bubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap_bubble). Because of the high [gas pressures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_pressure) associated with the lavas, continued activity is generally in the form of episodic [explosive eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explosive_eruption) accompanied by the distinctive loud blasts.[^cite_note-hvw-strom-13] During eruptions, these blasts occur as often as every few minutes.[^cite_note-ut-strom-15]

The term "Strombolian" has been used indiscriminately to describe a wide variety of volcanic eruptions, varying from small volcanic blasts to large [eruptive columns](1533051.md). In reality, true Strombolian eruptions are characterized by short-lived and explosive eruptions of lavas with intermediate [viscosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscosity), often ejected high into the air. Columns can measure hundreds of meters in height. The lavas formed by Strombolian eruptions are a form of relatively viscous [basaltic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basalt) lava, and its end product is mostly [scoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scoria).[^cite_note-hvw-strom-13] The relative passivity of Strombolian eruptions, and its non-damaging nature to its source vent allow Strombolian eruptions to continue unabated for thousands of years, and also makes it one of the least dangerous eruptive types.[^cite_note-ut-strom-15]



An example of the lava arcs formed during Strombolian activity. This image is of [Stromboli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stromboli) itself.

Strombolian eruptions eject [volcanic bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_bomb) and [lapilli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapilli) fragments that travel in parabolic paths before landing around their source vent.[^cite_note-16] The steady accumulation of small fragments builds [cinder cones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinder_cone) composed completely of basaltic [pyroclasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroclast). This form of accumulation tends to result in well-ordered rings of [tephra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tephra).[^cite_note-hvw-strom-13]

Strombolian eruptions are similar to [Hawaiian eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_eruption), but there are differences. Strombolian eruptions are noisier, produce no sustained [eruptive columns](1533051.md), do not produce some volcanic products associated with Hawaiian volcanism (specifically [Pele's tears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pele's_tears) and [Pele's hair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pele's_hair)), and produce fewer molten lava flows (although the eruptive material does tend to form small rivulets).[^cite_note-hvw-strom-13][^cite_note-ut-strom-15]

Volcanoes known to have Strombolian activity include:

- [Parícutin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Par%C3%ADcutin), [Mexico](3966054.md), which erupted from a fissure in a cornfield in 1943. Two years into its life, pyroclastic activity began to wane, and the outpouring of lava from its base became its primary mode of activity. Eruptions ceased in 1952, and the final height was 424 m (1,391 ft). This was the first time that scientists are able to observe the complete life cycle of a volcano.

Vulcanian eruptions are a type of volcanic eruption named after the volcano [Vulcano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulcano).[^cite_note-hvw-vulc-24] It was named so following [Giuseppe Mercalli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Mercalli)'s observations of its 1888–1890 eruptions.[^cite_note-ut-vulc-25] In Vulcanian eruptions, intermediate [viscous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscosity) magma within the volcano make it difficult for [vesiculate gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesicular_texture) to escape. Similar to Strombolian eruptions, this leads to the buildup of high [gas pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_pressure), eventually popping the cap holding the magma down and resulting in an explosive eruption. Unlike Strombolian eruptions, ejected lava fragments are not aerodynamic; this is due to the higher viscosity of Vulcanian magma and the greater incorporation of [crystalline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystalline) material broken off from the former cap. They are also more explosive than their Strombolian counterparts, with [eruptive columns](1533051.md) often reaching between 5 and 10 km (3 and 6 mi) high. Lastly, Vulcanian deposits are [andesitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andesitic) to [dacitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dacitic) rather than [basaltic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basalt).[^cite_note-hvw-vulc-24]

Initial Vulcanian activity is characterized by a series of short-lived explosions, lasting a few minutes to a few hours and typified by the ejection of [volcanic bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_bomb) and [blocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_block). These eruptions wear down the [lava dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava_dome) holding the magma down, and it disintegrates, leading to much more quiet and continuous eruptions. Thus an early sign of future Vulcanian activity is lava dome growth, and its collapse generates an outpouring of [pyroclastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroclast) material down the volcano's slope.[^cite_note-hvw-vulc-24]



[Tavurvur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tavurvur) in [Papua New Guinea](22943.md) erupting

Deposits near the source vent consist of large [volcanic blocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_block) and [bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_bomb), with so-called "[bread-crust bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_bomb#Types_of_bombs)" being especially common. These deeply cracked volcanic chunks form when the exterior of ejected lava cools quickly into a [glassy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_glass) or [fine-grained](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_size_\(grain_size\)) shell, but the inside continues to cool and [vesiculate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesicular_texture). The center of the fragment expands, cracking the exterior. The bulk of Vulcanian deposits are fine grained [ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_ash). The ash is only moderately dispersed, and its abundance indicates a high degree of [fragmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debris#Geological), the result of high gas contents within the magma. In some cases these have been found to be the result of interaction with [meteoric water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteoric_water), suggesting that Vulcanian eruptions are partially [hydrovolcanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phreatomagmatic_eruption).[^cite_note-hvw-vulc-24]

Volcanoes that have exhibited Vulcanian activity include:

- [Sakurajima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakurajima), [Japan](15573.md) has been the site of Vulcanian activity near-continuously since 1955.

  [^cite_note-26]

- [Tavurvur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tavurvur), [Papua New Guinea](22943.md), one of several volcanoes in the [Rabaul Caldera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabaul_Caldera).

  [^cite_note-hvw-vulc-24]

- [Irazú Volcano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraz%C3%BA_Volcano) in [Costa Rica](5551.md) exhibited Vulcanian activity in [its 1963–1965 eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1963_eruption_of_Iraz%C3%BA_Volcano).

  [^cite_note-vhp-vulc-27]

- [Anak Krakatoa](1735509.md), Indonesia, repeated vulcanian activities since its rise in 1930 until the present time.

  [^cite_note-28]

  [^cite_note-29]

Vulcanian eruptions are estimated to make up at least half of all known [Holocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene) eruptions.[^cite_note-30]

### Peléan

Peléan eruptions (or [nuée ardente](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroclastic_flow)) are a type of volcanic eruption named after the volcano [Mount Pelée](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Pel%C3%A9e) in [Martinique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martinique), the site of a Peléan eruption in 1902 that is one of the worst natural disasters in history. In Peléan eruptions, a large amount of gas, dust, ash, and lava fragments are blown out the volcano's central crater,[^cite_note-ut-pel-31] driven by the collapse of [rhyolite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhyolite), [dacite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dacite), and [andesite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andesite) [lava domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava_dome) that often creates large [eruptive columns](1533051.md). An early sign of a coming eruption is the growth of a so-called Peléan or [lava spine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava_spine), a bulge in the volcano's summit preempting its total collapse.[^cite_note-nhd-volc-32] The material collapses upon itself, forming a fast-moving [pyroclastic flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroclastic_flow)[^cite_note-ut-pel-31] (known as a [block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_block)-and-[ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_ash) flow)[^cite_note-33] that moves down the side of the mountain at tremendous speeds, often over 150 km (93 mi) per hour. These [landslides](37208.md) make Peléan eruptions one of the most dangerous in the world, capable of tearing through populated areas and causing serious loss of life. The [1902 eruption of Mount Pelée](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Pel%C3%A9e#1902_eruption) caused tremendous destruction, killing more than 30,000 people and completely destroying [St. Pierre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Pierre,_Martinique), the [worst volcanic event in the 20th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_natural_disasters_by_death_toll#Volcanic_eruptions).[^cite_note-ut-pel-31]

Peléan eruptions are characterized most prominently by the [incandescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incandescence) pyroclastic flows that they drive. The mechanics of a Peléan eruption are very similar to that of a Vulcanian eruption, except that in Peléan eruptions the volcano's structure is able to withstand more pressure, hence the eruption occurs as one large explosion rather than several smaller ones.[^cite_note-34]

Volcanoes known to have Peléan activity include:

- [Mount Pelée](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Pel%C3%A9e), [Martinique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martinique). The 1902 eruption of Mount Pelée completely devastated the island, destroying [St. Pierre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Pierre,_Martinique) and leaving only 3 survivors.

  &#x20;The eruption was directly preceded by lava dome growth.

  [^cite_note-hvw-vulc-24]

- [Mayon Volcano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayon_Volcano), the [Philippines'](23440.md) most active volcano. It has been the site of many different types of eruptions, Peléan included. Approximately 40 ravines radiate from the summit and provide pathways for frequent [pyroclastic flows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroclastic_flow) and [mudflows](2431323.md) to the lowlands below. Mayon's most violent eruption occurred in 1814 and was responsible for over 1200 deaths.

  [^cite_note-36]

- The [1951 eruption of Mount Lamington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1951_eruption_of_Mount_Lamington). Prior to this eruption the peak had not even been recognized as a volcano. Over 3,000 people were killed, and it has become a benchmark for studying large Peléan eruptions.

  [^cite_note-37]

- [Mount Sinabung](8494859.md), Indonesia. History of its eruptions since 2013 are showing the volcano emits pyroclastic flows with frequent collapses of its lava domes.

### Plinian

Plinian eruptions (or Vesuvian eruptions) are a type of volcanic eruption named for the historical [eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eruption_of_Mount_Vesuvius_in_79) AD that buried the [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) towns of [Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompeii) and [Herculaneum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herculaneum) and, specifically, for its chronicler [Pliny the Younger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Younger).[^cite_note-hvw-plin-40] The process powering Plinian eruptions starts in the [magma chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma_chamber), where [dissolved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_\(chemistry\)) [volatile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volatile_\(astrogeology\)#Igneous_petrology) [gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas) are stored in the magma. The gases [vesiculate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesicular_texture) and accumulate as they rise through the [magma conduit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma). These bubbles agglutinate and once they reach a certain size (about 75% of the total volume of the magma conduit) they explode. The narrow confines of the conduit force the gases and associated magma up, forming an [eruptive column](1533051.md). Eruption velocity is controlled by the gas contents of the column, and low-strength surface rocks commonly crack under the pressure of the eruption, forming a flared outgoing structure that pushes the gases even faster.[^cite_note-hvw-erup-plin-41]

These massive eruptive columns are the distinctive feature of a Plinian eruption, and reach up 2 to 45 km (1 to 28 mi) into the [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere). The densest part of the plume, directly above the volcano, is driven internally by [gas expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_expansion). As it reaches higher into the air the plume expands and becomes less dense, [convection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convection) and [thermal expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_expansion) of [volcanic ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_ash) drive it even further up into the [stratosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratosphere). At the top of the plume, powerful winds may drive the plume away from the [volcano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcano).[^cite_note-hvw-erup-plin-41]



21 April 1990 [eruptive column](1533051.md) from [Redoubt Volcano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redoubt_Volcano), as viewed to the west from the [Kenai Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenai_Peninsula)

These highly [explosive eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explosive_eruption) are usually associated with volatile-rich [dacitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dacitic) to [rhyolitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhyolitic) lavas, and occur most typically at [stratovolcanoes](162404.md). Eruptions can last anywhere from hours to days, with longer eruptions being associated with more [felsic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felsic) volcanoes. Although they are usually associated with felsic magma, Plinian eruptions can occur at [basaltic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basalt) volcanoes, if the [magma chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma_chamber) [differentiates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igneous_differentiation) with upper portions rich in [silicon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_dioxide),[^cite_note-hvw-plin-40] or if magma ascends rapidly.[^cite_note-42]

Plinian eruptions are similar to both Vulcanian and Strombolian eruptions, except that rather than creating discrete explosive events, Plinian eruptions form sustained eruptive columns. They are also similar to Hawaiian [lava fountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava_fountain) in that both eruptive types produce sustained eruption columns maintained by the growth of bubbles that move up at about the same speed as the magma surrounding them.[^cite_note-hvw-plin-40]

Regions affected by Plinian eruptions are subjected to heavy [pumice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pumice) airfall affecting an area 0.5 to 50 km<sup>3</sup> (0 to 12 cu mi) in size.[^cite_note-hvw-plin-40] The material in the ash plume eventually finds its way back to the ground, covering the landscape in a thick layer of many cubic kilometers of ash.[^cite_note-ut-plinian-43]



[Lahar flows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lahar) from the 1985 eruption of [Nevado del Ruiz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevado_del_Ruiz), which totally [destroyed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armero_tragedy) [Armero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armero) in Colombia

The most dangerous eruptive feature are the [pyroclastic flows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroclastic_flow) generated by material collapse, which move down the side of the mountain at extreme speeds[^cite_note-hvw-plin-40] of up to 700 km (435 mi) per hour and with the ability to extend the reach of the eruption hundreds of kilometers.[^cite_note-ut-plinian-43] The ejection of hot material from the volcano's summit melts snowbanks and ice deposits on the volcano, which mixes with [tephra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tephra) to form [lahars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lahar), fast moving [mudflows](2431323.md) with the consistency of wet concrete that move at the speed of a [river rapid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapids).[^cite_note-hvw-plin-40]

Major Plinian eruptive events include:

- The [AD 79 eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eruption_of_Mount_Vesuvius_in_79_AD) of [Mount Vesuvius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Vesuvius) buried the [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) towns of [Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompeii) and [Herculaneum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herculaneum) under a layer of [ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_ash) and [tephra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tephra).

  &#x20;It is the model Plinian eruption. Mount Vesuvius has erupted several times since then. Its last eruption was in 1944 and caused problems for the allied armies as they advanced through Italy.

## Phreatomagmatic

Main article: [Phreatomagmatic eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phreatomagmatic_eruption)

Phreatomagmatic eruptions are eruptions that arise from interactions between [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) and [magma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma). They are driven by [thermal contraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_expansion) of magma when it comes in contact with water (as distinguished from magmatic eruptions, which are driven by thermal expansion). This temperature difference between the two causes violent water-lava interactions that make up the eruption. The products of phreatomagmatic eruptions are believed to be more regular in shape and [finer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granularity) [grained](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_size_\(grain_size\)) than the products of magmatic eruptions because of the differences in eruptive mechanisms.[^cite_note-vol_cash-1][^cite_note-phreato-49]

There is debate about the exact nature of phreatomagmatic eruptions, and some scientists believe that [fuel-coolant reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel-coolant_interaction) may be more critical to the explosive nature than thermal contraction.[^cite_note-phreato-49] Fuel coolant reactions may fragment the volcanic material by propagating [stress waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compressive_stress), widening cracks and increasing [surface area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_area) that ultimately leads to rapid cooling and explosive contraction-driven eruptions.[^cite_note-vol_cash-1]

### Surtseyan

Main article: [Surtseyan eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surtseyan_eruption)

Layers of [lava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava) and [ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_ash) 6. [Stratum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratum) 7. [Magma conduit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma_conduit) 8. [Magma chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma_chamber) 9. [Dike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dike_\(geology\))) Click for larger version.

A Surtseyan (or hydrovolcanic) eruption is a type of volcanic eruption characterized by shallow-water interactions between water and lava, named after its most famous example, the eruption and formation of the island of [Surtsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surtsey) off the coast of [Iceland](14531.md) in 1963. Surtseyan eruptions are the "wet" equivalent of ground-based [Strombolian eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strombolian_eruption), but because they take place in water they are much more explosive. As water is heated by lava, it [flashes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superheated_water) into [steam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam) and expands violently, fragmenting the magma it contacts into fine-grained [ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_ash). Surtseyan eruptions are typical of shallow-water [volcanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seamount) [oceanic islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_island), but they are not confined to seamounts. They can happen on land as well, where [rising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma_conduit) [magma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma) that comes into contact with an [aquifer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquifer) (water-bearing rock formation) at shallow levels under the volcano can cause them.[^cite_note-hvw-surtsyen-50] The products of Surtseyan eruptions are generally [oxidized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidized) [palagonite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palagonite) [basalts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basalt) (though [andesitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andesitic) eruptions do occur, albeit rarely), and like Strombolian eruptions Surtseyan eruptions are generally continuous or otherwise rhythmic.[^cite_note-ua-surtseyan-51]

A defining feature of a Surtseyan eruption is the formation of a [pyroclastic surge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroclastic_surge) (or *base surge*), a ground hugging radial cloud that develops along with the [eruption column](1533051.md). Base surges are caused by the gravitational collapse of a [vaporous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor) eruptive column, one that is denser overall than a regular volcanic column. The densest part of the cloud is nearest to the vent, resulting in a wedge shape. Associated with these laterally moving rings are [dune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sand_dune)-shaped depositions of rock left behind by the lateral movement. These are occasionally disrupted by [bomb sags](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bomb_sag), rock that was flung out by the explosive eruption and followed a [ballistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistics) path to the ground. Accumulations of wet, spherical ash known as [accretionary lapilli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accretionary_lapilli) are another common surge indicator.[^cite_note-hvw-surtsyen-50]

Over time Surtseyan eruptions tend to form [maars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maar), broad low-[relief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relief) [volcanic craters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_crater) dug into the ground, and [tuff rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuff_ring), circular structures built of rapidly quenched lava. These structures are associated with single vent eruptions. If eruptions arise along [fracture zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fracture_zone), [rift zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rift_zone) may be dug out. Such eruptions tend to be more violent than those which form tuff rings or maars, an example being the [1886 eruption of Mount Tarawera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Tarawera#1886_eruption).[^cite_note-hvw-surtsyen-50][^cite_note-ua-surtseyan-51] [Littoral cones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Littoral_cone) are another hydrovolcanic feature, generated by the explosive deposition of basaltic tephra (although they are not truly volcanic vents). They form when lava accumulates within cracks in lava, superheats and explodes in a [steam explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_explosion), breaking the rock apart and depositing it on the volcano's flank. Consecutive explosions of this type eventually generate the cone.[^cite_note-hvw-surtsyen-50]

Volcanoes known to have Surtseyan activity include:

- [Surtsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surtsey), [Iceland](14531.md). The volcano built itself up from depth and emerged above the [Atlantic Ocean](698.md) off the coast of Iceland in 1963. Initial hydrovolcanics were highly explosive, but as the volcano grew, rising lava interacted less with water and more with air, until finally Surtseyan activity waned and became more Strombolian.

  [^cite_note-hvw-surtsyen-50]

- [Ukinrek maars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukinrek_maars) in [Alaska](624.md), 1977, and [Capelinhos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capelinhos) in the [Azores](3226.md), 1957, both examples of above-water Surtseyan activity.

  [^cite_note-hvw-surtsyen-50]

- [Mount Tarawera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Tarawera) in [New Zealand](4913064.md) erupted along a [rift zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rift_zone) in 1886, killing 150 people.

  [^cite_note-hvw-surtsyen-50]

### Submarine

Main article: [Submarine eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_eruption)



Diagram of a [Submarine eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_eruption). (key: 1. [Water vapor cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor) 2. [Water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) 3. [Stratum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratum) 4. [Lava flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava) 5. [Magma conduit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma_conduit) 6. [Magma chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma_chamber) 7. [Dike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dike_\(geology\)) 8. [Pillow lava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pillow_lava)) Click to enlarge.

Submarine eruptions occur underwater. An estimated 75% of volcanic eruptive volume is generated by submarine eruptions near [mid ocean ridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid_ocean_ridge) alone. Problems detecting [deep sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_sea) volcanic eruptions meant their details were virtually unknown until advances in the 1990s made it possible to observe them.[^cite_note-noaa-submarine-54]

Submarine eruptions may produce [seamounts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seamount), which may break the surface and form volcanic islands.

Submarine volcanism is driven by various processes. Volcanoes near [plate boundaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divergent_boundary) and [mid-ocean ridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-ocean_ridge) are built by the [decompression melting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igneous_rock#Decompression) of mantle rock that rises on an upwelling portion of a convection cell to the crustal surface. Eruptions associated with [subducting zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subduction), meanwhile, are driven by subducting [plates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics) that add [volatiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volatile_\(astrogeology\)) to the rising plate, lowering its [melting point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melting_point). Each process generates different rock; mid-ocean ridge volcanics are primarily [basaltic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basalt), whereas subduction flows are mostly [calc-alkaline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calc-alkaline), and more explosive and [viscous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscous).[^cite_note-oceanography-geo-55]

Spreading rates along mid-ocean ridges vary widely, from 2 cm (0.8 in) per year at the [Mid-Atlantic Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-Atlantic_Ridge), to up to 16 cm (6 in) along the [East Pacific Rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Pacific_Rise). Higher spreading rates are a probable cause for higher levels of volcanism. The technology for studying seamount eruptions did not exist until advancements in [hydrophone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrophone) technology made it possible to "listen" to [acoustic waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustic_wave), known as T-waves, released by [submarine earthquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_earthquake) associated with submarine volcanic eruptions. The reason for this is that land-based [seismometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismometer) cannot detect sea-based earthquakes below a [magnitude](19467352.md) of 4, but acoustic waves travel well in water and over long periods of time. A system in the [North Pacific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pacific), maintained by the [United States Navy](20518076.md) and originally intended for the detection of [submarines](28825.md), has detected an event on average every 2 to 3 years.[^cite_note-noaa-submarine-54]

The most common underwater flow is [pillow lava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pillow_lava), a rounded lava flow named for its unusual shape. Less common are [glassy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_glass), marginal sheet flows, indicative of larger-scale flows. [Volcaniclastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcaniclastic) [sedimentary rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary_rock) are common in shallow-water environments. As plate movement starts to carry the volcanoes away from their eruptive source, eruption rates start to die down, and [water erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erosion#Water) grinds the volcano down. The final stages of eruption cap the seamount in [alkalic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkaline_magma_series) flows.[^cite_note-oceanography-geo-55] There are about 100,000 deepwater volcanoes in the world,[^cite_note-56] although most are beyond the active stage of their life.[^cite_note-oceanography-geo-55] Some exemplary seamounts are [Kamaʻehuakanaloa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kama%CA%BBehuakanaloa_Seamount) (formerly Loihi), [Bowie Seamount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowie_Seamount), [Davidson Seamount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davidson_Seamount), and [Axial Seamount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_Seamount).

### Subglacial

Main article: [Subglacial eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subglacial_eruption)



A diagram of a [Subglacial eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subglacial_eruption). (key: 1. [Water vapor cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor) 2. [Crater lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_crater_lake) 3. [Ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice) 4. Layers of [lava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava) and [ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_ash) 5. [Stratum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratum) 6. [Pillow lava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pillow_lava) 7. [Magma conduit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma_conduit) 8. [Magma chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma_chamber) 9. [Dike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dike_\(geology\))) Click for larger version.

Subglacial eruptions are a type of volcanic eruption characterized by interactions between lava and [ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice), often under a [glacier](12463.md). The nature of glaciovolcanism dictates that it occurs at areas of high [latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latitude) and high [altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude).[^cite_note-ubc-glacier-57] It has been suggested that subglacial volcanoes that are not actively erupting often dump [heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat) into the ice covering them, producing [meltwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meltwater).[^cite_note-bbc-antarctic-58] This meltwater mix means that subglacial eruptions often generate dangerous [jökulhlaups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%B6kulhlaup) ([floods](50482.md)) and [lahars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lahar).[^cite_note-ubc-glacier-57]

The study of glaciovolcanism is still a relatively new field. Early accounts described the unusual flat-topped steep-sided volcanoes (called [tuyas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuya)) in [Iceland](14531.md) that were suggested to have formed from eruptions below ice. The first English-language paper on the subject was published in 1947 by [William Henry Mathews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Mathews), describing the [Tuya Butte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuya_Butte) field in northwest [British Columbia](3392.md), [Canada](5042916.md). The eruptive process that builds these structures, originally inferred in the paper,[^cite_note-ubc-glacier-57] begins with volcanic growth below the glacier. At first the eruptions resemble those that occur in the deep sea, forming piles of [pillow lava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pillow_lava) at the base of the volcanic structure. Some of the lava shatters when it comes in contact with the cold ice, forming a [glassy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_glass) [breccia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breccia) called [hyaloclastite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyaloclastite). After a while the ice finally melts into a lake, and the more explosive eruptions of [Surtseyan activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surtseyan_eruption) begins, building up flanks made up of mostly hyaloclastite. Eventually the lake boils off from continued volcanism, and the lava flows become more [effusive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effusive_eruption) and thicken as the lava cools much more slowly, often forming [columnar jointing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columnar_jointing). Well-preserved tuyas show all of these stages, for example [Hjorleifshofdi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hjorleifshofdi?action=edit\&redlink=1) in Iceland.[^cite_note-about-tuya-59]

Products of volcano-ice interactions stand as various structures, whose shape is dependent on complex eruptive and environmental interactions. Glacial volcanism is a good indicator of past ice distribution, making it an important climatic marker. Since they are embedded in ice, as glacial ice retreats worldwide there are concerns that [tuyas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuya) and other structures may destabilize, resulting in mass [landslides](37208.md). Evidence of volcanic-glacial interactions are evident in [Iceland](14531.md) and parts of [British Columbia](3392.md), and it is even possible that they play a role in [deglaciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_age).[^cite_note-ubc-glacier-57]



[Herðubreið](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Her%C3%B0ubrei%C3%B0), a [tuya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuya) in [Iceland](14531.md)

Glaciovolcanic products have been identified in Iceland, the Canadian province of British Columbia, the U.S. states of [Hawaii](13270.md) and [Alaska](624.md), the [Cascade Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cascade_Range) of western North America, [South America](26769.md) and even on the planet [Mars](14640471.md).[^cite_note-ubc-glacier-57] Volcanoes known to have subglacial activity include:

- [Mauna Kea](267366.md) in tropical [Hawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii_\(island\)). There is evidence of past subglacial eruptive activity on the volcano in the form of a subglacial deposit on its summit. The eruptions originated about 10,000 years ago, during the last [ice age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_age), when the summit of Mauna Kea was covered in ice.

  [^cite_note-vw-glac-60]

- In 2008, the [British Antarctic Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Antarctic_Survey) reported a volcanic eruption under the [Antarctica](18959138.md) [ice sheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_sheet) 2,200 years ago. It is believed to be that this was the biggest eruption in Antarctica in the last 10,000 years. Volcanic ash deposits from the volcano were identified through an [airborne radar survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerial_survey), buried under later snowfalls in the [Hudson Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudson_Mountains), close to [Pine Island Glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pine_Island_Glacier).

  [^cite_note-bbc-antarctic-58]

- [Iceland](14531.md), well known for both [glaciers](12463.md) and [volcanoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcano), is often a site of subglacial eruptions. An example an eruption under the [Vatnajökull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vatnaj%C3%B6kull) [ice cap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_cap) in 1996, which occurred under an estimated 2,500 ft (762 m) of ice.

  [^cite_note-hvo-submarine-61]

- As part of the [search for life on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_on_Mars_\(planet\)), scientists have suggested that there may be subglacial volcanoes on the red planet. Several potential sites of such volcanism have been reviewed, and compared extensively with similar features in Iceland:

  [^cite_note-sd-mars-62]

> Viable microbial communities have been found living in deep (−2800 m) geothermal groundwater at 349 K and pressures >300 bar. Furthermore, microbes have been postulated to exist in basaltic rocks in rinds of altered volcanic glass. All of these conditions could exist in polar regions of Mars today where subglacial volcanism has occurred.

## Phreatic

Main article: [Phreatic eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phreatic_eruption)



Diagram of a [phreatic eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phreatic_eruption). (key: 1. [Water vapor cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor) 2. [Magma conduit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma_conduit) 3. Layers of [lava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava) and [ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_ash) 4. [Stratum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratum) 5. [Water table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_table) 6. [Explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explosive_eruption) 7. [Magma chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma_chamber))

Phreatic eruptions (or steam-blast eruptions) are a type of eruption driven by the expansion of [steam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam). When cold ground or surface water come into contact with hot rock or magma it [superheats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superheating) and [explodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_explosion), fracturing the surrounding rock[^cite_note-phreatic-mechanism-63] and thrusting out a mixture of steam, [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water), [ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_ash), [volcanic bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_bomb), and [volcanic blocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_block).[^cite_note-usgs-vhp-pheat-64] The distinguishing feature of phreatic explosions is that they only blast out fragments of pre-existing solid rock from the volcanic conduit; no new magma is erupted.[^cite_note-usgs-types-65] Because they are driven by the cracking of rock strata under pressure, phreatic activity does not always result in an eruption; if the rock face is strong enough to withstand the explosive force, outright eruptions may not occur, although cracks in the rock will probably develop and weaken it, furthering future eruptions.[^cite_note-phreatic-mechanism-63]

Often a precursor of future volcanic activity,[^cite_note-vw-phreatic-66] phreatic eruptions are generally weak, although there have been exceptions.[^cite_note-usgs-types-65] Some phreatic events may be triggered by [earthquake](10106.md) activity, another volcanic precursor, and they may also travel along [dike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dike_\(geology\)) lines.[^cite_note-phreatic-mechanism-63] Phreatic eruptions form [base surges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_surge), [lahars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lahar), [avalanches](73321.md), and [volcanic block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_block) "rain." They may also release deadly [toxic gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxic_gas) able to suffocate anyone in range of the eruption.[^cite_note-vw-phreatic-66]

Volcanoes known to exhibit phreatic activity include:

- [Mount St. Helens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_St._Helens), which exhibited phreatic activity just prior to its catastrophic [1980 eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_eruption_of_Mount_St._Helens) (which was itself [Plinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plinian_eruption)).

  [^cite_note-usgs-vhp-pheat-64]

- [Taal Volcano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taal_Volcano), [Philippines](23440.md), 1965

  [^cite_note-usgs-types-65]

  &#x20;2020

  [^cite_note-67]

- [La Soufrière](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Grande_Soufri%C3%A8re) of [Guadeloupe](12343.md) ([Lesser Antilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser_Antilles)), 1975–1976 activity.

  [^cite_note-usgs-types-65]

- [Soufrière Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soufri%C3%A8re_Hills) volcano on [Montserrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montserrat), West Indies, 1995–2012.

- [Poás Volcano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Po%C3%A1s_Volcano), has frequent geyser like phreatic eruptions from its crater lake.

- [Mount Bulusan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Bulusan), well known for its sudden phreatic eruptions.

- [Mount Ontake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Ontake), all historical eruptions of this volcano have been phreatic including the deadly [2014 eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Mount_Ontake_eruption).
