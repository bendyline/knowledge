# Schism

For other uses, see [Schism (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schism_\(disambiguation\)).

A **schism** ([/ˈsɪzəm, ˈskɪz-/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*SIZ-əm, SKIZ-*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key), or (less commonly) [/ˈʃɪzəm/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*SHIZ-əm*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key))[^cite_note-1] is a division between people, usually belonging to an [organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organization), [movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_movement), or [religious denomination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_denomination). The word is most frequently applied to a split in what had previously been a single religious body, such as the [East–West Schism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East%E2%80%93West_Schism) in [Christianity](5211.md) or the [Sunni–Shia divide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shia%E2%80%93Sunni_relations) in [Islam](6037917.md). It is also used of a split within a non-religious organization or movement or, more broadly, of a separation between two or more people, be it brothers, friends, lovers, etc.

A **schismatic** is a legalistic, often pejorative term for person who creates or incites schism in an organization or who is a member of a splinter group. People and groups declared schismatic often reject the label. As an adjective, *schismatic* means pertaining to a schism or schisms, or to those ideas, policies, etc. that are thought to lead towards or promote schism. While the concept of schism exists across a wide number of faiths, the specific term, implications, and jurisprudence around *schism* very widely across religions traditions and denominations. In academic scholarship on schisms, a different [gloss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloss_\(annotation\)#In_linguistics), or the underlying term untranslated, is sometimes used to reflect distinctions of that religion's conception of schism.

In some religions, such as Buddhism and much of Christianity, the charge of schism is distinguished from that of [heresy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heresy), since the offence of schism concerns not differences of belief or doctrine but promotion of, or the state of division,[^cite_note-2] especially among groups with differing pastoral jurisdictions and authority. However, schisms frequently involve mutual accusations of heresy. Other faiths view heresy and schism as inexorably linked, and even within a faith this can change depending on context. In Roman Catholicism, every heresy is a schism, though not every schism is a heresy.[^cite_note-3] This is further complicated by mutual charges of heresy between schismatic groups and the institutions they split from, with schismatics often declaring themselves as staying true to a religion's teachings and the main body heretically deviant, as with the 2026 schism between Roman Catholicism and the [Society of Saint Pius X](232299.md).[^cite_note-4] Whether something is schismatic, heretical, or both, is an issue of ideological perception when both groups claim legitimacy.

## Buddhism

In Buddhism, schism ([Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language): *saṁghabheda*, [Pali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali_language): *saṅghabheda*), is a strict legal term and is not defined by doctrinal differences, but rather differences in interpretation of monastic rule within a single monastic community.[^cite_note-5] Schism is considered one of the five [Ānantarya karma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anantarika-karma), resulting in immediate and overwhelming karmic consequences. According to [Buddhist tradition](3267529.md), the first schism was set up by [Devadatta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devadatta), during [Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gautama_Buddha)'s life.[^cite_note-6]

The Theravada canon strictly defines schism as nine or more monks separating and performing their own ecclesiastical services.[^cite_note-7] Mahayana and Vajrayana schools rely on different vinaya traditions ([Dharmaguptaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharmaguptaka) in schools descended from Chinese Buddhism and [Mulasarvastivada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulasarvastivada) in Tibetan Buddhism) and have slight differences in the number of monks to be considered schismatic, but retain the essential definition of "schism" as a strict legal term, and are recognized as valid by monastics ordained under a different Vinaya.[^cite_note-8] Though frequently discussed as such, Mahayana and Theravada Buddhism are not considered schismatic by each other.[^cite_note-9] The frequent English use of *schism* is typically adopted due to a lack of precise language to describe the Buddhist process of inter-sangha disagreement.[^cite_note-10]

The words *schism* and *schismatic* are used to denote splits within a church, denomination or religious body. In this context, "schismatic", as a noun, denotes a person who creates or incites schism in a church or a person who is a member of a splinter Church; as an adjective, "schismatic" refers to ideas and activities that are thought to lead to or constitute schism, and ultimately to departure from what the user of the word considers to be the true Christian Church. These words have been used to denote both the phenomenon of Christian group-splintering in general, and certain significant historical splits in particular.

One can make a distinction[^cite_note-11] between [heresy](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/heresy) and [schism](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/schism). Heresy is rejection of a [doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctrine) that a Church considered to be essential. Schism is a rejection of [communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communion_\(Christian\)) with the authorities of a Church, and not every break of communion is necessarily about doctrine, as is clear from examples such as the [Western Schism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Schism) and the breaking of the communion that existed between [Patriarch Bartholomew I of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_Bartholomew_I_of_Constantinople) and [Archbishop Christodoulos of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_Christodoulos_of_Athens) in 2004.[^cite_note-12] However, when for any reason people withdraw from communion, two distinct ecclesiastical entities may result, each of which, or at least some members thereof, may then accuse the other(s) of heresy.

In [Roman Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church) [canon law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_law_\(Catholic_Church\)), an act of schism, like an act of [apostasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostasy) or [heresy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heresy), automatically brings the penalty of [excommunication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excommunication) on the individual who commits it.[^cite_note-13] As stated in [canon 1312 §1 1°](http://www.intratext.com/IXT/ENG0017/_P4S.HTM) of the [1983 Code of Canon Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1983_Code_of_Canon_Law), this penalty is intended to be medicinal, so as to lead to restoration of unity. Roman Catholic theology considers formal schismatics to be outside the Church, understanding by "formal schismatics" "persons who, knowing the true nature of the Church, have personally and deliberately committed the sin of schism".[^cite_note-Nichols-14] The situation, for instance, of those who have been brought up from childhood within a group not in full communion with [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Rome), but who have orthodox faith, is different: these are considered to be imperfectly, though not fully, related to the Church.[^cite_note-Nichols-14] This nuanced view applies especially to the Churches of [Eastern Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Christianity), more particularly still to the [Eastern Orthodox Church](10186.md).[^cite_note-Nichols-14] While they don't possess "full communion" (*communio in sacris*) with the Catholic Church, they are still considered much more linked to it than the Protestant ecclesial communities, which have markedly different theological beliefs and rejected the concept of [apostolic succession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostolic_succession) (with the exception of the Anglicans, which, however, are viewed by the Catholic Church as not having a valid priesthood).

The [First Council of Nicaea](11118.md) (A.D. 325) distinguished between schism and heresy. It declared [Arian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arian) and non-[Trinitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinitarianism) teachings to be heretical and excluded their adherents from the Church. It also addressed the schism between [Peter of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Peter_I_of_Alexandria) and [Meletius of Lycopolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meletius_of_Lycopolis), considering their quarrel to be a matter of discipline, not of faith.

The divisions that came to a head at the Councils of [Ephesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Council_of_Ephesus) (A.D. 431) and [Chalcedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Chalcedon) (A.D. 451) were seen as matters of heresy, not merely of schism. Thus the [Eastern Orthodox Church](10186.md) and [Oriental Orthodoxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Orthodoxy) regard each other as heretical, not orthodox, because of the Oriental Orthodox Church's rejection and the Eastern Orthodox Church's acceptance of the [Confession of Chalcedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Chalcedon#Confession_of_Chalcedon) about the two natures (human and divine) of Christ. However, this view has been challenged in the recent [Ecumenical discussion](146656.md) between these two groups, classifying the matter of Chalcedon as a matter of schism, not of heresy.

In its extended and final form (possibly derived from the [First Council of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Council_of_Constantinople) in 381 although only known from the Acts of the [Council of Chalcedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Chalcedon) seventy years later),[^cite_note-15] what is commonly called the [Nicene Creed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicene_Creed) declares belief in the [One Holy Catholic and Apostolic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Holy_Catholic_and_Apostolic_Church). Some who accept this creed believe they should be united in a single Church or group of Churches in communion with each other. Others who accept this creed believe it does not speak of a visible organization but of all those baptized who hold the Christian faith, referred to as "[Christendom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christendom)". Some churches consider themselves to be the One Holy Catholic and Apostolic Church. For instance, the [Roman Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church) claims that title and considers the [Eastern Orthodox Church](10186.md) to be in schism, while the Eastern Orthodox Church also claims that title and holds the view that the Catholic Church is schismatic. Some [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant) Churches believe that they also represent the One Holy Catholic and Apostolic Church and consider the Catholic and Orthodox Churches to be in error, while others do not expect a union of all Christian churches on earth. See also [One true church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_true_church) and [Great Apostasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Apostasy).

Protestant groups, lacking the stronger traditional authority-structures of (say) Roman Catholicism or Eastern Orthodoxy, and often riven by politico-national divides (sometimes resulting from *[cuius regio, eius religio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuius_regio,_eius_religio)*), show a high degree of fissibility, which ecumenical efforts may only intensify.[^cite_note-16]

Schisms have occurred particularly frequently among [Anabaptists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anabaptists), to the extent that divisions over even minute details of doctrine and theology are common and scholars have dubbed the phenomenon *Täuferkrankheit* or "The Anabaptist Disease".[^cite_note-17] Emphasizing fully voluntary membership in the church, and without an established authority of hierarchical structure, Anabaptists, especially [Mennonites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mennonites) have experienced dozens of schisms, resulting in the establishment of dozens of various unaffiliated Mennonite churches.

A current dispute with an acknowledged risk of schism for the [Anglican Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Communion) involves [responses to homosexuality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homosexuality_and_Anglicanism).

In 2018 [Eastern Orthodoxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy) suffered a schism, [the 2018 Moscow-Constantinople schism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Moscow%E2%80%93Constantinople_schism) between the primatial See of Eastern Orthodoxy, the [Ecumenical Patriarchate of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarchate_of_Constantinople) and the [Russian Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church) over the issue of Constantinople granting [autocephaly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocephaly) to the [Orthodox Church of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodox_Church_of_Ukraine).

## Hinduism

The [Sringeri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sringeri_Sharada_Peetham) Matha, also called the '[Tunga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunga_River) Sringeri Math' diverged after a schism with the [Koodli Sringeri Matha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koodli) during the 16th century after which the Tunga Math gained prominence.[^cite_note-18]

The [Kanchi Math](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanchi_Kamakoti_Peetham) was originally established as the Kumbakonam Mutt in 1821 by the [Maratha king of Tanjore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thanjavur_Maratha_kingdom), [Serfoji II Bhonsle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serfoji_II), as a branch of the [Sringeri Mutt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sringeri_Sharada_Peetham), one of the four cardinal [Shankaracharya Maths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shankaracharya) of the mainstream [Smarta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smarta_tradition) denomination. It became a schismatic institution when Tanjore and the [Wodeyars of Mysore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadiyar_dynasty) went to war against each other. It is on record that in 1839 the Kumbakonam Mutt applied for permission from the English Collector of Arcot to perform the "kumbhabhishekham" of the Kamakshi temple in Kanchipuram.

In 1842, the [East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India_Company) headquartered at [Fort William, Calcutta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_William,_India) appointed the head of the mutt as the sole trustee of the Kamakshi temple. The protests of the traditional priests of the Kamakshi temple are well documented and preserved. Incidentally, Fort William is also the first [Freemason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freemasonry) lodge of India.[^cite_note-19] Since then, the Math has maintained cordial relations with the [British Raj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Raj) though the main math at Sringeri fell sour with the colonial power[^cite_note-20]

Thus, the Kanchi Mutt can at best claim its origin to be in 1844.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22]

## Islam

Further information: [Islamic schools and branches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_schools_and_branches), [Succession to Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Succession_to_Muhammad), [Pan-Islamism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Islamism), and [Shia–Sunni divide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shia%E2%80%93Sunni_divide)

After the death of the Islamic prophet [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad), there have arisen many [Muslim sects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_sects) by means of [schools of thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhhab), traditions and related faiths.[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24] According to a [hadith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith) report (collections of accounts of the life and teachings of Muhammad), Muhammad is said to have [prophesied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophecy) "My [Ummah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ummah) (Community or Nation) will be fragmented into seventy-three sects, and all of them will be in the [Hell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahannam) fire except one." The [Sahaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahaba) (his companions) asked him which group that would be, whereupon he replied, "It is the one to which I and my companions belong" (reported in [Sunan al-Tirmidhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunan_al-Tirmidhi) Hadith No. 171).

[Sunni Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam), often referred to as *Ahl as-Sunnah wa'l-Jamā'h* or *Ahl as-Sunnah*, are the largest [denomination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_schools_and_branches) of [Islam](6037917.md). The word *Sunni* comes from the word *[Sunnah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunnah)*, which means the teachings and actions or examples of the [Islamic prophet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophets_of_Islam), [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad); therefore, the term Sunni refers to those who follow or maintain the Sunnah of Muhammad. The Sunni believe that Muhammad died without appointing a successor to lead the [Ummah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ummah) (Muslim community). After an initial period of confusion, a group of his most prominent companions gathered and elected [Abu Bakr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Bakr), Muhammad's close friend and father-in-law, as the first [Caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliph). Sunnis regard the first four caliphs – [Abu Bakr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Bakr), [Umar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umar) (\`Umar ibn al-Khattāb), [Uthman Ibn Affan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uthman_Ibn_Affan), and [Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali) (Ali ibn Abu Talib) – as the *al-Khulafā'ur-Rāshidūn* or "[Rashidun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun)" (The Rightly Guided Caliphs). Sunnis believe that the position of Caliph may be democratically chosen, but after the first four Rightly Guided Caliphs the position turned into a hereditary [dynastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynasty) rule. There has not been another widely recognized Caliph since the fall of the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md) in 1923.

[Shia Islam](26961.md) is the second largest [denomination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_schools_and_branches) of [Islam](6037917.md). Shia Muslims believe that, similar to the appointment of prophets, [Imams](15240.md) after Muhammad are also chosen by God. According to Shias, [Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali) was chosen by Allah and thus appointed by Muhammad to be the direct successor and leader of the Muslim community. They regard him as the first [Shia Imam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imamah_\(Shia_doctrine\)), which continued as a hereditary position through [Fatimah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimah) and Ali's descendants.

[Sufism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism) is a [mystical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysticism)-[ascetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asceticism) form of Islam practised by both Shia and Sunni Muslims. Some Sufi followers consider themselves Sunni or Shia, while others consider themselves as just Sufi or Sufi-influenced. Sufism is usually considered to be complementary to orthodox Islam, although Sufism has often been accused by the [salafi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi#Influence_on_contemporary_Salafism) of being an unjustified *[Bid'ah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bid'ah)* or religious innovation. By focusing on the more spiritual aspects of religion, Sufis strive to obtain direct experience of God by making use of "intuitive and emotional faculties" that one must be trained to use.[^cite_note-25] One starts with [sharia](28840.md) (Islamic law), the [exoteric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exoteric) or mundane practice of Islam, and then is initiated into the mystical ([esoteric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_esotericism)) path of a [Tariqah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tariqah) (Sufi Order).

[Kharijite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharijites) (lit. 'those who seceded') is a general term embracing a variety of Islamic sects which, while originally supporting the Caliphate of [Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali), eventually rejected his legitimacy after he negotiated with [Mu'awiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muawiyah_I) during the 7th Century Islamic civil war ([First Fitna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Fitna)). Their complaint was that the Imam must be spiritually pure, whereas Ali's compromise with Mu'awiya was a compromise of his spiritual purity and therefore of his legitimacy as Imam or Caliph. While there are few remaining Kharijite or Kharijite-related groups, the term is sometimes used to denote Muslims who refuse to compromise with those with whom they disagree.

## Jainism

Main article: [Jain schools and branches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_schools_and_branches)



Picture showing a diagrammatic view of the schisms in [Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism) along with the [timeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_Jainism)

The first schism in Jainism happened around the fourth century BCE, leading to rise of two major sects, [Digambara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digambara) and [Śvetāmbara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Avet%C4%81mbara), which were later subdivided in further sub-sects.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEClarkeBeyer2009326-26] This division arose primarily over a disagreement of [monastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_monasticism) dress, with the Digambara emphasising the importance for the nakedness of monks and disallowing the ordination of women, and the Śvetāmbara allowing for simple white monastic garments and women as monastics.[^cite_note-27]
