**Bahrain**,[^cite_note-16] officially the **Kingdom of Bahrain**,[^cite_note-17] is an [island country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island_country) in the eastern part of [Arabian Peninsula](47858.md) in [West Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Asia). Situated near the western shore of the [Persian Gulf](24761.md), the country comprises a small [archipelago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archipelago) of 33 natural islands and an additional 50 [artificial islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_island), centred on [Bahrain Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_Island), which makes up around 80 percent of the country's landmass. Bahrain is situated between [Qatar](8238258.md) and the northeastern coast of [Saudi Arabia](349303.md), to which it is connected by the [King Fahd Causeway](1360149.md). The population is 1,588,670 as of 2024, of whom 739,736 (46.6% of the population) are Bahraini nationals, and 848,934 are expatriates (53.4% of the population).[^cite_note-Census2020-2] Bahrain spans some 760 square kilometres (290 sq mi)[^cite_note-18] and is the [third-smallest nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_area) in Asia after [Maldives](19117.md) and [Singapore](27318.md).[^cite_note-19] The capital and largest city is [Manama](20481.md).

The area that straddles the present-day territory of Bahrain was once the site of the ancient [Dilmun civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilmun_civilization).[^cite_note-\:1-20] It has been famed since antiquity for its [pearl fisheries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_fisheries), which were considered the best in the world into the 19th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE''EB''1878-21] Bahrain was one of the [earliest areas to be influenced by Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spread_of_Islam), during the lifetime of [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad) in 628. Following a period of Arab rule, Bahrain was ruled by the [Portuguese Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_Empire) from 1521 until 1602, when they were expelled by Shah [Abbas the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbas_the_Great) of the [Safavid Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Iran). In 1783, the [Bani Utbah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bani_Utbah) and allied tribes captured Bahrain from [Nasr Al-Madhkur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasr_Al-Madhkur). It has since been ruled by the [Al Khalifa royal family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Khalifa), with [Ahmed al Fateh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_ibn_Muhammad_ibn_Khalifa) as Bahrain's first [*hakim*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakim_\(title\)#In_Arab_countries).

In the late 19th century, following successive treaties with the [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland), Bahrain became a [protectorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protectorate) of the United Kingdom.[^cite_note-22] In 1971, it [declared independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_Day_\(Bahrain\)). Formerly an [emirate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate), Bahrain was declared a [semi-constitutional monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-constitutional_monarchy) in 2002, and Article 2 of the [constitution](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Constitution%20of%20the%20Kingdom%20of%20Bahrain%20\(2002\)) made [sharia](28840.md) a principal source for legislation. In 2011, the [country experienced protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Bahraini_uprising) inspired by the regional [Arab Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Spring).[^cite_note-23] The ruling [Sunni Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Muslim) [Al Khalifa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Khalifa) royal family has been criticised for [violating the human rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_Bahrain) of groups including dissidents, political opposition figures, and its [Shia Muslim population](55955732.md).[^cite_note-24]

Bahrain is known as one of the first post-oil economies in the [Persian Gulf](24761.md),[^cite_note-25] the result of decades of investing in the banking and tourism sectors.[^cite_note-EDB-26] Many of the world's largest financial institutions have a presence in Manama, but oil revenues still constitute a significant part of its government budget. It is recognised by the [World Bank](36752.md) as a [high-income economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Bank_high-income_economy). Bahrain is a member of the [United Nations](31769.md), the [Non-Aligned Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Aligned_Movement), the [Arab League](52625.md), the [Organisation of Islamic Cooperation](47725.md), and the [Gulf Cooperation Council](220950.md).[^cite_note-27] It is a Dialogue partner of the [Shanghai Cooperation Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghai_Cooperation_Organization).[^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29]

## Etymology

*Bahrain* is the [dual form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_form) of the [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) word *Bahr* (meaning "[sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea)"); as such, *al-Bahrayn* originally means "the two seas". However, the name has been lexicalised as a feminine [proper noun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_noun) and does not follow the grammatical rules for duals; thus its form is always *Bahrayn* and never *Bahrān*, the expected nominative form. Endings are added to the word with no changes, as in the name of the national anthem *Bahraynunā* ("our Bahrain") or the [demonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demonym) *Bahraynī*. The medieval grammarian [al-Jawahari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ismail_ibn_Hammad_al-Jawhari) commented on this, saying that the more formally correct term *Bahrī* (lit. 'belonging to the sea') would have been misunderstood and so was unused.[^cite_note-30]

It remains disputed which "two seas" the name *Bahrayn* originally refers to.[^cite_note-EoI-31] The term appears five times in the [Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran) but does not refer to the modern island—originally known to the Arabs as *Awal*.[^cite_note-EoI-31] Today, Bahrain's "two seas" are generally taken to be the bay east and west of the island,[^cite_note-32] the seas north and south of the island,[^cite_note-33] or the salt and fresh water present above and below the ground. In addition to wells, there are areas of the sea north of Bahrain where fresh water bubbles up in the middle of the saltwater as noted by visitors since antiquity.[^cite_note-34] An alternative theory concerning Bahrain's toponymy is offered by the al-Ahsa region, which suggests that the two seas were the "Great Green Ocean" (the [Persian Gulf](24761.md)) and [Al-Asfar Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Asfar_Lake) on the [Arabian mainland](47858.md).

Until the late [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), "Bahrain" referred to the [region of Eastern Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Arabia) that included southern [Iraq](7515928.md), [Kuwait](7515890.md), [Al-Hasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ahsa_Oasis), [Qatif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qatif), and Bahrain. The region stretched from [Basra](57580.md) in Iraq to the [Strait of Hormuz](29005.md) in [Oman](22316.md). This was Iqlīm al-Bahrayn's "Bahrayn Province". When the term "Bahrain" began to refer solely to the Awal archipelago is unknown.[^cite_note-Rentz-35] The entire coastal strip of Eastern Arabia was known as "Bahrain" for a millennium.[^cite_note-maj-36] The island and kingdom were also commonly spelled **Bahrein**.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE''EB''1878-21][^cite_note-FOOTNOTE''EB''1911-37]

## History

Main article: [History of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Bahrain)

### Antiquity

Bahrain was home to [Dilmun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilmun), an important [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age) trade centre linking [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia) and the [Indus Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_River).[^cite_note-38] Bahrain was later ruled by the [Sumerians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer) and [Babylonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia).[^cite_note-39] From the 6th to 3rd century BC, Bahrain was part of the [Achaemenid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire). By about 250 BC, [Parthia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthia) brought the Persian Gulf under its control and extended its influence as far as Oman. The Parthians established garrisons along the southern coast of the Persian Gulf to control trade routes.[^cite_note-40]

During the [classical era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_era), Bahrain was referred to by the [ancient Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) as *[Tylos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tylos)*, the centre of pearl trading, when the Greek admiral [Nearchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nearchus) serving under [Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) landed on Bahrain.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELarsen198313-41] Nearchus is believed to have been the first of Alexander's commanders to visit the island, and he found a verdant land that was part of a wide trading network; he recorded: "That on the island of Tylos, situated in the Persian Gulf, are large plantations of cotton trees, from which are manufactured clothes called *sindones*, of strongly differing degrees of value, some being costly, others less expensive. The use of these is not confined to India, but extends to Arabia."[^cite_note-42]

The Greek philosopher and a botanist [Theophrastus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theophrastus)[^cite_note-Theophrastus-43] states that much of Bahrain was covered by these cotton trees and that Bahrain was famous for exporting walking canes engraved with emblems that were customarily carried in Babylon.[^cite_note-44] Alexander had planned to settle Greek colonists in Bahrain, and although it is not clear that this happened on the scale he envisaged, Bahrain became very much part of the [Hellenised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenization) world: the language of the upper classes was Greek, with [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic_language) in everyday use. Local coinage shows a seated Zeus, who may have been worshipped there as a syncretised form of the Arabian sun-god [Shams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shams_\(deity\)).[^cite_note-45] Tylos was also the site of Greek athletic contests.[^cite_note-46]

&#x20;The Greek historian [Strabo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strabo) believed the [Phoenicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicians) originated from Bahrain.[^cite_note-47] [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus) also believed that the homeland of the Phoenicians was Bahrain.[^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49] This theory was accepted by the 19th-century German classicist [Arnold Heeren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Heeren) who said "In the Greek geographers, for instance, we read of two islands, named Tyrus or Tylos, and [Aradus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arad,_Bahrain), which boasted that they were the mother country of the Phoenicians, and exhibited relics of Phoenician temples."[^cite_note-50] The people of [Tyre](30796.md) in particular have long maintained [Persian Gulf](24761.md) origins, and the similarity in the words "Tylos" and "Tyre" has been commented upon.[^cite_note-51] However, there is little evidence of any human settlement at all on Bahrain during the time when such migration had supposedly taken place.[^cite_note-52]



A 1745 [Bellin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques-Nicolas_Bellin) map of the historical region of Bahrain



A map showing the locations of Bahrain's [ancient burial mounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilmun_Burial_Mounds). There are an estimated 350,000 burial mounds.

The name Tylos is thought to be a Hellenisation of the Semitic *Tilmun* (from [Dilmun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilmun)).[^cite_note-53] The term Tylos was commonly used for the islands until [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy)'s *[Geographia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographia_\(Ptolemy\))* when the inhabitants are referred to as Thilouanoi.[^cite_note-54] Some place names in Bahrain go back to the Tylos era; for instance the name of Arad, a residential suburb of [Muharraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muharraq), is believed to originate from "Arados", the ancient Greek name for Muharraq.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELarsen198313-41]

In the 3rd century, [Ardashir I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ardashir_I), the first ruler of the [Sassanid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sassanid_dynasty), marched on Oman and Bahrain, where he defeated Sanatruq the ruler of Bahrain.[^cite_note-books.google-55]

Bahrain was the site of worship of an ox deity called [Awal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awal) (Arabic: اوال). Worshipers built a large statue to Awal in [Muharraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muharraq), although it has now been lost. For many centuries after *Tylos*, Bahrain was known as *Awal*. By the 5th century, Bahrain became a centre for [Nestorian Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nestorianism), with the village [Samahij](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samahij)[^cite_note-56] as the seat of bishops. In 410, according to the Oriental Syriac Church synodal records, a bishop named Batai was excommunicated from the church in Bahrain.[^cite_note-Jean_Francois_Salles_p132-57] As a sect, the Nestorians were often persecuted as heretics by the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire), but Bahrain was outside the empire's control, offering some safety. The names of several Muharraq villages today reflect Bahrain's Christian legacy, with [Al Dair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Dair) meaning "the monastery".

Bahrain's pre-Muslim population consisted of [Christian Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Christians) (mostly [Abd al-Qays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Qays)), Persians ([Zoroastrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrians)), [Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Bahrain#Early_history),[^cite_note-orig-58] and [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic_language)-speaking agriculturalists.[^cite_note-ram-59][^cite_note-om-60][^cite_note-61] According to [Robert Bertram Serjeant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bertram_Serjeant), the [Baharna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baharna) may be the [Arabised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabization) "descendants of converts from the original population of Christians (Aramaeans), Jews and Persians inhabiting the island and cultivated coastal provinces of [Eastern Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Arabia) at the time of the [Muslim conquest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslim_conquests)".[^cite_note-ram-59][^cite_note-Robert_Bertram_Serjeant_1968_488-62] The sedentary people of pre-Muslim Bahrain were [Aramaic speakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic_language#Geographic_distribution) and to some degree Persian speakers, while [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language) functioned as a [liturgical language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_language).[^cite_note-om-60]

### Arrival of Islam

Main article: [List of expeditions of Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_expeditions_of_Muhammad)



A [facsimile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facsimile) of a letter sent by [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad) to [Munzir ibn-Sawa al-Tamimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munzir_ibn-Sawa_al-Tamimi), governor of Bahrain, in AD 628

[Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad)'s first interaction with the people of Bahrain was the [Al Kudr Invasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Kudr_Invasion). Muhammad ordered a surprise attack on the [Banu Salim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Salim) tribe for plotting to attack Medina. He had received news that some tribes were assembling an army in Bahrain and preparing to attack the mainland, but the tribesmen retreated when they learned Muhammad was leading an army to do battle with them.[^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64]



&#x20;The [Sassanid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire) on the eve of the Arab conquest, c. 600 AD

Traditional Muslim accounts state that [Al-Ala'a Al-Hadrami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ala'a_Al-Hadrami) was sent as an envoy during the [Expedition of Zayd ibn Harithah (Hisma)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expedition_of_Zayd_ibn_Harithah_\(Hisma\))[^cite_note-65][^cite_note-history-66] to the [Bahrain region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Arabia) by [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad) in AD 628 and that [Munzir ibn Sawa Al Tamimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munzir_ibn_Sawa_Al_Tamimi), the local ruler, responded to his mission and converted the entire area.[^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68]

### Middle Ages

In 899, the [Qarmatians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qarmatians), a [millenarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millenarianism) [Ismaili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isma'ilism) Muslim sect, seized Bahrain, seeking to create a [utopian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utopia) society based on reason and redistribution of property among initiates. Thereafter, the Qarmatians demanded [tribute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribute) from the caliph in [Baghdad](4492.md), and in 930 [sacked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sack_of_Mecca) [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca), bringing the sacred [Black Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Stone) back to their base in [Ahsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_\(historical_region\)), in medieval Bahrain, for ransom. According to historian [Al-Juwayni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Juwayni), the stone was returned 22 years later in 951 under mysterious circumstances. Wrapped in a sack, it was thrown into the [Great Mosque of Kufa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Kufa) in Iraq, accompanied by a note saying "By command we took it, and by command, we have brought it back." The theft and removal of the Black Stone caused it to break into seven pieces.[^cite_note-69][^cite_note-glasse-70][^cite_note-71]

Following their defeat in 976 by the [Abbasids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate),[^cite_note-autogenerated2-72] the Qarmatians were overthrown by the Arab [Uyunid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uyunid_dynasty) of [al-Hasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ahsa_Oasis), who took over the entire Bahrain region in 1076.[^cite_note-73] The Uyunids controlled Bahrain until 1235, when the archipelago was briefly occupied by the Persian ruler of [Fars](448049.md). In 1253, the [Usfurids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usfurids) brought down the Uyunid dynasty, thereby gaining control over eastern [Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabia), including the islands of Bahrain.[^cite_note-Rentz-35]

In 1330, the archipelago became a tributary state of the rulers of [Hormuz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ormus).[^cite_note-Rentz-35] Locally, the islands were controlled by the Shia [Jarwanid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jarwanids) dynasty of [Qatif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qatif).[^cite_note-74] In the mid-15th century, the archipelago came under the rule of the [Jabrids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabrids), a Bedouin dynasty also based in [Al-Ahsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ahsa_Oasis) that ruled most of eastern Arabia.[^cite_note-75]

### Portuguese and early modern era

Main articles: [Safavid Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Bahrain), [Bani Utbah invasion of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bani_Utbah_invasion_of_Bahrain), [Bahrain and its Dependencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_and_its_Dependencies), and [Qatari–Bahraini War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qatari%E2%80%93Bahraini_War)



The [Portuguese Fort of Barém](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qal'at_al-Bahrain), built by the Portuguese when they ruled Bahrain from 1521 to 1602

In 1521, the Portuguese allied with Hormuz and seized Bahrain from the [Jabrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabrids) ruler [Muqrin ibn Zamil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muqrin_ibn_Zamil), who was killed during the takeover. Portuguese rule lasted for around 80 years, during which time they depended mainly on [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) Persian governors.[^cite_note-Rentz-35] The Portuguese were expelled from the islands in 1602 by [Abbas I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbas_I_of_Persia) of the [Safavid Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Iran),[^cite_note-76] which gave impetus to [Shia Islam](26961.md).[^cite_note-77]

For the next two centuries, Persian rulers retained control of the archipelago, interrupted by the 1717 and 1738 invasions of the [Ibadis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibadi) of Oman.[^cite_note-autogenerated4-78] During most of this period, they resorted to governing Bahrain indirectly, either through the city of [Bushehr](205669.md) or through [immigrant](4599312.md) Sunni Arab clans. The latter were tribes returning to the Arabian side of the Persian Gulf from Persian territories in the north who were known as *[Huwala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huwala)*.[^cite_note-Rentz-35][^cite_note-79][^cite_note-McCoy-80] In 1753, the Huwala clan of [Nasr Al-Madhkur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasr_Al-Madhkur) invaded Bahrain on behalf of the Iranian [Zand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zand_dynasty) leader [Karim Khan Zand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karim_Khan_Zand) and restored direct Iranian rule.[^cite_note-McCoy-80]

In 1783, Al-Madhkur lost the islands of Bahrain following his defeat by the [Bani Utbah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bani_Utbah) clan and allied tribes at the 1782 Battle of [Zubarah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zubarah). Bahrain was not new territory to the Bani Utbah; they had been a presence there since the 17th century.[^cite_note-81] During that time, they started purchasing date palm gardens in Bahrain; a document shows that 81 years before the arrival of the Al Khalifa, one of the sheikhs of the [Al Bin Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Bin_Ali) tribe (an offshoot of the Bani Utbah) had bought a palm garden from Mariam bint Ahmed Al Sanadi in [Sitra](4006219.md) island.[^cite_note-82]



Purple – [Portuguese Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_Empire) in the Persian Gulf in the 16th and 17th centuries (modern boundaries shown). Main cities, ports and routes.

The Al Bin Ali were the dominant group controlling the town of Zubarah on the Qatar peninsula,[^cite_note-Arabia's_Frontiers_p44-83][^cite_note-84] originally the centre of power of the Bani Utbah. After the Bani Utbah gained control of Bahrain, the Al Bin Ali had a practically independent status there as a self-governing tribe. They used a flag with four red and three white stripes, called the [Al-Sulami flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Sulami_flag)[^cite_note-85] in Bahrain, Qatar, [Kuwait](7515890.md), and the Eastern province of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. Later, different Arab family clans and tribes from Qatar moved to Bahrain to settle after the fall of Nasr Al-Madhkur of [Bushehr](205669.md). These families included the [House of Khalifa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Khalifa), Al-Ma'awdah, Al-Buainain, Al-Fadhil, Al-Kuwari, Al-Mannai, Al-Noaimi, Al-Rumaihi, Al-Sulaiti, Al-Sadah, Al-Thawadi and other families and tribes.[^cite_note-86]

The House of Khalifa moved from Qatar to Bahrain in 1799. Originally, their ancestors were expelled from [Umm Qasr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umm_Qasr) in central Arabia by the [Ottomans](22278.md) due to their predatory habits of preying on caravans in [Basra](57580.md) and trading ships in [Shatt al-Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatt_al-Arab) waterway until Turks expelled them to Kuwait in 1716, where they remained until 1766.[^cite_note-qdl1-87]

Around the 1760s, the [Al Jalahma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Jalahma) and House of Khalifa, both belonging to the Utub Federation, migrated to [Zubarah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zubarah) in modern-day Qatar, leaving Al Sabah as the sole proprietors of Kuwait.[^cite_note-88]

### 19th century and later

Main article: [State of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_Bahrain)

In the early 19th century, Bahrain was invaded by both the Omanis and the [Al Sauds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Saud). In 1802 it was governed by a 12-year-old child, when the Omani ruler Sayyid Sultan installed his son, Salim, as governor in the [Arad Fort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arad_Fort).[^cite_note-89] In 1816, the British political resident in the Persian Gulf, William Bruce, received a letter from the Sheikh of Bahrain who was concerned about a rumour that Britain would support an attack on the island by the Imam of Muscat. He sailed to Bahrain to reassure the Sheikh that this was not the case and drew up an informal agreement assuring the Sheikh that Britain would remain a neutral party.[^cite_note-90]



The coronation of Hamad bin Isa Al Khalifa as the Hakim of Bahrain in February 1933

In 1820, the Al Khalifa tribe were recognised by the United Kingdom as the rulers ("Al-Hakim" in Arabic) of Bahrain after signing a [treaty relationship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Maritime_Treaty_of_1820).[^cite_note-91] However, ten years later they were forced to pay yearly tributes to Egypt despite seeking Persian and British protection.[^cite_note-NAS-92]



A map of Bahrain in 1825

In 1860, the Al Khalifas used the same tactic when the British tried to overpower Bahrain. Writing letters to the Persians and [Ottomans](22278.md), Al Khalifas agreed to place Bahrain under the latter's protection in March due to offering better conditions. Eventually, the [Government of British India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Raj) overpowered Bahrain when the Persians refused to protect it. Colonel [Pelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Pelly) signed a new treaty with Al Khalifas placing Bahrain under British rule and protection.[^cite_note-NAS-92]



Manama Harbour, c. 1870

Following the [Qatari–Bahraini War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qatari%E2%80%93Bahraini_War) in 1868, British representatives signed another agreement with the Al Khalifas. It specified that the ruler could not dispose of any of his territories except to the United Kingdom and could not enter into relationships with any foreign government without British consent.[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-TPG-94] In return the British promised to protect Bahrain from all aggression by sea and to lend support in case of land attack.[^cite_note-TPG-94] More importantly the British promised to support the rule of the Al Khalifa in Bahrain, securing its unstable position as rulers of the country. Other agreements in 1880 and 1892 sealed the protectorate status of Bahrain to the British.[^cite_note-TPG-94]

Unrest amongst the people of Bahrain began when Britain officially established complete dominance over the territory in 1892. The first revolt and widespread uprising took place in March 1895 against Sheikh Issa bin Ali, then ruler of Bahrain.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMojtahed-Zadeh1999130-95] Sheikh Issa was the first of the Al Khalifa to rule without Persian relations. [Sir Arnold Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Wilson), Britain's representative in the Persian Gulf and author of *The Persian Gulf*, arrived in Bahrain from Muscat at this time.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMojtahed-Zadeh1999130-95] The uprising developed further with some protesters killed by British forces.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMojtahed-Zadeh1999130-95]

Before the development of the petroleum industry, the island was largely devoted to [pearl fisheries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_hunting) and, as late as the 19th century, was considered to be the finest in the world.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE''EB''1878-21] In 1903, German explorer [Hermann Burchardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Burchardt) visited Bahrain and took many photographs of historical sites, including the old *Qaṣr es-Sheikh*, photos now stored at the [Ethnological Museum of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnological_Museum_of_Berlin).[^cite_note-96] Before the [First World War](4764461.md), there were about 400 vessels hunting pearls and an annual export of more than £30,000.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE''EB''1911-37]

In 1911, a group of Bahraini merchants demanded restrictions on the British influence in the country. The group's leaders were subsequently arrested and exiled to India. In 1923, the British introduced [administrative reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_administrative_reforms_of_the_1920s) and replaced Sheikh Issa bin Ali with his son. Some clerical opponents and families, such as [Al Dosari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dawasir), left or were exiled to Saudi Arabia.[^cite_note-97] Three years later the British placed the country under the *de facto* rule of [Charles Belgrave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Belgrave) who operated as an adviser to the ruler until 1957.[^cite_note-All_at_sea_over_'the_Gulf'-98][^cite_note-archiveeditions-99] Belgrave brought a number of reforms such as establishment of the country's first modern school in 1919 and the abolition of [slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_slave_trade) in 1937.[^cite_note-100] At the same time, the pearl diving industry developed at a rapid pace.

In 1927, [Rezā Shāh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rez%C4%81_Sh%C4%81h), then [Shah of Iran](13877.md), demanded sovereignty over Bahrain in a letter to the [League of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations), a move that prompted Belgrave to undertake harsh measures including encouraging conflicts between [Shia](26961.md) and Sunni Muslims to bring down the uprisings and limit the Iranian influence.[^cite_note-ICB-101] Belgrave even went further by suggesting to rename the [Persian Gulf](24761.md) to the "Arabian Gulf"; however, the proposal was refused by the British government.[^cite_note-All_at_sea_over_'the_Gulf'-98] Britain's interest in Bahrain's development was motivated by concerns over Saudi and Iranian ambitions in the region.



The First Oil Well in Bahrain, with oil first being extracted in 1931

The [Bahrain Petroleum Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_Petroleum_Company) (Bapco), a subsidiary of the [Standard Oil Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Oil_Company) of California (Socal),[^cite_note-102] discovered oil in 1932.[^cite_note-103]

In the early 1930s, Bahrain Airport was developed. [Imperial Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Airways) flew there, including the [Handley Page HP42](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handley_Page_HP42) aircraft. Later in the same decade, the Bahrain Maritime Airport was established, for flying boats and seaplanes.[^cite_note-104]

Bahrain [participated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Participants_in_World_War_II#Bahrain) in the [Second World War](32927.md) on the [Allied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II) side, joining on 10 September 1939. On 19 October 1940, four Italian [SM.82s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savoia-Marchetti_SM.82) bombers [bombed Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombing_of_Bahrain_in_World_War_II) alongside [Dhahran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhahran) oilfields in Saudi Arabia,[^cite_note-105] attacking Allied-operated oil refineries.[^cite_note-Hamza-106] Although minimal damage was caused in both locations, the attack forced the Allies to upgrade Bahrain's defences, an action which further stretched Allied military resources.[^cite_note-Hamza-106]



Overview of Manama, 1953

After World War II, increasing sentiments against British occupation spread throughout Arab countries and led to protests in Bahrain. The protests focused on the Jewish community.[^cite_note-MOMENT-107] In 1948, following [rising hostilities and looting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1947_Manama_riots),[^cite_note-israelnationalnews.com-108] most members of Bahrain's Jewish community abandoned their properties and fled to [Bombay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombay), later settling in Israel ([Pardes Hanna-Karkur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pardes_Hanna-Karkur)) and the United Kingdom. As of 2008, 37 Jews remained in the country.[^cite_note-israelnationalnews.com-108] In the 1950s, the [National Union Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Union_Committee), formed by reformists following sectarian clashes, demanded an elected popular assembly, removal of Belgrave and carried out a number of protests and general strikes. In 1965 a month-long [uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_Intifada) broke out after hundreds of workers at the Bahrain Petroleum Company were laid off.[^cite_note-BBC_11.5.2012-109]

### Independence

See also: [Bahrain Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_Province)



[Manama souq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manama_souq) in 1965

On 15 August 1971,[^cite_note-110][^cite_note-111] though the [Shah of Iran](13877.md) was claiming historical sovereignty over Bahrain, he accepted [a referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahraini_independence_survey,_1970) held by the United Nations and eventually Bahrain declared independence and signed a new treaty of friendship with Britain. Bahrain joined the United Nations and the [Arab League](52625.md) later in the year.[^cite_note-112] The oil boom of the 1970s benefited Bahrain greatly, although the subsequent downturn hurt the economy. The country had already begun diversification of its economy and benefited further from the [Lebanese Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Civil_War) in the 1970s and 1980s, when Bahrain replaced [Beirut](37428.md) as the Middle East's financial hub after Lebanon's large banking sector was driven out of the country by the war.

In 1981, following the [1979 revolution in Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_revolution_in_Iran), the Bahraini Shia population orchestrated a [failed coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1981_Bahraini_coup_attempt) attempt under the auspices of a front organisation, the [Islamic Front for the Liberation of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Front_for_the_Liberation_of_Bahrain). The coup would have installed a Shia cleric exiled in Iran, [Hujjatu l-Islām](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hujjat_al-Islam) [Hādī al-Mudarrisī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadi_al-Modarresi), as supreme leader heading a [theocratic government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theocracy).[^cite_note-113] In December 1994, a group of youths threw stones at female runners for running bare-legged during an international marathon. The resulting clash with police soon grew into civil unrest.[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115]

A [popular uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1990s_uprising_in_Bahrain) occurred between 1994 and 2000 in which leftists, liberals and fundamentalists joined forces.[^cite_note-116] The event resulted in approximately forty deaths and ended after Hamad bin Isa Al Khalifa became the Emir of Bahrain in 1999.[^cite_note-117] He instituted elections for parliament, gave women the right to vote, and released all political prisoners.[^cite_note-AI_Bahr_promising-118] A referendum on 14–15 February 2001 massively supported the [National Action Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Action_Charter_of_Bahrain).[^cite_note-UNDP-POGAR-119] As part of the adoption of the National Action Charter on 14 February 2002, Bahrain changed its formal name from the State (*dawla*) of Bahrain to the Kingdom of Bahrain.[^cite_note-120] At the same time, the title of the Head of State, Hamad bin Isa al-Khalifa, was changed from Emir to King.[^cite_note-121]

The country participated in [military action](19666611.md) against the [Taliban](30635.md) in October 2001 by deploying a [frigate](11464.md) in the Arabian Sea for rescue and humanitarian operations.[^cite_note-Europa-122] As a result, in November of that year, American president [George W. Bush](3414021.md)'s administration designated Bahrain as a "[major non-NATO ally](3812445.md)".[^cite_note-Europa-122] Bahrain opposed the [2003 American-led invasion of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_American_invasion_of_Iraq) and had offered [Saddam Hussein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddam_Hussein) asylum in the days before the invasion.[^cite_note-Europa-122] Relations improved with neighbouring [Qatar](8238258.md) after the border dispute over the [Hawar Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawar_Islands) was resolved by the [International Court of Justice](14918.md) in [The Hague](30269.md) in 2001.[^cite_note-123] Following the political liberalisation of the country, Bahrain negotiated a [free trade agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain%E2%80%93United_States_Free_Trade_Agreement) with America in 2004.[^cite_note-124]

In 2005, [Qal'at al-Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qal'at_al-Bahrain), a fort and archaeological complex was inscribed as a [UNESCO World Heritage Site](44940.md).

### 2011 Bahraini protests

Main articles: [2011 Bahraini uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Bahraini_uprising) and [Saudi-led intervention in Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi-led_intervention_in_Bahrain)

Inspired by the regional [Arab Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Spring), Bahrain's Shia population [started](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_of_Rage_\(Bahrain\)) large protests against its Sunni rulers in early 2011.[^cite_note-reutbahsoe-125][^cite_note-126] The government initially allowed [protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_of_loyalty_to_martyrs) following a [pre-dawn raid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloody_Thursday_\(Bahrain\)) on protesters camped in [Pearl Roundabout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_Roundabout).[^cite_note-127] A month later it requested security assistance from Saudi Arabia and other [Gulf Cooperation Council](220950.md) countries and declared a three-month state of emergency.[^cite_note-bici-128] The government then launched a crackdown on the opposition that included conducting thousands of arrests and [systematic torture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torture_during_the_Bahraini_uprising_\(2011%E2%80%93present\)).[^cite_note-BBC_News-129][^cite_note-130][^cite_note-131] Almost daily clashes between protesters and security forces led to [dozens of deaths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casualties_of_the_2011_Bahraini_uprising_and_its_aftermath).[^cite_note-AJE_23.4.2012-132] Protests, sometimes staged by opposition parties, were ongoing.[^cite_note-reutbahral-133][^cite_note-bbcbahmar-134][^cite_note-reutbahmass-135] More than 80 civilians and 13 policemen have been killed as of March 2014.[^cite_note-136] According to [Physicians for Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicians_for_Human_Rights), 34 of these deaths were related to government usage of [tear gas](1202646.md) originally manufactured by America-based [Federal Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Laboratories).[^cite_note-phr-137][^cite_note-ukforeignaffairscommittee-138] The lack of coverage by Arab media in the Persian Gulf,[^cite_note-Hammond-139] as compared to other Arab Spring uprisings, has sparked several controversies.

According to [Amnesty International](18947898.md), "Ten years after Bahrain's popular uprising, systemic injustice has intensified and political repression targeting dissidents, human rights defenders, clerics and independent civil society have effectively shut any space for the peaceful exercise of the right to freedom of expression or peaceful activism".[^cite_note-140] Bahrain remains militarily and financially dependent on Saudi Arabia and the UAE,[^cite_note-france1-141] though this is changing with the economic reforms being implemented by the government.[^cite_note-142]

### 2026 Iran war

Main article: [2026 Iranian strikes on Bahrain](82555742.md)

On 28 February 2026, the [United States](3434750.md) and [Israel](9282173.md) launched joint airstrikes on Iran, [killing the supreme leader of Iran](82537558.md) and many other Iranian officials, starting a war with the stated goal of [regime change](154074.md). Iran responded with missile and [drone strikes](38212231.md) against Israel and [US bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_bases_around_the_world) and US allies in the region, including [Bahrain](82555742.md), which suffered material and civilian casualties.[^cite_note-143] In July 2026, Bahrain launched airstrikes on Iran for the first time in retaliation, along with Kuwait.[^cite_note-144]

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Bahrain)

See also: [Land reclamation in Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reclamation_in_Bahrain)



Topography

Bahrain is a generally flat and arid [archipelago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archipelago) in the Persian Gulf. It consists of a low desert plain rising gently to a low central escarpment with the highest point the 134 m (440 ft) [Mountain of Smoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_of_Smoke) *(Jabal ad Dukhan)*.[^cite_note-145][^cite_note-Britannica-146] Bahrain had a total area of 665 km<sup>2</sup> (257 sq mi) but due to [land reclamation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reclamation), the area increased to 780 km<sup>2</sup> (300 sq mi).[^cite_note-Britannica-146]

Often described as an archipelago of 33 islands,[^cite_note-147] extensive land reclamation projects have changed this; by August 2008 the number of islands and island groups had increased to 84.[^cite_note-148] Bahrain does not share a land boundary with another country but does have a 161 km (100 mi) coastline. The country also claims a further 22 km (12 nmi) of [territorial sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_sea) and a 44 km (24 nmi) [contiguous zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contiguous_zone). Bahrain's largest islands are Bahrain Island, the [Hawar Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawar_Islands), [Muharraq Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muharraq_Island), [Umm an Nasan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umm_an_Nasan), and [Sitra](4006219.md). Bahrain has mild winters and very hot, humid summers. The country's natural resources include large quantities of oil and natural gas as well as fish in the offshore waters. Arable land constitutes only 2.82%[^cite_note-CIA-149] of the total area.

About 92% of Bahrain is desert with periodic droughts and dust storms, the main natural hazards for Bahrainis.[^cite_note-TR-150] In Bahrain [forest cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_cover) is around 1% of the total land area, equivalent to 700 hectares (ha) of forest in 2020, up from 220 hectares (ha) in 1990. For the year 2015, 100% of the forest area was reported to be under [public ownership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_ownership).[^cite_note-151][^cite_note-152]

Environmental issues facing Bahrain include desertification resulting from the degradation of limited arable land, coastal degradation (damage to coastlines, [coral reefs](87410.md), and sea vegetation) resulting from oil spills and other discharges from large tankers, oil refineries, distribution stations, and illegal land reclamation at places such as [Tubli Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tubli_Bay). The agricultural and domestic sectors' over-utilisation of the Dammam Aquifer, the principal aquifer in Bahrain, has led to its [salinisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_salinity) by adjacent brackish and saline water bodies. A hydrochemical study identified the locations of the sources of aquifer salinisation and delineated their areas of influence. The investigation indicates that the aquifer water quality is significantly modified as groundwater flows from the northwestern parts of Bahrain, where the aquifer receives its water by lateral underflow from eastern Saudi Arabia to the southern and southeastern parts. Four types of salinisation of the aquifer are identified: brackish-water up-flow from the underlying brackish-water zones in north-central, western, and eastern regions; seawater intrusion in the eastern region; intrusion of [sabkha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabkha) water in the southwestern region; and irrigation return flow in a local area in the western region. Four alternatives for the management of groundwater quality that are available to the water authorities in Bahrain are discussed and their priority areas are proposed, based on the type and extent of each salinisation source, in addition to groundwater use in that area.[^cite_note-153]

### Climate

Main article: [Climate of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_Bahrain)

&#x20;\
&#x20;The [Zagros Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zagros_Mountains) across the Persian Gulf in Iran cause low-level winds to be directed toward Bahrain. Dust storms from Iraq and Saudi Arabia transported by northwesterly winds, locally called [shamal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamal_\(wind\)) wind, cause reduced visibility in the months of June and July.[^cite_note-154]

Summers are very hot. The seas around Bahrain are very shallow, heating up quickly in the summer to produce very high [humidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humidity), especially at night. This is in stark contrast to other desert climates such as those of [Baghdad](4492.md) or [Phoenix](49121.md). Summer temperatures may reach up to 40 °C (104 °F) under the right conditions.[^cite_note-155] Rainfall in Bahrain is minimal and irregular. Precipitation mostly occurs in winter, with an average of 70.8 millimetres or 2.8 inches of rainfall recorded annually. The country experienced [widespread flooding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Persian_Gulf_floods) in April 2024 after heavy rainfall affected the Gulf region.

Due to [climate change](5042951.md) Bahrain is experiencing more frequent extreme heat, drought, flooding and [dust storms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_storm) and the threat of sea level rise. These conditions threaten Bahrain's food and water security, and are expected to become more severe in the future.[^cite_note-156] Despite being a relatively low-emitting country overall, Bahrain was the second highest [greenhouse gas](21350772.md) emitter per capita in 2023, at approximately 42 tonnes per person.[^cite_note-157] Most of Bahrain's emissions arise from burning [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel) in the energy sector.[^cite_note-Climate_Watch-158] The nation has committed to [net zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net-zero_emissions) by 2060[^cite_note-Climate_Watch-158] and also aims to reduce its greenhouse gas emissions by 30% by 2035.[^cite_note-159]

### Biodiversity

Main articles: [Wildlife of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildlife_of_Bahrain) and [List of birds of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_birds_of_Bahrain)



&#x20;[Greater flamingos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_flamingo) (*Phoenicopterus roseus*) are native to Bahrain.

More than 330 species of birds were recorded in the Bahrain archipelago, 26 species of which breed in the country. Millions of migratory birds pass through the Persian Gulf region in the winter and autumn months.[^cite_note-Fuller-160] One globally endangered species, *[Chlamydotis undulata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houbara_bustard)*, is a regular migrant in the autumn.[^cite_note-Fuller-160] The many islands and shallow seas of Bahrain are globally important for the breeding of the [Socotra cormorant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socotra_cormorant); up to 100,000 pairs of these birds were recorded over the Hawar Islands.[^cite_note-Fuller-160] Bahrain's national bird is the [bulbul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulbul) while its national animal is the [Arabian oryx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_oryx). And the national flower of Bahrain is the beloved Deena.

Only 18 species of mammals are found in Bahrain, animals such as [gazelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gazelle), desert rabbits and [hedgehogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedgehog) are common in the wild but the [Arabian oryx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_oryx) was hunted to [extinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinction) on the island.[^cite_note-Fuller-160] Twenty-five species of [amphibians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphibian) and [reptiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reptile) were recorded as well as 21 species of [butterflies](48338.md) and 307 species of [flora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flora).[^cite_note-Fuller-160] The marine [biotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biotope) are diverse and include extensive [sea grass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_grass) beds and [mudflats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mudflats), patchy [coral reefs](87410.md) as well as offshore islands. Sea grass beds are important foraging grounds for some threatened species such as [dugongs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dugong) and the [green turtle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_turtle).[^cite_note-CBD-161] In 2003, Bahrain banned the capture of [sea cows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sirenia), [marine turtles](142905.md) and [dolphins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolphin) within its territorial waters.[^cite_note-Fuller-160]

The Hawar Islands Protected Area provides valuable feeding and breeding grounds for a variety of migratory seabirds; it is an internationally recognised site for [bird migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_migration). The breeding colony of [Socotra cormorant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socotra_cormorant) on Hawar Islands is the largest in the world, and the dugongs foraging around the archipelago form the second-largest dugong aggregation after Australia.[^cite_note-CBD-161]

Bahrain has five designated [protected areas](100406.md), four of which are marine environments.[^cite_note-Fuller-160] They are:

- [Hawar Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawar_Islands)

- Mashtan Island, off the coast of Bahrain.

- [Arad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arad,_Bahrain) bay, in Muharraq.

- [Tubli Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tubli_Bay)

- [Al Areen Wildlife Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Areen_Wildlife_Park), which is a zoo and a breeding centre for [endangered animals](20110668.md), is the only protected area on land and also the only protected area which is managed on a day-to-day basis.

## Government and politics

Main article: [Politics of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_Bahrain)



Shaikh Hamad bin Isa Al Khalifa, the King of Bahrain

Bahrain is a [semi-constitutional monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-constitutional_monarchy) headed by King Shaikh Hamad bin Isa Al Khalifa. King Hamad enjoys wide executive powers which include appointing the [prime minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Bahrain) and his [ministers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_of_Bahrain), commanding the Bahrain Defence Force, chairing the [Higher Judicial Council](33282951.md), appointing the [parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assembly_\(Bahrain\))'s [upper house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consultative_Council_\(Bahrain\)) and dissolving its elected [lower house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Representatives_\(Bahrain\)).[^cite_note-\:2-162] The head of government is the prime minister. In 2010, about half of [the cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_of_Bahrain) was composed of the [Al Khalifa family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Khalifa).[^cite_note-163]

Bahrain has a [bicameral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicameralism) National Assembly (*al-Majlis al-Watani*) consisting of the Shura Council (*Majlis Al-Shura*) with 40 seats and the Council of Representatives (*Majlis Al-Nuwab*) with 40 seats. The forty members of the Shura are appointed by the king. In the Council of Representatives, 40 members are elected by absolute majority vote in [gerrymandered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerrymandered) single-member constituencies to serve four-year terms.[^cite_note-164] The appointed council "exercises a *de facto* veto" over the "rubber-stamp" elected, because draft acts must be approved so they may pass into law. After approval, the king may ratify and issue the act or return it within six months to the National Assembly where it may only pass into law if approved by two-thirds of both councils.[^cite_note-\:2-162]

In 1973, the country held its first [parliamentary elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahraini_general_election,_1973); however, two years later, the [late emir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isa_bin_Salman_Al_Khalifa) dissolved the parliament and suspended the [constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Bahrain#Constitution_of_1973) after parliament rejected the [State Security Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Security_Law_in_Bahrain).[^cite_note-BBC_11.5.2012-109] The period between 2002 and 2010 saw three parliamentary elections. The [first](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahraini_parliamentary_election,_2002), held in 2002 was boycotted by the opposition, [Al Wefaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Wefaq), which won a majority in the [second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahraini_general_election,_2006) in 2006 and [third](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahraini_general_election,_2010) in 2010.[^cite_note-165] [A 2011 by-election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahraini_parliamentary_by-election,_2011) was held to replace 18 members of Al Wefaq who resigned in protest against government crackdown.[^cite_note-ThReut_boycott-166][^cite_note-NYT_violence-167]

According to the [V-Dem Democracy indices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-Dem_Democracy_indices) Bahrain is 2023 the 4th least [electoral democratic country in the Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy_in_the_Middle_East_and_North_Africa).[^cite_note-vdem_dataset-168] Similarly, according to [International IDEA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Institute_for_Democracy_and_Electoral_Assistance)'s Global State of Democracy (GSoD) Indices and Democracy Tracker, Bahrain performs in the low range on overall democratic measures, with particular weaknesses in social group equality, judicial independence, and freedom of association and assembly.[^cite_note-169][^cite_note-170][^cite_note-171]

The opening up of politics saw big gains for both [Shīa](26961.md) and [Sunnī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunn%C4%AB) [fundamentalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_fundamentalists) in elections, which gave them a parliamentary platform to pursue their policies.[^cite_note-172] It gave a new prominence to clerics within the political system, with the most senior Shia religious leader, Sheikh [Isa Qassim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isa_Qassim), playing a vital role.[^cite_note-173] This was especially evident when in 2005 the government called off the Shia branch of the "Family law" after over 100,000 Shia took to the streets. Muslim fundamentalists opposed the law because "neither elected MPs nor the government has the authority to change the law because these institutions could misinterpret the word of God". The law was supported by women activists who said they were "suffering in silence". They managed to organise a rally attended by 500 participants.[^cite_note-174][^cite_note-175][^cite_note-176] [Ghada Jamsheer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghada_Jamsheer), a leading woman activist[^cite_note-177] said the government was using the law as a "bargaining tool with opposition Islamic groups".[^cite_note-GJ_18.12.2006-178]

Analysts of democratisation in the Middle East cite the fundamentalist' references to respect human rights in their justification for these programmes as evidence that these groups can serve as a progressive force in the region.[^cite_note-179] Some fundamentalist parties have been particularly critical of the government's readiness to sign international treaties such as the [United Nations](31769.md)' International Convention on Civil and Political Rights. At a parliamentary session in June 2006 to discuss ratification of the convention, Sheikh [Adel Mouwda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adel_Mouwda), the former leader of a [salafist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafi_movement) party, [Asalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Asalah), explained the party's objections: "The convention has been tailored by our enemies, God kill them all, to serve their needs and protect their interests rather than ours. This why we have eyes from the American Embassy watching us during our sessions, to ensure things are swinging their way".[^cite_note-180]

### Military

Main article: [Bahrain Defence Force](67658.md)



[RBNS *Sabha*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RBNS_Sabha) of the [Royal Bahraini Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Bahraini_Navy)



An [F-16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-16_Fighting_Falcon) of the [Royal Bahraini Air Force](1268112.md)

The kingdom has a small but professional and well-equipped military called the Bahrain Defence Force (BDF), numbering around 8,200 personnel, including 6,000 in the [Royal Bahraini Army](23989642.md), 700 in the [Royal Bahraini Naval Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Bahraini_Naval_Force), and 1,500 in the Royal Bahraini Air Force. The BDF command structure includes the [Bahrain Royal Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_Royal_Guard), which is the size of one battalion and has its own armoured vehicles and artillery. The [Bahrain National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_National_Guard) is separate from the BDF, though it is tasked with assisting it in defence from external threats, and it has about 2,000 personnel.[^cite_note-181][^cite_note-iiss2023-182]

The supreme commander of the Bahraini military is King Hamad bin Isa Al Khalifa. The deputy supreme commander is the Crown Prince, [Salman bin Hamad bin Isa Al Khalifa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salman_bin_Hamad_bin_Isa_Al_Khalifa).[^cite_note-183][^cite_note-184] The Commander-in-Chief of the BDF has been Field Marshal [Khalifa bin Ahmed Al Khalifa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khalifa_bin_Ahmed_Al_Khalifa) since 2008.[^cite_note-185]

The BDF is primarily equipped with American made equipment, such as the F-16 Fighting Falcon, [F-5 Freedom Fighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-5_Freedom_Fighter), [UH-60 Blackhawk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UH-60_Blackhawk), [M60A3 tanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M60_Patton), and the ex-[USS *Jack Williams*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Jack_Williams), an [Oliver Hazard Perry class frigate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Hazard_Perry_class_frigate) renamed the [RBNS *Sabha*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RBNS_Sabha).[^cite_note-iiss2023-182][^cite_note-186][^cite_note-187] In August 2020, the UK's *[HMS Clyde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Clyde_\(P257\))* was transferred to the Royal Bahrain Naval Force, with the ship renamed as RBNS *Al-Zubara*.[^cite_note-188][^cite_note-189] In January 2024 the Bahraini Navy received a second *Oliver Hazard Perry*-class frigate, the former [USS *Robert G. Bradley*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Robert_G._Bradley), which was renamed RBNS *Khalid bin Ali*.[^cite_note-190][^cite_note-191]

Bahrain was the first country in the Gulf to operate the F-16. In 2024 the Royal Bahraini Air Force expects to receive 16 aircraft of the modernised F-16 Block 70 variant,[^cite_note-aircombat-192] in addition to its current 20 F-16C/D and 12 F-5E/F fighters. The Royal Bahraini Army has 180 M60A3 main battle tanks, with 100 in active service and 80 in storage.[^cite_note-iiss2023-182]

The Government of Bahrain has [close relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain%E2%80%93United_States_relations) with America, having signed a cooperative agreement with the [American military](32212.md), and has provided America a base in [Juffair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juffair) since the early 1990s. An American naval presence has existed since 1948.[^cite_note-193] This is the home of the headquarters for Commander, *American Naval Forces Central Command* ([COMUSNAVCENT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COMUSNAVCENT)) / *American Fifth Fleet* (COMFIFTHFLT),[^cite_note-194] and around 6,000 American military personnel.[^cite_note-195]

Bahrain participates in the [Saudi Arabian-led intervention in Yemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Arabian-led_intervention_in_Yemen) against the [Houthis](17240332.md) and forces loyal to former President [Ali Abdullah Saleh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Abdullah_Saleh),[^cite_note-196] who was deposed in the 2011 [Arab Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Spring) uprising.[^cite_note-197]

The permanent British [Royal Navy](26061.md) base at [Mina Salman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mina_Salman), *[HMS Jufair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Jufair)*, was officially opened in April 2018.[^cite_note-198]

### Foreign relations

Main article: [Foreign relations of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_Bahrain)



King Hamad bin Isa Al Khalifa meets American president [Donald Trump](4848272.md) in May 2017.

Bahrain has established [bilateral relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilateral_relations) with 190 countries worldwide.[^cite_note-199] As of 2012, Bahrain maintains a network of 25 [embassies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embassies), three [consulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consulates) and four permanent missions to the Arab League, United Nations and [European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain%E2%80%93European_Union_relations) respectively.[^cite_note-200] Bahrain also hosts 36 embassies. America designated Bahrain a [major non-NATO ally](3812445.md) in 2001.[^cite_note-Voice_of_America-201] Bahrain plays a modest, moderating role in regional politics and adheres to the views of the Arab League on Middle East peace and [Palestinian](241405.md) rights by supporting the [two state solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_state_solution).[^cite_note-202] Bahrain is also one of the founding members of the [Gulf Cooperation Council](220950.md).[^cite_note-203] Relations with Iran tend to be tense as a result of a [failed coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1981_Bahraini_coup_d'%C3%A9tat_attempt) in 1981 which Bahrain blames Iran for and occasional claims of Iranian sovereignty over Bahrain by [ultra-conservative](6675.md) elements in the Iranian public.[^cite_note-204][^cite_note-205] In 2016, following the storming of the Saudi embassy in Tehran, both Saudi Arabia and Bahrain cut diplomatic relations with Iran. Bahrain and Israel established bilateral relations in 2020 under the [Bahrain–Israel normalisation agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain%E2%80%93Israel_normalization_agreement).[^cite_note-206]

Bahrain is the 81st most peaceful country in the world, according to the 2024 [Global Peace Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Peace_Index).[^cite_note-207]

### Human rights

Main article: [Human rights in Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_Bahrain)



[Bahraini protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahraini_uprising_of_2011) against the ruling Al Khalifa family in 2011

The period between 1975 and 1999, known as the "[State Security Law Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Security_Law_in_Bahrain)", saw wide range of human rights violations including arbitrary arrests, detention without trial, torture and forced exile.[^cite_note-Amnesty_27.9.1995-208][^cite_note-HRW_1.6.1997-209] After Emir (now King) Hamad Al Khalifa succeeded his father Isa Al Khalifa in 1999, he introduced wide reforms and human rights improved significantly.[^cite_note-210] These moves were described by Amnesty International as representing a "historic period of human rights".[^cite_note-AI_Bahr_promising-118]

Consensual male and female homosexual relations between adults over the age of 21 are legal in Bahrain, which is the only Muslim Gulf country where it has been legal since 1976.[^cite_note-211]



Protesters at the Pearl Roundabout just before it was demolished

Human rights conditions started to decline by 2007 when [torture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torture_in_Bahrain) began to be employed again.[^cite_note-HRW_Torture_Redux-212] In 2011, Human Rights Watch described the country's human rights situation as "dismal".[^cite_note-213] Due to this, Bahrain lost some of the high [International rankings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_rankings_of_Bahrain) it had gained before.[^cite_note-Freedom_in_the_World_2012-214][^cite_note-FH_FotN_2011-215][^cite_note-RWB_2002-216][^cite_note-RWB_2010-217][^cite_note-FH_Press_Freedom_2011-218]

In 2011, Bahrain was criticised for its crackdown on the [Arab spring uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahraini_uprising_of_2011). In September, a government-appointed [commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_Independent_Commission_of_Inquiry) confirmed [reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_reports_on_the_Bahraini_uprising_of_2011) of grave human rights violations, including [systematic torture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torture_during_the_Bahraini_uprising_\(2011%E2%80%93present\)). The government promised to introduce reforms and avoid repeating the "painful events".[^cite_note-219] However, reports by human rights organisations Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch issued in April 2012 said the same violations were still happening.[^cite_note-220][^cite_note-221]

Amnesty International's 2015 report on the country points to the continued suppression of dissent, restricted freedom of expression, unjust imprisonment, and frequent torture and other ill-treatment of its citizens.[^cite_note-222] As of October 2014, Bahrain is ruled by an "[authoritarian regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authoritarian_regime)" and is rated as "Not Free" by America-based non-governmental [Freedom House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_House).[^cite_note-FH2013-223] Freedom House continues to label Bahrain as "not free" in its 2021 report.[^cite_note-224] On 7 July 2016, the [European Parliament](9581.md) adopted, with a large majority, a resolution condemning human rights abuses performed by Bahraini authorities, and strongly called for an end to the ongoing repression against the country's human rights defenders, political opposition and civil society.[^cite_note-225]



&#x20;Several people held a sit-in in solidarity with human rights activist [Nabeel Rajab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabeel_Rajab).

In August 2017, American Secretary of State [Rex Tillerson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rex_Tillerson) spoke against the discrimination of Shias in Bahrain, saying, "Members of the Shia community there continue to report ongoing discrimination in government employment, education, and the justice system," and that "Bahrain must stop discriminating against the Shia communities." He also stated that "In Bahrain, the government continue to question, detain and arrest Shia clerics, community members and opposition politicians."[^cite_note-226][^cite_note-227] However, in September 2017, the American State Department has approved arms sales packages worth more than \$3.8 billion to Bahrain including F-16 jets, upgrades, missiles and patrol boats.[^cite_note-228][^cite_note-229] In its latest report Amnesty International accused both the American and British governments of turning a blind eye to horrific abuses of human rights by the ruling Bahraini regime.[^cite_note-230] On 31 January 2018, Amnesty International reported that the Bahraini government expelled four of its citizens after having revoked their nationality in 2012, turning them into stateless people.[^cite_note-231] On 21 February 2018, human rights activist [Nabeel Rajab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabeel_Rajab) was sentenced to a further five years in jail for tweets and documentation of human rights violations.[^cite_note-232] On behalf of the ruling family, Bahraini police have received training on how to deal with public protests from the British government.[^cite_note-233][^cite_note-234]

On 11 July 2020, a government watchdog in Bahrain claimed that the confessions of two pro-democracy campaigners were extracted by torture. Mohammed Ramadhan and Husain Moosa from Bahrain were leading figures in the pro-democracy protests of 2011. They were arrested in 2014 and accused of killing a police officer.[^cite_note-235] On 13 July 2020, the highest Court in Bahrain overruled the previous judgment and upheld the death sentences for both men. The judgment was criticised by Sayed Ahmed Alwadaei, the director of advocacy at the [Bahrain Institute for Rights and Democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_Institute_for_Rights_and_Democracy) (BIRD), who stated: "Today's verdict is yet another dark stain in the struggle for human rights in Bahrain."[^cite_note-236]

The 761-page *World Report 2021* published by the *[Human Rights Watch](46530.md)* in January 2021 revealed that the situation of human rights did not improve in Bahrain in 2020. It highlighted that the repression against social media activities escalated, death sentences were upheld by the courts against opposition activists after unfair trials, and the critics continued to be prosecuted for peaceful expression. The country also increased the use of the death penalty, while it denied medical treatment to some of the prominent opposition figures being kept in detention. The Human Rights Watch said that Bahrain uses several repressive tools to silence and punish every person who dares to criticise the government.[^cite_note-237]

In March 2021, Human Rights Watch (HRW) and the London-based Bahrain Institute for Rights and Democracy claimed that 13 children aged between 11 and 17 were beaten and threatened with rape and electric shocks after being detained in protest-related cases.[^cite_note-238]

#### Women's rights

Main article: [Women's rights in Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_rights_in_Bahrain)

Women in Bahrain acquired voting rights and the right to stand in national elections in the 2002 election.[^cite_note-239] However, no women were elected to office in that year's polls.[^cite_note-240] In response to the failure of women candidates, six were appointed to the Shura Council, which also includes representatives of the kingdom's indigenous Jewish and Christian communities.[^cite_note-241] [Nada Haffadh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nada_Haffadh) became the country's first female cabinet minister on her appointment as Minister of Health in 2004. The quasi-governmental women's group, the [Supreme Council for Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Council_for_Women), trained female candidates to take part in the 2006 general election. When Bahrain was elected to head the United Nations General Assembly in 2006 it appointed lawyer and women's rights activist [Haya bint Rashid Al Khalifa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haya_Rashed_Al-Khalifa) President of the United Nations General Assembly, only the third woman in history to head the world body.[^cite_note-242] Female activist Ghada Jamsheer said "The government used women's rights as a decorative tool on the international level." She referred to the reforms as "artificial and marginal" and accused the government of "hinder\[ing] non-governmental women societies".[^cite_note-GJ_18.12.2006-178]

In 2006, [Lateefa Al Gaood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateefa_Al_Gaood) became the first female MP after winning by default.[^cite_note-243] The number rose to four after the 2011 by-elections.[^cite_note-244] In 2008, [Houda Nonoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houda_Nonoo) was appointed ambassador to the United States making her the first Jewish ambassador of any Arab country.[^cite_note-245] In 2011, [Alice Samaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alees_Samaan), a Christian woman, was appointed ambassador to Britain.[^cite_note-246]

### Media

The predominant forms of media in Bahrain consists of weekly and daily newspapers, television, and radio.

Newspapers are widely available in multiple languages such as Arabic, English, Malayalam, etc. to support the varied population. *[Akhbar Al Khaleej](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akhbar_Al_Khaleej)* (أخبار الخليج) and *[Al Ayam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Ayam_\(Bahrain\))* (الأيام) are examples of major Arabic newspapers published daily. *[Gulf Daily News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_Daily_News)* and *[Daily Tribune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daily_Tribune_\(Bahrain\))* publish daily newspapers in English. *[Gulf Madhyamam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_Madhyamam)* is a newspaper published in Malayalam.

The country's television network operates five networks, all of which are by the [Information Affairs Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_Affairs_Authority). Radio, much like the television network, is mostly state-run and usually in Arabic. *Radio Bahrain* is a long-running English language radio station, and *Radio Mirchi 104.2* is a radio station serving the large expatriate population from the Indian subcontinent living in the country.

By June 2012, Bahrain had 961,000 internet users.[^cite_note-BBC-247] The platform "provides a welcome free space for journalists, although one that is increasingly monitored", according to Reporters Without Borders. Rigorous filtering targets political, human rights, religious material and content deemed obscene. Bloggers and other [netizens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netizens) were among those detained during protests in 2011.[^cite_note-ReferenceB-248]

Bahraini journalists risk prosecution for offences that include "undermining" the government and religion. [Self-censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-censorship) is widespread. Journalists were targeted by officials during anti-government protests in 2011. Three editors from the now-banned opposition daily [*Al-Wasat*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Wasat_\(Bahraini_newspaper\)) were sacked and later fined for publishing "false" news. Several foreign correspondents were expelled.[^cite_note-ReferenceB-248] An independent commission, set up to look into the unrest, found that state media coverage was at times inflammatory. It said opposition groups suffered from lack of access to mainstream media and recommended that the government "consider relaxing censorship". Assessments by Reporters Without Borders have consistently found Bahrain to be one of the world's most restrictive regimes.[^cite_note-249]

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Bahrain)



GDP per capita development in Bahrain

According to a January 2006 report by the [United Nations Economic and Social Commission for Western Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Economic_and_Social_Commission_for_Western_Asia), Bahrain has the fastest-growing economy in the Arab world.[^cite_note-Ref_2008-251] Bahrain's economy is ranked the 55th freest in the 2025 *[Index of Economic Freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_of_Economic_Freedom)* and 4th out of 14 countries in the Middle East/North Africa region.

In 2008, Bahrain was named the world's fastest-growing financial centre by the City of London's [Global Financial Centres Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Financial_Centres_Index).[^cite_note-252][^cite_note-autogenerated1-253] Bahrain's banking and financial services sector, particularly [Muslim banking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_banking_and_finance), have benefited from the regional boom driven by demand for oil.[^cite_note-autogenerated5-254] Petroleum production and processing is Bahrain's most exported product, accounting for 60% of export receipts, 70% of government revenues, and 11% of [GDP](12594.md).[^cite_note-CIA-149] [Aluminium production](28513407.md) is the second-most exported product, followed by finance and construction materials.[^cite_note-CIA-149]



Manama skyline as viewed from [Juffair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juffair)



A view of the Grand Avenue section of the Avenues



The view from outside one of the entrance gates at the Avenues

Economic conditions have fluctuated with the changing price of oil since 1985, for example during and following the [Persian Gulf crisis of 1990–91](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War). With its highly developed communication and transport facilities, Bahrain is home to a number of multinational firms and construction proceeds on several major industrial projects. A large share of exports consists of petroleum products made from imported crude oil, which accounted for 51% of the country's imports in 2007.[^cite_note-TR-150] In October 2008, the Bahraini government introduced a long-term economic vision for Bahrain known as 'Vision 2030' which aims to transform Bahrain into a diversified and sustainable economy.

In recent years, the government has undertaken several economic reforms in order to improve its financial dependency and also to boost its image as an island tourist destination that is compact, has short travel times and provides a much more "authentic Arab experience" than the regional economic and tourism powerhouse of Dubai.[^cite_note-255] The Avenues is one such example of the recent developments. It is a waterfront facing shopping mall that was opened in October 2019.[^cite_note-256] Bahrain depends heavily on food imports to feed its growing population—it imports large amounts of meat and 75% of its fruit.[^cite_note-257][^cite_note-DT-258]

Since only 2.9% of the country's land is [arable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arable_land), [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture) contributes to 0.5% of Bahrain's GDP.[^cite_note-DT-258] In 2004, Bahrain signed the [Bahrain–US Free Trade Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain%E2%80%93United_States_Free_Trade_Agreement), which will reduce certain trade barriers between the two nations.[^cite_note-autogenerated8-259] In 2011, due to the combination of the [Great Recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession) and the [2011 Bahraini uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Bahraini_uprising), its GDP growth rate decreased to 1.3%, which was the lowest growth rate since 1994.[^cite_note-260] The country's public debt in 2020 is \$44.5 billion, or 130% of GDP. It is expected to rise to 155 per cent of GDP in 2026, according to IMF estimates. The [military expenditure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_expenditure) is the main reason for this increase in debt.[^cite_note-mondediplo1-261]

Access to [biocapacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biocapacity) in Bahrain is much lower than the world average. In 2016, Bahrain had 0.52 global hectares[^cite_note-footprintdata-262] of biocapacity per person within its territory, much less than the world average of 1.6 global hectares per person.[^cite_note-263]

In 2016, Bahrain used 8.6 global hectares of biocapacity per person – their [ecological footprint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_footprint) of consumption. This means they use 16.5 times as much biocapacity as Bahrain contains. As a result, Bahrain is running a biocapacity deficit.[^cite_note-footprintdata-262]

Unemployment, especially among the young, and the depletion of both oil and underground water resources are major long-term economic problems. In 2008, the jobless figure was at 4%,[^cite_note-Ref_2008a-264] with women overrepresented at 85% of the total.[^cite_note-Ref_2008b-265] In 2007 Bahrain became the first Arab country to institute [unemployment benefits](378430.md) as part of a series of labour reforms instigated under Minister of Labour, [Majeed Al Alawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majeed_Al_Alawi).[^cite_note-266]

As of Q2 2023, total employment in Bahrain stood at 770,129 workers.[^cite_note-267] This included both Bahraini and Non-Bahraini workers. These employment levels represented a full recovery of employment since the downturn caused by the COVID pandemic.[^cite_note-268]

### Energy

Main article: [Energy in Bahrain](33334880.md)

Bahrain have [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_energy_resources_and_consumption) and [electricity](9540.md) production, consumption and imports which is making a net energy exporter.

Bahrain was the first place on the Arabian side of the [Persian Gulf](24761.md) where oil was discovered. The [First Oil Well, Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Oil_Well,_Bahrain) situated below [Jabal al Dukhan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_of_Smoke) has operated since 1932. It was operated by [Bahrain Petroleum Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_Petroleum_Company).

In 2023, the petroleum production of Bahrain was rated at about 189,000 barrels per day.[^cite_note-269] The petroleum reserves were quoted as 168 million barrels in 2023.[^cite_note-oilgas2023-270]

As of 2023, Bahrain was said to produce 16.7 billion cubic metres (bcm) of natural gas per year and have reserves of 68 bcm.[^cite_note-oilgas2023-270]

### Tourism

Main article: [Tourism in Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_Bahrain)



The cities of [Muharraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muharraq) (*foreground*) and Manama (background)



Manama seen from [Muharraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muharraq)

As a tourist destination, Bahrain received over eleven million visitors in 2019.[^cite_note-271] Most of these are from the surrounding Arab states, although an increasing number hail from outside the region due to growing awareness of the kingdom's heritage and partly due to its higher profile as a result of the [Bahrain Grand Prix](511204.md).

The kingdom combines modern Arab culture and the archaeological legacy of five thousand years of civilisation. The island is home to forts including [Qalat Al Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qalat_Al_Bahrain) which has been listed by [UNESCO](21786641.md) as a [World Heritage Site](44940.md). The [Bahrain National Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_National_Museum) has artefacts from the country's history dating back to the island's first human inhabitants some 9000 years ago and the [Beit Al Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beit_Al_Quran) (Arabic: بيت القرآن, meaning: the House of Qur'an) is a museum that holds Muslim artefacts of the [Qur'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qur'an). Some of the popular historical tourist attractions in the kingdom are the [Al Khamis Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Khamis_Mosque), which is one of the oldest mosques in the region, the [Arad fort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arad_fort) in Muharraq, [Barbar temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbar_temple), which is an ancient temple from the Dilmunite period of Bahrain, as well as the [A'ali Burial Mounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilmun_Burial_Mounds) and the [Saar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sar,_Bahrain) temple.[^cite_note-272] The [Tree of Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_of_Life,_Bahrain), a 400-year-old tree that grows in the [Sakhir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakhir) desert with no nearby water, is also a popular tourist attraction.[^cite_note-273]



The [Tree of Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_of_Life,_Bahrain), a 9.75 metre high [Prosopis cineraria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosopis_cineraria) tree that is over 400 years old

[Bird watching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_watching) (primarily in the [Hawar Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawar_Islands)), [scuba diving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scuba_diving), and [horse riding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse_riding) are popular tourist activities in Bahrain. Many tourists from nearby Saudi Arabia and across the region visit Manama primarily for the [shopping malls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shopping_malls) in the capital Manama, such as the [Bahrain City Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_City_Centre) and [Seef Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seef_Mall) in the [Seef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seef) district of Manama. The Manama Souq and *Gold Souq* in the old district of Manama are also popular with tourists.[^cite_note-MoFA-274]

In January 2019 the state-run Bahrain News Agency announced the summer 2019 opening of an underwater theme park covering about 100,000 square metres with a sunken [Boeing 747](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_747) as the site's centrepiece. The project is a partnership between the Supreme Council for Environment, Bahrain Tourism and Exhibitions Authority (BTEA), and private investors. Bahrain hopes scuba divers from around the world will visit the underwater park, which will also include artificial [coral reefs](87410.md), a copy of a Bahraini pearl merchant's house, and sculptures.[^cite_note-275] The park is intended to become the world's largest eco-friendly underwater theme park.[^cite_note-276]

Since 2005, Bahrain hosts an annual festival in March, titled *Spring of Culture*, which features internationally renowned musicians and artists performing in concerts.[^cite_note-TA-277] Manama was named the [Arab Capital of Culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Capital_of_Culture) for 2012 and *Capital of Arab Tourism* for 2013 by the Arab League and Asian Tourism for 2014 with the Gulf Capital of Tourism for 2016 by The Gulf Cooperation Council. The 2012 festival featured concerts starring [Andrea Bocelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_Bocelli), [Julio Iglesias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julio_Iglesias) and other musicians.[^cite_note-TOB-278]

### Value Added Tax (VAT)

The Kingdom of Bahrain introduced the Value Added Tax with effect from 1 January 2019.[^cite_note-279] This is a multipoint tax on the sale of goods and services in Kingdom of Bahrain. This has been managed by the government through the national bureau of revenue. The ultimate burden of this tax is passed on the consumer. To start with the maximum rate of VAT was 5% which is increased to 10% with effect from 1 January 2022.[^cite_note-280] The government of Bahrain is assuring compliance through high penalties on defaults and tighter audits. This first of its kind VAT has invited qualified chartered accounting firms mainly from India to advise on VAT matters. Firms like KPMG, KeyPoint, Assure Consulting and APMH have set up offices looking at the need for consulting in this domain of VAT.

## Infrastructure

Main article: [Transport in Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_in_Bahrain)



The new terminal of the Bahrain International Airport

Bahrain has one main [international airport](1519705.md), the [Bahrain International Airport](464443.md) (BAH) which is located on the island of [Muharraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muharraq_Island), in the north-east. The airport handled almost 100,000 flights and more than 9.5 million passengers in 2019.[^cite_note-Stats_2019-281] On 28 January 2021, Bahrain opened its new airport terminal as part of its economic vision 2030.[^cite_note-english.alarabiya.net-282] The new airport terminal is capable of handling 14 million passengers and is a big boost to the country's aviation sector.[^cite_note-english.alarabiya.net-282] Bahrain's national carrier, [Gulf Air](377403.md) operates and bases itself in the BIA.



The King Fahd Causeway as seen from space

Bahrain has a well-developed [road network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road_transport_in_Bahrain), particularly in Manama. The discovery of oil in the early 1930s accelerated the creation of multiple roads and [highways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highways) in Bahrain, connecting several isolated villages, such as [Budaiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budaiya), to Manama.[^cite_note-Yasser-283]

To the east, a bridge connected Manama to [Muharraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muharraq_Island) since 1929, a new causeway was built in 1941 which replaced the old wooden bridge.[^cite_note-Yasser-283] Currently there are three modern bridges connecting the two locations.[^cite_note-284] Transits between the two islands peaked after the construction of the Bahrain International Airport in 1932.[^cite_note-Yasser-283] [Ring roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_roads) and highways were later built to connect Manama to the villages of the [Northern Governorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Governorate) and towards towns in central and southern Bahrain.

The four main islands and all the towns and villages are linked by well-constructed roads. There were 3,164 km (1,966 mi) of roadways in 2002, of which 2,433 km (1,512 mi) were paved. A [causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causeway) stretching over 2.8 km (2 mi), connect Manama with [Muharraq Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muharraq_Island), and another bridge joins [Sitra](4006219.md) to the main island. The [King Fahd Causeway](1360149.md), measuring 24 km (15 mi), links Bahrain with the Saudi Arabian mainland via the island of [Umm an-Nasan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umm_an-Nasan). It was completed in December 1986, and financed by Saudi Arabia. In 2008, there were 17,743,495 passengers transiting through the causeway.[^cite_note-285] A second causeway, which will have both road and rail connection, between Bahrain and Saudi Arabia called 'King Hamad Causeway' is currently being discussed and is in the planning phase.[^cite_note-286]

Bahrain's port of [Mina Salman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mina_Salman) is the main [seaport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seaport) of the country and consists of 15 [berths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berth_\(moorings\)).[^cite_note-autogenerated9-287] In 2001, Bahrain had a merchant fleet of eight ships of 1,000 [GT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_Tonnage) or over, totalling 270,784 GT.[^cite_note-288] Private vehicles and taxis are the primary means of transportation in the city.[^cite_note-289] A [nationwide metro system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_Metro) is currently under construction and is due to be operational by 2025.

### Telecommunications

Main articles: [Telecommunications in Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications_in_Bahrain) and [Internet in Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_in_Bahrain)

The [telecommunications](33094374.md) sector in Bahrain officially started in 1981 with the establishment of Bahrain's first telecommunications company, [Batelco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batelco) and until 2004, it [monopolised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopoly) the sector. In 1981, there were more than 45,000 telephones in use in the country. By 1999, Batelco had more than 100,000 mobile contracts.[^cite_note-RE-290] In 2002, under pressure from international bodies, Bahrain implemented its telecommunications law which included the establishment of an independent *[Telecommunications Regulatory Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications_Regulatory_Authority_of_Bahrain)* (TRA).[^cite_note-RE-290] In 2004, [Zain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zain_Group) (a rebranded version of [MTC Vodafone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MTC_Vodafone)) started operations in Bahrain and in 2010 VIVA (owned by [STC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Telecom_Company) Group) became the third company to provide mobile services.[^cite_note-291]

Bahrain has been connected to the internet since 1995 with the country's [domain suffix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_suffix) being '[.bh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/.bh)'. The country's connectivity score (a statistic which measures both Internet access and fixed and mobile telephone lines) is 210.4 per cent per person, while the regional average in [Arab States of the Persian Gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_States_of_the_Persian_Gulf) is 135.37 per cent.[^cite_note-292] The number of Bahraini [internet users](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_users) has risen from 40,000 in 2000[^cite_note-293] to 250,000 in 2008,[^cite_note-294] or from 5.95 to 33 per cent of the population. As of August 2013, the TRA has licensed 22 [Internet service providers](100245.md).[^cite_note-295]

## Science and technology

### Policy framework

The *Bahraini Economic Vision 2030* published in 2008 does not indicate how the stated goal of shifting from an economy built on oil wealth to a productive, globally competitive economy will be attained. Bahrain has already diversified its exports to some extent, out of necessity. It has the smallest hydrocarbon reserves of any Persian Gulf state, producing 48,000 barrels per day from its one onshore field.[^cite_note-296] The bulk of the country's revenue comes from its share in the offshore field administered by Saudi Arabia. The gas reserve in Bahrain is expected to last for less than 27 years, leaving the country with few sources of capital to pursue the development of new industries. Investment in research and development remained very low in 2013.[^cite_note-\:0-297]

Apart from the Ministry of Education and the Higher Education Council, the two main hives of activity in science, technology, and innovation are the University of Bahrain (established in 1986) and the Bahrain Centre for Strategic, International, and Energy Studies. The latter was founded in 2009 to undertake research with a focus on strategic security and energy issues to encourage new thinking and influence policymaking.[^cite_note-\:0-297]

### New infrastructure for science and education

Bahrain hopes to build a science culture within the kingdom and to encourage technological innovation, among other goals. In 2013, the Bahrain Science Centre was launched as an interactive educational facility targeting 6- to 18-year-olds. The topics covered by current exhibitions include junior engineering, human health, the five senses, Earth sciences and biodiversity.[^cite_note-\:0-297]

In April 2014, Bahrain launched its National Space Science Agency. The agency has been working to ratify international space-related agreements such as the [Outer Space Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_Space_Treaty), the Rescue Agreement, the [Space Liability Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Liability_Convention), the Registration Convention and the Moon Agreement. The agency plans to establish infrastructure for the observation of both outer space and the Earth.[^cite_note-\:0-297]

In November 2008, an agreement was signed to establish a Regional Centre for Information and Communication Technology in Manama under the auspices of UNESCO. The aim is to establish a knowledge hub for the six-member states of the Gulf Cooperation Council. In March 2012, the centre hosted two high-level workshops on ICTs and education. In 2013, Bahrain topped the Arab world for internet penetration (90% of the population), trailed by the United Arab Emirates (86%) and Qatar (85%). Just half of Bahrainis and Qataris (53%) and two-thirds of those in the United Arab Emirates (64%) had access in 2009.[^cite_note-\:0-297]

### Investment in education and research

In 2012, the government devoted 2.6% of GDP to education, one of the lowest ratios in the Arab world. This ratio was on a par with investment in education in Lebanon and higher only than that in Qatar (2.4% in 2008) and Sudan (2.2% in 2009).[^cite_note-\:0-297] Bahrain was ranked 72nd in the [Global Innovation Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Innovation_Index) in 2024,[^cite_note-298] but jumped to the 62nd rank according to GII 2025.[^cite_note-299] Bahrain invests little in research and development. In 2009 and 2013, this investment reportedly amounted to 0.04% of GDP, although the data were incomplete, covering only the higher education sector. The lack of comprehensive data on research and development poses a challenge for policymakers, as data inform evidence-based policymaking.[^cite_note-\:0-297]

The available data for researchers in 2013 cover only the higher education sector. Here, the number of researchers is equivalent to 50 per million inhabitants, compared to a global average for all employment sectors of 1,083 per million.[^cite_note-\:0-297]

The [University of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bahrain) had over 20,000 students in 2014, 65% of whom are women, and around 900 faculty members, 40% of whom are women. From 1986 to 2014, university staff published 5,500 papers and books. The university spent about US\$11 million per year on research in 2014, which was conducted by a contingent of 172 men and 128 women. Women thus made up 43% of researchers at the University of Bahrain in 2014.[^cite_note-\:0-297]

Bahrain was one of 11 Arab states which counted a majority of female university graduates in science and engineering in 2014. Women accounted for 66% of graduates in natural sciences, 28% of those in engineering and 77% of those in health and welfare. It is harder to judge the contribution of women to research, as the data for 2013 only cover the higher education sector.[^cite_note-\:0-297]

### Trends in research output

In 2014, Bahraini scientists published 155 articles in internationally catalogued journals, according to Thomson Reuters' Web of Science (Science Citation Index Expanded). This corresponds to 15 articles per million inhabitants, compared to a global average of 176 per million inhabitants in 2013. Scientific output has risen slowly from 93 articles in 2005 and remains modest. By 2014, only Mauritania and Palestine had a smaller output in this database among Arab states.[^cite_note-300][^cite_note-\:0-297]

Between 2008 and 2014, Bahraini scientists collaborated most with their peers from Saudi Arabia (137 articles), followed by Egypt (101), the Britain (93), the America (89) and Tunisia (75).[^cite_note-\:0-297]

## Demographics

Main article: [Demographics of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_Bahrain)



Bahrainis observing public prayers in Manama



The Manama Souq on Bahrain National Day

In 2010, Bahrain's population was 1.2 million, of which 568,399 were Bahraini and 666,172 were non-nationals.[^cite_note-2010-census-301] It had risen from 1.05 million (517,368 non-nationals) in 2007, the year when Bahrain's population crossed the one million mark.[^cite_note-Ref_2008c-302] Though a majority of the population is Middle Eastern, a sizeable number of people from South Asia live in the country. In 2008, approximately 290,000 [Indian nationals](26998691.md) lived in Bahrain, making them the single largest expatriate community in the country, the majority from the south Indian state of [Kerala](4349459.md).[^cite_note-Ref_2008d-303][^cite_note-IndEmb-304]

Bahrain is the fourth [most densely populated sovereign state in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states_and_dependent_territories_by_population_density) with a population density of 1,646 people per km<sup>2</sup> in 2010.[^cite_note-2010-census-301] The only sovereign states with larger population densities are [city states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_states). Much of this population is concentrated in the north of the country with the [Southern Governorate](1292836.md) being the least densely populated part.[^cite_note-2010-census-301] The north of the country is so urbanised that it is considered by some to be one large [metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_area).

### Ethnic groups

Main articles: [Bahrani people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrani_people) and [Ethnic, cultural and religious groups of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic,_cultural_and_religious_groups_of_Bahrain)

Bahraini people are ethnically diverse.

Shia Bahrainis are divided into two main ethnic groups: [Baharna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baharna) and [Ajam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajam_of_Bahrain). The Baharna are ethnically [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab), and the Ajam are of [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persians) origin. Shia Persians form large communities in Manama and Muharraq. A small minority of Shia Bahrainis are ethnic Hasawis from [Al-Hasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ahsa_Oasis).

Sunni Bahrainis are mainly divided into two main ethnic groups: Arabs (al Arab) and [Huwala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huwala). Sunni Arabs are the most influential ethnic group in Bahrain. They hold most government positions and the [Bahraini monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Khalifa) are Sunni Arabs. Sunni Arabs have traditionally lived in areas such as Zallaq, Muharraq, [West Riffa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riffa) and Hawar islands. The Huwala are descendants of Sunni Iranians; some of them are Sunni Persians,[^cite_note-mig-305][^cite_note-306] while others Sunni Arabs.[^cite_note-307][^cite_note-308] There are also Sunnis of [Baloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baloch_people) origin. Most African Bahrainis come from [East Africa](260987.md) and have traditionally lived in Muharraq Island and Riffa.[^cite_note-309]

The state religion of Bahrain is [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Bahrain) and most Bahrainis are Muslim. The majority of Bahraini Muslims are [Sunni Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Muslims) with a recent estimate giving them a 51% majority and [Shia Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shia_Muslims) with a 49% minority.[^cite_note-310] It was previously one of the three countries in the [Middle East](19323.md) in which Shia were the majority, the other two nations being Iraq and Iran, though the rise of Sunni migrants made Sunnis the majority.[^cite_note-BBCShia2-311] Unofficial sources estimate sectarian identification to be approximately 55% [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni) and 45% [Shia](26961.md).[^cite_note-312][^cite_note-313] Public surveys are rare in Bahrain, but the [US department of state](31975.md)'s report on religious freedom in Bahrain estimated that Shias constituted approximately 55% of Bahrain's citizen population in 2018.[^cite_note-314] The royal family and most Bahrani elites are Sunni.[^cite_note-Pollock2-315] The country's two Muslim communities are united on some issues, but disagree sharply on others.[^cite_note-Pollock2-315] Shia have often complained of being politically and economically discriminated against in Bahrain; as a result, most of the protestors in the [Bahraini uprising of 2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahraini_uprising_of_2011) were Shia.[^cite_note-316][^cite_note-317][^cite_note-318]

The Shrinathji temple in Manama

[Christians in Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Bahrain) make up about 14.5% of the population.[^cite_note-2010-census-301] There is a native Christian community in Bahrain. Non-Muslim Bahraini residents numbered 367,683 per the 2010 census, most of whom are Christians.[^cite_note-2010Census-319] Expatriate Christians make up the majority of Christians in Bahrain, while native Christian Bahrainis (who hold Bahraini citizenship) make up a smaller community. Native Christians who hold Bahraini citizenship number approximately 1,000 persons.[^cite_note-2010Census-319] [Alees Samaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alees_Samaan), a former Bahraini ambassador to Britain is a native Christian. Bahrain also has a native [Jewish community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Bahrain) numbering thirty-seven Bahraini citizens.[^cite_note-Yaar-320] Various sources cite Bahrain's native Jewish community as being from 36 to 50 people.[^cite_note-321] According to Bahraini writer [Nancy Khedouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy_Khedouri), the Jewish community of Bahrain is one of the youngest in the world, having its origins in the migration of a few families to the island from then-Iraq and then-Iran in the late 1880s.[^cite_note-322] [Houda Nonoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houda_Nonoo), former ambassador to America, is Jewish. There is also a Hindu community on the island. They constitute the third largest religious group. The [Shrinathji temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrinathji_Temple,_Bahrain) located in old Manama is the oldest Hindu temple in the [GCC](220950.md) and the Arab world. It is over 200 years old and was built by the Thattai Hindu community in 1817.[^cite_note-323]

According to the 2001 census, 81.2% of Bahrain's population was Muslim, 10% were Christian, and 9.8% practised [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism_in_Arab_states) or [other religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_Bahrain#Religion).[^cite_note-CIA-149] The 2010 census records that the Muslim proportion had fallen to 70.2% (the 2010 census did not differentiate between the non-Muslim religions).[^cite_note-2010-census-301]

### Languages

[Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) is the official language of Bahrain, though English is widely used.[^cite_note-BO-324] [Bahrani Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrani_Arabic) is the most widely spoken dialect of the Arabic language and is different from standard Arabic, like all Arabic dialects. Arabic plays an important role in political life, as, according to Bahrain's constitution, an MP must be fluent in Arabic to stand for parliament.[^cite_note-325]

[Balochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balochi_language) is the second largest and widely spoken language in Bahrain. The Baloch are fluent in Arabic and Balochi. Among the Bahraini and non-Bahraini population, many people speak [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language), the official language of Iran, or [Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu), an official language in Pakistan and a regional language in India.[^cite_note-BO-324]

[Nepali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nepali_language) is also widely spoken in the [Nepalese workers and Gurkha Soldiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurkha) community. [Malayalam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malayalam), [Tamil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_language), [Telugu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telugu_language), [Bangla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangla_language) and [Hindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindi) are spoken among significant Indian communities.[^cite_note-BO-324] All commercial institutions and road signs are [bilingual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilingual_sign), displaying both English and Arabic.[^cite_note-326]

### Education

Main article: [Education in Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_Bahrain)

See also: [List of universities in Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_universities_in_Bahrain)



Female students at the [University of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bahrain) dressed in traditional garb

Bahrain boasts a diverse education system that includes a variety of private schools offering international curricula. Among these are British, American, and other international schools, catering to a wide range of educational needs.

Education is compulsory for children between the ages of 6 and 14.[^cite_note-327] Education is free for Bahraini citizens in [government schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_school), with the Bahraini [Ministry of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Education_\(Bahrain\)) providing free textbooks. [Coeducation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coeducation) is not used in government schools, with boys and girls segregated into separate schools.[^cite_note-328]

At the beginning of the 20th century, Qur'anic schools (*[Kuttab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuttab)*) were the only form of education in Bahrain.[^cite_note-MoE-329] They were traditional schools aimed at teaching children and youth the reading of the [Qur'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qur'an). After [World War I](4764461.md), Bahrain became open to western influences, and a demand for modern educational institutions appeared. 1919 marked the beginning of modern government school system in Bahrain when the Al-Hidaya Al-Khalifia School for boys opened in [Muharraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muharraq).[^cite_note-MoE-329] In 1926, the Education Committee opened the second government school for boys in Manama, and in 1928 the first government school for girls was opened in Muharraq.[^cite_note-MoE-329] In 2011, there were 126,981 students studying in government schools.[^cite_note-330]

In 2004, King [Hamad ibn Isa Al Khalifa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamad_ibn_Isa_Al_Khalifa) introduced the "King Hamad Schools of Future" project that uses Information Communication Technology to support [K–12 education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%E2%80%9312_education) in Bahrain.[^cite_note-331] The project's objective is to connect all schools within the kingdom with the Internet.[^cite_note-332] In addition to British intermediate schools, the island is served by the [Bahrain School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_School) (BS). The BS is an [American Department of Defense](7279897.md) school that provides a K–12 curriculum including [International Baccalaureate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Baccalaureate) offerings. There are also international schools that offer either the [IB Diploma Programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IB_Diploma_Programme) or Britain's [A-Levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A-Levels).

Bahrain also encourages institutions of higher learning, drawing on expatriate talent and the increasing pool of Bahrain nationals returning from abroad with advanced degrees. The [University of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bahrain) was established for standard undergraduate and graduate study, and the [King Abdulaziz University College of Health Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Abdulaziz_University_College_of_Health_Sciences), operating under the direction of the Ministry of Health, trains [physicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physician), [nurses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nursing), [pharmacists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacist) and [paramedics](75006.md). The 2001 National Action Charter paved the way for the formation of international universities such as the [Ahlia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahlia_University) in Manama and [University College of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_of_Bahrain) in [Saar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saar,_Bahrain).

The Royal University for Women (RUW), established in 2005, was the first purpose-built, international university in Bahrain dedicated solely to educating women. The [University of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_London) External has appointed MCG (Management Consultancy Group) as the regional representative office in Bahrain for distance learning programmes.[^cite_note-333] MCG is one of the oldest international institutes in the country. Institutes have also opened which educate South Asian students, such as the [Pakistan Urdu School, Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_Urdu_School,_Bahrain?action=edit\&redlink=1) and the [Indian School, Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_School,_Bahrain).

A few prominent institutions are the [American University of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_University_of_Bahrain) established in 2019,[^cite_note-334] the [Bahrain Institute of Banking and Finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_Institute_of_Banking_and_Finance), the Ernst & Young Training Institute, and the [Birla Institute of Technology International Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birla_Institute_of_Technology_International_Centre). In 2004, the [Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_College_of_Surgeons_in_Ireland) (RCSI) set up a constituent [medical university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RCSI-Bahrain) in the country. In addition to the [Arabian Gulf University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_Gulf_University), [AMA International University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AMA_International_University) and the [College of Health Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_of_Health_Sciences,_Bahrain), these are the only medical schools in Bahrain.

### Health

Main articles: [Health in Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_in_Bahrain) and [Healthcare in Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthcare_in_Bahrain)



The [King Hamad American Mission Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mission_Hospital).

Bahrain has a [universal health care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_health_care) system, dating back to 1960.[^cite_note-335] Government-provided health care is free to Bahraini citizens and heavily subsidised for non-Bahrainis. Healthcare expenditure accounted for 4.5% of Bahrain's GDP, according to the [World Health Organisation](33583.md). Bahraini physicians and nurses form a majority of the country's workforce in the health sector, unlike neighbouring Gulf states.[^cite_note-336]

The first [hospital](18994221.md) in Bahrain was the [American Mission Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mission_Hospital), which opened in 1893 as a dispensary.[^cite_note-337] The first public hospital, and also [tertiary hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_referral_hospital), to open in Bahrain was the [Salmaniya Medical Complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmaniya_Medical_Complex), in the [Salmaniya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmaniya) district of Manama, in 1957.[^cite_note-338] Private hospitals are also present throughout the country, such as the [International Hospital of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Hospital_of_Bahrain).

The [life expectancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_expectancy) in Bahrain is 73 for males and 76 for females. Compared to many countries in the region, the prevalence of [AIDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS) and [HIV](14170.md) is relatively low.[^cite_note-UNDP-339] [Malaria](20423.md) and [tuberculosis](30653.md) (TB) do not constitute major problems in Bahrain as neither disease is indigenous to the country. As a result, cases of malaria and TB have declined in recent decades with cases of contractions amongst Bahraini nationals becoming rare.[^cite_note-UNDP-339] The Ministry of Health sponsors regular vaccination campaigns against TB and other diseases such as [hepatitis B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepatitis_B).[^cite_note-UNDP-339][^cite_note-340]

Bahrain has an [obesity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obesity) epidemic, with 28.9% of all males and 38.2% of all females are classified as obese in 2012.[^cite_note-341] In 2010, Bahrain had one of the highest prevalence of [diabetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diabetes_mellitus) in the world, at 5th place. In 2010, more than 15% of the Bahraini population were affected by diabetes, and they account for 5% of deaths in Bahrain.[^cite_note-342] [Cardiovascular diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiovascular_disease) account for 32% of all deaths in Bahrain, being the number one cause of death in the country (the second being [cancer](105219.md)).[^cite_note-343] [Sickle-cell anaemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sickle-cell_disease) and [thalassaemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thalassaemia) are prevalent in Bahrain. 18% of Bahrainis are carriers of sickle-cell anaemia. 24% are carriers of thalassaemia.[^cite_note-344]

## Culture

Main article: [Culture of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Bahrain)



The [Isa ibn Ali Al Khalifa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isa_ibn_Ali_Al_Khalifa) house is an example of traditional architecture in Bahrain.

Islam is the main religion, and Bahrainis are known for their tolerance towards the practice of other faiths.[^cite_note-345] Intermarriages between Bahrainis and expatriates are not uncommon—there are many Filipino Bahrainis like Filipino child actress [Mona Marbella Al-Alawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mona_Louise_Rey).[^cite_note-346]

Rules regarding female attire are generally relaxed compared to regional neighbours; the traditional attire of women usually include the [hijab](68301.md) or the [abaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abaya).[^cite_note-Britannica-146] Although the traditional male attire is the [thobe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thawb), which also includes traditional headdresses such as the [keffiyeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keffiyeh), [ghutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keffiyeh) and [agal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agal_\(accessory\)), Western clothing is common in the country.[^cite_note-Britannica-146]

Although Bahrain legalised [homosexuality](5488304.md) in 1976, many homosexuals have since been arrested, often for violating broadly written laws against public immorality and public indecency.[^cite_note-347][^cite_note-348][^cite_note-349]

### Art

Main article: [Bahraini art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahraini_art)



An artisan making pottery using the traditional mud and water mixture on a revolving wheel

The modern art movement in the country officially emerged in the 1950s, culminating in the establishment of an art society. [Expressionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expressionism) and [surrealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrealism), as well as [calligraphic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calligraphy) are the popular forms of art in the country. [Abstract expressionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_expressionism) has gained popularity in recent decades.[^cite_note-TGE-350] [Pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pottery)-making and [textile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textile)-weaving are also popular products that were widely made in Bahraini villages.[^cite_note-TGE-350] Arabic calligraphy grew in popularity as the [Bahraini government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahraini_government) was an active patron in [Muslim art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_art), culminating in the establishment of a Muslim museum, [Beit Al Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beit_Al_Quran).[^cite_note-TGE-350]

The [Bahrain National Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_National_Museum) houses a permanent [contemporary art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemporary_art) exhibition.[^cite_note-351] The annual Spring of Culture[^cite_note-352] festival run by the Bahrain Authority for Culture and Antiquities[^cite_note-353] has become a popular event promoting performance arts in the kingdom. The [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture) of Bahrain is similar to that of its neighbours in the Persian Gulf. The [wind tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windcatcher), which generates natural ventilation in a house, is a common sight on old buildings, particularly in the old districts of Manama and [Muharraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muharraq).[^cite_note-Ali-354]

### Literature

Main article: [Literature of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature_of_Bahrain)



The [National Library of Bahrain at Isa Cultural Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Library_of_Bahrain_at_Isa_Cultural_Centre)

Literature retains a strong tradition in the country; most traditional writers and poets write in the [classical Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Arabic) style. In recent years, the number of younger poets influenced by [western literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_literature) are rising, most writing in [free verse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_verse) and often including political or personal content.[^cite_note-EC-355] [Ali Al Shargawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Al_Shargawi), a decorated longtime poet, was described in 2011 by *Al Shorfa* as the literary icon of Bahrain.[^cite_note-AS-356]

In literature, Bahrain was the site of the ancient land of [Dilmun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilmun) mentioned in the [Epic of Gilgamesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_of_Gilgamesh). Legend also states that it was the location of the [Garden of Eden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_Eden).[^cite_note-NYT-357][^cite_note-LAT-358]

### Media

Main article: [Media of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_of_Bahrain)

### Music

Main article: [Music of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Bahrain)

The music style in Bahrain is similar to that of its neighbours. The [Khaliji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khaliji_\(music\)) style of music, which is [folk music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_music), is popular in the country. The [sawt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sawt_\(music\)) style of music, which involves a complex form of urban music, performed by an [Oud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oud) (plucked lute), a [violin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violin) and [mirwas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirwas) (a drum), is also popular in Bahrain.[^cite_note-AUC-359] [Ali Bahar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Bahar) was one of the most famous singers in Bahrain. He performed his music with his Band *Al-Ekhwa* (*The Brothers*). Bahrain was also the site of the first [recording studio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recording_studio) amongst the Persian Gulf states.[^cite_note-AUC-359]

### Entertainment

See also: [Category\:Entertainment in Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Entertainment_in_Bahrain)

With regards to cultural and tourism activities, the Ministry of Culture[^cite_note-360] organises a number of annual festivals. such as the Spring of Culture in March and April, the [Bahrain Summer Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_Summer_Festival?action=edit\&redlink=1) and Ta'a Al-Shabab from August to September, and the Bahrain International Music Festival in October which features musical and theatrical performances, lectures, and much more.

As for cultural sites, residents, visitors, and tourists can re-live history through Bahrain's many historical sites.

### Sports

Main article: [Sport in Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_in_Bahrain)



A [mixed martial arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_Martial_Arts) event in Bahrain

Bahrain is the second nation after the United States to host [International Mixed Martial Arts (MMA) Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Mixed_Martial_Arts_Federation) World Championships of Amateur MMA in partnership with [Brave Combat Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brave_Combat_Federation).[^cite_note-361] Bahrain recorded an influx in global athletes visiting the nation for MMA training during 2017.[^cite_note-362] Brave Combat Federation is a Bahrain-based MMA promotion that has hosted events in 30 nations which is a record for hosting events in most number of nations by an MMA promotion.[^cite_note-363]

Bahrain MMA Federation (BMMAF) has been set up under the patronage of Sheikh [Khalid bin Hamad Al Khalifa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khalid_bin_Hamad_Al_Khalifa) and the jurisdiction of the Sports Minister, Sheikh [Nasser bin Hamad Al Khalifa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasser_bin_Hamad_Al_Khalifa).[^cite_note-364] The development of MMA in the nation is convened through KHK MMA, which owns [Brave Combat Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brave_Combat_Federation) which is the largest Mixed Martial Arts promotion in the Middle East.[^cite_note-365] Bahrain will be hosting Amateur World Championships 2017 in association with [International Mixed Martial Arts Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Mixed_Martial_Arts_Federation). Bahrain will be the first Asian and [Arab](2185.md) country to host the amateur MMA championship.[^cite_note-366] Bahrain is also home to KHK [MMA](228344.md) Fight Team, that facilitates training for some of the prominent talent in [Mixed Martial Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_Martial_Arts) in the world who compete in BRAVE Combat Federation, [PFL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professional_Fighters_League), and [UFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UFC).[^cite_note-367]

In 2018, [Cricket](25675557.md) was introduced in Bahrain under the initiative of KHK Sports and Exelon.[^cite_note-368] Bahrain Premier League 2018 comprised six franchise squads of 13 resident cricketers competing in the T20 format. The teams were SRam MRam Falcons, Kalaam Knight-Riders, Intex Lions, Bahrain Super Giants, Four Square Challengers and Awan Warriors.[^cite_note-369]



The Bahrain national football team playing [Australia](4792249.md) in June 2009, in a World Cup qualifier

[Football](10568.md) is also a popular sport in Bahrain.[^cite_note-autogenerated6-370] [Bahrain's national football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_national_football_team) has competed multiple times at the [Asian Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFC_Asian_Cup), [Arab Nations Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Nations_Cup) and played in the [FIFA World Cup](11370.md) qualifiers, though it has never qualified for the World Cup.[^cite_note-371] Bahrain's national football team won the [West Asian Football Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Asian_Football_Federation) cup and the [Arabian Gulf Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_Gulf_Cup) in 2019.[^cite_note-372][^cite_note-373] Both the cups came under the helm of Helio Sousa who is the manager of the nation's national football team. Bahrain has its own [top-tier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranking) domestic professional [football league](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_association_football), the [Bahraini Premier League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahraini_Premier_League). On 3 August 2020, the Kingdom of Bahrain bought a minority stake in the [Paris F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_FC), a team that plays in France's second tier. Bahrain's entry into the soccer club spurred criticism that the country is trying to whitewash its [human rights](13831.md) record and this is another way of buying influence in Europe.[^cite_note-374]

[Basketball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_in_Bahrain#Basketball), [rugby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_in_Bahrain#Rugby) and [horse racing](74711.md) are also widely popular in the country.[^cite_note-autogenerated6-370] The government of Bahrain also sponsors a [UCI WorldTeam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCI_WorldTeam) cycling team, [Bahrain Victorius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain%E2%80%93Merida_Pro_Cycling_Team), which participated in the [2017 Tour de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Tour_de_France).[^cite_note-375][^cite_note-376]



The podium ceremony at the [2007 Bahrain Grand Prix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_Bahrain_Grand_Prix)

Bahrain has a [Formula One race track](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_International_Circuit), which hosted the inaugural [Gulf Air](377403.md) [Bahrain Grand Prix](511204.md) on 4 April 2004, the first in an Arab country. This was followed by the Bahrain Grand Prix in 2005. Bahrain hosted the opening Grand Prix of the 2006 season on 12 March of that year. Both the above races were won by [Fernando Alonso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernando_Alonso) of [Renault](162292.md). The race has since been hosted annually, except for [2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Bahrain_Grand_Prix) when it was cancelled due to [ongoing anti-government protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahraini_uprising_of_2011), and the 2026 Bahrain Grand Prix which was moved to Malaysia due to the ongoing 2026 Iran war.[^cite_note-autosport89525-377] The [2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Bahrain_Grand_Prix) race occurred despite concerns of the safety of the teams and the ongoing [protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Bahrain_Grand_Prix_protests) in the country.[^cite_note-378] The decision to hold the race despite ongoing protests and violence[^cite_note-race_on-379] has been described as "controversial" by [Al Jazeera English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Jazeera_English),[^cite_note-380] [CNN](62028.md),[^cite_note-381] [AFP](471981.md)[^cite_note-382] and [Sky News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_News).[^cite_note-383] *[The Independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Independent)* named it "one of the most controversial in the history of the sport".[^cite_note-384]

In 2006, Bahrain also hosted its inaugural Australian [V8 Supercar](54162.md) event dubbed the "*Desert 400*". The V8s returned every November to the [Sakhir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakhir) circuit until 2010, in which it was the second event of the series. The series has not returned since. The [Bahrain International Circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_International_Circuit) also features a full-length [dragstrip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragstrip) where the Bahrain Drag Racing Club has organised invitational events featuring some of Europe's top drag racing teams to try to raise the profile of the sport in the Middle East.[^cite_note-385]

On 10 June 2024, the Bahrain Olympic Academy received the [Athena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena) honorary distinction for its role in aiding and supporting advancement of sports in its region. The honorary medal was presented to his highness Shaikh [Khalid bin Hamad Al Khalifa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khalid_bin_Hamad_Al_Khalifa) by Isidoros Kouvelos, President of [IOA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Olympic_Academy).[^cite_note-386][^cite_note-387]
