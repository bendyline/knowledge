# Oil refinery

An **oil refinery** or **petroleum refinery** is an [industrial process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_processes) [plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory) where [petroleum](23195.md) (crude oil) is transformed and [refined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refining) into products such as [gasoline](23639.md) (petrol), [diesel fuel](81761.md), [asphalt base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitumen), [fuel oils](392547.md), [heating oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heating_oil), [kerosene](16992.md), [liquefied petroleum gas](361384.md) and [petroleum naphtha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_naphtha).[^cite_note-Handwerk2-1][^cite_note-Leffler2-2][^cite_note-speight-3] [Petrochemical](67728.md) feedstock like [ethylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethylene) and [propylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propene) can also be produced directly by [cracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cracking_\(chemistry\)) crude oil without the need of using refined products of crude oil such as naphtha.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5] The crude oil feedstock has typically been processed by an [oil production plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_production_plant). In 2020, the total capacity of global refineries for crude oil was about 101.2 million barrels per day.[^cite_note-6]

Oil refineries are typically large, sprawling industrial complexes with extensive [piping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piping) running throughout, carrying streams of [fluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid) between large [chemical processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_process) units, such as [distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distillation) columns. In many ways, oil refineries use many different technologies and can be thought of as types of [chemical plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_plant). Since December 2008, the world's largest oil refinery has been the [Jamnagar Refinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamnagar_Refinery) owned by [Reliance Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reliance_Industries), located in [Gujarat](53707.md), India, with a processing capacity of 1.24 million barrels (197,000 m<sup>3</sup>) per day.

Oil refineries are an essential part of the petroleum industry's [downstream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downstream_\(petroleum_industry\)) sector.[^cite_note-7]

The Chinese were among the first civilizations to refine oil.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDengWang201140-8] As early as the first century, the Chinese were refining crude oil for use as an energy source.[^cite_note-Spataru_2017-9][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDengWang201140-8] Between 512 and 518, in the late [Northern Wei dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Wei_dynasty), the Chinese geographer, writer and politician [Li Daoyuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_Daoyuan) introduced the process of refining oil into various lubricants in his famous work *[Commentary on the Water Classic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commentary_on_the_Water_Classic)*.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-Spataru_2017-9][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDengWang201140-8]

[Crude oil](23195.md) was often distilled by [Persian chemists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy_and_chemistry_in_medieval_Islam), with clear descriptions given in handbooks such as those of [Muhammad ibn Zakarīya Rāzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Zakar%C4%ABya_R%C4%81zi) (c. 865–925).[^cite_note-11] The streets of [Baghdad](4492.md) were paved with [tar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tar), derived from petroleum that became accessible from natural fields in the region. In the 9th century, [oil fields](2910801.md) were exploited in the area around modern [Baku](4566.md), Azerbaijan. These fields were described by the [Arab geographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_geography) [Abu al-Hasan 'Alī al-Mas'ūdī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_al-Hasan_'Al%C4%AB_al-Mas'%C5%ABd%C4%AB) in the 10th century, and by [Marco Polo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marco_Polo) in the 13th century, who described the output of those wells as hundreds of shiploads.[^cite_note-12] [Arab and Persian chemists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy_and_chemistry_in_Islam) also distilled crude oil in order to produce [flammable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flammability) products for military purposes. Through [Islamic Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus), distillation became available in [Western Europe](33800.md) by the 12th century.[^cite_note-13]

In the [Northern Song dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Song_dynasty) (960–1127), a workshop called the "Fierce Oil Workshop", was established in the city of Kaifeng to produce refined oil for the Song military as a weapon. The troops would then fill iron cans with refined oil and throw them toward the enemy troops, causing a fire – effectively the world's first "[fire bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incendiary_device)". The workshop was one of the world's earliest oil refining factories where thousands of people worked to produce Chinese oil-powered weaponry.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDengWang201141-14]

Prior to the nineteenth century, petroleum was known and utilized in various fashions in [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon), [Egypt](8087628.md), [China](5405.md), [Philippines](23440.md), [Rome](25458.md) and [Azerbaijan](746.md). However, the modern history of the petroleum industry is said to have begun in 1846 when [Abraham Gesner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Pineo_Gesner) of [Nova Scotia](21184.md), [Canada](5042916.md) devised a process to produce kerosene from coal. Shortly thereafter, in 1854, [Ignacy Łukasiewicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignacy_%C5%81ukasiewicz) began producing kerosene from hand-dug oil wells near the town of [Krosno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krosno), [Poland](22936.md).

Romania was registered as the first country in world oil production statistics, according to the [Academy Of World Records](https://www.worldrecordacademy.org/technology/worlds-first-oil-refinery-ploiesti-218277).[^cite_note-pbs-15][^cite_note-16]

In North America, the first oil well was drilled in 1858 by [James Miller Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Miller_Williams) in [Oil Springs, Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_Springs,_Ontario), Canada.[^cite_note-17] In the United States, the petroleum industry began in 1859 when [Edwin Drake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Drake) found oil near [Titusville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titusville,_Pennsylvania), [Pennsylvania](23332.md).[^cite_note-18] The industry grew slowly in the 1800s, primarily producing kerosene for oil lamps. In the early twentieth century, the introduction of the internal combustion engine and its use in automobiles created a market for gasoline that was the impetus for fairly rapid growth of the petroleum industry. The early finds of petroleum like those in Ontario and [Pennsylvania](23332.md) were soon outstripped by large oil "booms" in [Oklahoma](22489.md), [Texas](29810.md) and [California](5407.md).[^cite_note-19]

[Samuel Kier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Kier) established America's first oil refinery in Pittsburgh on Seventh Avenue near Grant Street, in 1853.[^cite_note-The_American_Manufacturer_and_Iron_World-20] Polish pharmacist and inventor [Ignacy Łukasiewicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignacy_%C5%81ukasiewicz) established an oil refinery in [Jasło](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jas%C5%82o), then part of the Austro-Hungarian Empire (now in [Poland](22936.md)) in 1854.

The first large refinery opened at [Ploiești](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ploie%C8%99ti), Romania, in 1856–1857.[^cite_note-pbs-15] It was in Ploiesti that, 51 years later, in 1908, [Lazăr Edeleanu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laz%C4%83r_Edeleanu), a Romanian chemist of Jewish origin who got his PhD in 1887 by discovering [amphetamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphetamine), invented, patented and tested on industrial scale the first modern method of liquid extraction for refining crude oil, the [Edeleanu process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edeleanu_process). This increased the refining efficiency compared to pure [fractional distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_distillation) and allowed a massive development of the refining plants. Successively, the process was implemented in France, Germany, U.S. and in a few decades became worldwide spread. In 1910 Edeleanu founded "Allgemeine Gesellschaft für Chemische Industrie" in Germany, which, given the success of the name, changed to Edeleanu GmbH, in 1930. During the time of Nazi rule, the company was bought by the Deutsche Erdöl-AG and Edeleanu, being of Jewish origin, moved back to Romania. After the war, the trademark was used by the successor company EDELEANU Gesellschaft mbH Alzenau (RWE) for many petroleum products, while the company was lately integrated as EDL in the [Pörner Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C3%B6rner_Group). The Ploiești refineries, after being taken over by [Nazi Germany](21212.md), were bombed in the 1943 [Operation Tidal Wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Tidal_Wave) by the [Allies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II), during the [Oil Campaign of World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_Campaign_of_World_War_II).

Another close contender for the title of hosting the world's oldest oil refinery is [Salzbergen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salzbergen) in [Lower Saxony](18435.md), Germany. Salzbergen's refinery was opened in 1860.

At one point, the refinery in [Ras Tanura](1719051.md), [Saudi Arabia](349303.md) owned by [Saudi Aramco](290527.md) was claimed to be the largest oil refinery in the world. For most of the 20th century, the largest refinery was the [Abadan Refinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abadan_Refinery) in [Iran](14653.md). This refinery suffered extensive damage during the [Iran–Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War). Since 25 December 2008, the world's largest refinery complex is the [Jamnagar Refinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamnagar_Refinery) Complex, consisting of two refineries side by side operated by [Reliance Industries Limited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reliance_Industries_Limited) in Jamnagar, India with a combined production capacity of 1,240,000 barrels per day (197,000 m<sup>3</sup>/d).

Prior to [World War II](32927.md) in the early 1940s, most petroleum refineries in the United States consisted simply of crude oil distillation units (often referred to as atmospheric crude oil distillation units). Some refineries also had [vacuum distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_distillation) units as well as [thermal cracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_cracking) units such as [visbreakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visbreaker) (viscosity breakers, units to lower the [viscosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscosity) of the oil). All of the many other refining processes discussed below were developed during the war or within a few years after the war. They became commercially available within 5 to 10 years after the war ended and the worldwide petroleum industry experienced very rapid growth. The driving force for that growth in technology and in the number and size of refineries worldwide was the growing demand for automotive gasoline and aircraft fuel.

&#x20;In the United States, for various complex economic and political reasons, the construction of new refineries came to a virtual stop in about the 1980s. However, many of the existing refineries in the United States have revamped many of their units and/or constructed add-on units in order to: increase their crude oil processing capacity, increase the [octane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octane) rating of their product gasoline, lower the [sulfur](27127.md) content of their diesel fuel and home heating fuels to comply with environmental regulations and comply with environmental air pollution and water pollution requirements.

### United States

Main article: [Petroleum refining in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_refining_in_the_United_States)



Refinery, Bayport Industrial Complex, Harris County, Texas

In the 19th century, refineries in the U.S. processed crude oil primarily to recover the [kerosene](16992.md). There was no market for the more volatile fraction, including gasoline, which was considered waste and was often dumped directly into the nearest river. The invention of the [automobile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automobile) shifted [demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demand) to [gasoline](23639.md) and [diesel](81761.md), which remain the primary refined products today.[^cite_note-22]

Today, national and state legislation require refineries to meet stringent air and water cleanliness standards. In fact, oil companies in the U.S. perceive obtaining a permit to build a modern refinery to be so difficult and costly that no new refineries were built (though many have been expanded) in the U.S. from 1976 until 2014 when the small Dakota Prairie Refinery in North Dakota began operation.[^cite_note-23] More than half the refineries that existed in 1981 are now closed due to low utilization rates and accelerating mergers.[^cite_note-24] As a result of these closures total US refinery capacity fell between 1981 and 1995, though the operating capacity stayed fairly constant in that time period at around 15,000,000 barrels per day (2,400,000 m<sup>3</sup>/d).[^cite_note-25] Increases in facility size and improvements in efficiencies have offset much of the lost physical capacity of the industry. In 1982 (the earliest data provided), the United States operated 301 refineries with a combined capacity of 17.9 million barrels (2,850,000 m<sup>3</sup>) of crude oil each calendar day. In 2010, there were 149 operable U.S. refineries with a combined capacity of 17.6 million barrels (2,800,000 m<sup>3</sup>) per calendar day.[^cite_note-26] By 2025 the number of refineries had decreased to 132 but the total capacity increased to 18.4 million barrels (2,930,000 m<sup>3</sup>) per calendar day. To reduce operating costs and depreciation, refining is operated in fewer sites but of bigger capacity.[^cite_note-27]

In 2009 through 2010, as revenue streams in the oil business dried up and profitability of oil refineries fell due to lower demand for product and high reserves of supply preceding the [economic recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late-2000s_recession), oil companies began to close or sell the less profitable refineries.[^cite_note-28]

## Operation



[Neste Oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neste_Oil) refinery in [Porvoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porvoo), Finland



The oil refinery in [Haifa, Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haifa,_Israel), is capable of processing about 9 million tons (66 million barrels) of [crude oil](23195.md) a year. Its two [cooling towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooling_tower) are landmarks of the city's skyline.

Raw or unprocessed crude oil is not generally useful in industrial applications, although "light, sweet" (low viscosity, low [sulfur](27127.md)) crude oil has been used directly as a burner fuel to produce steam for the propulsion of seagoing vessels. The lighter elements, however, form explosive vapors in the fuel tanks and are therefore hazardous, especially in [warships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warships).

The hundreds of different hydrocarbon molecules in crude oil are separated in a refinery into components that can be used as [fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuels), [lubricants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lubricants), and feedstocks in [petrochemical](67728.md) processes that manufacture such products as [plastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic), [detergents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detergent), [solvents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvent), [elastomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastomer), and [fibers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber) such as [nylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nylon) and [polyesters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyesters).

[Petroleum](23195.md) [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuels) are burned in internal combustion engines to provide power for [ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ships), [automobiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automobiles), [aircraft engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_engine), [lawn mowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawn_mower), [dirt bikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirt_bikes), and other machines. Different [boiling points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling_point) allow the [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) to be separated by [distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distillation). Since the lighter liquid products are in great demand for use in internal combustion engines, a modern refinery will convert heavy hydrocarbons and lighter gaseous elements into these higher-value products.[^cite_note-29]

Oil can be used in a variety of ways because it contains hydrocarbons of varying [molecular masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_mass), forms and lengths such as [paraffins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkane), [aromatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aromatic), [naphthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naphthene) (or [cycloalkanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycloalkane)), [alkenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkene), [dienes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diene), and [alkynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkyne).[^cite_note-30] While the molecules in crude oil include different atoms such as sulfur and nitrogen, the hydrocarbons are the most common form of molecules, which are molecules of varying lengths and complexity made of [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) and [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon) [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom), and a small number of oxygen atoms. The differences in the structure of these molecules account for their varying [physical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_property) and [chemical properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_property), and it is this variety that makes crude oil useful in a broad range of several applications.

Once separated and purified of any contaminants and impurities, the fuel or lubricant can be sold without further processing. Smaller molecules such as [isobutane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobutane) and [propylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propylene) or [butylenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butylene) can be recombined to meet specific [octane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octane_rating) requirements by processes such as [alkylation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkylation), or more commonly, [dimerization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimer_\(chemistry\)). The octane grade of gasoline can also be improved by [catalytic reforming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalytic_reforming), which involves removing [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) from hydrocarbons producing compounds with higher octane ratings such as [aromatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aromatics). Intermediate products such as [gasoils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gasoil) can even be reprocessed to break a heavy, long-chained oil into a lighter short-chained one, by various forms of [cracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cracking_\(chemistry\)) such as [fluid catalytic cracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_catalytic_cracking), [thermal cracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_cracking), and [hydrocracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocracking). The final step in gasoline production is the blending of fuels with different octane ratings, [vapor pressures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vapor_pressure), and other properties to meet product specifications. Another method for reprocessing and upgrading these intermediate products (residual oils) uses a [devolatilization](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/devolatilization) process to separate usable oil from the waste asphaltene material. Certain cracked streams are particularly suitable to produce petrochemicals includes polypropylene, heavier polymers, and block polymers based on the molecular weight and the characteristics of the olefin specie that is cracked from the source feedstock.[^cite_note-31]

Oil refineries are large-scale plants, processing about a hundred thousand to several hundred thousand [barrels](594043.md) of crude oil a day. Because of the high capacity, many of the units operate [continuously](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_production), as opposed to processing in [batches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batch_production), at [steady state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steady_state) or nearly steady state for months to years. The high capacity also makes [process optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process_optimization) and [advanced process control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_process_control) very desirable.

[Crude oil](23195.md) is separated into fractions by [fractional distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_distillation). The fractions at the top of the [fractionating column](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractionating_column) have lower [boiling points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling_point) than the fractions at the bottom. The heavy bottom fractions are often [cracked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cracking_\(chemistry\)) into lighter, more useful products. All of the fractions are processed further in other refining units.



A breakdown of the products made from a typical barrel of US oil

[^cite_note-32]

[Petroleum products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_products) are materials derived from crude oil ([petroleum](23195.md)) as it is processed in [oil refineries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_refineries). The majority of petroleum is converted to petroleum products, which includes several classes of fuels.[^cite_note-33]

Oil refineries also produce various intermediate products such as [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen), light hydrocarbons, [reformate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformate) and [pyrolysis gasoline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrolysis_gasoline). These are not usually transported but instead are blended or processed further on-site. Chemical plants are thus often adjacent to oil refineries or a number of further chemical processes are integrated into it. For example, light hydrocarbons are [steam-cracked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_cracking) in an [ethylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethylene) plant, and the produced ethylene is polymerized to produce [polyethene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyethene).

To ensure both proper separation and environmental protection, a very low sulfur content is necessary in all but the heaviest products. The crude sulfur contaminant is transformed to [hydrogen sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_sulfide) via catalytic [hydrodesulfurization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodesulfurization) and removed from the product stream via [amine gas treating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amine_gas_treating). Using the [Claus process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claus_process), hydrogen sulfide is afterward transformed to elemental sulfur to be sold to the chemical industry. The rather large heat energy freed by this process is directly used in the other parts of the refinery. Often an electrical power plant is combined into the whole refinery process to take up the excess heat.

According to the composition of the crude oil and depending on the demands of the market, refineries can produce different shares of petroleum products. The largest share of oil products is used as "energy carriers", i.e. various grades of [fuel oil](392547.md) and [gasoline](23639.md). These [fuels](21188370.md) include or can be blended to give gasoline, [jet fuel](1205435.md), [diesel fuel](81761.md), [heating oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heating_oil), and heavier fuel oils. Heavier (less [volatile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volatility_\(chemistry\))) fractions can also be used to produce [asphalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitumen), [tar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tar), [paraffin wax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraffin_wax), [lubricating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lubricant) and other heavy oils. Refineries also produce other [chemicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemicals), some of which are used in [chemical processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_process) to produce [plastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastics) and other useful materials. Since petroleum often contains a few percent [sulfur](27127.md)-containing molecules, elemental sulfur is also often produced as a petroleum product. [Carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon), in the form of [petroleum coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_coke), and hydrogen may also be produced as petroleum products. The hydrogen produced is often used as an intermediate product for other oil refinery processes such as [hydrocracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocracking) and [hydrodesulfurization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodesulfurization).[^cite_note-34]

### Categories of petroleum products

#### light distillates

[Fuel gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_gas) is one of a number of [fuels](21188370.md) that under ordinary conditions are [gaseous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas). Most fuel gases are composed of [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) (such as [methane](18582230.md) and [propane](23643.md)), [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen), [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide), or mixtures thereof. Such gases are sources of [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) that can be readily transmitted and distributed through pipes.

[Liquefied petroleum gas](361384.md), also referred to as liquid petroleum gas (LPG or LP gas), is a [fuel gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_gas) which contains a [flammable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flammable) mixture of [hydrocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) gases, specifically [propane](23643.md), [butane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butane) and [isobutane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobutane). It can also contain some [propylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propylene), [butylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butylene), and [isobutylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobutylene).[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39]

[Naphtha](184342.md) ([/ˈnæfθə/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English), more rarely: [/ˈnæpθə/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English)[^cite_note-40]) is a flammable liquid hydrocarbon mixture. Generally, it is a fraction of [crude oil](23195.md), but it can also be produced from [natural-gas condensates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural-gas_condensate), [petroleum distillates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_distillate), and the [fractional distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_distillation) of [coal tar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_tar) and [peat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peat). In some industries and regions, the name *naphtha* refers to [crude oil](23195.md) or refined petroleum products such as [kerosene](16992.md) or [diesel fuel](81761.md).

[Gasoline](23639.md) ([North American English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_English)) or petrol (in other English-speaking countries) is a [petrochemical](67728.md) product, a transparent, yellowish and [flammable liquid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flammable_liquid) normally used as a [fuel](21188370.md) for spark-ignited [internal combustion engines](41228673.md). It is chemically composed of [organic compounds](22203.md) derived from the [fractional distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_distillation) of [petroleum](23195.md) and later chemically enhanced with [gasoline additives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gasoline_additive). It is a high-volume profitable product produced in crude oil refineries.[^cite_note-41]

#### middle distillates

[Kerosene](16992.md), also known as paraffin, is a [combustible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustibility) [hydrocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) [liquid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid) which is derived from [petroleum](23195.md). It is widely used as a [fuel](21188370.md) in [aviation](642298.md) as well as households. Its name derives from the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) [κηρός](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CE%BA%CE%B7%CF%81%CF%8C%CF%82#Ancient_Greek) (*kērós*) meaning "wax"; it was registered as a trademark by [Nova Scotia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nova_Scotian) geologist and inventor [Abraham Gesner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Pineo_Gesner) in 1854 before evolving into a [generic trademark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generic_trademark). It is sometimes spelled kerosine in scientific and industrial usage.[^cite_note-42]

[Jet fuel](1205435.md) or aviation turbine fuel (ATF, also abbreviated avtur) is a type of [aviation fuel](642298.md) designed for use in [aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft) powered by [gas-turbine engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_turbine). It is colorless to straw-colored in appearance. The most commonly used fuels for commercial aviation are Jet A and Jet A-1, which are produced to a standardized international specification. The only other jet fuel commonly used in civilian turbine-engine powered aviation is Jet B, which is used for its enhanced cold-weather performance.

[Fuel oil](392547.md) is any of various [fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_distillation) obtained from the [distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distillation) of [petroleum](23195.md) (crude oil). Such oils include [distillates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distillate) (the lighter fractions) and [residues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residue_\(chemistry\)) (the heavier fractions). Fuel oils include [heavy fuel oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_fuel_oil) (bunker fuel), [marine fuel oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_fuel_oil) (MFO), furnace oil (FO), [gas oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_oil) (gasoil), [heating oils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heating_oil) (such as home heating oil), [diesel fuel](81761.md), and others.

[Diesel fuel](81761.md), also called diesel oil, fuel oil (historically), or simply diesel, is any [liquid fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_fuel) specifically designed for use in a [diesel engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_engine), a type of [internal combustion engine](41228673.md) in which fuel ignition takes place as a result of compression of the inlet air and then injection of fuel without a spark. Therefore, diesel fuel needs good compression ignition characteristics.

#### heavy distillates and residuum

These require blending various feedstocks, mixing appropriate additives, providing short-term storage, and preparation for bulk loading to trucks, barges, product ships, and railcars. This classification is based on the way crude oil is distilled and separated into fractions.[^cite_note-Leffler2-2]

[Heavy fuel oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_fuel_oil) (HFO) is a [fuel oil](392547.md) of a [tar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tar)-like consistency. Also known as bunker fuel or residual fuel oil, HFO is the residual mixture left over from the distillation and [cracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cracking_\(chemistry\)) of [crude oil](23195.md) in oil refineries.[^cite_note-Heavy_fuel_oil_\:0-43] Generally, it has a boiling temperature between 350 and 500°C and is significantly more viscous than [diesel fuel](81761.md).[^cite_note-44] As it is created through the extraction of more valuable components of its [petroleum](23195.md) precursor, HFO contains various undesirable compounds and elements, which include [aromatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aromatics), [sulfur](27127.md), [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_pollution), [vanadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanadium), and others.[^cite_note-Heavy_fuel_oil_\:0-43] These non-hydrocarbon contaminants significantly increase toxic gas and particulate emissions upon combustion,[^cite_note-45] such as sulfur dioxide, [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide), and [nitrogen oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_oxide).[^cite_note-Heavy_fuel_oil_McKee-2013-46][^cite_note-47]

[Motor oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_oil), engine oil, or engine lubricant is any one of various substances used for the [lubrication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lubrication) of [internal combustion engines](41228673.md). Motor oils typically consist of [base oils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_oil) enhanced with various additives, particularly [antiwear additives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiwear_additive), [detergents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detergent), [dispersants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispersant), and, for multi-grade oils, [viscosity index improvers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscosity_index_improver).[^cite_note-48] The main function of motor oil is to reduce [friction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friction) and [wear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wear) on [moving parts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moving_parts) and to clean the engine from [sludge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sludge) (one of the functions of [dispersants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispersant)) and varnish (detergents). It also neutralizes acids that originate from fuel and from oxidation of the [lubricant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lubricant) (detergents), improves the sealing of piston rings, and cools the engine by carrying [heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat) away from moving parts.[^cite_note-49]

[Paraffin wax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraffin_wax) (or petroleum wax) is a soft colorless solid derived from [petroleum](23195.md), [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal), or [oil shale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_shale) that consists of a mixture of [hydrocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) molecules containing between 20 and 40 carbon atoms. It is solid at room temperature and [begins to melt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melting_point) above approximately 37 °C (99 °F),[^cite_note-Paraffin_wax_PF-50] and its boiling point is above 370 °C (698 °F).[^cite_note-Paraffin_wax_PF-50] Common applications for paraffin wax include [lubrication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lubrication), [electrical insulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_insulation), and [candles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candle);[^cite_note-51] dyed paraffin wax can be made into [crayons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crayon).

[Tar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tar) is a dark brown or black [viscous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscosity) liquid of [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) and free [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon), obtained from a wide variety of [organic materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_matter) through [destructive distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destructive_distillation). Tar can be produced from carbon-rich materials such as [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal), [wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood), [petroleum](23195.md), [peat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peat), and other organic matter.[^cite_note-Tar_Definition-52]

[Bitumen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitumen) ([UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English): [/ˈbɪtʃʊmɪn/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*BIH-chuum-in*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key), [US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English): [/bɪˈtjuːmɪn, baɪ-/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/2/2e/LL-Q1860\_%28eng%29-Naomi_Persephone_Amethyst\_%28NaomiAmethyst%29-bitumen.wav/LL-Q1860\_%28eng%29-Naomi_Persephone_Amethyst\_%28NaomiAmethyst%29-bitumen.wav.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/2/2e/LL-Q1860_%28eng%29-Naomi_Persephone_Amethyst_%28NaomiAmethyst%29-bitumen.wav/LL-Q1860_%28eng%29-Naomi_Persephone_Amethyst_%28NaomiAmethyst%29-bitumen.wav.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup> [*bih-TEW-min, by-*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key))[^cite_note-53] is an immensely [viscous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscosity) constituent of [petroleum](23195.md). Depending on its exact composition, it can be a sticky, black liquid or an apparently solid mass that behaves as a liquid over very large time scales. In [American English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English), the material is commonly referred to as *asphalt*. Whether found in natural deposits or refined from petroleum, the substance is classed as a [pitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_\(resin\)).[^cite_note-54] Prior to the 20th century, the term asphaltum was in general use.[^cite_note-Bitumen_Abraham1938-55] The word derives from the [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) word [ἄσφαλτος](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E1%BC%84%CF%83%CF%86%CE%B1%CE%BB%CF%84%CE%BF%CF%82#Ancient_Greek) (*ásphaltos*), which referred to natural bitumen or pitch. The largest natural deposit of bitumen in the world is the [Pitch Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_Lake) of southwest [Trinidad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinidad), which is estimated to contain 10 million tons.[^cite_note-56]

#### Other products

[Sulfur](27127.md) (or [sulfuric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfuric_acid)), byproducts of sulfur removal from petroleum which may have up to a couple of percent sulfur as organic sulfur-containing compounds. Sulfur and sulfuric acid are useful industrial materials. Sulfuric acid is usually prepared and shipped as the acid precursor [oleum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oleum).

[Petroleum coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_coke), abbreviated coke, pet coke or petcoke, is a final [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon)-rich solid material that derives from oil refining, and is one type of the group of fuels referred to as [cokes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_\(fuel\)). Petcoke is the coke that, in particular, derives from a final [cracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cracking_\(chemistry\)) process—a thermo-based chemical engineering process that splits long chain hydrocarbons of petroleum into shorter chains—that takes place in units termed [coker units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coker_unit).[^cite_note-Petroleum_coke_GoldBookP04522-57] (Other types of [coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_\(fuel\)) are derived from [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal).) Stated succinctly, coke is the "[carbonization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonization) product of high-boiling hydrocarbon fractions obtained in petroleum processing (heavy residues)".[^cite_note-Petroleum_coke_GoldBookP04522-57] Petcoke is also produced in the production of [synthetic crude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_crude) oil (syncrude) from [bitumen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitumen) extracted from Canada's [oil sands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_sand) and from Venezuela's [Orinoco oil sands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orinoco_oil_sands).[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-Petroleum_coke_petc-59] In petroleum coker units, residual [oils](4207510.md) from other [distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distillation) processes used in [petroleum refining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_refining) are treated at a high temperature and pressure leaving the petcoke after driving off gases and volatiles, and separating off remaining light and heavy oils. These processes are termed "coking processes", and most typically employ [chemical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_engineering) plant operations for the specific process of [delayed coking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delayed_coking).

[Petrochemicals](67728.md) (sometimes abbreviated as petchems[^cite_note-60]) are the [chemical products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_\(chemistry\)) obtained from [petroleum](23195.md) by refining. Some [chemical compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound) made from petroleum are also obtained from other [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel), such as [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) or [natural gas](22131.md), or renewable sources such as [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize), [palm fruit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elaeis) or [sugar cane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_cane).

Over 6,000 items are made from petroleum waste by-products, including [fertilizer](37401.md), floor coverings, [perfume](98581.md), [insecticide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insecticide), [petroleum jelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_jelly), [soap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap), and vitamin capsules.[^cite_note-61]

### Desalter

A [desalter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desalter) is a process unit in an oil refinery that removes salt from the [crude oil](23195.md). The salt is dissolved in the water in the crude oil, not in the crude oil itself. The desalting is usually the first process in crude oil refining. The salt content after the desalter is usually measured in PTB – pounds of salt per thousand [barrels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrel) of crude oil.[^cite_note-62] Another specification is [Basic sediment and water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_sediment_and_water). [^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64][^cite_note-Beychok3-65]

### Atmospheric and vacuum distillation

See also: [continuous distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_distillation)

Atmospheric and [vacuum distillation of crude oils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_distillation_of_crude_oils) are the main primary separation processes producing various straight run products, e.g., gasoline to lube oils/vacuum gas oils. [Distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distillation) of crude oil is typically performed first[^cite_note-66] under [atmospheric pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_pressure) and then under a [vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum). Low boiling fractions usually vaporize below 400°C at atmospheric pressure without cracking the hydrocarbon compounds. Therefore, all the low boiling fractions of crude oil are separated by atmospheric distillation. A [crude oil distillation unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_refining_processes#Crude_oil_-_Distillation_Unit) (CDU) consists of the pre-flash distillation column. The petroleum products obtained from the distillation process are light, medium, and heavy [naphtha](184342.md), [kerosene](16992.md), [diesel](81761.md), and oil residue. [^cite_note-67] [^cite_note-kroschwitz-68][^cite_note-mccabe-69][^cite_note-kister-70][^cite_note-king-71][^cite_note-perry-72] [Vacuum distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_distillation) or distillation under reduced pressure is a type of [distillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distillation) performed under reduced pressure, which allows the purification of compounds not readily distilled at ambient pressures or simply to save time or energy. This technique separates compounds based on differences in their boiling points. This technique is used when the boiling point of the desired compound is *difficult to achieve or will cause the compound to decompose.*[^cite_note-Vacuum_distillation_Adventures_in_vacuum_chemistry-73] Reduced pressures decrease the boiling point of compounds. The reduction in boiling point can be calculated using a temperature-pressure [nomograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomogram) using the [Clausius–Clapeyron relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clausius%E2%80%93Clapeyron_relation).[^cite_note-74] [^cite_note-kroschwitz-68][^cite_note-mccabe-69][^cite_note-kister-70][^cite_note-king-71][^cite_note-perry-72]

### Hydrodesulfurization

[Hydrodesulfurization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodesulfurization) (HDS), also called hydrotreatment or hydrotreating, is a [catalytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalytic) chemical process widely used to [remove sulfur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desulfurization) (S) from [natural gas](22131.md) and from refined petroleum products, such as [gasoline or petrol](23639.md), [jet fuel](1205435.md), [kerosene](16992.md), [diesel fuel](81761.md), and [fuel oils](392547.md).[^cite_note-75][^cite_note-Hydrodesulfurization_Gary-76][^cite_note-77] The purpose of removing the sulfur, and creating products such as [ultra-low-sulfur diesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultra-low-sulfur_diesel), is to reduce the [sulfur dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur_dioxide) (SO<sub>2</sub>) emissions that result from using those fuels in automotive [vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicles), [aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft), railroad [locomotives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locomotives), [ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ships), gas or oil burning [power plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil-fuel_power_station), residential and industrial furnaces, and other forms of fuel [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion). [^cite_note-Handwerk2-1][^cite_note-yamaguchi-78][^cite_note-79]

### Catalytic reforming

[Catalytic reforming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalytic_reforming) is a chemical process used to convert [naphthas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_naphtha) from [crude oil](23195.md) into liquid products called reformates, which are premium "blending stocks" for high-octane [gasoline](23639.md). The process converts low-octane linear [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) (paraffins) into [branched alkanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branched_alkane) (isoparaffins) and cyclic [naphthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naphthene), which are then partially [dehydrogenated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dehydrogenated) to produce high-octane [aromatic hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aromatic_hydrocarbon).[^cite_note-Catalytic_reforming_Ullmann-80] The dehydrogenation also produces significant amounts of byproduct [hydrogen gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_gas), which is fed into other refinery processes such as [hydrocracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocracking) and [hydrodesulfurization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodesulfurization). A side reaction is [hydrogenolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogenolysis), which produces light hydrocarbons of lower value, such as [methane](18582230.md), [ethane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethane), [propane](23643.md) and [butanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butanes). [^cite_note-81][^cite_note-82]

### Fluid catalytic cracking

[Fluid catalytic cracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_catalytic_cracking) (FCC) is the conversion process used in petroleum refineries to convert the high-boiling point, high-molecular weight [hydrocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) fractions of [petroleum](23195.md) (crude oils) into [gasoline](23639.md), [alkene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkene) gases, and other petroleum products.[^cite_note-Fluid_catalytic_cracking_Gary-83][^cite_note-Fluid_catalytic_cracking_Speight-84][^cite_note-Fluid_catalytic_cracking_Reza-85] The cracking of petroleum hydrocarbons was originally done by [thermal cracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_cracking), now virtually replaced by catalytic cracking, which yields greater volumes of high [octane rating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octane_rating) gasoline; and produces by-product gases, with more carbon-carbon double bonds (i.e. alkenes), that are of greater economic value than the gases produced by thermal cracking. [^cite_note-gary2-86][^cite_note-speight-3][^cite_note-reza-87]

### Hydrocracker

Hydrocracking is a catalytic cracking process assisted by the presence of added [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) gas. Unlike a [hydrotreater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrotreater), hydrocracking uses hydrogen to break C–C bonds (hydrotreatment is conducted prior to hydrocracking to protect the catalysts in a hydrocracking process). In 2010, 265 million tonnes (261,000,000 long tons; 292,000,000 short tons) of petroleum was processed with this technology. The main feedstock is vacuum gas oil, a heavy fraction of petroleum.[^cite_note-Cracking_(chemistry)_Weitkamp-88][^cite_note-89] [^cite_note-ullmann-90][^cite_note-ILOPet-91]

### Merox

[Merox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merox) is an acronym for [mercaptan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercaptan) [oxidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation). It is a [proprietary](29960.md) [catalytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalytic) chemical process developed by [UOP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UOP_LLC) used in oil refineries and [natural gas processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_processing) plants to remove mercaptans from [LPG](361384.md), [propane](23643.md), [butanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butane), light [naphthas](184342.md), [kerosene](16992.md), and [jet fuel](1205435.md) by converting them to liquid [hydrocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) [disulfides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disulfides).  Alternative processes for removing mercaptans are known, e.g. [doctor sweetening process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_sweetening_process) and caustic washing.

### Coker unit

A coker or [coker unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coker_unit) is an oil refinery processing unit that converts the residual oil from the [vacuum distillation column](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_distillation#Industrial-scale_applications) into low molecular weight [hydrocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) [gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas), [naphtha](184342.md), light and heavy [gas oils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_oil), and [petroleum coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_coke). The process thermally [cracks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cracking_\(chemistry\)) the long chain hydrocarbon molecules in the residual oil feed into shorter chain molecules leaving behind the excess carbon in the form of petroleum coke.  A [delayed coker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delayed_coker) is a type of [coker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coker_unit) whose process consists of heating a residual oil feed to its [thermal cracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_cracking) temperature in a [furnace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_furnace) with multiple parallel passes. This cracks the heavy, long-chain [hydrocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) molecules of the residual oil into coker [gas oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_oil) and [petroleum coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_coke).[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94]&#x20;

### Alkylation unit

An [alkylation unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkylation_unit) (alky) is one of the conversion [processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_process) used in [petroleum refineries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_industry). It is used to convert [isobutane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobutane) and low-molecular-weight [alkenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkene) (primarily a mixture of [propene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propene) and [butenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butene)) into alkylate, a high octane gasoline component. The process occurs in the presence of an acid such as [sulfuric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfuric_acid) (H<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>) or [hydrofluoric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrofluoric_acid) (HF) as [catalyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalyst). Depending on the acid used, the unit is called a sulfuric acid alkylation unit (SAAU) or hydrofluoric acid alkylation unit (HFAU). In short, the alky produces a high-quality gasoline blending stock by combining two shorter hydrocarbon molecules into one longer chain gasoline-range molecule by mixing isobutane with a light olefin such as propylene or butylene from the refinery's [fluid catalytic cracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_catalytic_cracking) unit (FCCU) in the presence of an acid catalyst.[^cite_note-95]  [^cite_note-96]

### Dimerization unit

[Dimerization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimer_\(chemistry\)) unit converts [olefins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olefins) into higher-octane gasoline blending components. For example, [butenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butene) can be dimerized into isooctene which may subsequently be hydrogenated to form [isooctane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isooctane). There are also other uses for dimerization. Gasoline produced through dimerization is highly unsaturated and very reactive. It tends spontaneously to form gums. For this reason, the effluent from the dimerization needs to be blended into the finished gasoline pool immediately or hydrogenated.

### Isomerization

[Isomerization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomerization) converts linear molecules such as normal [pentane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentane) to higher-octane branched molecules for blending into gasoline or feed to alkylation units. Also used to convert linear normal [butane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butane) into isobutane for use in the alkylation unit.

### Steam reforming

[Steam reforming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_reforming) or steam methane reforming (SMR) is a method for producing [syngas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syngas) ([hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) and [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide)) by reaction of [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) with water. Commonly, [natural gas](22131.md) is the feedstock. The main purpose of this technology is often [hydrogen production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_production), although syngas has multiple other uses such as production of [ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia_production) or [methanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methanol). ### LNG storage tank

A liquefied natural gas storage tank or a [LNG storage tank](6697465.md) is a specialized type of storage tank used for the storage of [Liquefied Natural Gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquefied_Natural_Gas), or LNG. LNG storage tanks can be found in ground, above ground or in [LNG carriers](12378960.md).&#x20;

### Amine gas treater

[Amine gas treating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amine_gas_treating), also known as amine scrubbing, gas sweetening and acid gas removal, refers to a group of processes that use aqueous solutions of various [alkylamines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amine#Aliphatic_amines) (commonly referred to simply as [amines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amine)) to remove [hydrogen sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_sulfide) (H<sub>2</sub>S) and [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) (CO<sub>2</sub>) from gases.[^cite_note-Amine_gas_treating_Kohl-98][^cite_note-Amine_gas_treating_Gary-99][^cite_note-100] It is a common [unit process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_process) used in refineries, and is also used in [petrochemical](67728.md) plants, [natural gas processing plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_processing) and other industries.  The [Claus process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claus_process) is a [desulfurizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desulfurisation) process, recovering elemental [sulfur](27127.md) from gaseous mixtures containing [hydrogen sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_sulfide) (H<sub>2</sub>S). First patented in 1883 by the chemist [Carl Friedrich Claus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Claus), the Claus process remains the most important desulfurization process in the [petrochemicals](67728.md) industry. It is standard at oil refineries, [natural gas processing plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_processing), and [gasification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gasification) or [synthesis gas plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syngas). ### Power generation

## Crude oil distillation unit

The crude oil distillation unit (CDU) is the first processing unit in virtually all petroleum refineries. The CDU distills the incoming crude oil into various fractions of different boiling ranges, each of which is then processed further in the other refinery processing units. The CDU is often referred to as the *atmospheric distillation unit* because it operates at slightly above atmospheric pressure.[^cite_note-Handwerk2-1][^cite_note-Leffler2-2][^cite_note-kister-70] Below is a schematic flow diagram of a typical crude oil distillation unit. The incoming crude oil is preheated by exchanging heat with some of the hot, distilled fractions and other streams. It is then desalted to remove inorganic salts (primarily sodium chloride).

Following the desalter, the crude oil is further heated by exchanging heat with some of the hot, distilled fractions and other streams. It is then heated in a fuel-fired furnace (fired heater) to a temperature of about 398 °C and routed into the bottom of the distillation unit.

The cooling and condensing of the distillation tower overhead is provided partially by exchanging heat with the incoming crude oil and partially by either an air-cooled or water-cooled condenser. Additional heat is removed from the distillation column by a pumparound system as shown in the diagram below.

As shown in the flow diagram, the overhead distillate fraction from the distillation column is naphtha. The fractions removed from the side of the distillation column at various points between the column top and bottom are called *sidecuts*. Each of the sidecuts (i.e., the kerosene, light gas oil, and heavy gas oil) is cooled by exchanging heat with the incoming crude oil. All of the fractions (i.e., the overhead naphtha, the sidecuts, and the bottom residue) are sent to intermediate storage tanks before being processed further.

A party searching for a site to construct a refinery or a chemical plant needs to consider the following issues:

- The site has to be reasonably far from residential areas.

- Infrastructure should be available for the supply of raw materials and shipment of products to markets.

- Energy to operate the plant should be available.

- Facilities should be available for waste disposal.

**Factors affecting site selection for oil refinery:**

- Availability of land

- Conditions of traffic and transportation

- Conditions of utilities – power supply, water supply

- Availability of labours and resources

Refineries that use a large amount of steam and cooling water need to have an abundant source of water. Oil refineries, therefore, are often located nearby navigable rivers or on a seashore, nearby a port. Such location also gives access to transportation by river or by sea. The advantages of transporting crude oil by pipeline are evident, and oil companies often transport a large volume of fuel to distribution terminals by pipeline. A pipeline may not be practical for products with small output, and railcars, road tankers, and barges are used.

Petrochemical plants and solvent manufacturing (fine fractionating) plants need spaces for further processing of a large volume of refinery products, or to mix chemical additives with a product at source rather than at blending terminals.

## Safety and environment



Fire-extinguishing operations after the [Texas City refinery explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_City_refinery_explosion)

The refining process releases a number of different chemicals into the [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_atmosphere) (see *[AP 42 Compilation of Air Pollutant Emission Factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AP_42_Compilation_of_Air_Pollutant_Emission_Factors)*) and a notable [odor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odor) normally accompanies the presence of a refinery. Aside from air pollution impacts there are also wastewater concerns,[^cite_note-Beychok-106] risks of [industrial accidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_accident) such as fire and explosion, and [noise health effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise_health_effects) due to [industrial noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_noise).[^cite_note-Morata_141–149-110]

Many governments worldwide have mandated restrictions on contaminants that refineries release, and most refineries have installed the equipment needed to comply with the requirements of the pertinent environmental protection regulatory agencies. In the United States, there is strong pressure to prevent the development of new refineries, and no major refinery has been built in the country since [Marathon's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marathon_Petroleum_Company) [Garyville, Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garyville,_Louisiana) facility in 1976. However, many existing refineries have been expanded during that time. Environmental restrictions and pressure to prevent the construction of new refineries may have also contributed to rising fuel prices in the United States.[^cite_note-111] Additionally, many refineries (more than 100 since the 1980s) have closed due to obsolescence and/or merger activity within the industry itself.[^cite_note-112]

Environmental and safety concerns mean that oil refineries are sometimes located some distance away from major urban areas. Nevertheless, there are many instances where refinery operations are close to populated areas and pose health risks.[^cite_note-113][^cite_note-114] In California's [Contra Costa County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contra_Costa_County) and [Solano County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solano_County), a shoreline necklace of refineries, built in the early 20th century before this area was populated, and associated chemical plants are adjacent to urban areas in [Richmond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevron_Richmond_Refinery), [Martinez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martinez,_California), [Pacheco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacheco,_California), [Concord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concord,_California), [Pittsburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburg,_California), [Vallejo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vallejo,_California) and [Benicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benicia,_California), with occasional accidental events that require "[shelter in place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelter_in_place)" orders to the adjacent populations. A number of refineries are located in [Sherwood Park, Alberta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherwood_Park,_Alberta), directly adjacent to the City of [Edmonton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonton), which has a population of over 1,000,000 residents.[^cite_note-115]

[NIOSH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_for_Occupational_Safety_and_Health) criteria for [occupational exposure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_exposure_limit) to refined petroleum solvents have been available since 1977.[^cite_note-116]

## Worker health

### Background

Modern [petroleum refining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_refining_processes) involves a complicated system of interrelated chemical reactions that produce a wide variety of petroleum-based products.[^cite_note-117][^cite_note-118] Many of these reactions require precise temperature and pressure parameters.[^cite_note-119] The equipment and monitoring required to ensure the proper progression of these processes is complex, and has evolved through the advancement of the scientific field of [petroleum engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_engineering).[^cite_note-120][^cite_note-121]

The wide array of high pressure and/or high temperature reactions, along with the necessary chemical additives or extracted contaminants, produces an astonishing number of potential health hazards to the oil refinery worker.[^cite_note-122][^cite_note-123] Through the advancement of technical chemical and petroleum engineering, the vast majority of these processes are automated and enclosed, thus greatly reducing the potential health impact to workers.[^cite_note-\:0-124] However, depending on the specific process in which a worker is engaged, as well as the particular method employed by the refinery in which he/she works, significant health hazards remain.[^cite_note-125]

Although occupational injuries in the United States were not routinely tracked and reported at the time, reports of the health impacts of working in an oil refinery can be found as early as the 1800s. For instance, an explosion in a Chicago refinery killed 20 workers in 1890.[^cite_note-126] Since then, numerous fires, explosions, and other significant events have from time to time drawn the public's attention to the health of oil refinery workers.[^cite_note-127] Such events continue in the 21st century, with explosions reported in refineries in Wisconsin and Germany in 2018.[^cite_note-128]

However, there are many less visible hazards that endanger oil refinery workers.

### Chemical exposures

Given the highly automated and technically advanced nature of modern petroleum refineries, nearly all processes are contained within engineering controls and represent a substantially decreased risk of exposure to workers compared to earlier times.[^cite_note-\:0-124] However, certain situations or work tasks may subvert these safety mechanisms, and expose workers to a number of chemical (see table above) or physical (described below) hazards.[^cite_note-\:1-129][^cite_note-\:2-130] Examples of these scenarios include:

- System failures (leaks, explosions, etc.).

A 2021 [systematic review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematic_review) associated working in the [petrochemical industry](2293201.md) with increased risk of various cancers, such as [mesothelioma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesothelioma). It also found reduced risks of other cancers, such as [stomach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stomach_cancer) and [rectal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectal_cancer). The systematic review did mention that several of the associations were not due to factors directly related to the petroleum industry, rather were related to lifestyle factors such as [smoking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoking). Evidence for adverse health effects for nearby residents was also weak, with the evidence primarily centering around neighborhoods in [developed countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developed_countries).[^cite_note-133]

[BTX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BTX_\(chemistry\)) stands for benzene, [toluene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toluene), [xylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylene). This is a group of common [volatile organic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volatile_organic_compound) (VOCs) that are found in the oil refinery environment, and serve as a paradigm for more in depth discussion of occupational exposure limits, chemical exposure and surveillance among refinery workers.[^cite_note-134][^cite_note-135]

The most important route of exposure for BTX chemicals is inhalation due to the low boiling point of these chemicals. The majority of the gaseous production of BTX occurs during tank cleaning and fuel transfer, which causes offgassing of these chemicals into the air.[^cite_note-136] Exposure can also occur through ingestion via contaminated water, but this is unlikely in an occupational setting.[^cite_note-\:17-137] Dermal exposure and absorption is also possible, but is again less likely in an occupational setting where appropriate personal protective equipment is in place.[^cite_note-\:17-137]

In the United States, the [Occupational Safety and Health Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_Safety_and_Health_Administration) (OSHA), [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_for_Occupational_Safety_and_Health) (NIOSH), and [American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Conference_of_Governmental_Industrial_Hygienists) (ACGIH) have all established [occupational exposure limits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_exposure_limit) (OELs) for many of the chemicals above that workers may be exposed to in petroleum refineries.[^cite_note-\:16-138][^cite_note-139][^cite_note-140]

Benzene, in particular, has multiple [biomarkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomarker) that can be measured to determine exposure. ### Physical hazards

Workers are at risk of physical injuries due to a large number of high-powered machines in the relatively close proximity of the oil refinery. The high pressure required for many of the chemical reactions also presents the possibility of localized system failures resulting in blunt or penetrating trauma from exploding system components.[^cite_note-162]

Heat is also a hazard. The temperature required for the proper progression of certain reactions in the refining process can reach 1,600 °F (870 °C).[^cite_note-\:0-124] As with chemicals, the operating system is designed to safely contain this hazard without injury to the worker. However, in system failures, this is a potent threat to workers' health. Concerns include both direct injury through a [heat illness or injury](17557798.md), as well as the potential for devastating burns should the worker come in contact with super-heated reagents/equipment.[^cite_note-\:0-124]

Noise is another hazard. Refineries can be very loud environments, and have previously been shown to be associated with hearing loss among workers.[^cite_note-163] The interior environment of an oil refinery can reach levels in excess of 90 [dB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decibel).[^cite_note-164][^cite_note-Morata_141–149-110] In the United States, an average of 90 dB is the [permissible exposure limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permissible_exposure_limit) (PEL) for an 8-hour work-day.[^cite_note-165] Noise exposures that average greater than 85 dB over an 8-hour require a [hearing conservation program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearing_conservation_program) to regularly evaluate workers' hearing and to promote its protection.[^cite_note-166] Regular evaluation of workers' auditory capacity and faithful use of [properly vetted hearing protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearing_protection_device) are essential parts of such programs.[^cite_note-167]

While not specific to the industry, oil refinery workers may also be at risk for hazards such as [vehicle-related accidents](19740545.md), machinery-associated injuries, work in a confined space, explosions/fires, [ergonomic hazards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergonomic_hazard), [shift-work related sleep disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shift_work_sleep_disorder), and falls.[^cite_note-168]

### Hazard controls

The theory of [hierarchy of controls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hierarchy_of_hazard_controls) can be applied to petroleum refineries and their efforts to ensure worker safety.

[Elimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazard_elimination) and [substitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazard_substitution) are unlikely in petroleum refineries, as many of the raw materials, waste products, and finished products are hazardous in one form or another (e.g. flammable, carcinogenic).[^cite_note-\:3-148][^cite_note-169]

Examples of [engineering controls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering_controls) include a [fire detection/extinguishing system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_detection_system), pressure/chemical sensors to detect/predict loss of structural integrity,[^cite_note-\:8-170] and adequate maintenance of piping to prevent hydrocarbon-induced [corrosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrosion) (leading to structural failure).[^cite_note-\:4-131][^cite_note-\:5-132][^cite_note-\:6-171][^cite_note-\:7-172] Other examples employed in petroleum refineries include the post-construction protection of steel components with [vermiculite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermiculite) to improve heat/fire resistance.[^cite_note-173] [Compartmentalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compartmentalization_\(fire_protection\)) can help to prevent a fire or other systems failure from spreading to affect other areas of the structure, and may help prevent dangerous reactions by keeping different chemicals separate from one another until they can be safely combined in the proper environment.[^cite_note-\:8-170]

[Administrative controls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Administrative_controls) include careful planning and oversight of the refinery cleaning, maintenance, and turnaround processes. These occur when many of the engineering controls are shut down or suppressed and may be especially dangerous to workers. Detailed coordination is necessary to ensure that maintenance of one part of the facility will not cause dangerous exposures to those performing the maintenance, or to workers in other areas of the plant. Due to the highly flammable nature of many of the involved chemicals, smoking areas are tightly controlled and carefully placed.[^cite_note-\:1-129]

[Personal protective equipment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_protective_equipment) (PPE) may be necessary depending on the specific chemical being processed or produced. Particular care is needed during sampling of the partially completed product, tank cleaning, and other high-risk tasks as mentioned above. Such activities may require the use of impervious outerwear, acid hood, disposable coveralls, etc.[^cite_note-\:1-129] More generally, all personnel in operating areas should use appropriate [hearing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearing_protection_device) and [vision protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safety_goggles), avoid clothes made of flammable material ([nylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nylon), [Dacron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dacron), [acrylic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acrylic_fiber), or blends), and full-length pants and sleeves.[^cite_note-\:1-129]

### Regulations

#### United States

Worker health and safety in oil refineries is closely monitored at a national level by both the [Occupational Safety and Health Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_Safety_and_Health_Administration) (OSHA) and [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_for_Occupational_Safety_and_Health) (NIOSH).[^cite_note-174][^cite_note-175] In addition to [federal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Federal_Government) monitoring, [California](5407.md)'s [CalOSHA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Division_of_Occupational_Safety_and_Health) has been particularly active in protecting worker health in the industry, and adopted a policy in 2017 that requires petroleum refineries to perform a "Hierarchy of Hazard Controls Analysis" (see above "Hazard controls" section) for each [process safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process_safety) hazard.[^cite_note-176] Safety regulations have resulted in a below-average injury rate for refining industry workers. In a 2018 report by the [US Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Bureau_of_Labor_Statistics), they indicate that petroleum refinery workers have a significantly lower rate of occupational injury (0.4 OSHA-recordable cases per 100 full-time workers) than all industries (3.1 cases), oil and gas extraction (0.8 cases), and petroleum manufacturing in general (1.3 cases).[^cite_note-177]

## Corrosion



Oil refinery in Iran

Corrosion of metallic components is a major factor of inefficiency in the refining process. Because it leads to equipment failure, it is a primary driver for the refinery maintenance schedule. Corrosion-related direct costs in the U.S. petroleum industry as of 1996 were estimated at US\$3.7 billion.[^cite_note-\:7-172][^cite_note-179]

Corrosion occurs in various forms in the refining process, such as pitting corrosion from water droplets, embrittlement from hydrogen, and stress corrosion cracking from sulfide attack.[^cite_note-180] From a materials standpoint, carbon steel is used for upwards of 80 percent of refinery components, which is beneficial due to its low cost. [Carbon steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_steel) is resistant to the most common forms of corrosion, particularly from hydrocarbon impurities at temperatures below 205 °C, but other corrosive chemicals and environments prevent its use everywhere. Common replacement materials are [low alloy steels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_alloy_steel) containing [chromium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromium) and [molybdenum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molybdenum), with [stainless steels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steels) containing more chromium dealing with more corrosive environments. More expensive materials commonly used are [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel), [titanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanium), and [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper) alloys. These are primarily saved for the most problematic areas where extremely high temperatures and/or very corrosive chemicals are present.[^cite_note-181]

Corrosion is fought by a complex system of monitoring, preventative repairs, and careful use of materials. Monitoring methods include both offline checks taken during maintenance and online monitoring. Offline checks measure corrosion after it has occurred, telling the engineer when equipment must be replaced based on the historical information they have collected. This is referred to as preventative management.

Online systems are a more modern development and are revolutionizing the way corrosion is approached. There are several types of online corrosion monitoring technologies such as linear polarization resistance, [electrochemical noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_noise) and electrical resistance. Online monitoring has generally had slow reporting rates in the past (minutes or hours) and been limited by process conditions and sources of error but newer technologies can report rates up to twice per minute with much higher accuracy (referred to as real-time monitoring). This allows process engineers to treat corrosion as another process variable that can be optimized in the system. Immediate responses to process changes allow the control of corrosion mechanisms, so they can be minimized while also maximizing production output.[^cite_note-\:6-171] In an ideal situation having on-line corrosion information that is accurate and real-time will allow conditions that cause high corrosion rates to be identified and reduced. This is known as predictive management.

Materials methods include selecting the proper material for the application. In areas of minimal corrosion, cheap materials are preferable, but when bad corrosion can occur, more expensive but longer-lasting materials should be used. Other materials methods come in the form of protective barriers between corrosive substances and the equipment metals. These can be either a lining of refractory material such as standard [Portland cement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portland_cement) or other special acid-resistant cement that is shot onto the inner surface of the vessel. Also available are thin overlays of more expensive metals that protect cheaper metal against corrosion without requiring much material.[^cite_note-182]
