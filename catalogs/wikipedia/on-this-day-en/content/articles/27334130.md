The **Assad family**[^cite_note-1] ruled [Syria](57134800.md) from 1970 to 2024, spanning the rule of [Hafez al-Assad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad) and his son, [Bashar al-Assad](364813.md). From 1963 until the [collapse of Ba'athist rule](78548066.md) in 2024, Syria was governed by the [Ba'ath Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Socialist_Ba'ath_Party_%E2%80%93_Syria_Region), and under the Assad family from 1971 onward, it became a [totalitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totalitarianism) [hereditary dictatorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynasty).[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4] The Assad family was referred to as the **Assad dynasty**.[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6]

The Assad family originated from [Qardaha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qardaha), in the [Latakia Sanjak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latakia_Sanjak) of the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md). They belonged to the [Alawite](436616.md) [Kalbiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalbiyya) tribe. In 1927, [Ali Sulayman al-Wahsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_al-Assad), Hafez al-Assad's father, who was born in 1875 and lived in the village of [Qardaha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qardaha) in the Syrian coastal mountains, changed his surname from *al-Wahsh*, Arabic for "the savage", to *al-Assad*, meaning "the lion". This change was possibly connected to his social standing as a local mediator and his political activities. All members of the extended Assad family descended from Ali Sulayman and his second wife, Naissa, who came from a village in the [Syrian Coastal Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Coastal_Mountain_Range).[^cite_note-Stäheli-7][^cite_note-PSeale9-8]

The Assad family's rule began when [Hafez al-Assad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad) seized power in the [1970 coup d'état](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1970_Syrian_coup_d'etat). He became [president](516106.md) in 1971 under the Syrian Ba'ath Party following a [sham election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_Syrian_presidential_election) and ruled until [his death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_and_state_funeral_of_Hafez_al-Assad) in 2000. He was succeeded by his son, [Bashar al-Assad](364813.md), who maintained a similar grip on power until he was [ousted on 8 December 2024](78548066.md) during a [major offensive](78460726.md) by [opposition forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_opposition_\(2011%E2%80%932024\)).[^cite_note-reuters.com-9][^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11] During his early reign in the 1970s, Hafez al-Assad created patronage networks of [Ba'ath party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Socialist_Ba'ath_Party_%E2%80%93_Syria_Region) elites loyal to his family. Members of the Assad family established control over vast swathes of the Syrian economy, and [corruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corruption_in_Syria) became endemic in the public and private sectors.[^cite_note-12] After Hafez's death, family connections continued to be important in Syrian politics. Several close family members of Hafez also held vital positions in the government since his rise to power, an arrangement which existed until the fall of the Assad regime.[^cite_note-\:1-13][^cite_note-Bar_S.380-14] The Syrian [bureaucracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureaucracy) and business community were also dominated by members of the Assad family and individuals affiliated with them.[^cite_note-The_Syria_Report-15][^cite_note-16]

The Assad family is affiliated with the [Alawite](436616.md) sect, a syncretic religious tradition with links to early [Shi'ism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shi'ism). Since coming to power in 1970, the Assad family has traditionally relied on support from Alawite communities as an important component of its political rule. Many Alawites were appointed to key positions in the bureaucracy, security forces, [military](27479.md), judiciary, and other state institutions, helping consolidate the Assad family's grip on power.[^cite_note-\:3-17] Hafez al-Assad built his regime into a bureaucracy that was marked by a [cult of personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cult_of_personality). Images, portraits, quotes and praises of Assad are displayed everywhere from schools to public markets and government offices. Hafez was referred to as the "Immortal Leader" and the *al-Muqaddas* ("Sanctified One") in official [Assadist](36629941.md) ideology. Hafez re-organised Syrian society in militaristic lines and persistently invoked conspiratorial rhetoric on the dangers of foreign-backed plots abetted by [fifth columnists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_columnists) and promoted the [armed forces](27479.md) as a central aspect of public life. After Hafez al-Assad's death, his son and successor Bashar al-Assad inherited the existing personality cult, with the party hailing him as the "Young Leader" and "Hope of the People". Drawing influence from the [veneration of the Kim dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Korean_cult_of_personality) in North Korea's [hereditary leadership model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_family_\(North_Korea\)) led by [Kim Il Sung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Il_Sung), official propaganda in Syria ascribed divine features to the Assad family and revered the Assad patriarchs as the founding fathers of modern Syria.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20]

Opposition to the Assad family's rule coalesced into the [Syrian Civil War](30741795.md), which began on 15 March 2011. On 8 December 2024, Bashar al-Assad was reported to have fled [Damascus](8914.md), signalling the [end of the Assad family's rule in Syria](78548066.md). Following the [fall of the Assad regime](78548066.md), Bashar al-Assad and his family, including his wife [Asma al-Assad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asma_al-Assad), fled to Moscow, Russia. However, remnants and loyalists of the Assad regime subsequently engaged in a [conflict](78735576.md) against the [new Syrian government](79584988.md).[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-reuters.com-9][^cite_note-22]

## Origin

The Assad family originates from [Ali Sulayman al-Wahsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_al-Assad), Hafez al-Assad's father who was born in 1875 and lived in the village of [Qardaha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qardaha) in the coastal Syrian mountains. The locals reportedly nicknamed him "Wahsh", Arabic for "wild beast", because he was physically strong and a good fighter. Al-Wahsh remained the family name until the 1920s, when it was changed to al-[Assad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asad), Arabic for "lion".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZahler200925-23][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlianak2007128-24]

Because of Sulayman's reported strength and marksmanship, he was respected in his village. At the outbreak of [World War I](4764461.md), the Ottoman governor of the [Aleppo Vilayet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleppo_Vilayet) sent troops to the area to collect taxes and round up recruits. The troops were reportedly fought off by Sulayman and his friends who were armed only with sabres and old muskets.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeale19903-25] Because Sulayman was respected, he was a local mediator between quarreling families. He was also one of the local chieftains who were the de facto rulers of the area. The chieftains from the powerful families would provide protection to their neighbours and in return they gained loyalty and respect.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeale19904-26] He lived until 1963, long enough to see his son's rise to power. He married twice and over three decades had eleven children. His first wife Sa'ada was from the district of Haffeh. They had three sons and two daughters. His second wife was Na'isa, twenty years younger than him. She was the daughter of Uthman Abbud from the village of [Al-Qutailibiyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Qutailibiyah), a dozen kilometres further up the mountain. They had a daughter and five sons. Hafez was born on 6 October 1930 and was the fourth child.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeale19905-27]

Al-Assad family is affiliated with the [Alawite](436616.md) sect, a syncretic sect with links to early [Shi'ism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shi'ism). Since coming to power in 1970, the Assad family traditionally used sectarian loyalty from the Alawite sect as a vital component to legitimize their dynastic rule. Many Alawite loyalists were assigned to crucial posts in the bureaucracy, security forces, [military](27479.md), judiciary, etc., in-order to consolidate Assad family's grip on power.[^cite_note-\:3-17]

## Syria under the Assad family (1970–2024)

### Hafez al-Assad

Main article: [Hafez al-Assad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad)



[Hafez al-Assad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad)

Hafez al-Assad was born on 6 October 1930, in [Qardaha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qardaha), a town in northwest Syria. He was born into a poor [Alawite](436616.md) peasant family belonging to the [Kalbiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalbiyya) tribe of [Alawites](436616.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBengio1998135-28][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJessup199841-29][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEReich199052-30] Later, Assad recalled at the congresses of the Peasants' General Union: "I had a passion for threshing the harvest... But I took part in all the phases of farming..., lived your emotions and understand what your life signifies. I still have mental pictures of the injustices of the time. No matter how far the past sinks away, it is necessary to keep these images alive in our minds, not to nurse hatred against anyone but to see into them, for what we endured forms an essential part of the way we view things and of the foundation on which we build the present and the future."[^cite_note-\:32-31] His paternal grandfather, Sulayman al-Wahhish, gained the nickname *al-Wahhish* (wild beast) for his strength.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeale19903-25]

Hafez al-Assad's parents were Na'isa Shalish and [Ali al-Assad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_al-Assad).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlianak2007127–128-32] His father married twice and had eleven children.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeale19905-27] Hafez was his ninth son and the fourth from his second marriage.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlianak2007127–128-32] [Hafez al-Assad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad) married [Anisa Makhlouf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anisa_Makhlouf) and had five children: Bushra al-Assad, [Bassel al-Assad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bassel_al-Assad), [Bashar al-Assad](364813.md), Majd al-Assad, and [Maher al-Assad](3139640.md).[^cite_note-33] The couple also had a daughter named Bushra, who died in infancy before 1960.[^cite_note-34]

The [Ba'athist regime](57134800.md) emerged in 1963 following a [coup d'état](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1963_Syrian_coup_d'%C3%A9tat) led by [Alawite](436616.md) [Ba'athist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba'athism) military officers.[^cite_note-USDoS-35][^cite_note-Britan22-36] Another coup in [1966](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1966_Syrian_coup_d'%C3%A9tat) brought [Salah Jadid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salah_Jadid) to de facto power, while [Nureddin al-Atassi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nureddin_al-Atassi) assumed the presidency.[^cite_note-Britan22-36][^cite_note-\:02-37] In 1970, Jadid and al-Atassi were overthrown by [Hafez al-Assad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad) during the [Corrective Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrective_Revolution_\(Syria\)).[^cite_note-ps-38] The Assad family's rule began when [Hafez al-Assad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad) seized power in the [1970 coup d'état](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1970_Syrian_coup_d'etat) and became [President of Syria](516106.md) in 1971 following a [sham election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_Syrian_presidential_election) held under the [Syrian Ba'ath Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba'ath_Party_\(Syrian-dominated_faction\)). Under Hafez, Ba'athist Syria became a police state that suppressed dissent. Following his [death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_and_state_funeral_of_Hafez_al-Assad) in June 2000, he was succeeded by his son, [Bashar al-Assad](364813.md).[^cite_note-\:02-37][^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41]

### Bashar al-Assad

Main article: [Bashar al-Assad](364813.md)



[Bashar al-Assad](364813.md)

Bashar Hafez al-Assad was born in Damascus on 11 September 1965, as the second son and third child of [Anisa Makhlouf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anisa_Makhlouf) and [Hafez al-Assad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZisser200720-42][^cite_note-43] Unlike his brothers Bassel and [Maher](3139640.md), and second sister, also named [Bushra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bushra_al-Assad), Bashar was quiet, reserved and lacked interest in politics or the military.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZisser200721-44][^cite_note-Bar-45][^cite_note-Republic-46] The Assad children reportedly rarely saw their father,[^cite_note-Roula-47] and Bashar later stated that he only entered his father's office once while he was president.[^cite_note-48] He was described as "soft-spoken",[^cite_note-49] and according to a university friend, he was timid, avoided eye contact and spoke in a low voice.[^cite_note-50]

Assad received his primary and secondary education in the Arab-French al-Hurriya School in Damascus.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZisser200721-44] In 1982, he graduated from high school and then studied medicine at [Damascus University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus_University).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELeverett200559-51] In 1988, Assad graduated from medical school and began working as an army doctor at Tishrin Military Hospital on the outskirts of Damascus.[^cite_note-Ladno-52][^cite_note-\:6-53] Four years later, he settled in London to start postgraduate training in [ophthalmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ophthalmology) at [Western Eye Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Eye_Hospital).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELeverett200560-54] He was described as a "geeky I.T. guy" during his time in London.[^cite_note-55] Bashar had few political aspirations,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMinahan200283-56] and his father had been grooming Bashar's older brother Bassel as future president.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETuckerRoberts2008167-57] Shortly after Bassel died in a car accident in 1994, Bashar was recalled to the Syrian Army. State propaganda soon began elevating Bashar's public image as "the hope of the masses" to prepare the public for a continuation of the rule of the [Assad dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assad_dynasty).[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59]

Soon after the death of Bassel, Hafez al-Assad decided to make Bashar the new [heir apparent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heir_apparent).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZisser200735-60] After the death of Hafez al-Assad on 10 June 2000, the [Constitution of Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Syria) was amended. The minimum age requirement for the presidency was lowered from 40 to 34, which was Bashar's age at the time.[^cite_note-61] The sole candidate of the presidential referendum, Assad was subsequently [confirmed president on 10 July 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_Syrian_presidential_election), with 97.29% support for his leadership.[^cite_note-CBS_%-62] In line with his role as President of Syria, he was also appointed the [commander-in-chief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander-in-chief) of the [Syrian Armed Forces](27479.md) and Regional Secretary of the Ba'ath Party.[^cite_note-facing_down_rebellion-63]

Bashar's presidency was marked by authoritarian rule and the suppression of political dissent. His government faced mounting criticism over [corruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corruption_in_Ba'athist_Syria), [human rights abuses](78548501.md), and its [violent crackdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_government_response_to_the_Syrian_revolution) on the [2011 protests](39750126.md), which eventually led to the outbreak of the [Syrian Civil War](30741795.md).[^cite_note-64] His rule as president was a highly [personalist dictatorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictatorship#Personalist)[^cite_note-MF-65][^cite_note-dictator2-66] that governed Syria as a [totalitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totalitarian) [police state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_state),[^cite_note-67] becoming arguably more [repressive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_repression) than his father.[^cite_note-HRW-68] The war [involved numerous international actors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_involvement_in_the_Syrian_civil_war), with countries like [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_intervention_in_the_Syrian_civil_war) and [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_intervention_in_the_Syrian_civil_war) supporting [Assad's regime](57134800.md), while [opposition groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_opposition_\(2011%E2%80%932024\)) received backing from Western and regional powers.[^cite_note-69] In November 2024, a coalition of Syrian rebels launched [several offensives](78460726.md) aimed at overthrowing Assad's government. On 8 December 2024, the Assad family's rule collapsed after rebel forces entered and captured [Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Damascus_\(2024\)), the Syrian capital, during the [2024 Syrian opposition offensives](78460726.md). Assad and his family subsequently left Syria for Moscow, the Russian capital, where they were granted asylum.[^cite_note-\:4-70][^cite_note-71][^cite_note-72] In August 2026, Bashar al-Assad was convicted of war crimes and crimes against humanity by a Syrian court and sentenced to death [*in absentia*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_absentia_\(law\)).[^cite_note-\:5-73][^cite_note-\:7-74]

## Cult of personality

Main article: [Assad family's cult of personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assad_family's_cult_of_personality)

> In no other country in recent memory ... not [Mao's China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maoist_China), nor [Tito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tito)'s [Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Yugoslavia), has the intensity of the [personality cult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality_cult) reached such extremes. Asad's image, speaking, smiling, listening, benevolent or stern, solemn or reflective, is everywhere. Sometimes there are half a dozen pictures of him in a row. His face envelops telephone poles and trucks, churches and mosques. His is the visage a Syrian sees when he opens his newspaper.

— [Middle East Insight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East_Insight) magazine

[^cite_note-75]

[^cite_note-76]



A square in [Aleppo](159244.md) displaying the statue and portrait of Hafez al-Assad (2001)

During the 1950s, Syrian [Alawites](436616.md) started becoming influential in the [Syrian Armed Forces](27479.md) and [Ba'ath Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba'ath_Party). Led by Alawite military officers, like [Salah Jadid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salah_Jadid), Ba'athist factions staged a series of coups during the 1960s and built up a [one-party state](102459.md). The party cemented its total control over the state and society by purging civilian elites, pursued an aggressive propaganda policy of "state-nationalist indoctrination" and established patronage networks based on sectarian lines to mobilise support.[^cite_note-77] Following the [1970 coup d'etat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1970_Syrian_coup_d'%C3%A9tat) that ousted his rival [Salah Jadid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salah_Jadid); [Hafez al-Assad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad) developed a [Stalinist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalinism)-style [personality cult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cult_of_personality) around him; which depicted him as the [father figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Father_figure) of Syrian nation. After Hafez's death, the personality cult was extended to his son, [Bashar al-Assad](364813.md). Monuments, pictures, statues, symbols and billboards of both the leaders extensively pervade Syrian society, designed to consolidate the notion of "Assad's Syria". Observers view the state propaganda efforts as a strategy for securing the compliance of the masses and identifying [Syrian nationhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_nationalism) with the Assad dynasty.[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-79][^cite_note-80][^cite_note-81][^cite_note-82]

In addition to criminalising any and all critiques of the regime; the modes of conveying messages between the state and civil society were restricted strictly within bounds of what is officially acceptable. The state further banned private political opinions critical of the regime and encouraged citizens to report relatives and friends who exhibited undesirable attitudes. The policies of [economic liberalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_liberalization) implemented during the 2000s worsened the corruption because the chief grantees of the outcomes were businessmen and relatives close to the Assad family, such as [Rami Makhlouf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rami_Makhlouf).[^cite_note-Shamaileh_2017_16–17-83][^cite_note-Turku_2018_74-84][^cite_note-Aslan_Ozgul_2019_9–10,_41–44,_227-85]

&#x20;Unlike other Arab dictatorships, this feature of the [Ba'ath regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baathist_Syria) and the total centralisation of power in the hands of the Assad patriarchs enabled it to instill [apoliticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apoliticism) amongst its citizens; where the ritualisation of state slogans and symbolism had led to de facto compliance. As a result, there were far fewer avenues of free political activism for ordinary [Syrians](8862873.md) as compared to other Arab states. Until recently, political activism was shunned by many people; instead preferring the stability offered by the regime. The rise of internet and [satellite channels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_television) alongside the proliferation of civil society groups and independent political activists during the 2000s increasingly began to challenge state monopoly on information, which led to rising political dissidence amongst the younger generations.[^cite_note-Aslan_Ozgul_2019_9–10,_41–44,_227-85][^cite_note-86][^cite_note-Shamaileh_2017_16–17-83][^cite_note-Turku_2018_74-84] Describing the hardships to raise the political consciousness of Syrian citizens by contrasting their situation with other Arab protestors, Caroline, a Syrian Christian and civic activist imprisoned by regime during the 2011–12 [Arab Spring protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Spring), states:

> "Before the [revolution in Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Egyptian_revolution), people were allowed to gather, had political parties; people were exposed to political life. In Syria, we were away from politics. We were raised in Syria and our parents used to tell us that we shouldn't talk with anyone about our religion or about politics."[^cite_note-87]

Since Hafez al-Assad's [seizure of power in 1970](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrective_Movement_\(Syria\)); state propaganda promoted a new national discourse based on unifying [Syrians](8862873.md) under "a single imagined [Ba'athist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-ba'athist) identity" and [Assadism](36629941.md). Fervently loyalist paramilitaries known as the *[Shabiha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shabiha)* (tr. ghosts) deify the Assad dynasty through slogans such as *There is no God except Bashar!* (Arabic: لا إله الا بشار) and pursue [psychological warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_warfare) against non-conformist populations.[^cite_note-88]

### Makhlouf family

The Makhloufs belong to the [Alawi](436616.md) Haddad tribe,[^cite_note-Bar_S.381-112][^cite_note-ivan12nov-131] both Hafez and Rifaat are related through marriage to the Makhloufs. The Makhlouf family rose from humble beginnings to become the [financial advisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_advisor) to Hafez al-Assad after the former President married Makhlouf's sister. The family headed by Mohammad Makhlouf has established a vast financial empire in the telecommunication, retail, banking, power generation, and oil and gas sectors.
