**Sulawesi** ([/ˌsuːləˈweɪsi/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*SOO-lə-WAY-see*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key), Indonesian: [\[ˌsulaˈwesi\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Malay)),[^cite_note-1] also known as **Celebes** ([/ˈsɛlɪbiːz, səˈliːbiːz/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*SEL-ib-eez, sə-LEE-beez*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key)),[^cite_note-2] is an island in [Indonesia](14579.md). One of the four [Greater Sunda Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Sunda_Islands), and the [world's 11th-largest island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_islands_by_area), it is situated east of [Borneo](4518.md), west of the [Maluku Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maluku_Islands), and south of [Mindanao](20546.md) and the [Sulu Archipelago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulu_Archipelago). Within Indonesia, only [Sumatra](28679.md), Borneo, and [Papua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Guinea) are larger in territory, and only [Java](69336.md) and Sumatra are more populous.

The landmass of Sulawesi includes four peninsulas: the northern [Minahasa Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minahasa_Peninsula), the [East Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Peninsula,_Sulawesi), the [South Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Peninsula,_Sulawesi), and the [Southeast Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Peninsula,_Sulawesi). Three gulfs separate these peninsulas: the [Gulf of Tomini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Tomini) between the northern Minahasa and East peninsulas, the [Gulf of Tolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Tolo) between the East and Southeast peninsulas, and the [Gulf of Boni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Boni) between the South and Southeast peninsulas. The [Makassar Strait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makassar_Strait) runs along the western side of the island and separates the island from [Borneo](4518.md).

## Etymology

The name *Sulawesi* possibly comes from the words *sula* ("island") and *besi* ("iron") and may refer to the historical export of [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron) from the rich [Lake Matano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Matano) [iron deposits](145352.md).[^cite_note-3] The name came into common use in English following [Indonesian independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_independence).

The name *Celebes* was originally given to the island by [Portuguese explorers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_explorers). While its direct translation is unclear, it might be a Portuguese rendering of the native name "Sulawesi".[^cite_note-4]

## Geography

Sulawesi is the [world's eleventh-largest island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_islands_by_area),[^cite_note-5] covering an area of 186,064.34 km<sup>2</sup> (71,840 sq mi) (including minor islands administered as part of Sulawesi). The central part of the island is ruggedly mountainous, such that the island's peninsulas have traditionally been remote from each other, with better connections by sea than by road. The three bays that divide Sulawesi's peninsulas are, from north to south, the [Tomini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_of_Tomini), the [Tolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_of_Tolo_\(Indonesia\)) and the [Boni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Boni).[^cite_note-9] These separate the [Minahasa or Northern Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minahasa_Peninsula), the [East Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Peninsula,_Sulawesi), the [Southeast Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Peninsula,_Sulawesi) and the [South Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Peninsula,_Sulawesi).

The [Strait of Makassar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strait_of_Makassar) runs along the western side of the island.[^cite_note-10] The island is surrounded by [Borneo](4518.md) to the west, by the [Philippines](23440.md) to the north, by [Maluku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maluku_Islands) to the east, and by [Flores](11426.md) and [Timor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timor) to the south.

### Minor islands

The [Selayar Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selayar_Islands) make up a chain stretching southwards from Southwest Sulawesi into the [Flores Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flores_Sea) are administratively part of Sulawesi. The [Sangihe Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sangihe_Islands) and [Talaud Islands](31153243.md) stretch northward from the northeastern tip of Sulawesi, while [Buton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buton) and [Muna Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muna_Island) and their neighbors lie off its southeast peninsula, the [Togian Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Togian_Islands) are in the Gulf of Tomini, and [Peleng Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peleng_Island) and the [Banggai Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banggai_Sea_Regency) form a cluster between Sulawesi and [Maluku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maluku_Islands). All the above-mentioned islands and many smaller ones off the coasts of Sulawesi are administratively part of Sulawesi's six provinces.[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12]

## Geology



[Mount Tongkoko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Tongkoko) is a volcano in North Sulawesi

The island slopes up from the shores of the deep seas surrounding the island to a high and mostly non-volcanic, mountainous interior. Active volcanoes are found in the northern [Minahasa Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minahasa_Peninsula), stretching north to the [Sangihe Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sangihe_Islands). The northern peninsula contains several active volcanoes such as [Mount Lokon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Lokon), [Mount Awu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Awu), [Soputan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soputan) and [Karangetang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karangetang).

According to [plate reconstructions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_reconstruction), the island is believed to have been formed by the collision of [terranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrane) from the [Asian Plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_Plate) (forming the west and southwest) and from the [Australian Plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Plate) (forming the southeast and [Banggai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banggai_regency)), with [island arcs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island_arc) previously in the Pacific (forming the north and east peninsulas).[^cite_note-13] Because of its several tectonic origins, various [faults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_\(geology\)) scar the land and as a result the island is prone to [earthquakes](10106.md), including the deadly [2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Sulawesi_earthquake_and_tsunami) and [2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_West_Sulawesi_earthquake) quakes.

Off the eastern coast of Sulawesi, the North Banda Sea was created through subduction rollback during the early Miocene. Evidence for this tectonic event lies with the extensive interconnected fault network found in the area, a volcanic seamount with its surrounding ridges, and an accretionary wedge. Off the coast of east Selawesti and Banggai is an accumulation of carbonate rocks from the late Miocene. These carbonates are likely pinnacle reefs and the carbonate platform has a total thickness of around 180–770 meters.[^cite_note-14]

Sulawesi, in contrast to most of the other islands in the [biogeographical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biogeography) region of [Wallacea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallacea), is not truly oceanic, but a composite island at the centre of the Asia-Australia [collision zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collision_zone).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEVon_Rintelen_&_al.2014-15] Parts of the island were formerly attached to either the [Asian](689.md) or [Australian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia_\(continent\)) continental margin and became separated from these areas by [vicariant processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vicariance).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEVon_Rintelen_&_al.2014-15]

In the west, the opening of the [Makassar Strait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makassar_Strait) separated West Sulawesi from [Sundaland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundaland) in the [Eocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eocene) c. 45 Mya.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEVon_Rintelen_&_al.2014-15] In the east, the traditional view of collisions of multiple [micro-continental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micro-continent) fragments sliced from New Guinea with an active volcanic margin in West Sulawesi at different times since the [Early Miocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Miocene) c. 20 Mya has recently been replaced by the hypothesis that extensional fragmentation has followed a single Miocene collision of West Sulawesi with the Sula Spur, the western end of an ancient folded belt of [Variscan origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variscan_orogeny) in the Late Paleozoic.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEVon_Rintelen_&_al.2014-15]

### Bone Basin

The Bone Basin lies between the south-eastern and southern arms of Sulawesi. According to recent studies, the basin has been opened up due to extensional forces.[^cite_note-16] The basin is bounded by normal faults on each side of the basin with each side of the basin surrounded by uplifted basement rock with young sediments found in the middle.

The past geological history has allowed for a large accumulation of carbonates which could lead to a higher potential of oil and gas occurrences. However, the faults present in the basin makes it a very complicated system.

## Prehistory

See also: [Prehistoric Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistoric_Indonesia)

The oldest evidence for humans on Sulawesi are stone tools produced by [archaic humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaic_humans), dating to at least 1.04 million years ago and possibly as old as 1.48 million years ago, found at the Calio site near the village of Ujung in Lilirilau district of [Soppeng Regency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soppeng_Regency), southwestern Sulawesi.[^cite_note-17] Other archaic human produced stone tools, dating to over 200,000 to 100,000 years ago, have been found at the Talepu site near Cabenge village, which is also located in the Lilirilau district.[^cite_note-18]

Before October 2014, the settlement of South Sulawesi by modern humans had been dated to c. 30,000 BC on the basis of radiocarbon dates obtained from rock shelters in [Maros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maros).[^cite_note-19] No earlier evidence of human occupation had at that point been found, but the island almost certainly formed part of the land bridge used for the settlement of Australia and [New Guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Guinea) by at least 40,000 BC.[^cite_note-20]

There is no evidence of *[Homo erectus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_erectus)* having reached Sulawesi; crude stone tools first discovered in 1947 on the right bank of the [Walanae River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walanae_River) at [Barru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barru_Regency) (now part of [Bone Regency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone_Regency)), which were thought to date to the Pleistocene on the basis of their association with vertebrate fossils,[^cite_note-21] are now thought to date to perhaps 50,000 BC.[^cite_note-22]



A typical dwelling, taken between 1900 and 1940.

Following Peter Bellwood's model of a southward migration of [Austronesian-speaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_languages) farmers (AN),[^cite_note-23] radiocarbon dates from caves in Maros suggest a date in the mid-second millennium BC for the arrival of a group from east Borneo speaking a Proto-[South Sulawesi language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Sulawesi_languages) (PSS). Initial settlement was probably around the mouth of the Sa'dan river, on the northwest coast of the peninsula, although the south coast has also been suggested.[^cite_note-24]

Subsequent migrations across the mountainous landscape resulted in the geographical isolation of PSS speakers and the evolution of their languages into the eight families of the South Sulawesi language group.[^cite_note-25] If each group can be said to have a homeland, that of the [Bugis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bugis) – today the most numerous group – was around lakes [Témpé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Tempe) and [Sidénréng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Sidenreng) in the Walennaé depression. Here for some 2,000 years lived the linguistic group that would become the modern Bugis; the archaic name of this group (which is preserved in other local languages) was Ugiq. Despite the fact that today they are closely linked with the [Makassarese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makassarese_people), the closest linguistic neighbors of the Bugis are the [Torajans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torajan_people).



[Megalith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megalith) in [Central Sulawesi](355454.md).

Pre-1200 Bugis society was most likely organized into [chiefdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiefdoms). Some [anthropologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropologist) have speculated these chiefdoms would have warred and, in times of peace, interbred. Further, they have speculated that personal security would have been negligible and head-hunting an established cultural practice. The political economy would have been a mixture of hunting and gathering and swidden or shifting agriculture. Speculative planting of [wet rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wet_rice) may have taken place along the margins of the lakes and rivers.

In Central Sulawesi, there are more than 400 granite [megaliths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megalith) (Behoa Valley [Pokekea Megalithic Site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pokekea_Megalithic_Site), [Bada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bada_Valley) and Napu valleys within the [Lore Lindu National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lore_Lindu_National_Park#Megaliths)), which various archaeological studies have dated to be from 3000 BC to AD 1300. They vary in size from a few centimeters to approximately 4.5 meters (15 ft). The original purpose of the megaliths is unknown. Approximately 30 of the megaliths represent human forms. Other megaliths are in form of large pots (*Kalamba*) and stone plates (*Tutu'na*).[^cite_note-National_Geographic-26][^cite_note-Jakarta_Post-27]

A burial of a woman associated with the hunter-gatherer [Toalean culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toalean_culture) dating to 7,000 years ago has yielded DNA that has provided rare insight into early migrations in and through the region.[^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29]

### Cave art

In October 2014, it was announced that [cave paintings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cave_paintings) in Maros had been dated as being approximately 40,000 years old. One of a hand was 39,900 years old,[^cite_note-Hoffmann2018-30] which brings it among the oldest known [hand stencils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_stencil) in the world.[^cite_note-Hoffmann2018-30]



[Stencils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_stencils) of right hands in Pettakere Cave in Maros are among the oldest known examples of human artwork

Dr. Maxime Aubert, of [Griffith University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griffith_University) in [Queensland](59051.md), [Australia](4689264.md), said that was the minimum age for the outline in Pettakere Cave in [Maros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maros), and added: "Next to it is a pig that has a minimum age of 35,400 years old, and this is one of the oldest figurative depictions in the world, if not the oldest one."[^cite_note-31]

On 11 December 2019, a team of researchers led by Dr. Maxime Aubert announced the discovery of the oldest hunting scenes in prehistoric art in the world that is more than 44,000 years old from the [limestone cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solutional_cave) of Leang Bulu' Sipong 4. Archaeologists determined the age of the depiction of hunting a pig and buffalo thanks to the calcite 'popcorn', different isotope levels of radioactive [uranium](31743.md) and [thorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorium).[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34]

In March 2020, two small stone '[plaquettes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaquette)' were found by [Griffith University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griffith_University) archaeologists in the Leang Bulu Bettue cave, dated to a time between 26,000 and 14,000 years ago.[^cite_note-35] While one of the stones contained an [anoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anoa) (water buffalo) and what may be a flower, star, or eye, another depicted astronomic rays of light.[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38]



Researchers discovered cave art in Leang Tedongnge, Sulawesi, Indonesia, in 2021, estimated to be at least 45,500 years old, making it the world's oldest known representational artwork.

In January 2021, archaeologists announced the discovery of cave art that is at least 45,500 years old in a Leang Tedongnge cave. According to the [journal *Science Advances*](45385909.md), the cave painting of a warty pig is the earliest evidence of human settlement of the region. An adult male pig, measuring 136 cm x 54 cm and what is likely a Sulawesi or [Celebes warty pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celebes_warty_pig) (Sus celebensis), was depicted with horn-like facial warts and two hand prints above its hindquarters.[^cite_note-39] According to co-author Adam Brumm, there are two other pigs that are partly preserved and it appears the warty pig was observing a fight between the two other pigs.[^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43]

The discoveries in Sulawesi have significantly expanded understanding of early human symbolic behavior and have established Indonesia as one of the world's most important locations for the study of prehistoric rock art, with several sites containing some of the oldest known examples of figurative art.[^cite_note-44]

In January 2026, newly discovered images of hand stencils and human figures in caves on the Southwestern peninsula of Sulawesi were dated to be at least 67,800 years old, making them the earliest known evidence of rock art in the world and predating the previous record-holding images in the [Cave of Maltravieso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cave_of_Maltravieso) by over one thousand years.[^cite_note-45]

## History

### Hindu-Buddhist era

See also: [Hinduism in Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism_in_Southeast_Asia), [Buddhism in Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism_in_Southeast_Asia), [Hinduism in Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism_in_Indonesia), [Buddhism in Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism_in_Indonesia), [Greater India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_India), and [Mandala (political model)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_\(political_model\))

A bronze [Amaravathi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amaravati_art) statue was discovered at [Sikendeng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikendeng?action=edit\&redlink=1), South Sulawesi near [Karama river](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karama_River) in 1921 which was dated to 2nd–7th century AD by Bosch (1933).[^cite_note-46] In 1975, small locally made Buddhist statues from 10th-11th century were also discovered in Bontoharu, on the island of [Selayar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selayar_Islands), South Sulawesi.[^cite_note-47]

Starting in the 13th century, access to prestige trade goods and to sources of iron started to alter long-standing cultural patterns and to permit ambitious individuals to build larger political units. It is not known why these two ingredients appeared together; one was perhaps the product of the other.

In 1367, several identified polities located on the island were mentioned in the Javanese manuscript [Nagarakretagama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarakretagama) dated from the [Majapahit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majapahit) period. Canto 14 mentioned polities including Gowa, Makassar, [Luwu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luwu) and Banggai. It seems that by the 14th century, polities in the island were connected in an archipelagic maritime trading network, centered in the Majapahit port in East Java. By 1400, a number of nascent agricultural principalities had arisen in the western Cenrana valley, as well as on the south coast and on the west coast near modern Parepare.[^cite_note-48]

### Christian colonial era

The first Europeans to visit the island (which they believed to be an archipelago due to its contorted shape) were the [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_people) sailors Simão de Abreu in 1523, and [Gomes de Sequeira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gomes_de_Sequeira) (among others) in 1525, sent from the Moluccas in search of gold, which the islands had the reputation of producing.[^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50] A Portuguese base was installed in Makassar in the first decades of the 16th century, lasting until 1665, when it was taken by the Dutch. The Dutch had arrived in Sulawesi in 1605 and were quickly followed by the English, who established a [factory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_\(trading_post\)) in Makassar.[^cite_note-51] From 1660, the Dutch were at war with [Gowa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gowa), the major Makassar west coast power. In 1669, Admiral Speelman forced the ruler, Sultan Hasanuddin, to sign the [Treaty of Bongaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Bongaja), which handed control of trade to the [Dutch East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_East_India_Company). The Dutch were aided in their conquest by the Bugis warlord Arung Palakka, ruler of the Bugis kingdom of [Bone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone_state). The Dutch built a fort at Ujung Pandang, while Arung Palakka became the regional overlord and Bone the dominant kingdom. Political and cultural development seems to have slowed as a result of the status quo.

In 1905, the entire island became part of the Dutch state colony of the [Netherlands East Indies](23476997.md) until [Japanese occupation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_occupation_of_the_Dutch_East_Indies) in the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War). During the [Indonesian National Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_National_Revolution), the Dutch Captain ['Turk' Westerling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Westerling) led campaigns in which hundreds, maybe thousands died during the [South Sulawesi Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Sulawesi_campaign_of_1946%E2%80%931947).[^cite_note-Kahin145-52] Following the transfer of sovereignty in December 1949, Sulawesi became part of the [federal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_government) [United States of Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_of_Indonesia), which in 1950 became absorbed into the [unitary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_state) [Republic of Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Indonesia).[^cite_note-53]

### Central Sulawesi



[Toraja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toraja) burial site. *Tau-tau*, the statue representing the buried people, can be seen in niches on the cliff.

The Portuguese were rumoured to have a fort in Parigi in 1555.[^cite_note-54] The Kaili were an important group based in the [Palu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palu_River) valley and related to the Toraja. Scholars relate that their control swayed under Ternate and Makassar, but this might have been a decision by the Dutch to give their vassals a chance to govern a difficult group. Padbruge commented that in the 1700s the Kaili population was larger and that it was a militarized society. In the 1850s, a civil war erupted between the Kaili groups, including the Banawa, in which the Dutch colonial government decided to intervene.[^cite_note-55]

In the late 19th century, the Sarasins journeyed through the [Palu](1432484.md) valley as part of a major initiative to bring the Kaili under Dutch rule. Some interesting photographs were taken of shamans called Tadulako. Further Christian religious missions entered the area to make one of the most detailed ethnographic studies in the early 20th century.[^cite_note-56] The Swede [Walter Kaudern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Kaudern) later studied much of the literature and produced a synthesis. Erskine Downs in the 1950s produced a summary of Kruyts and Andrianis work: "The religion of the Bare'e-Speaking Toradja of Central Celebes," which is invaluable for English-speaking researchers. One of the most recent publications is "When the bones are left," a study of the material culture of central Sulawesi,[^cite_note-57] offering extensive analysis. Also worthy of study are the brilliant works of Monnig Atkinson on the Wana shamans who live in the Mori area.

[Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Indonesia) is the majority religion in Sulawesi. The conversion of the lowlands of the south western peninsula (South Sulawesi) to Islam occurred in the early 17th century. The kingdom of Luwu in the Gulf of Bone was the first to embrace Islam in February 1605; the Makassar kingdom of Gowa-Talloq, centred on the modern-day city of [Makassar](389464.md), followed suit in September.[^cite_note-64] However, the [Gorontalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gorontaloan_people) and the [Mongondow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongondow_people) peoples of the northern peninsula largely converted to Islam only in the 19th century. Most Sulawesi Muslims are [Sunnis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni).

[Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Indonesia) form a substantial minority on the island. According to the [demographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demography) Toby Alice Volkman, 17% of Sulawesi's population is [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism_in_Indonesia) and less than 2% is [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_Indonesia). Christians are concentrated on the tip of the northern peninsula around the city of [Manado](844858.md), which is inhabited by the [Minahasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minahasa), a predominantly Protestant people, and the northernmost [Sangir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sangir_Islands) and [Talaud Islands](31153243.md). The [Toraja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toraja) people of [Tana Toraja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tana_Toraja) in South Sulawesi have largely converted to Christianity since Indonesia's independence. There are also substantial numbers of Christians around [Lake Poso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Poso) in Central Sulawesi, among the [Pamona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pamona) speaking peoples of Central Sulawesi, and near Mamasa.

Though most people identify themselves as Muslims or Christians, they often [subscribe to local beliefs and deities as well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_syncretism).

Smaller communities of [Buddhists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism_in_Indonesia) and [Hindus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism_in_Indonesia) are also found on Sulawesi, usually among the [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Indonesian), [Balinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balinese_people), and [Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Indonesian) communities.

## Administration

The island was administered as one province between 1945 and 1960. In 1960 it was divided into two provinces – North and Central Sulawesi, and South and Southeast Sulawesi. In 1964 both of these were again divided, the former into North Sulawesi and Central Sulawesi, and the latter into South Sulawesi and Southeast Sulawesi. Today, it is subdivided into six [provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provinces_of_Indonesia): [Gorontalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gorontalo_\(province\)), [West Sulawesi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Sulawesi), [South Sulawesi](389458.md), [Central Sulawesi](355454.md), [Southeast Sulawesi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Sulawesi), and [North Sulawesi](453688.md). Among these, the newest provinces are Gorontalo, established in 2000 from part of North Sulawesi, and West Sulawesi, established in 2004 from part of South Sulawesi.

The largest cities on the island are the provincial capitals of [Makassar](389464.md), [Manado](844858.md), [Palu](1432484.md), [Kendari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kendari), and [Gorontalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gorontalo_\(city\)) (the provincial capital of West Sulawesi – the town of Mamuju – is not rated as a city); there are six other cities on Sulawesi – Bitung, Palopo, Bau-Bau, Parepare, Kotamobagu and Tomohun.

## Flora and fauna

See also: [Sulawesi lowland rain forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulawesi_lowland_rain_forests) and [Sulawesi montane rain forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulawesi_montane_rain_forests)



The colorful bark of *[Eucalyptus deglupta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucalyptus_deglupta)*

Sulawesi is part of [Wallacea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallacea), meaning that it has a mix of both [Indomalayan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indomalayan_realm) and [Australasian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australasian_realm) species that reached the island by [crossing deep-water oceanic barriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_dispersal).[^cite_note-Hall1998-66][^cite_note-WWF-67] The flora includes one native eucalypt, *[E. deglupta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucalyptus_deglupta)*. There are 8 [national parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_national_parks_of_Indonesia#Sulawesi) on the island, of which 4 are mostly [marine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_park). The parks with the largest terrestrial area are [Bogani Nani Wartabone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogani_Nani_Wartabone_National_Park) with 2,871 km<sup>2</sup> and [Lore Lindu National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lore_Lindu_National_Park) with 2,290 km<sup>2</sup>. [Bunaken National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunaken_National_Park), which protects a rich coral ecosystem, has been proposed as a UNESCO [World Heritage Site](44940.md). The coast of northern tip of Sulawesi is identified as a site of highest marine biodiversity importance in the [Coral Triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral_Triangle).[^cite_note-Asaad18-68]

### Mammals

Early in the Pleistocene, Sulawesi had a [dwarf elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_elephant) and a [dwarf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insular_dwarfism) form of *[Stegodon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stegodon)*, (an elephant relative, *S. sompoensis*);[^cite_note-Corlett2010-69] later both were replaced by larger forms.[^cite_note-Aziz2000-70][^cite_note-Morwood2016-71] A giant [suid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suidae), *[Celebochoerus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celebochoerus)*, was also formerly present.[^cite_note-Metcalfe2001-72] It is thought that many of the migrants to Sulawesi arrived via the [Philippines](23440.md), while Sulawesi in turn served as a way station for migrants to [Flores](11426.md).[^cite_note-73] A Pleistocene faunal turnover is recognized, with the competitive displacement of several indigenous tarsiers by more recently arriving ones and of *[Celebochoerus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celebochoerus)* by other medium-sized herbivores like the [babirusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babirusa), [anoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anoa) and [Celebes warty pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celebes_warty_pig).[^cite_note-74]



The [north Sulawesi babirusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sulawesi_babirusa) is endemic to Sulawesi.

There are 127 known extant native mammalian species in Sulawesi. A large percentage, 62% (79 species) are [endemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endemism), meaning that they are found nowhere else in the world. The largest of these are the two species of [anoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anoa) or dwarf buffalo. Other artiodactyl species inhabiting Sulawesi are the [warty pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celebes_warty_pig) and the [babirusas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babirusas), which are aberrant pigs. The only native [carnivoran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnivora) is the [Sulawesi palm civet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulawesi_palm_civet)[^cite_note-Metcalfe2001-72] ([Asian palm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_palm_civet) and [Malayan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malayan_civet) civets have been introduced[^cite_note-Macrogalidia_iucn-75]). Primates present include a number of [nocturnal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nocturnal) [tarsiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarsier) (*[T. fuscus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarsius_fuscus)*, [Dian's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dian's_tarsier), [Gursky's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gursky's_spectral_tarsier), [Jatna's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jatna's_tarsier), [Wallace's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallace's_tarsier), the [Lariang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lariang_tarsier) and [pygmy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pygmy_tarsier) tarsiers) as well as [diurnal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diurnality) [macaques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macaque) ([Heck's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heck's_macaque), the [booted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booted_macaque), [crested black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celebes_crested_macaque), [Gorontalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gorontalo_macaque), [moor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moor_macaque), and [Tonkean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonkean_macaque) macaques). While most of Sulawesi's mammals are [placental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutheria) and have Asian relatives, several species of [cuscus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuscus), arboreal [marsupials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsupials) of Australasian origin, are also present ([Sulawesi bear cuscus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulawesi_bear_cuscus) and [Sulawesi dwarf cuscus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulawesi_dwarf_cuscus), which are diurnal and nocturnal, respectively).

Sulawesi is home to a large number of endemic [rodent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodent) genera. [Murid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muridae) rodent genera endemic to Sulawesi and immediately adjacent islands (such as the [Togian Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Togian_Islands), [Buton Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buton), and [Muna Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muna_Island)) are *[Bunomys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunomys)*, *[Echiothrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echiothrix)*, *[Margaretamys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaretamys)*, *[Taeromys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taeromys)* and *[Tateomys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tateomys)* as well as the single-species genera *[Eropeplus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eropeplus)*, *[Hyorhinomys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyorhinomys)*, *[Melasmothrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melasmothrix)*, *[Paucidentomys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paucidentomys)*, *[Paruromys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paruromys)*, *[Sommeromys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sommeromys)* and the [semiaquatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiaquatic) *[Waiomys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waiomys)*. All nine [sciurids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sciuridae) are from three endemic genera, *[Hyosciurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyosciurus)*, *[Prosciurillus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosciurillus)* and *[Rubrisciurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubrisciurus)*.

While over 20 bat species are present on Sulawesi, only a portion of these are endemic: *[Rhinolophus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhinolophus) [tatar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhinolophus_tatar)*, *[Scotophilus celebensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotophilus_celebensis)* and the [megabats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megabat) *[Acerodon celebensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acerodon_celebensis)*, *[Boneia bidens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boneia_bidens)*, *[Dobsonia exoleta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dobsonia_exoleta)*, *[Harpyionycteris celebensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harpyionycteris_celebensis)*, *[Neopteryx frosti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neopteryx_frosti)*, *[Rousettus celebensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rousettus_celebensis)* and *[Styloctenium wallacei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Styloctenium_wallacei)*.

Several endemic shrews, the [Sulawesi shrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulawesi_shrew), [Sulawesi tiny shrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulawesi_tiny_shrew) and the [Sulawesi white-handed shrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulawesi_white-handed_shrew), are found on the island.

Sulawesi has no [gliding mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gliding_mammal), being situated between Borneo with its [colugos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunda_flying_lemur) and [flying squirrels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_squirrel), and Halmahera with its [sugar gliders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_glider).

### Birds



The endemic [ornate lorikeet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornate_lorikeet)&#x20;

By contrast, Sulawesian bird species tend to be found on other nearby islands as well, such as [Borneo](4518.md); 31% of Sulawesi's birds are found nowhere else. One endemic (also found on small neighboring islands) is the largely ground-dwelling, chicken-sized [maleo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maleo), a [megapode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megapode) which sometimes uses hot sand close to the island's volcanic vents to incubate its eggs. An international partnership of conservationists, donors, and local people have formed the Alliance for Tompotika Conservation,[^cite_note-76] in an effort to raise awareness and protect the nesting grounds of these birds on the central-eastern arm of the island. Other endemic birds include the flightless [snoring rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snoring_rail), the [fiery-browed starling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiery-browed_starling), the [Sulawesi masked owl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulawesi_masked_owl), the [Sulawesi myna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulawesi_myna), the [satanic nightjar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satanic_nightjar) and the [grosbeak starling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grosbeak_starling). There are around 350 known bird species in Sulawesi.

### Reptiles

The larger reptiles of Sulawesi are not endemic and include [reticulated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reticulated_python) and [Burmese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burmese_python) pythons, the [Pacific ground boa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_ground_boa), [king cobras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_cobra), [water monitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_monitor), [sailfin lizards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrosaurus_amboinensis),[^cite_note-Whitten2012-77] [saltwater crocodiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saltwater_crocodile)[^cite_note-Whitten2012-77][^cite_note-Sideleau2016-78] and [green sea turtles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_sea_turtle). An extinct giant [tortoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tortoise), *[Megalochelys atlas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megalochelys_atlas)*, was formerly present, but disappeared by 840,000 years ago, possibly because of the arrival of [Homo erectus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_erectus).[^cite_note-Corlett2010-69][^cite_note-Morwood2016-71] Similarly, [komodo dragons](17360.md) or similar lizards appear to have inhabited the island, being among its [apex predators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apex_predator).[^cite_note-79] The smaller snakes of Sulawesi include nonendemic forms such as the gliding species *[Chrysopelea paradisi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrysopelea_paradisi)* and endemic forms such as *[Calamaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calamaria) [boesemani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calamaria_boesemani)*, *[Calamaria muelleri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calamaria_muelleri)*, *[Calamaria nuchalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calamaria_nuchalis)*, *[Cyclotyphlops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclotyphlops)*, *[Enhydris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enhydris) [matannensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enhydris_matannensis?action=edit\&redlink=1)*, *[Ptyas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptyas) [dipsas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptyas_dipsas)*, *[Rabdion grovesi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabdion_grovesi)*, *[Tropidolaemus laticinctus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropidolaemus_laticinctus)* and *[Typhlops conradi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhlops_conradi)*. Similarly, the smaller lizards of Sulawesi include nonendemic species such as *[Bronchocela jubata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronchocela_jubata)*, *[Dibamus novaeguineae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dibamus_novaeguineae)* and *[Gekko smithii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gekko_smithii)*, as well as endemic species such as *[Lipinia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipinia) [infralineolata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipinia_infralineolata)* and *[Gekko iskandari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gekko_iskandari)*.

Sulawesi also harbours several species of freshwater chelonians, two of which are endemic. They include the [Forsten's tortoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forsten's_tortoise) and the [Sulawesi forest turtle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulawesi_forest_turtle), both of which likely attribute their respective origins to the dispersal of the mainland Asian [elongated tortoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elongated_tortoise) and [Malayan flat-shelled turtle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malayan_flat-shelled_turtle) from the then-exposed subcontinent of [Sundaland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundaland) during the [Pleistocene epoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene_epoch).

The remaining two species consist of the non-endemic [Malayan box turtle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malayan_box_turtle) of the Wallacean subspecies, and the [Asiatic softshell turtle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asiatic_softshell_turtle).

### Amphibians

The amphibians of Sulawesi include the endemic frogs *[Hylarana celebensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hylarana_celebensis)*, *[H. macrops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hylarana_macrops)*, *[H. mocquardi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hylarana_mocquardi)*, *[Ingerophrynus celebensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingerophrynus_celebensis)*, *[Limnonectes arathooni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limnonectes_arathooni)*, *[L. larvaepartus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limnonectes_larvaepartus)*, *[L. microtympanum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limnonectes_microtympanum)*, *[Occidozyga celebensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occidozyga_celebensis)*, *[O. semipalmata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occidozyga_semipalmata)* and *[O. tompotika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occidozyga_tompotika)* as well as the endemic "[flying frogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_frog)" *[Rhacophorus edentulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhacophorus_edentulus)* and *[R. georgii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhacophorus_georgii)*.

### Freshwater fish



15 species of [viviparous halfbeaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viviparous_halfbeak) are endemic to Sulawesi, including 12 *[Nomorhamphus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomorhamphus)* (depicted), *[Dermogenys orientalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dermogenys_orientalis?action=edit\&redlink=1)*, *[D. vogti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dermogenys_vogti?action=edit\&redlink=1)*, and *[Tondanichthys kottelati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tondanichthys_kottelati)*.

[^cite_note-SysNomorhamphus-80]

[^cite_note-FishBaseNomorhamphus-81]

[^cite_note-82]

Sulawesi is home to more than 70 [freshwater fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freshwater_fish) species,[^cite_note-83] including more than 55 endemics.[^cite_note-Parenti2011-84] Among these are the genus *[Nomorhamphus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomorhamphus)*, a [species flock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species_flock) of [viviparous halfbeaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viviparous_halfbeak) containing 12 species that only are found on Sulawesi (others are from the Philippines).[^cite_note-SysNomorhamphus-80][^cite_note-FishBaseNomorhamphus-81] In addition to *Nomorhamphus*, the majority of Sulawesi's freshwater fish species are [ricefishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricefish), gobies (*[Glossogobius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossogobius)* and *[Mugilogobius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mugilogobius)*) and [Telmatherinid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telmatherinidae) sail-fin silversides.[^cite_note-Parenti2011-84] The last family is almost entirely restricted to Sulawesi, especially the Malili Lake system, consisting of [Matano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Matano) and [Towuti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Towuti), and the small [Lontoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Lontoa?action=edit\&redlink=1) (Wawantoa), [Mahalona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Mahalona?action=edit\&redlink=1) and [Masapi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Masapi?action=edit\&redlink=1).[^cite_note-85] Another unusual endemic is *[Lagusia micracanthus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagusia_micracanthus)* from rivers in [South Sulawesi](389458.md), which is the sole member of its genus and among the smallest [grunters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terapontidae).[^cite_note-86] The [gudgeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleotridae) *[Bostrychus microphthalmus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bostrychus_microphthalmus)* from the [Maros](31153043.md) Karst is the only described species of [cave-adapted fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavefish) from Sulawesi,[^cite_note-87] but an apparently [undescribed species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undescribed_species) from the same region and genus also exists.[^cite_note-88]

### Freshwater crustaceans and snails



Orange delight shrimp (*[Caridina loehae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caridina_loehae)*) from Sulawesi.

Many species of *[Caridina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caridina)* freshwater shrimp and [parathelphusid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parathelphusidae) freshwater crabs (*[Migmathelphusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migmathelphusa)*, *[Nautilothelphusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nautilothelphusa?action=edit\&redlink=1)*, *[Parathelphusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parathelphusa)*, *[Sundathelphusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundathelphusa)* and *[Syntripsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntripsa)*) are endemic to Sulawesi.[^cite_note-shrimp-89][^cite_note-crab-90] Several of these species have become very popular in the aquarium hobby, and since most are restricted to a single lake system, they are potentially vulnerable to [habitat loss](1707053.md) and [overexploitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overexploitation).[^cite_note-shrimp-89][^cite_note-crab-90] There are also several endemic cave-adapted shrimp and crabs, especially in the Maros Karst. This includes *[Cancrocaeca xenomorpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancrocaeca_xenomorpha)*, which has been called the "most highly [cave-adapted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troglobite) species of crab known in the world".[^cite_note-91]

The genus *[Tylomelania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tylomelania)* of [freshwater snails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freshwater_snail) is also endemic to Sulawesi, with the majority of the species restricted to [Lake Poso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Poso) and the Malili Lake system.[^cite_note-92]

### Insects

The *[Trigonopterus selayarensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonopterus_selayarensis?action=edit\&redlink=1)* is a flightless [weevil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weevil) endemic to Sulawesi.[^cite_note-93]

### Miscellaneous

The [Indonesian coelacanth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_coelacanth) and the [mimic octopus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimic_octopus) are present in the waters off Sulawesi's coast.

### Conservation

Sulawesi island was the subject of an Ecoregional Conservation Assessment in 2005, coordinated by [The Nature Conservancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Nature_Conservancy). Detailed reports about the vegetation of the island are available.[^cite_note-94] The assessment produced a detailed and annotated list of 'conservation portfolio' sites. This information was widely distributed to local government agencies and nongovernmental organizations. Detailed conservation priorities have also been outlined in a recent publication.[^cite_note-95]

The lowland forests on the island have mostly been removed.[^cite_note-96] Because of the relative geological youth of the island and its dramatic and sharp topography, the lowland areas are naturally limited in their extent. The past decade has seen dramatic conversion of this rare and endangered habitat. The island also possesses one of the largest outcrops of [serpentine soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serpentine_soil) in the world, which support an unusual and large community of specialized plant species. Overall, the flora and fauna of this unique center of global biodiversity is very poorly documented and understood and remains critically threatened.

The islands of Pepaya, Mas, and Raja islands, located in Sumalata Village – [North Gorontalo Regency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Gorontalo_Regency) (about 30 km from [Saronde Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saronde_Island)), have been named a nature reserve since the Dutch colonial time in 1936. Four of the only seven species of [sea turtles](142905.md) can be found in the islands, the world's best turtle habitat. They include penyu hijau (*[Chelonia midas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_sea_turtle)*), penyu sisik (*[Eretmochelys imbricata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawksbill_sea_turtle)*), penyu tempayan (*[Caretta caretta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caretta_caretta)*) and penyu belimbing (*[Dermochelys coriacea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dermochelys_coriacea)*). In 2011, the habitat was threatened by human activities such as illegal poaching and fish bombing activities; furthermore, many coral reefs, which represent a source of food for turtles, have been damaged.[^cite_note-97]

## Environment



[Bunaken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunaken) Island seen from Manado Tua island.

The largest environmental issue in Sulawesi is [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation). In 2007, scientists found that 80 percent of Sulawesi's forest had been lost or degraded, especially centered in the lowlands and the mangroves.[^cite_note-98] Forests have been felled for logging, large agricultural projects, and, increasingly, nickel mining. Loss of forest has resulted in many of Sulawesi's endemic species becoming endangered. In addition, 99 percent of Sulawesi's wetlands have been lost or damaged.

Other environmental threats included bushmeat hunting and mining.[^cite_note-Sulawesi_Profile-99]

### Parks

The island of Sulawesi has six national parks and nineteen nature reserves. In addition, Sulawesi has three marine protected areas. Many of Sulawesi's parks are threatened by logging, mining, and deforestation for agriculture.[^cite_note-Sulawesi_Profile-99]
