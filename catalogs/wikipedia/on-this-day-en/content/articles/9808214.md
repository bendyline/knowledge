A **combustible** material is a material that can [burn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion) (i.e. sustain a [flame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flame)) in air under certain conditions. A material is **flammable** or **inflammable** if it [ignites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_making) easily at [room temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_temperature). In other words, a combustible material takes more effort to ignite and a flammable material ignites almost immediately when exposed to a flame.

The degree of flammability in air depends largely on how easy the material [vaporizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaporization). This is related to its composition-specific [vapor pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vapor_pressure), which is temperature dependent. The quantity of vapor produced can be enhanced by increasing the [surface area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_area) of the material, forming a mist or dust. Wood is an example. Finely divided [wood dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_dust) can undergo [explosive flames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_explosion) and produce a blast wave. A piece of paper (made from [pulp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulp_\(paper\))) catches on fire quite easily. A heavy oak desk is much harder to ignite, even though the wood fibers are the same in all three materials.

Common sense, and [scientific consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phlogiston_theory) until the mid-1700s, suggests that material disappears when burned, as only the [ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ash) is left. Further scientific research has found that [conservation of mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_mass) holds for chemical reactions. The burning of a solid material may appear to lose mass if the mass of combustion gases (such as [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) and [water vapor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor)) is not taken into account. The original mass of flammable material and the mass of the oxygen consumed (from the surrounding air) equals the mass of the flame products (ash, water, carbon dioxide, and other gases). Some metals gain mass when they burn to support the idea (because those chemical reactions capture oxygen atoms into solid compounds rather than gaseous water).[^cite_note-1]

## Definitions

Historically, *flammable*, *inflammable* and *combustible* meant *capable of burning*.[^cite_note-2] The word "inflammable" came through French from the Latin *inflammāre* = "to set fire to", where the Latin preposition "in-"[^cite_note-3] means "in" as in "indoctrinate", rather than "not" as in "invisible" and "ineligible".

The word inflammable may be erroneously thought to mean non-flammable.[^cite_note-4] The erroneous usage of the word inflammable is a significant [safety hazard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safety_hazard). Therefore, since the 1950s, efforts to use flammable in place of inflammable were accepted by linguists, and it is now the accepted standard in American English and British English.[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6] Antonyms of flammable or inflammable include: *non-flammable*, *non-inflammable*, *incombustible*, *non-combustible*, *not flammable*, and *fireproof*.

*Flammable* applies to *combustible* materials that ignite easily and thus are more dangerous and more highly regulated. Less easily ignited and less-vigorously burning materials are *combustible*. For example, in the United States [flammable liquids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flammable_liquid), by definition, have a [flash point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_point) below 100 °F (38 °C). Combustible liquids have a flash point above 100 °F (38 °C). [Flammable solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HAZMAT_Class_4_Flammable_solids) are solids that are readily combustible, may cause, or contribute to fire through friction. Readily combustible solids are [powdered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powder), granular, or pasty substances that easily ignite by brief contact with an ignition source, such as a burning match, and spread flame rapidly.[^cite_note-OSHA-7] The technical definitions vary between countries so the [United Nations](31769.md) created the [Globally Harmonized System of Classification and Labeling of Chemicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globally_Harmonized_System_of_Classification_and_Labeling_of_Chemicals), which defines the flash point temperature of flammable liquids as between 0 and 140 °F (60 °C) and combustible liquids between 140 °F (60 °C) and 200 °F (93 °C).[^cite_note-OSHA-7]

## Flammability

*Flammability* is the ease with which a combustible substance can be ignited causing [fire](11145.md), [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion), or an explosion. The degree of difficulty required to cause the combustion of a substance is quantified through [fire testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_test). A variety of test protocols exist to quantify flammability. The ratings achieved are used in [building codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building_code), [insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insurance) requirements, [fire codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_code), and other regulations that govern the use of building materials as well as the storage and handling of highly flammable substances. This includes inside and outside of structures and in surface and air transportation. For instance, changing an occupancy by altering the flammability of the contents requires the owner of a [building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building) to apply for a building permit to make sure that the overall [fire protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_protection) design of the facility takes the change into account.

### Furniture flammability

Flammability of furniture is of concern as cigarettes and candle accidents can trigger domestic fires. In 1975, California began implementing [Technical Bulletin 117](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_Bulletin_117) (TB 117), which required that materials such as [polyurethane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyurethane) foam used to fill furniture be able to withstand a small open flame, equivalent to a candle, for at least 12 seconds.[^cite_note-TB117-14] In polyurethane foam, furniture manufacturers typically meet TB 117 with additive halogenated organic [flame retardants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flame_retardant). No other U.S. states had similar standards. California had such a large market, [manufacturers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing) meet TB 117 in products that they distribute across the United States. The proliferation of flame retardants, especially halogenated organic flame retardants, in furniture across the United States is strongly linked to TB 117. When it became apparent that the risk-benefit ratio of this approach was unfavorable and the industry had used falsified documentation (i.e. see [David Heimbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Heimbach)) for the use of flame retardants, California modified TB 117 to require that fabric covering upholstered furniture meet a smolder test that replaced the open [flame test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flame_test).[^cite_note-15] Governor [Jerry Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_Brown) signed the modified TB117-2013, which became effective in 2014.[^cite_note-gov.brown-16]

### Fabric flammability

Lightweight textiles with porous surfaces are the most flammable fabrics.[^cite_note-Sikker-17] Wool is less flammable than cotton, linen, silk, or viscose ([rayon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayon)).[^cite_note-Sikker-17][^cite_note-Phoenix-18] Polyester and nylon resist ignition and melt rather than catch fire.[^cite_note-Sikker-17][^cite_note-Phoenix-18] [Acrylic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acrylic_fiber) is the most flammable synthetic fiber.[^cite_note-Sikker-17]

## Combustibility

**Combustibility** is a measure of how easily a substance bursts into flame, through [fire](11145.md) or [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion). This is an important property to consider when a substance is used for [construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction) or is being stored. It is also important in processes that produce combustible substances as a [by-product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/By-product). Special precautions are usually required for substances that are easily combustible. These measures may include installation of [fire sprinklers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_sprinkler) or storage remote from possible sources of ignition.

Substances with low combustibility may be selected for construction where the fire risk must be reduced, such as apartment buildings, houses, or offices. If combustible resources are used there is greater chance of fire accidents and deaths. [Fire resistant](6774137.md) substances are preferred for building materials and furnishings.

### Non-combustible material

A non-combustible material[^cite_note-20] is a substance that does not ignite, burn, support combustion, or release flammable vapors when subject to fire or heat. Any solid substance complying with either of two sets of passing criteria, listed in Section 8 of ASTM E 136 when the substance is tested in accordance with the procedure specified in ASTM E 136, is considered to be non-combustible.[^cite_note-21]

### Combustible dust

Main article: [Dust explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_explosion)

A number of industrial processes produce combustible dust as a by-product. The most common is [wood dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_dust). Combustible dust has been defined by [OSHA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OSHA) as: *a solid material composed of distinct particles or pieces, regardless of size, shape, or chemical composition, which presents a fire or deflagration [hazard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazard) when [suspended in air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particulates) or some other oxidizing medium over a range of concentrations.*[^cite_note-OSHA-2009-22] In addition to wood, combustible dusts include [metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal), specifically magnesium, titanium, and aluminum, as well as other carbon-based dusts.[^cite_note-OSHA-2009-22] There are at least 140 known substances that produce combustible dust.[^cite_note-CSBFinalReport-23]<sup>: 38 </sup>[^cite_note-24] While the particles in a combustible dusts may be of any size, normally they have a diameter of less than 420 [μm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CE%9Cm).[^cite_note-OSHA-2009-22][^cite_note-25] As of 2012, the [United States Occupational Safety and Health Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Occupational_Safety_and_Health_Administration) has yet to adopt a comprehensive set of rules on combustible dust.[^cite_note-26]

When suspended in air (or any oxidizing environment), the fine particles of combustible dust present a potential for explosions. Accumulated dust, even when not suspended in air, remains a fire hazard. The National Fire Protection Association (U.S.) specifically addresses the prevention of fires and dust explosions in agricultural and food products facilities in NFPA Code section 61,[^cite_note-27] and other industries in NFPA Code sections 651–664.[^cite_note-28] [Collectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_collector) designed to reduce airborne dust account for more than 40 percent of all dust explosions.[^cite_note-29] Other important processes are [grinding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grinding_\(abrasive_cutting\)) and [pulverizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulverization), transporting powders, filing silos and containers (which produces powder), and the mixing and blending of powders.[^cite_note-30]

Investigation of 200 dust explosions and fires, between 1980 and 2005, indicated approximately **100 fatalities and 600 injuries**.[^cite_note-CSBFinalReport-23]<sup>: 105–106 </sup> In January 2003, a [polyethylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyethylene) powder explosion and fire at the West Pharmaceutical Services plant in [Kinston, North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinston,_North_Carolina) resulted in the deaths of six workers and injuries to 38 others.[^cite_note-CSBFinalReport-23]<sup>: 104 </sup> In February 2008, an [explosion of sugar dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Georgia_sugar_refinery_explosion) occurred at the [Imperial Sugar Company's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Sugar) plant at [Port Wentworth, Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Wentworth,_Georgia),[^cite_note-31] resulting in thirteen deaths.[^cite_note-32]

### Flash point

A material's [flash point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_point) is a metric of how easy it is to ignite the vapor of the material as it evaporates into the [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere). It is defined as the lowest material temperature required for fuel oils in the materials to begin to give off flammable vapors in a quantity high enough to support a flash of fire when ignited by an external source.[^cite_note-ThangarasuAnand2019-33] A lower flash point indicates higher flammability. Materials with flash points below 100 [°F](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fahrenheit) (38 [°C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celsius)) are regulated in the [United States](3434750.md) by [OSHA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_Safety_and_Health_Administration) as potential workplace [hazards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazards).

### Flame point

Main article: [Fire point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_point)

The *flame point* of a material is a temperature value at which sustained flame can be supported on the material once ignited by an external source.[^cite_note-ThangarasuAnand2019-33] Once the flame point of a material is reached, it produces enough fuel vapors or oils to support continuous burning.

### Flammability or explosive range

The *[lower flammability limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_flammability_limit)* or *lower explosive limit (LFL/LEL)* represents the lowest air to fuel vapor concentration required for combustion to take place when ignited by an external source, for any chemical.[^cite_note-Manha2009-34] Any concentration lower than this could not produce a flame or result in combustion. The *upper flammability limit* or *[upper explosive limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_explosive_limit)* (UFL/UEL) represents the highest air to fuel vapor concentration at which combustion can take place when ignited by an external source.[^cite_note-Manha2009-34] Any fuel-air mixture higher than this would be too concentrated to result in combustion. The values existing between these two limits represent the flammable or explosive range. Within this threshold, given an external ignition source, combustion of the particular fuel would likely happen.

### Vapor pressure

The [vapor pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vapor_pressure) of a liquid is a measure of how much the vapor of the liquid tends to concentrate in the surrounding atmosphere as the liquid evaporates.[^cite_note-35] Vapor pressure is a major determinant of the flash point and flame point, with higher vapor pressures leading to lower flash points and higher flammability ratings.

## Codes

Main article: [Building code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building_code)

The [International Code Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Building_Code) (ICC) developed fire code requirements to provide adequate protection to a building and its occupants.[^cite_note-36] These codes specify the combustibility rating for materials, the entrance and exit requirements, active fire protection requirements, and numerous other things. In the U.S., other agencies have also developed building codes that specify combustibility ratings. Following the requirements of these fire codes is crucial for higher occupancy buildings.



Flammable liquid cabinet often required by fire code.

For existing buildings, fire codes focus on maintaining the occupancies as originally intended. In other words, if a portion of a building were designed as an [apartment](265567.md), one could not suddenly load it with flammable liquids and turn it into a gas storage facility. The fire load and smoke development in that one apartment would be so immense it would overtax the [active and](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_fire_protection) [passive fire protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_fire_protection) means for the building. The handling and use of flammable substances inside a building is subject to the local fire code, which is enforced by the local fire prevention officer.

## Fire service relevance

Understanding combustibility and flammability is important for fire service operations. Flammable materials, like gasoline, produce vapors that can ignite at low temperatures. This can contribute to rapid fire spread, even to remote locations.[^cite_note-37] Combustible materials, like wood, require a higher temperature to ignite, but will burn longer once it reaches that temperature.[^cite_note-38]

Knowing what materials are present in a building is very important for firefighters. Knowing whether a material is flammable or combustible can help firefighters know what to expect as far as fire behavior and how quickly the fire may spread. Buildings with flammable liquids or gases would be at a higher risk for a quick growing fire with a lot of heat early on. Buildings storing combustibles may have a lower rate of spread, but can sustain burning longer.[^cite_note-39]

There are many different factors that influence how materials will burn. Moisture, surface area, and the ventilation in the area are a few factors, as well as, flash points and ignition temperatures. Armed with knowledge like the flash point and whether it is considered flammable or combustible helps firefighters predict fire behavior and use the correct suppression methods. This increases scene safety overall.[^cite_note-osha-40]

### Fire testing

Main article: [Fire test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_test)

Various countries have tests for determining non-combustibility of materials. Most involve the heating of a specified quantity of the test specimen for a set duration. Usually, the material must not support combustion and must not lose more than a specified amount of mass. As a general rule of thumb, concrete, steel, and ceramics - in other words inorganic substances - pass these tests. Building codes list them as suitable and sometimes mandate their use in certain applications. In [Canada](5042916.md), for instance, [firewalls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firewall_\(construction\)) must be made of [concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete).
