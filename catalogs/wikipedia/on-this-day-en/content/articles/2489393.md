**Panjgur District** ([Balochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balochi_language): پنجگور دمگ; [Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu_language): ضلع پنجگور) is a [district](639402.md) in the western part of [Balochistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balochistan_\(Pakistan\)) province, [Pakistan](23235.md), covering an area of 16,891 square kilometres (6,522 sq mi) with a population of 509,781 as of the [2023 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Pakistani_census). It borders [Iran](14653.md) to the west along the [Iran–Pakistan border](11715031.md) and lies within the broader [Makran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makran) region. [Chitkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chitkan,_Pakistan) serves as the principal urban centre and district headquarters.

Panjgur was one of three tehsils of the former Makran District until 1 July 1977, when [Makran Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makuran_Division) was created and Panjgur constituted as a separate district alongside [Kech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kech_District) (Turbat) and [Gwadar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwadar_District).[^cite_note-bhc-3] The district is best known as one of Pakistan's principal producers of Muzati (Mazafati) dates and as the only highland agricultural zone in the Makran region.

## Etymology

The name Panjgur is most commonly interpreted as a compound of the [Balochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balochi_language) words *panj* (five) and *goran* (highland), meaning "land of five highlands". Some scholars link *goran* to the [Avestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avestan) word *gairi* (mountain), a derivation supported by nearby place names such as Chokgoran ("small highland") and Mazangoran ("big highland"). A second derivation combines *panj* with *goor* (grave), yielding "five graves", in reference to five venerated local tombs. A third interpretation reads the name as a compound of *panj* and *kor* (stream), meaning "five streams", which is said to have shifted over time to "Panjgur".[^cite_note-smeda-4] None of these etymologies has been conclusively established.

## Geography

Panjgur District covers an area of 16,891 square kilometres (6,522 sq mi) in western Balochistan, sharing an international border with [Sistan and Baluchestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sistan_and_Baluchestan_Province) province of [Iran](14653.md) to the west. This boundary, known as the [Goldsmid Line](11715031.md), was formalised as the Perso-Baloch frontier in the nineteenth century and continues to shape the economic and social life of the district's western tehsils. The district sits within a highland basin at an elevation of approximately 980 metres (3,220 ft) around [Chitkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chitkan,_Pakistan), ringed by arid mountain ranges that distinguish it from the lower-lying Makran coast. The [Siahan Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siahan_Range) forms the principal mountain barrier to the north. In the western and southwestern parts of the district, around [Paroom Tehsil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paroom_Tehsil), elevations generally exceed 900 metres (3,000 ft) with peaks surpassing 1,300 metres (4,300 ft).[^cite_note-ppaf-5]

The [Rakhshan River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rakhshan_River) is the principal waterway, flowing through the centre of the district and dividing it broadly into northern and southern zones. Most human settlements are concentrated along its banks. The [Gichk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gichk) valley, in the southeastern part of the district, is a historically significant sub-region and the ancestral seat of the [Gichki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gichki) ruling dynasty of Makran. The River Tank, the district's second principal waterway, flows through the southeastern parts of the district, with the Gichki and Raghai streams among its tributaries.[^cite_note-ppaf-5]

Agriculture is the principal economic activity along the river corridor; irrigation depends mainly on *[karez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karez)* underground channels fed by the subsurface flow of the [Rakhshan River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rakhshan_River) and *kaurjos*, small water channels drawn from pits dug into the perennial river flow and directed to fields.[^cite_note-ppaf-5][^cite_note-ICUN-6] The remainder of the district is sparsely populated and characterised by arid and rocky terrain with [xerophytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerophyte) vegetation typical of the Makran region.

## History

The town of [Panjgur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panjgur) is among the oldest documented settlements in the Makran region. [Al-Muqaddasī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Maqdisi), writing in 985 AD, recorded it, then known in [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) as *Bannajbur*, as the capital of Makran and noted that it was inhabited by a people called *Balūṣh*, constituting the earliest known Arabic reference to the [Baloch people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baloch_people).[^cite_note-7] In 643 AD, Islamic forces under Abdullah conquered Makran and the region subsequently came under successive Arab rulers; Arab geographers of the era described it as largely desert.[^cite_note-smeda-4] Over subsequent centuries the area passed through the hands of various conquerors, including the [Deilamis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daylamites), [Seljuks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuk_dynasty), [Ghaznavids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghaznavid_Empire), [Ghurids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghurid_dynasty), and [Mongols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasions_of_India), though local rulers such as the Hots, [Rinds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rind_\(tribe\)), Maliks, [Buledais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buledi), and Gichkis maintained practical authority, as outside powers had no intention of permanent settlement.[^cite_note-8]

The Buledais rose to prominence in the region through their association with the [Zikri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zikri) sect and maintained links with the rulers of Muscat; they held power for over a century before converting to mainstream Islam in their later years.[^cite_note-smeda-4] The [Gichki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gichki) tribe, initially settled in the [Gichk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gichk) valley of Panjgur, subsequently rose to become the ruling class of the state of Makran from approximately 1740 until 1955. Their origins are debated; the most widely cited account describes them as [Rajput](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajput) migrants who arrived in Makran between the fifteenth and seventeenth centuries, gradually converting to Islam and establishing themselves through diplomacy and strategic alliances with local ruling families.[^cite_note-9] The Gichkis were also initially associated with the Zikri sect, which brought them into conflict with the [Khan of Kalat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khan_of_Kalat), [Mir Nasir Khan I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mir_Nasir_Khan_I), who conducted several expeditions against Panjgur in the late eighteenth century with the aim of suppressing the Zikri faith. These campaigns resulted in revenue-sharing agreements between the Khan and the Gichkis rather than their outright subjugation.[^cite_note-smeda-4]

Major [Frederic John Goldsmid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederic_John_Goldsmid) visited the Panjgur region in 1861 as part of British efforts to demarcate western Balochistan. By 1863 an Assistant Political Agent had been appointed in Gwadar with oversight over the broader district. In 1882, internal feuds led to the killing of Mir Gajian, Sardar of Panjgur and the Khan's representative, by Mir Nauroz Khan Nausherwani, Chief of Kharan.[^cite_note-smeda-4] In 1903, a dedicated Assistant Political Agent was posted in Panjgur itself, also serving as Commandant of the Makran Levy Corps.[^cite_note-smeda-4] Panjgur remained under the nominal suzerainty of the Khan of Kalat throughout the colonial period. The [Goldsmid Line](11715031.md), formalised as the Perso-Baloch boundary, bisected several historical communities in the western parts of the district, separating populations with shared tribal, linguistic, and kinship ties across what became an international frontier.

Following the [Partition of British India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_British_India) in 1947, the Makran region joined the Balochistan States Union in early 1949. In October 1955, Makran attained the status of a district within West Pakistan, and on 1 July 1970, following the dissolution of the One Unit scheme, it became one of eight districts of the new Balochistan province. Panjgur was formally designated as a separate district on 1 July 1977.[^cite_note-bhc-3]

### Post-independence

In its post-independence history, Panjgur faced two significant natural disasters. In 1958–59, heavy rains and subsequent [flooding](50482.md) caused extensive damage to date trees and crops across the district. In 1960, [cholera outbreaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cholera_outbreaks) occurred in the villages of [Bonistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonistan) and Isai, resulting in a serious loss of lives; these villages have continued to experience sporadic cholera cases owing to a lack of clean drinking water.[^cite_note-smeda-4]

## Places of interest

The district contains several sites of historical and archaeological significance. Kuhna Kalat, constructed with large baked red bricks, is among the notable ancient structures. The tomb of Malik Asa and the ancient dam known as Band-e-Gillar are further points of historical interest. In the village of Khudabadan, remnants of the fort of Nawab Habibullah Khan of Kharan survive. The district also houses numerous old shrines, including those of Shah Qalandar and Pir Umar, which feature distinctive architectural elements such as baked tiles adorned with figures of humans and animals.[^cite_note-smeda-4] The tomb of Allang Essai in [Panjgur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panjgur) is a well-known local monument. Vestiges of the old settlement of Isai also survive within the district.

The [Paroom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paroom_Tehsil) area in the southwest of the district served historically as a principal junction for trade routes connecting [Afghanistan](737.md), [Nushki](4190351.md), and [Kharan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharan,_Pakistan) with the coastal ports of [Gwadar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwadar), [Jiwani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiwani), and Iranian [Chabahar](1658958.md), making control of the region a source of considerable local power. The fort at Diz, whose name itself means "fort" in Balochi, was the main historical stronghold of the area.[^cite_note-10]

[Panjgur Tehsil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panjgur_Tehsil) is by far the most populous, accounting for over three-quarters of the district's total population and containing the city of [Panjgur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panjgur) and the district headquarters at [Chitkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chitkan,_Pakistan). [Paroom Tehsil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paroom_Tehsil) lies along the Iranian border in the southwest of the district.

## Economy

Dates of Panjgoor are very famous

Panjgoor's dates

Agriculture forms the backbone of the district's economy, employing approximately 67% of the total workforce, with around 6% of land under cultivation, mostly in small landholdings.[^cite_note-ppaf-5] The district is one of Pakistan's foremost producers of Muzati (Mazafati) dates, a high-quality table variety, with production estimated to account for approximately 10% of total district revenue.[^cite_note-smeda-4]

Panjgur's dates are widely consumed domestically and exported internationally; demand has grown on account of their recognised health benefits and their use as an ingredient in processed food products. The global market for dates was projected to grow at a compound annual growth rate of 3.6% between 2020 and 2025, and Panjgur's dates are noted for their favourable taste and texture.[^cite_note-smeda-4]

Irrigated land, supplied through traditional *karez* underground channels fed by the subsurface flow of the [Rakhshan River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rakhshan_River), is mainly used for fruit cultivation. Rain-fed and flood-irrigated land supports wheat, [jowar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghum), barley, and rice.[^cite_note-ppaf-5] To protect the karez system, the development of tubewells and dugwells has been banned across the district, with communal karez ownership having developed as a collective norm over time.[^cite_note-ICUN-6] Livestock rearing remains a significant component of rural livelihoods throughout the district.

Cross-border trade with Iran is an important feature of the economy in the western tehsils, particularly [Paroom Tehsil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paroom_Tehsil), where goods including fuel, tiles, and daily-use commodities flow across the border.[^cite_note-12]

## Security

Panjgur District lies within an area affected by the [Baloch insurgency](4591548.md), which has intensified across the province since the early 2000s. The [Balochistan Liberation Army](6010135.md) (BLA), designated a terrorist organisation by Pakistan, the United States, and the European Union, has carried out attacks on security forces and infrastructure across the Makran region. The insurgency is not confined to the district headquarters area; the more remote and sparsely policed tehsils of [Gichk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gichk_Tehsil) and [Paroom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paroom_Tehsil) are also affected, with the latter's position along the Iranian border adding a further dimension to security challenges. In February 2022, the BLA's Majeed Brigade launched simultaneous assaults on military camps in Panjgur and [Nushki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nushki_District), described by analysts as a tactical shift towards temporarily seizing and holding territory.[^cite_note-16]

## Transport

[Panjgur Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panjgur_Airport) (IATA: PJG, ICAO: OPPG) is a domestic airport serving the district. The district is connected to [Quetta](93764.md) via the [N-85 National Highway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-85_National_Highway); long-distance bus services along this route are the primary mode of travel for most residents travelling to larger cities. In the western tehsils, informal cross-border movement with Iran via the [Paroom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paroom_Tehsil) area is a longstanding feature of local life, though no formal border crossing point is currently operational in the district.[^cite_note-17]
