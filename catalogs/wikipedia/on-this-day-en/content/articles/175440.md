**Medical cannabis**, **medicinal cannabis** or **medical marijuana** (**MMJ**) includes [cannabis products](1481886.md) and [cannabinoid molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabinoid) that are [prescribed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prescription_drug) by [physicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physician) for their patients.[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4] The use of cannabis as medicine has a long history, but has not been as rigorously tested as other medicinal plants due to legal and governmental restrictions, resulting in [limited research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_cannabis_research) to define the safety and efficacy of using cannabis to treat diseases.[^cite_note-5]

There is mixed and inconclusive evidence on the benefits of cannabis-based medicines, frequent mild adverse effects, and generally low-to-moderate quality of evidence.[^cite_note-pratt-6] Cannabis-based medicines may offer modest relief for [chronic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronic_pain), especially [neuropathic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuropathic_pain), pain and slight improvements in function and sleep in chronic pain patients, but evidence is limited and inconsistent; mild harms may outweigh the benefit and placebo effects may influence trial outcomes.[^cite_note-pratt-6][^cite_note-JAMA2015-7][^cite_note-wang2021-8][^cite_note-\:6-9] Recent clinical reviews have concluded that evidence remains insufficient to support the use of cannabis or cannabinoids for most medical indications, emphasizing that modest potential benefits must be weighed against established risks.[^cite_note-10]

Short-term use increases the risk of minor and major adverse effects.[^cite_note-JAMA2015-7] Common side effects include [dizziness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dizziness), feeling tired, vomiting, and [hallucinations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallucination).[^cite_note-JAMA2015-7] Long-term effects of cannabis are not clear.[^cite_note-JAMA2015-7] Concerns include memory and cognition problems, risk of addiction, [schizophrenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schizophrenia) in young people, and the risk of children taking it by accident.[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11]

&#x20;Many cultures have used cannabis for therapeutic purposes for thousands of years.[^cite_note-BenAmar2006-12] Some American medical organizations have requested removal of cannabis from the list of [Schedule I controlled substances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_Substances_Act#Schedule_I), emphasizing that rescheduling would enable more extensive research and regulatory oversight to ensure safe access.[^cite_note-ANA-13][^cite_note-AAFP-14] Others oppose its legalization, such as the [American Academy of Pediatrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Pediatrics).[^cite_note-AAP-15] Countries that [allow the medical use of whole-plant cannabis](168917.md) include Argentina, Australia, Canada, Chile, Colombia, Germany, Greece, Israel, Italy, the Netherlands, Peru, Poland, Portugal, Spain, and Uruguay. In the United States, 38 states and the District of Columbia have legalized cannabis for medical purposes, beginning with the passage of California's [Proposition 215](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proposition_215) in 1996.[^cite_note-NCSL-16]

## Classification

In the U.S., the [National Institute on Drug Abuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_on_Drug_Abuse) defines medical cannabis as "using the whole, unprocessed marijuana plant or its basic extracts to treat symptoms of illness and other conditions".[^cite_note-nida-17]

A cannabis plant includes more than 400 different chemicals, of which about 70 are [cannabinoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabinoid).[^cite_note-Consumer_Reports_April_2016-18] In comparison, typical government-approved medications contain only one or two chemicals.[^cite_note-Consumer_Reports_April_2016-18] The number of active chemicals in cannabis is one reason why treatment with cannabis is difficult to classify and study.[^cite_note-Consumer_Reports_April_2016-18]

A 2014 review stated that the variations in ratio of CBD-to-THC in botanical and pharmaceutical preparations determines the therapeutic vs psychoactive effects (CBD attenuates THC's psychoactive effects[^cite_note-Schubart_et_al.2014-19]) of cannabis products.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-20]

## Medical uses and research

Main article: [Medical cannabis research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_cannabis_research)



*Cannabis* as illustrated in Köhler's *Book of Medicinal Plants*, 1897

A scoping review of 72 systematic reviews found mixed and inconclusive evidence on the benefits of cannabis-based medicines, frequent mild adverse effects, and generally low-to-moderate [quality of evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence-based_medicine).[^cite_note-pratt-6]

Low quality evidence suggests its use for reducing [nausea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiemetic) during [chemotherapy](7172.md), improving appetite in [HIV/AIDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS), improving sleep, and improving [tics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tic) in [Tourette syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourette_syndrome).[^cite_note-JAMA2015-7] When usual treatments are ineffective, [cannabinoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabinoid) have also been recommended for [anorexia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anorexia), [arthritis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthritis), [glaucoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaucoma).[^cite_note-Sachs_et_al._2015-21]

It is unclear whether American states might be able to mitigate the adverse effects of the [opioid epidemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opioid_epidemic) by prescribing medical cannabis as an alternative pain management drug.[^cite_note-22]

Cannabis should not be used in [pregnancy](1771587.md).[^cite_note-23]

### Pain

Cannabis-based medicines may provide modest relief for chronic pain, particularly neuropathic pain, as well as small improvements in physical function and sleep in chronic pain patients.[^cite_note-wang2021-8][^cite_note-aviram-24][^cite_note-pmid356670662-25] Cannabis-based medicines show limited and inconsistent evidence for pain relief, with mild harms commonly reported that may outweigh potential benefits.[^cite_note-pratt-6]

Short-term use of oral or sublingual cannabis, particularly high-THC synthetic or comparable-THC extracted products, may modestly reduce chronic pain but increases risks of dizziness, sedation, and other adverse effects, with limited evidence on long-term outcomes.[^cite_note-pmid356670662-25]

[Placebo effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placebo) contribute to pain reduction in cannabinoid clinical trials, and while media attention is high, it does not directly correlate with clinical outcomes but may influence future expectations and trial results.[^cite_note-\:6-9]

### Insomnia

Cannabis administration does not have a noted effect on sleep. Cannabis withdrawal is associated with a reduction in hours spent asleep, an increase in the duration of [sleep onset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleep_onset), and [rapid eye movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapid_eye_movement_sleep) rebounds.[^cite_note-26]

### Nausea and vomiting

Medical cannabis is somewhat effective in [chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemotherapy-induced_nausea_and_vomiting) (CINV)[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11][^cite_note-Sachs_et_al._2015-21] and may be a reasonable option in those who do not improve following preferential treatment.[^cite_note-Grotenhermen2012-27] Comparative studies have found cannabinoids to be more effective than some conventional antiemetics such as [prochlorperazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prochlorperazine), [promethazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promethazine), and [metoclopramide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metoclopramide) in controlling CINV,[^cite_note-Bowels2012-28] but these are used less frequently because of side effects including dizziness, dysphoria, and hallucinations.[^cite_note-Wang2008-29][^cite_note-Jordan2007-30] Long-term cannabis use may cause nausea and vomiting, a condition known as [cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabinoid_hyperemesis_syndrome) (CHS).[^cite_note-Nicolson2012-31]

A 2016 [Cochrane review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochrane_Collaboration) said that cannabinoids were "probably effective" in treating chemotherapy-induced nausea in children, but with a high side-effect profile (mainly drowsiness, dizziness, altered moods, and increased appetite). Less common side effects were "ocular problems, orthostatic hypotension, muscle twitching, pruritus, vagueness, hallucinations, lightheadedness and dry mouth".[^cite_note-32]

### HIV/AIDS

Evidence is lacking for both efficacy and safety of cannabis and cannabinoids in treating patients with [HIV/AIDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS) or for [anorexia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anorexia_nervosa) associated with AIDS. As of 2013, current studies suffer from the effects of bias, small sample size, and lack of long-term data.[^cite_note-Lutge2013-33]

### Neurological conditions

Cannabis' efficacy is not clear in treating neurological problems, including [multiple sclerosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_sclerosis) (MS) and movement problems.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-20] Evidence also suggests that oral cannabis extract is effective for reducing patient-centered measures of spasticity.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-20] A trial of cannabis is deemed to be a reasonable option if other treatments have not been effective.[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11] Its use for MS is approved in ten countries.[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11][^cite_note-Clark2011-34] A 2012 review found no problems with tolerance, abuse, or addiction.[^cite_note-35] In the United States, [cannabidiol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabidiol), one of the cannabinoids found in the marijuana plant, has been approved for treating two severe forms of epilepsy, [Lennox–Gastaut syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lennox%E2%80%93Gastaut_syndrome) and [Dravet syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dravet_syndrome).[^cite_note-\:4-36]

### Mental health

See also: [Cannabis-induced psychotic disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis-induced_psychotic_disorder)

Evidence for cannabis and cannabinoids in mental health treatment is limited and low-quality; they show no benefit for most mental disorders, increase general adverse events, and are not justified for routine use.[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-\:7-38] Risks (including [cannabis-induced psychotic disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis-induced_psychotic_disorder), worsening bipolar symptoms, and [cannabis use disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_use_disorder)) are substantial, especially in vulnerable populations.[^cite_note-\:7-38]

The relationship between cannabis use and [anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety) symptoms is complex, and while some users report relief, the overall evidence from observational studies and clinical trials remains inconclusive.[^cite_note-39]

Cannabis use, especially at high doses, is associated with a higher risk of psychosis, particularly in individuals with a genetic predisposition to psychotic disorders like schizophrenia. Some studies have shown that cannabis can trigger a temporary psychotic episode, which may increase the risk of developing a psychotic disorder later.[^cite_note-\:5-40]

The impact of cannabis on depression is less clear. Some studies suggest a potential increase in depression risk among adolescents who use cannabis, though findings are inconsistent across studies.[^cite_note-\:5-40]

## Adverse effects



American medical hashish

### Medical use

There is insufficient data to draw strong conclusions about the safety of medical cannabis.[^cite_note-oxpain-41] Typically, adverse effects of medical cannabis use are not serious;[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11] they include tiredness, dizziness, increased appetite, and cardiovascular and psychoactive effects. Other effects can include impaired short-term memory; impaired motor coordination; altered judgment; and paranoia or psychosis at high doses.[^cite_note-42] Tolerance to these effects develops over a period of days or weeks. The amount of cannabis normally used for medicinal purposes is not believed to cause any permanent cognitive impairment in adults, though long-term treatment in adolescents should be weighed carefully as they are more susceptible to these impairments. Withdrawal symptoms are rarely a problem with controlled medical administration of cannabinoids. The ability to drive vehicles or to operate machinery may be impaired until a tolerance is developed.[^cite_note-Grotenhermen2012-27] Although supporters of medical cannabis say that it is safe,[^cite_note-oxpain-41] further research is required to assess the long-term safety of its use.[^cite_note-Wang2008-29][^cite_note-barceloux866931-43]

### Cognitive effects

Further information: [long-term effects of cannabis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long-term_effects_of_cannabis)

Recreational use of cannabis is associated with cognitive deficits, especially for those who begin to use cannabis in adolescence. As of 2021 there is a lack of research into long-term cognitive effects of medical use of cannabis, but one 12-month observational study reported that "MC patients demonstrated significant improvements on measures of [executive function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_function) and clinical state over the course of 12 months".[^cite_note-44]

### Impact on psychosis

Main article: [Cannabis-induced psychotic disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis-induced_psychotic_disorder)

Exposure to THC can cause [cannabis-induced psychotic disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis-induced_psychotic_disorder) (CIPD) in healthy individuals and people susceptible to schizophrenia.[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46]

A 2007 meta analysis concluded that cannabis use reduced the average age of onset of psychosis by 2.7 years relative to non-cannabis use.[^cite_note-47] A 2005 meta analysis concluded that adolescent use of cannabis increases the risk of psychosis, and that the risk is dose-related.[^cite_note-48] A 2004 literature review on the subject concluded that cannabis use is associated with a two-fold increase in the risk of psychosis, but that cannabis use is "neither necessary nor sufficient" to cause psychosis.[^cite_note-Arseneault2004-49] A French review from 2009 came to a conclusion that cannabis use, particularly that before age 15, was a factor in the development of schizophrenic disorders.[^cite_note-Laqueille-50] A 2026 meta-analysis linked cannabis use to an earlier age of psychosis onset.[^cite_note-51]

## Pharmacology

The [genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genus) *[Cannabis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis)* contains two species which produce useful amounts of psychoactive cannabinoids: *[Cannabis indica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_indica)* and *[Cannabis sativa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_sativa)*, which are listed as Schedule I medicinal plants in the US;[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11] a third species, *[Cannabis ruderalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_ruderalis)*, has few psychogenic properties.[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11] Cannabis contains more than 460 compounds;[^cite_note-BenAmar2006-12] at least 80 of these are [cannabinoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabinoid)[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53] – [chemical compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound) that interact with [cannabinoid receptors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabinoid_receptor) in the brain.[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11] As of 2012, more than 20 cannabinoids were being studied by the U.S. FDA.[^cite_note-Svrakic2012-54]

The most psychoactive cannabinoid found in the cannabis plant is [tetrahydrocannabinol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahydrocannabinol) (or delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol, commonly known as THC).[^cite_note-BenAmar2006-12] Other cannabinoids include [delta-8-tetrahydrocannabinol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta-8-tetrahydrocannabinol), [cannabidiol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabidiol) (CBD), [cannabinol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabinol) (CBN), [cannabicyclol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabicyclol) (CBL), [cannabichromene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabichromene) (CBC) and [cannabigerol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabigerol) (CBG); they have less psychotropic effects than THC, but may play a role in the overall effect of cannabis.[^cite_note-BenAmar2006-12] The most studied are THC, CBD and CBN.[^cite_note-Gordon2013-55]

CB1 and CB2 are the primary cannabinoid receptors responsible for several of the effects of cannabinoids, although other receptors may play a role as well. Both belong to a group of receptors called G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs). CB1 receptors are found in very high levels in the brain and are thought to be responsible for psychoactive effects.[^cite_note-56] CB2 receptors are found peripherally throughout the body and are thought to modulate pain and inflammation.[^cite_note-57]

### Absorption

Cannabinoid absorption is dependent on its route of administration.

Inhaled and vaporized THC have similar absorption profiles to smoked THC, with a [bioavailability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioavailability) ranging from 10 to 35%. Oral administration has the lowest bioavailability of approximately 6%, variable absorption depending on the vehicle used, and the longest time to peak plasma levels (2 to 6 hours) compared to smoked or vaporized THC.[^cite_note-\:0-58]

Similar to THC, CBD has poor oral bioavailability, approximately 6%. The low bioavailability is largely attributed to significant first-pass metabolism in the liver and erratic absorption from the gastrointestinal tract. However, oral administration of CBD has a faster time to peak concentrations (2 hours) than THC.[^cite_note-\:0-58]

Due to the poor bioavailability of oral preparations, alternative routes of administration have been studied, including sublingual and rectal. These alternative formulations maximize bioavailability and reduce first-pass metabolism. Sublingual administration in rabbits yielded bioavailability of 16% and time to peak concentration of 4 hours.[^cite_note-59] Rectal administration in monkeys doubled bioavailability to 13.5% and achieved peak blood concentrations within 1 to 8 hours after administration.[^cite_note-\:1-60]

### Distribution

Like cannabinoid absorption, distribution is also dependent on route of administration. Smoking and inhalation of vaporized cannabis have better absorption than do other routes of administration, and therefore also have more predictable distribution.[^cite_note-\:1-60][^cite_note-\:3-61] THC is highly protein bound once absorbed, with only 3% found unbound in the plasma. It distributes rapidly to highly vascularized organs such as the heart, lungs, liver, spleen, and kidneys, as well as to various glands. Low levels can be detected in the brain, testes, and unborn fetuses, all of which are protected from systemic circulation via barriers.[^cite_note-62] THC further distributes into fatty tissues a few days after administration due to its high lipophilicity, and is found deposited in the spleen and fat after redistribution.[^cite_note-\:3-61][^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64]

### Metabolism

Delta-9-THC is the primary molecule responsible for the effects of cannabis. Delta-9-THC is metabolized in the liver and turns into 11-OH-THC.[^cite_note-\:2-65] 11-OH-THC is the first metabolic product in this pathway. Both Delta-9-THC and 11-OH-THC are psychoactive. The metabolism of THC into 11-OH-THC plays a part in the heightened psychoactive effects of edible cannabis.[^cite_note-66]

Next, 11-OH-THC is metabolized in the liver into 11-COOH-THC, which is the second metabolic product of THC.[^cite_note-67] 11-COOH-THC is not psychoactive.[^cite_note-\:2-65]

Ingestion of edible cannabis products lead to a slower onset of effect than the inhalation of it because the THC travels to the liver first through the blood before it travels to the rest of the body. Inhaled cannabis can result in THC going directly to the brain, where it then travels from the brain back to the liver in recirculation for metabolism.[^cite_note-\:2-65] Eventually, both routes of metabolism result in the metabolism of psychoactive THC to inactive 11-COOH-THC.

### Excretion

Due to substantial metabolism of THC and CBD, their metabolites are excreted mostly via [feces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feces), rather than by urine.[^cite_note-\:0-58][^cite_note-devinsky-68] After delta-9-THC is hydroxylated into 11-OH-THC via CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP3A4, it undergoes phase II metabolism into more than 30 metabolites, a majority of which are products of [glucuronidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucuronidation). Approximately 65% of THC is excreted in feces and 25% in the urine, while the remaining 10% is excreted by other means.[^cite_note-\:0-58] The terminal half-life of THC is 25 to 36 hours,[^cite_note-69] whereas for CBD it is 18 to 32 hours.[^cite_note-devinsky-68]

CBD is hydroxylated by P450 liver enzymes into 7-OH-CBD. Its metabolites are products of primarily CYP2C19 and CYP3A4 activity, with potential activity of CYP1A1, CYP1A2, CYP2C9, and CYP2D6.[^cite_note-70] Similar to delta-9-THC, a majority of CBD is excreted in feces and some in the urine.[^cite_note-\:0-58] The terminal half-life is approximately 18–32 hours.[^cite_note-71]

## Administration



Illustrating various forms of medicinal cannabis

[Smoking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoking) has been the means of administration of cannabis for many users, but it is not suitable for the use of cannabis as a medicine.[^cite_note-CurtisClarke2009-72] It was the most common method of medical cannabis consumption in the US as of 2013.[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11] It is difficult to predict the pharmacological response to cannabis because concentration of cannabinoids varies widely, as there are different ways of preparing it for consumption (smoked, applied as oils, eaten, infused into other foods, or drunk) and a lack of production controls.[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11] The potential for adverse effects from smoke inhalation makes smoking a less viable option than oral preparations.[^cite_note-CurtisClarke2009-72] [Cannabis vaporizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaporizer_\(cannabis\)) have gained popularity because of a perception among users that fewer harmful chemicals are ingested when components are inhaled via aerosol rather than smoke.[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11] Cannabinoid medicines are available in pill form ([dronabinol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dronabinol) and [nabilone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabilone)) and liquid extracts formulated into an oromucosal spray ([nabiximols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabiximols)).[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11] Oral preparations are "problematic due to the uptake of cannabinoids into fatty tissue, from which they are released slowly, and the significant first-pass liver metabolism, which breaks down Δ9THC and contributes further to the variability of plasma concentrations".[^cite_note-CurtisClarke2009-72]

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has not approved smoked cannabis for any condition or disease, as it deems that evidence is lacking concerning safety and efficacy.[^cite_note-FDA2006-73] The FDA issued a 2006 advisory against *smoked* medical cannabis stating: "marijuana has a high potential for abuse, has no currently accepted medical use in treatment in the United States, and has a lack of accepted safety for use under medical supervision."[^cite_note-FDA2006-73]

## History

Main article: [History of medical cannabis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_medical_cannabis)

### Ancient

Cannabis, called *má* [麻](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E9%BA%BB) (meaning "hemp; cannabis; numbness") or *dàmá* [大麻](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%A4%A7%E9%BA%BB) (with "big; great") in Chinese, was used in Taiwan for fiber starting about 10,000 years ago.[^cite_note-74] The botanist [Hui-lin Li](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hui-lin_Li) wrote that in China, "The use of Cannabis in medicine was probably a very early development. Since ancient humans used hemp seed as food, it was quite natural for them to also discover the medicinal properties of the plant."[^cite_note-75] Emperor [Shen-Nung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shen-Nung), who was also a pharmacologist, wrote a book on treatment methods in 2737 BCE that included the medical benefits of cannabis. He recommended the substance for many ailments, including constipation, gout, rheumatism, and absent-mindedness.[^cite_note-autogenerated2-76] Cannabis is one of the [50 "fundamental" herbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/50_fundamental_herbs) in [traditional Chinese medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_medicine).[^cite_note-Wong-77]

The [Ebers Papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebers_Papyrus) (c. 1550 BCE) from [Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) describes medical cannabis.[^cite_note-ebers-78] The ancient Egyptians used hemp (cannabis) in [suppositories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suppositories) for relieving the pain of [hemorrhoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemorrhoid).[^cite_note-79]

Surviving texts from [ancient India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_India) confirm that cannabis' psychoactive properties were recognized, and doctors used it for treating a variety of illnesses and ailments, including insomnia, headaches, gastrointestinal disorders, and pain, including during childbirth.[^cite_note-80]

The [Ancient Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greeks) used cannabis to dress wounds and sores on their horses,[^cite_note-hawthorne-81] and in humans, dried leaves of cannabis were used to treat nose bleeds, and cannabis seeds were used to expel tapeworms.[^cite_note-hawthorne-81]

In the [medieval Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age), [Arabic physicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_medicine) made use of the [diuretic](40496970.md), [antiemetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiemetic), [antiepileptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiepileptic), [anti-inflammatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-inflammatory), [analgesic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analgesic) and [antipyretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipyretic) properties of *[Cannabis sativa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_sativa)*, and used it extensively as medication from the 8th to 18th centuries.[^cite_note-82]

### Landrace strains



Evolution of cultivated cannabis strains. The [cultivar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultivar), *Cannabis ruderalis*, still grows wild today.

Cannabis seeds may have been used for food, rituals or religious practices in ancient Europe and China.[^cite_note-Holland_2018-83]<sup>: 19–22 </sup> Harvesting the plant led to the spread of cannabis throughout [Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasia) about 10,000 to 5,000 years ago, with further distribution to the Middle East and Africa about 2,000 to 500 years ago.[^cite_note-Holland_2018-83]<sup>: 18–19 </sup> A [landrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landrace) [strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_\(biology\)) of cannabis developed over centuries.[^cite_note-84] They are cultivars of the plant that originated in one specific region.

Widely cultivated strains of cannabis, such as "Afghani" or "Hindu Kush", are indigenous to the Pakistan and Afghanistan regions, while "Durban Poison" is native to Africa.[^cite_note-Holland_2018-83]<sup>: 45–48 </sup> There are approximately 16 landrace strains of cannabis identified from Pakistan, Jamaica, Africa, Mexico, Central America and Asia.[^cite_note-85]

### Modern

An Irish physician, [William Brooke O'Shaughnessy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Brooke_O'Shaughnessy), is credited with introducing cannabis to Western medicine.[^cite_note-Mack-86] O'Shaughnessy discovered cannabis in the 1830s while living abroad in India, where he conducted numerous experiments investigating the drug's medical utility (noting in particular its [analgesic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analgesic) and [anticonvulsant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticonvulsant) effects).[^cite_note-Booth-87] He returned to England with a supply of cannabis in 1842, after which its use spread through Europe and the United States.[^cite_note-Grinspoon-88] In 1845 French physician [Jacques-Joseph Moreau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques-Joseph_Moreau) published a book about the use of cannabis in psychiatry.[^cite_note-89] In 1850 cannabis was entered into the [United States Pharmacopeia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Pharmacopeia).[^cite_note-Booth-87] An anecdotal report of *[Cannabis indica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_indica)* as a treatment for [tetanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetanus) appeared in [Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American) in 1880.[^cite_note-90]

The use of cannabis in medicine began to decline by the end of the 19th century, due to difficulty in controlling dosages and the rise in popularity of synthetic and [opium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opium)-derived drugs.[^cite_note-Grinspoon-88] Also, the advent of the hypodermic [syringe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syringe) allowed these drugs to be injected for immediate effect, in contrast to cannabis which is not water-soluble and therefore cannot be injected.[^cite_note-Grinspoon-88]

In the United States, the medical use of cannabis further declined with the passage of the [Marihuana Tax Act of 1937](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marihuana_Tax_Act_of_1937), which imposed new regulations and fees on physicians prescribing cannabis.[^cite_note-91] Cannabis was removed from the U.S. Pharmacopeia in 1941, and officially banned for any use with the passage of the [Controlled Substances Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_Substances_Act) of 1970.[^cite_note-Grinspoon-88]

Cannabis began to attract renewed interest as medicine in the 1970s and 1980s, in particular due to its use by cancer and AIDS patients who reported relief from the effects of [chemotherapy](7172.md) and [wasting syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cachexia).[^cite_note-92] In 1996, [California](5407.md) became the first U.S. state to legalize medical cannabis in defiance of federal law.[^cite_note-93] In 2001, Canada became the first country to adopt a system regulating the medical use of cannabis.[^cite_note-94]

#### International law

See also: [Removal of cannabis and cannabis resin from Schedule IV of the Single Convention on narcotic drugs, 1961](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Removal_of_cannabis_and_cannabis_resin_from_Schedule_IV_of_the_Single_Convention_on_narcotic_drugs,_1961)

Cannabis and its derivatives are subject to regulation under three [United Nations](31769.md) [drug control treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_drug_control_conventions): the 1961 [Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_Convention_on_Narcotic_Drugs), the 1971 [Convention on Psychotropic Substances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_on_Psychotropic_Substances), and the 1988 [Convention Against Illicit Traffic in Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Convention_Against_Illicit_Traffic_in_Narcotic_Drugs_and_Psychotropic_Substances).[^cite_note-128]

Cannabis and cannabis resin are classified as a Schedule I drug under the Single Convention treaty, meaning that medical use is considered "indispensable for the relief of pain and suffering" but that it is considered to be an addictive medication with risks of abuse.[^cite_note-129] Countries have an obligation to provide access and sufficient availability of drugs listed in Schedule I for the purposes of medical uses.[^cite_note-130][^cite_note-131]

Prior to December 2020 cannabis and cannabis resin were also included in Schedule IV, a more restrictive level of control, which is for only the most dangerous drugs such as heroin and fentanyl.[^cite_note-132] They were removed after an independent scientific assessment by the [World Health Organization](33583.md) in 2018–2019.

Member nations of the [UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Commission_on_Narcotic_Drugs) voted 27–25 to remove it from Schedule IV on 2 December 2020,[^cite_note-133] following a [World Health Organization](33583.md) recommendation for removal in January 2019.[^cite_note-134][^cite_note-135]

#### United States

See also: [Medical cannabis in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_cannabis_in_the_United_States)

In the United States, the use of cannabis for medical purposes is legal in 38 states, four out of five permanently inhabited [U.S. territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territories_of_the_United_States), and the [District of Columbia](108956.md).[^cite_note-NCSL-16] An additional 10 states have more restrictive laws allowing the use of low-THC products.[^cite_note-NCSL-16] Cannabis remains illegal at the federal level under the [Controlled Substances Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_Substances_Act), which classifies it as a Schedule I drug with a high potential for abuse and no accepted medical use. In December 2014, however, the [Rohrabacher–Farr amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rohrabacher%E2%80%93Farr_amendment) was signed into law, prohibiting the [Justice Department](52563.md) from prosecuting individuals acting in accordance with state medical cannabis laws.[^cite_note-136]

In the US, the FDA has approved two oral cannabinoids for use as medicine in 1985:[^cite_note-137] [dronabinol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahydrocannabinol#Marinol) (pure delta-9-THC; brand name Marinol) and [nabilone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabilone) (a [synthetic neocannabinoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_cannabinoids); brand name Cesamet).[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11] In the US, they are both listed as Schedule II, indicating high potential for side effects and addiction.[^cite_note-Svrakic2012-54][^cite_note-138]

### Economics

#### Distribution



[Medical marijuana dispensary in Denver, Colorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispensary#Cannabis_\(North_America\))&#x20;

The method of obtaining medical cannabis varies by region and by legislation. In the US, most consumers grow their own or buy it from [cannabis dispensaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_dispensaries_in_the_United_States) in states where it is legal.[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11][^cite_note-BaltSun-139] [Marijuana vending machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marijuana_vending_machine) for selling or dispensing cannabis are in use in the United States and are planned to be used in Canada.[^cite_note-FirstForeignMarket-140] In 2014, the startup Meadow began offering on-demand delivery of medical marijuana in the San Francisco Bay Area, through their mobile app.[^cite_note-141]

Almost 70% of medical cannabis is exported from the United Kingdom, according to a 2017 United Nations report, with much of the remaining amount coming from Canada and the Netherlands.[^cite_note-142]

#### Insurance

In the United States, health insurance companies may not pay for a medical marijuana prescription as the [Food and Drug Administration](11632.md) must approve any substance for medicinal purposes. Before this can happen, the FDA must first permit the study of the medical benefits and drawbacks of the substance, which it has not done since it was placed on Schedule I of the Controlled Substances Act in 1970. Therefore, all expenses incurred fulfilling a medical marijuana prescription will possibly be incurred as out-of-pocket.[^cite_note-143] However, the [New Mexico Court of Appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Mexico_Court_of_Appeals) has ruled that [workers' compensation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workers'_compensation) insurance must pay for prescribed marijuana as part of the state's Medical Cannabis Program.[^cite_note-144]

### Positions of medical organizations

Medical organizations that have issued statements in support of allowing access to medical cannabis include the [American Nurses Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Nurses_Association),[^cite_note-ANA-13] [American Public Health Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Public_Health_Association),[^cite_note-145] [American Medical Student Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Medical_Student_Association),[^cite_note-146] [National Multiple Sclerosis Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Multiple_Sclerosis_Society),[^cite_note-147] [Epilepsy Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epilepsy_Foundation),[^cite_note-148] and [Leukemia & Lymphoma Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leukemia_&_Lymphoma_Society).[^cite_note-149]

Organizations that oppose the legalization of medical cannabis include the [American Academy of Pediatrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Pediatrics) (AAP)[^cite_note-AAP-15] and [American Psychiatric Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Psychiatric_Association).[^cite_note-150] However, the AAP also supports rescheduling for the purpose of facilitating research.[^cite_note-AAP-15]

The [American Medical Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Medical_Association)[^cite_note-151] and [American College of Physicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_College_of_Physicians)[^cite_note-152] do not take a position on the legalization of medical cannabis, but have called for the Schedule I classification to be reviewed. The [American Academy of Family Physicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Family_Physicians)[^cite_note-AAFP-14] and [American Society of Addiction Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Society_of_Addiction_Medicine)[^cite_note-153] also do not take a position, but do support rescheduling to better facilitate research. The [American Heart Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Heart_Association) says that "many of the concerning health implications of cannabis include cardiovascular diseases" but that it supports rescheduling to allow "more nuanced ... marijuana legislation and regulation" and to "reflect the existing science behind cannabis".[^cite_note-154] The [American Cancer Society](522681.md)[^cite_note-ACS-155] and [American Psychological Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Psychological_Association)[^cite_note-APA-156] have noted the obstacles that exist for conducting research on cannabis, and have called on the federal government to better enable scientific study of the drug.

[Cancer Research UK](615793.md) say that while cannabis is being studied for therapeutic potential, "claims that there is solid "proof" that cannabis or cannabinoids can cure cancer is highly misleading to patients and their families, and builds a false picture of the state of progress in this area".[^cite_note-157]

- [Nabilone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabilone) is the INN for a synthetic cannabinoid analog (not present in *[Cannabis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis)* plants).

[Nabiximols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabiximols) is the generic name (but not recognized as an INN) of a mixture of Cannabidiol and Dronabinol. Its most common form is the oromucosal spray derived from two strains of *Cannabis sativa* and containing THC and CBD traded under the brand name Sativex®.[^cite_note-Svrakic2012-54] It is not approved in the United States, but is approved in several European countries, Canada, and New Zealand as of 2013.[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11]

As an [antiemetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiemetic), these medications are usually used when conventional treatment for nausea and vomiting associated with cancer chemotherapy fail to work.[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11]

[Nabiximols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabiximols) is used for treatment of spasticity associated with MS when other therapies have not worked, and when an initial trial demonstrates "meaningful improvement".[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11] Trials for FDA approval in the US are underway.[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11] It is also approved in several European countries for overactive bladder and [vomiting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emesis).[^cite_note-Svrakic2012-54] When sold under the trade name Sativex as a mouth spray, the prescribed daily dose in Sweden delivers a maximum of 32.4 mg of [THC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/THC) and 30 mg of [CBD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabidiol); mild to moderate dizziness is common during the first few weeks.[^cite_note-163]

Relative to inhaled consumption, peak concentration of oral THC is delayed, and it may be difficult to determine optimal dosage because of variability in patient absorption.[^cite_note-Borgelt2013-11]

In 1964, Albert Lockhart and [Manley West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manley_West) began studying the health effects of traditional cannabis use in [Jamaican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaicans) communities. They developed, and in 1987 gained permission to market, the pharmaceutical "Canasol", one of the first cannabis extracts.[^cite_note-FFY-164]
