The **Royal Palace** ([Norwegian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_language): *Slottet*, [lit.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation) 'the Palace' or *Det kongelige slott*) in [Oslo](22309.md) is the official residence of the [Norwegian monarch](4154892.md). It was built in the first half of the 19th century as the Norwegian residence of the [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_France)-born [Charles XIV John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_XIV_John), who reigned as king of [Norway and Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_between_Sweden_and_Norway).

The palace is located at the end of [Karl Johans gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Johans_gate) in central Oslo and is surrounded by the [Palace Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_Park) with the [Palace Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slottsplassen) in the front.

## History

Until the completion of the palace, Norwegian royalty resided in [Paléet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pal%C3%A9et), the magnificent townhouse in [Christiania](22309.md) that the wealthy merchant [Bernt Anker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernt_Anker) bequeathed to the State in 1805 to be used as a royal residence. During the last years of the [union with Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denmark%E2%80%93Norway) Paléet was used by the [viceroys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viceroy) of Norway, and in 1814 by the first king of independent Norway, [Christian Frederick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_VIII). King [Charles III John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_XIV_John) of the [House of Bernadotte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Bernadotte) resided there as crown prince and later as king during his frequent visits to his Norwegian capital.

### Construction

Charles John chose the site for the permanent royal palace on the western side of Christiania in 1821 and commissioned the officer and inexperienced architect, Danish-born [Hans Linstow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Linstow), to design the building. The [Parliament](172897.md) approved the stipulated cost of 150 000 [speciedaler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_rigsdaler) to be financed by the sale of government bonds. Work on the site started in 1824, and on 1 October 1825 Charles John laid down the foundation stone beneath the [altar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altar) of the future [palace chapel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slottskapellet_\(Oslo\)). Linstow originally planned a building of only two storeys with projecting wings on both sides of the main façade.[^cite_note-3]

The costly foundation works caused the budget to be exceeded, and the building stopped in 1827, only to be resumed in 1833. In the meantime, the Storting refused additional grants as a demonstration against the King's unpopular efforts to establish a closer union between his two kingdoms. In 1833, Linstow produced a less costly project without the projecting wings, but with a third storey as compensation. Improved relations with the king made the Storting grant the necessary funds to complete the building. The roof was laid in 1836, and the interiors were finished during the late 1840s.[^cite_note-4]

### The Royal Palace Chapel

Main article: [Slottskapellet (Oslo)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slottskapellet_\(Oslo\))

The Royal Palace contains its own chapel, consecrated in 1844. The [Church of Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Norway) is responsible for the religious services taking place there. It is the scene of many events of the Norwegian royal family like the royal [baptisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptism) and [confirmations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation). There are also concerts of [church music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_music) and [chamber music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamber_music).[^cite_note-rhn-5]

### During the Bernadotte dynasty

King Charles John never had the pleasure of residing in his palace before he died in 1844, and its first occupants were his son [Oscar I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_I_of_Sweden) and his queen [Josephine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephine_of_Leuchtenberg). It was soon found that the royal family needed a more spacious residence, and the wings facing the garden were extended. Before the official inauguration in 1849, the central [colonnade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonnade) that had been axed in 1833 was reintroduced, and the provisional steep roof was replaced by a more elegant and more expensive flat roof.

The next Bernadotte kings [Charles IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_XV) and [Oscar II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_II) continued to use the royal palace in Christiania, but spent most of their time in [Stockholm](26741.md). King Oscar's wife, [Sophia of Nassau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophia_of_Nassau), preferred to spend summers in Norway, but mostly stayed at the country manor [Skinnarbøl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skinnarb%C3%B8l) near the Swedish border for the sake of her health. Oscar II was absent from the palace during 1905, the year of the [dissolution of the union with Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_union_between_Norway_and_Sweden), but his son, then Crown Prince [Gustaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustaf_V), paid two short visits in his vain attempts to save the union.

### Permanent royal residence

The Bernadotte dynasty abdicated their Norwegian throne in 1905 and were succeeded by Prince Carl of Denmark, who took the name of [Haakon VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haakon_VII) when he accepted his election as king of a completely independent Norway. Haakon became the first monarch to use the palace permanently and the palace was therefore refurbished for two years before he, Queen [Maud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maud_of_Wales) and Crown Prince [Olav](66068.md) could move in. King Haakon would be the first monarch to greet the children's parade on the palace balcony during the [Norwegian Constitution Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_Day_\(Norway\)) celebration in Oslo. It was also King Haakon VII who in 1905 introduced the tradition of weekly meetings with the [Council of State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_State_\(Norway\)), a tradition which is still practiced with the meetings always being held in the palace's Council Chamber where the monarch's [throne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrones_of_Norway) is located.

### Modernization and public access

During the reign and residence of King [Olav V](66068.md) from 1957 to 1991, there was little funds for renovation, something the poorly built original structure direly needed. King Olav would therefore mostly reside at [Skaugum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skaugum) and the [Bygdøy Royal Estate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bygd%C3%B8y_Royal_Estate), but relocated to the palace in 1968 when he gave the Skaugum estate as a wedding gift to his son Crown Prince [Harald](66069.md) and his bride Crown Princess [Sonja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Sonja_of_Norway). Shortly after his ascension, King Harald V started a comprehensive renovation project of the palace. The renovations and improvements, all made by [*Statsbygg*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Directorate_of_Public_Construction_and_Property), included new fire alarm systems, the construction of new bathrooms, kitchens, offices and a general restructuring of the palace. The King was criticized because of the amount of money needed to bring the palace up to a satisfactory state even if much of this went to rectify construction deficits from a century and a half ago. With the renovations completed, the King and Queen relocated from Skaugum to the palace in 2001 as the Skaugum estate was to become the new home of the then Crown Prince [Haakon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haakon,_Crown_Prince_of_Norway) and his family.

The palace, like all royal residence in Norway, is guarded by [His Majesty The King's Guard; the Royal Guards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/His_Majesty_the_King's_Guard). Since public tours began in 2002, the general public has been able to view and appreciate the renovation and splendor that the palace now boasts. The daily [changing of the guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guard_mounting) has also become a popular tourist attraction in recent years.[^cite_note-kosloh-1][^cite_note-rooslo-6]

## The Palace Park

Main article: [Palace Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_Park)

The Palace Park ([Norwegian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_language): *Slottsparken*) is a surrounding [public park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_park), one of the largest parks of the capital. It is 22 hectares (54 acres).

## The Queen Sonja Art Stable

Main article: [The Queen Sonja Art Stable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Queen_Sonja_Art_Stable)

In 2017, the former palace stables were renovated and converted into a multipurpose art venue which was named *Dronning Sonja KunstStall*. The building will be used as an art gallery, museum and concert hall and is now open to the public.
