# Fuel

For other uses, see [Fuel (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_\(disambiguation\)).



[Firewood](481098.md) was one of the first fuels used by humans.

[^cite_note-1]

A **fuel** is any [material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Material) that can react with other substances to release energy as [thermal energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_energy) or to be used for [work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_\(physics\)). The concept was originally applied solely to those materials capable of releasing [chemical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_energy) but has since also been applied to other sources of heat energy, such as [nuclear energy](22153.md) (via [nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission) and [nuclear fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fusion)).

The [heat energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_energy) released by reactions of fuels can be converted into [mechanical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_energy) via a [heat engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_engine). Other times, the heat itself is valued for warmth, [cooking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooking), or industrial processes, as well as the illumination that accompanies [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion). Fuels are also used in the [cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_\(biology\)) of [organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism) in a process known as [cellular respiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration), where organic [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule) are oxidized to release usable energy. [Hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) and related organic molecules are by far the most common source of fuel used by humans, but other substances, including radioactive metals, are also utilized.

Fuels are contrasted with other substances or devices [storing potential energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_storage), such as those that directly release [electrical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_energy) (such as [batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_battery) and [capacitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacitor)) or mechanical energy (such as [flywheels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flywheel), springs, compressed air, or water in a reservoir).

## History

See also: [Energy transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transition)



Wood as fuel for combustion

The first known use of fuel was the [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion) of [firewood](481098.md) by *[Homo erectus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_erectus)* nearly two million years ago.[^cite_note-\:0-2] Throughout most of human history only fuels derived from plants or animal fat were used by humans. [Charcoal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charcoal), a wood derivative, has been used since at least 6,000 BCE for melting metals. It was only supplanted by [coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_\(fuel\)), derived from coal, as European forests started to become depleted around the 18th century. Charcoal briquettes are now commonly used as a fuel for [barbecue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbecue) cooking.[^cite_note-\:0-2]

[Crude oil](23195.md) was [distilled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distilled) by [Persian chemists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy_and_chemistry_in_medieval_Islam), with clear descriptions given in Arabic handbooks such as those of [Muhammad ibn Zakarīya Rāzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Zakar%C4%ABya_R%C4%81zi).[^cite_note-3] He described the process of distilling crude oil/petroleum into [kerosene](16992.md), as well as other hydrocarbon compounds, in his *Kitab al-Asrar* (*Book of Secrets*). Kerosene was also produced during the same period from [oil shale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_shale) and [bitumen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitumen) by heating the rock to extract the oil, which was then distilled. Rāzi also gave the first description of a [kerosene lamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerosene_lamp) using crude mineral oil, referring to it as the "naffatah".[^cite_note-4]

The streets of [Baghdad](4492.md) were paved with [tar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tar), derived from petroleum that became accessible from natural fields in the region. In the 9th century, [oil fields](2910801.md) were exploited in the area around modern [Baku](4566.md), [Azerbaijan](746.md). These fields were described by the [Arab geographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_geography) [Abu al-Hasan 'Alī al-Mas'ūdī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_al-Hasan_'Al%C4%AB_al-Mas'%C5%ABd%C4%AB) in the 10th century, and by [Marco Polo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marco_Polo) in the 13th century, who described the output of those wells as hundreds of shiploads.[^cite_note-5]

With the development of the [steam engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_engine) in the United Kingdom in 1769, coal came into more common use, the combustion of which releases [chemical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_energy) that can be used to turn water into steam.[^cite_note-6] Coal was later used to drive ships and [locomotives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locomotive). By the 19th century, gas extracted from coal was being used for street lighting in London. In the 20th and 21st centuries, the primary use of coal is to generate [electricity](9550.md), providing 40% of the world's electrical power supply in 2005.[^cite_note-7]

Coal use expanded during the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution), particularly as steam engines provided power for factories and transport. Over time, coal, oil and natural gas came to be used for electricity generation, heating, transport and industry. Burning these fuels releases carbon dioxide, which contributes to [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming). Efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions have led to policies and technologies aimed at reducing fossil fuel use, although it remains a major source of energy.[^cite_note-8]

Currently the trend has been towards renewable fuels, such as [biofuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofuel) like alcohols.

Chemical fuels are substances that release energy by reacting with substances around them, most notably by the process of [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion).

Chemical fuels are divided in two ways. First, by their physical properties, as a solid, liquid or gas. Secondly, on the basis of their occurrence: *primary (natural fuel)* and *secondary (artificial fuel)*. Thus, a general classification of chemical fuels is:

### Solid fuel

Main article: [Solid fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_fuel)



[Coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) is a solid fuel

Solid fuel refers to various types of [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid) material that are used as fuel to produce [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) and provide [heating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heating), usually released through combustion. Solid fuels include [wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood), [charcoal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charcoal), [peat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peat), [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal), [hexamine fuel tablets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexamine_fuel_tablet), and pellets made from wood (see [wood pellets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_pellets)), [corn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize), [wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat), [rye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rye) and other [grains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cereal). [Solid-fuel rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-fuel_rocket) technology also uses solid fuel (see [solid propellants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_fuel#Solid_propellants)). Solid fuels have been used by humanity for many years to [create fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Making_fire). Coal was the fuel source which enabled the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution), from firing [furnaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_furnace), to running [steam engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_engine). Wood was also extensively used to run [steam locomotives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_locomotive). Both peat and coal are still used in [electricity generation](9540.md) today. The use of some solid fuels (e.g. coal) is restricted or prohibited in some urban areas, due to unsafe levels of toxic emissions. The use of other solid fuels as wood is decreasing as heating technology and the availability of good quality fuel improves. In some areas, [smokeless coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthracite) is often the only solid fuel used. In Ireland, peat [briquettes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Briquette) are used as smokeless fuel. They are also used to start a coal fire.

### Liquid fuels

Main article: [Liquid fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_fuel)



A [filling station](61260.md)

Liquid fuels are combustible or energy-generating molecules that can be harnessed to create [mechanical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_energy), usually producing [kinetic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy). They must also take the shape of their container; the fumes of liquid fuels are flammable, not the fluids.

Most liquid fuels in widespread use are derived from the [fossilized remains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuels) of dead plants and animals by exposure to heat and pressure inside the Earth's crust. However, there are several types, such as [hydrogen fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_fuel) (for [automotive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automotive) uses), [ethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol), [jet fuel](1205435.md) and [bio-diesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bio-diesel), which are all categorized as liquid fuels. [Emulsified fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emulsified_fuel) of oil in water, such as [orimulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orimulsion), have been developed as a way to make heavy oil fractions usable as liquid fuels. Many liquid fuels play a primary role in transportation and the economy.

Some common properties of liquid fuels are that they are easy to transport and can be handled easily. They are also relatively easy to use for all engineering applications and in home use. Fuels like [kerosene](16992.md) are rationed in some countries, for example in government-subsidized shops in India for home use.

Conventional [diesel](81761.md) is similar to [gasoline](23639.md) in that it is a mixture of [aliphatic hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aliphatic_hydrocarbon) extracted from [petroleum](23195.md). Kerosene is used in [kerosene lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerosene_lamp) and as a fuel for cooking, heating, and small engines. [Natural gas](22131.md), composed chiefly of [methane](18582230.md), can only exist as a liquid at very low temperatures (regardless of pressure), which limits its direct use as a liquid fuel in most applications. [LP gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LP_gas) is a mixture of [propane](23643.md) and [butane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butane), both of which are easily compressible gases under standard atmospheric conditions. It offers many of the advantages of [compressed natural gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compressed_natural_gas) (CNG) but is denser than air, does not burn as cleanly, and is much more easily compressed. Commonly used for cooking and space heating, LP gas and compressed propane are seeing increased use in motorized vehicles. Propane is the third most commonly used motor fuel globally.

### Fuel gas

Main article: [Fuel gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_gas)



A 20-pound (9.1 kg) propane cylinder

Fuel gas is any one of a number of fuels that are [gaseous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas) under ordinary conditions. Many fuel gases are composed of [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) (such as [methane](18582230.md) or [propane](23643.md)), [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen), [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide), or mixtures thereof. Such gases are sources of potential [heat energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_energy) or [light energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_energy) that can be readily transmitted and distributed through pipes from the point of origin directly to the place of consumption. Fuel gas is contrasted with liquid fuels and from solid fuels, though some fuel gases are [liquefied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquefaction_of_gases) for storage or transport. While their gaseous nature can be advantageous, avoiding the difficulty of transporting solid fuel and the dangers of spillage inherent in liquid fuels, it can also be dangerous. It is possible for a fuel gas to be undetected and collect in certain areas, leading to the risk of a [gas explosion](1919621.md). For this reason, [odorizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odorizer) are added to most fuel gases so that they may be detected by a distinct smell. The most common type of fuel gas in current use is [natural gas](22131.md).

### Biofuels

Main articles: [Biofuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofuel) and [Biomass (energy)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass_\(energy\))

Biofuel can be broadly defined as solid, liquid, or gas fuel consisting of, or derived from [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass_\(energy\)). Biomass can also be used directly for heating or power—known as *biomass fuel*. Biofuel can be produced from any carbon source that can be replenished rapidly e.g. plants. Many different plants and plant-derived materials are used for biofuel manufacture.

Perhaps the earliest fuel employed by humans is wood. Evidence shows controlled fire was used up to 1.5 million years ago at [Swartkrans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swartkrans), South Africa. It is unknown which hominid species first used fire, as both *[Australopithecus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australopithecus)* and an early species of *[Homo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_\(genus\))* were present at the sites.[^cite_note-9] As a fuel, wood has remained in use up until the present day, although it has been superseded for many purposes by other sources. Wood has an [energy density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_density) of 10–20 [MJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule)/[kg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilogram).[^cite_note-10]

Recently biofuels have been developed for use in automotive transport (for example [bioethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioethanol) and [biodiesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiesel)), but there is widespread public debate about how [carbon neutral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_neutrality) these fuels are.

### Fossil fuels

Main article: [Fossil fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel)



Extraction of petroleum

Fossil fuels are [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon), primarily [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) and [petroleum](23195.md) ([liquid petroleum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_petroleum) or [natural gas](22131.md)), formed from the [fossilized remains](10958.md) of ancient plants and animals[^cite_note-elements1-11] by exposure to high heat and pressure in the absence of oxygen in the [Earth's crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_crust) over hundreds of millions of years.[^cite_note-12] Commonly, the term fossil fuel also includes hydrocarbon-containing [natural resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_resource) that are not derived entirely from biological sources, such as [tar sands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tar_sands). These latter sources are properly known as *mineral fuels*.

Fossil fuels contain high percentages of [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon) and include coal, petroleum, and natural gas.[^cite_note-13] They range from [volatile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volatility_\(chemistry\)) materials with low carbon:[hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) ratios like [methane](18582230.md), to liquid petroleum to nonvolatile materials composed of almost pure carbon, like [anthracite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthracite) coal. Methane can be found in hydrocarbon fields, alone, associated with oil, or in the form of [methane clathrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane_clathrate). Fossil fuels formed from the fossilized remains of dead plants[^cite_note-elements1-11] by exposure to heat and pressure in the Earth's crust over millions of years.[^cite_note-14] This [biogenic theory](23195.md#Biogenic_theory) was first introduced by German scholar [Georg Agricola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Agricola) in 1556 and later by [Mikhail Lomonosov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Lomonosov) in the 18th century.

It was estimated by the [Energy Information Administration](1704673.md) that in 2007 primary sources of energy consisted of petroleum 36.0%, coal 27.4%, natural gas 23.0%, amounting to an 86.4% share for fossil fuels in primary [energy consumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_consumption) in the world.[^cite_note-15] Non-fossil sources in 2006 included [hydroelectric](381399.md) 6.3%, [nuclear](22153.md) 8.5%, and others ([geothermal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geothermal_power), [solar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_energy), [tidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_power), [wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power), [wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_fuel), [waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waste-to-energy)) amounting to 0.9%.[^cite_note-16] [World energy consumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_energy_consumption) was growing about 2.3% per year.

Fossil fuels are [non-renewable resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-renewable_resource) because they take millions of years to form, and reserves are being depleted much faster than new ones are being made. So we must conserve these fuels and use them judiciously. The production and use of fossil fuels raise environmental concerns. A global movement toward the generation of [renewable energy](25784.md) is therefore under way to help meet increased energy needs. The burning of fossil fuels produces around 21.3 billion [tonnes](31185.md) (21.3 [gigatonnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gigatonne)) of [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) (CO<sub>2</sub>) per year, but it is estimated that natural processes can only absorb about half of that amount, so there is a net increase of 10.65 billion tonnes of atmospheric carbon dioxide per year (one tonne of atmospheric carbon is equivalent to 44⁄12 (this is the ratio of the molecular/atomic weights) or 3.7 tonnes of CO<sub>2</sub>.[^cite_note-17] Carbon dioxide is one of the [greenhouse gases](21350772.md) that enhances [radiative forcing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiative_forcing) and contributes to [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming), causing the [average surface temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_temperature_record) of the Earth to rise in response, which [the vast majority of climate scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_opinion_on_climate_change) agree will cause major [adverse effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_global_warming). Fuels are a source of energy.

The International Energy Agency (IEA) predicts that fossil fuel prices will decline, with oil stabilizing around \$75 to \$80 per barrel as electric vehicle adoption surges and renewable energy expands. Additionally, the IEA anticipates a notable increase in liquefied natural gas capacity, enhancing Europe's energy diversification.[^cite_note-18]

## Nuclear

Main article: [Nuclear fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fuel)



Two [CANDU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CANDU) ("CANada Deuterium Uranium") fuel bundles, each about 50 cm long and 10 cm in diameter

Nuclear fuel is any material that is consumed to derive [nuclear energy](22153.md). In theory, a wide variety of substances could be a nuclear fuel, as they can be made to release nuclear energy under the right conditions. However, the materials commonly referred to as nuclear fuels are those that will produce energy without being placed under extreme duress. Nuclear fuel can be "burned" by [nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission) (splitting nuclei apart) or [fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fusion) (combining nuclei together) to derive nuclear energy. "Nuclear fuel" can refer to the fuel itself, or to physical objects (for example bundles composed of **fuel rods**) composed of the fuel material, mixed with structural, [neutron moderating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_moderator), or neutron-reflecting materials.

Nuclear fuel has the highest [energy density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_density) of all practical fuel sources.

### Fission



[Nuclear fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fuel) pellets are used to release nuclear energy.

The most common type of nuclear fuel used by humans is heavy [fissile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fissile) elements that can be made to undergo [nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission) chain reactions in a [nuclear fission reactor](22151.md); *nuclear fuel* can refer to the material or to physical objects (for example fuel bundles composed of [fuel rods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_rod)) composed of the fuel material, perhaps mixed with structural, [neutron moderating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_moderator), or neutron reflecting materials.

When some of these fuels are struck by neutrons, they are in turn capable of emitting neutrons when they break apart. This makes possible a self-sustaining [chain reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_reaction) that releases energy at a controlled rate in a [nuclear reactor](22151.md), or at a very rapid uncontrolled rate in a [nuclear weapon](21785.md).

The most common fissile nuclear fuels are [uranium-235](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-235) (<sup>235</sup>U) and [plutonium-239](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pu-239) (<sup>239</sup>Pu). The actions of mining, refining, purifying, using, and ultimately disposing of nuclear fuel together make up the [nuclear fuel cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fuel_cycle). Not all types of nuclear fuels create energy from nuclear fission. [Plutonium-238](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium-238) and some other elements are used to produce small amounts of nuclear energy by [radioactive decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_decay) in [radioisotope thermoelectric generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioisotope_thermoelectric_generator) and other types of [atomic batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_battery).

### Fusion

In contrast to fission, some light [nuclides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclide) such as [tritium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tritium) (<sup>3</sup>H) can be used as fuel for [nuclear fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fusion). This involves two or more nuclei combining into larger nuclei. Fuels that produce energy by this method are currently not utilized by humans, but they are the main source of fuel for [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star). Fusion fuels are light elements such as [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) whose nuclei will combine easily. Energy is required to start fusion by raising the temperature so high that nuclei can collide together with enough energy that they stick together before repelling due to electric charge. This process is called fusion and it can give out energy.

In stars that undergo nuclear fusion, fuel consists of [atomic nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nuclei) that can release energy by the absorption of a [proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton) or [neutron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron). In most stars the fuel is provided by hydrogen, which can combine to form [helium](13256.md) through the [proton-proton chain reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton-proton_chain_reaction) or by the [CNO cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNO_cycle). When the hydrogen fuel is exhausted, nuclear fusion can continue with progressively heavier elements, although the net energy released is lower because of the smaller difference in nuclear binding energy. Once [iron-56](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron-56) or [nickel-56](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopes_of_nickel) nuclei are produced, no further energy can be obtained by nuclear fusion as these have the highest nuclear binding energies.[^cite_note-19] Any nucleii heavier than <sup>56</sup>Fe and <sup>56</sup>Ni would thus absorb energy instead of giving it off when fused. Therefore, fusion stops and the star dies. In attempts by humans, fusion is only carried out with hydrogen (<sup>2</sup>H (deuterium) or <sup>3</sup>H (tritium)) to form helium-4 as this reaction gives out the most net energy. Electric confinement ([ITER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITER)), [inertial confinement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_confinement) (heating by laser) and heating by strong electric currents are the popular methods.

## Liquid fuels for transportation

Most transportation fuels are liquids, because vehicles usually require high [energy density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_density). This occurs naturally in liquids and solids. High energy density can also be provided by an [internal combustion engine](41228673.md). These engines require clean-burning fuels. The fuels that are easiest to burn cleanly are typically liquids and gases. Thus, liquids meet the requirements of being both energy-dense and clean-burning. In addition, liquids (and gases) can be pumped, which means handling is easily mechanized, and thus less laborious. As there is a general movement towards a low carbon economy, the use of liquid fuels such as hydrocarbons is coming under scrutiny.
