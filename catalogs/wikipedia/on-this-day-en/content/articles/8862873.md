**Syrians** ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): سوريون; [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language): ܣܘܪܝܝܢ) are the native inhabitants of [Syria](7515849.md), most of whom have [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) in its [Levantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levantine_Arabic) dialect as a mother tongue. The [cultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Syria) and linguistic heritage of Syria is a blend of both indigenous elements and the foreign cultures that have come to rule the land and its people over the course of thousands of years. By the seventh century, most of the inhabitants of the Levant spoke [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic). In the centuries after the [Muslim conquest of the Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_the_Levant) in 634, Arabic gradually became the dominant language, but a minority of Syrians (particularly the [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_people) and [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terms_for_Syriac_Christians#Syriac_identity)-[Arameans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arameans)) retained [Aramaic (Syriac)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Aramaic_languages), which is still spoken in its [Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Aramaic_languages) and [Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Aramaic_languages) dialects.&#x20;

The national name "Syrian" was originally an Indo-European corruption of Assyrian and applied to Assyria in northern [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia), however by [antiquity](252905.md) it was used to denote the inhabitants of the Levant. Following the Muslim conquest of the Levant, Arab identity gradually became dominant among many Syrians, and the ethnonym "Syrian" was used mainly by [Christians](18337522.md) in Levant, Mesopotamia and Anatolia who spoke Syriac. In the 19th century, the name "Syrian" was revived amongst the Arabic speakers of the Levant. Following the establishment of the [Arab Kingdom of Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Kingdom_of_Syria) in 1920, the name "Syrian" began to spread amongst its Arabic speaking inhabitants. The term gained more importance during the [Mandate for Syria and the Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_for_Syria_and_the_Lebanon), becoming the accepted national name for the Arabic speakers of the Syrian Republic.&#x20;

Most Arabic-speaking Syrians identify as Arabs and are described as such by virtue of their modern-day language and bonds to [Arab culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_culture) [and history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_history). In terms of genetics, they are a blend of the various [Semitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitic_languages)-speaking groups indigenous to the region.[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBadroDouaihyHaberYouhanna2013-69][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEl-SibaiPlattHaberXue2009-70][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJoseph200030-71] There is no contradiction between being an Arab and a Syrian since the Syrian Arab identity is multi-layered and being Syrian complements being Arab. In addition to denoting Syrian Arabs, the term "Syrian" also refers to all Syrian citizens, regardless of their ethnic background. In 2018, Syria had an estimated population of 19.5 million, which includes, aside from the aforementioned majority, [Kurds](3821855.md), [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrians_in_Syria), [Turkmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Turkmen), [Armenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians_in_Syria) and others.&#x20;

Even before the [Syrian Civil War](30741795.md), there was a large [Syrian diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_diaspora) that had emigrated to North America ([United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Americans) and [Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Canadians)), [European Union member states](10890716.md) (including Sweden, France, and [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrians_in_Germany)), South America (mainly in [Brazil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Brazilians), [Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Argentines), [Venezuela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Venezuelans), and [Chile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrians_in_Chile)), the [West Indies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Indies),[^cite_note-72] Africa, [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Australians), and [New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_New_Zealanders).[^cite_note-dias-73] Six million [refugees of the Syrian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refugees_of_the_Syrian_Civil_War) also live outside Syria now, mostly in [Turkey](11125639.md), [Egypt](8087628.md), [Jordan](7515964.md), [Lebanon](17771.md), [Germany](11867.md) as well as [Sweden](5058739.md).&#x20;

## Etymology

Various sources indicate that the name *Syria* itself is derived from [Luwian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luwian) term "Sura/i", and the derivative [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) name: Σύριοι, *_Sýrioi_*, or Σύροι, *_Sýroi_*, both of which originally derived from the [Akkadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_language) word Aššūrāyu ([Assyria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyria)) in northern [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia), modern-day Iraq.[^cite_note-74][^cite_note-75] However, during the [Seleucid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid_Empire), this term was also applied to [The Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Levant), and henceforth the Greeks applied the term without distinction between the [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_people) of north Mesopotamia and [Arameans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arameans) of the Levant.[^cite_note-76][^cite_note-77]&#x20;

### Applications of the name in antiquity



A partial relief of [Tiglath-Pileser III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiglath-Pileser_III), r. 745–727 BC, under whom the [Neo-Assyrian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Assyrian_Empire) was consolidated, centralized and significantly expanded.

The Greeks used the terms "Syrian" and "Assyrian" interchangeably to indicate the indigenous [Arameans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arameans), [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_people) and other inhabitants of the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant) and [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia), [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus) considered "Syria" west of the [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates). Starting from the 2nd century BC onwards, ancient writers referred to the ruler of the [Seleucid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid_Empire) as the [King of Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Syrian_monarchs) or King of the Syrians.[^cite_note-78] The Seleucids designated the districts of [Seleucis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucis) and [Coele-Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coele-Syria) explicitly as Syria and ruled the Syrians as indigenous populations residing west of the Euphrates ([Aramea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramea)) in contrast to Assyrians who had their native [homeland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_homeland) in [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia) east of the Euphrates.[^cite_note-Syrian_Identity-79] However, the interchangeability between Assyrians and Syrians persisted during the [Hellenistic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period).[^cite_note-Syrian_Identity-79]&#x20;

In one instance, the [Ptolemaic dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_dynasty) of the Hellenistic kingdom of [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Egypt) applied the term "Syrian Village" as the name of a settlement in [Fayoum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fayoum). The Ptolemies referred to all peoples originating from Modern Syria and [Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine_\(region\)) as Syrian.[^cite_note-80]&#x20;

The term *Syrian* was imposed upon Arameans of modern [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant) by the [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome). [Pompey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompey) created the province of [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Syria), which included modern-day Lebanon and Syria west of the Euphrates, framing the province as a regional social category with civic implications.[^cite_note-Syrian_Identity2-81] In Roman usage, the term could refer narrowly to people of Aramean descent, but it was also applied more broadly to all inhabitants of the province, among them descendants of Greek and Italian settlers.[^cite_note-\:10-82] Authors varied in their descriptions: [Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch) described the indigenous people of this newly created Roman province as "Syrians",[^cite_note-83] so did [Strabo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strabo), who observed that Syrians resided west of the Euphrates in Roman Syria,[^cite_note-Syrian_Identity2-81] and he explicitly mentions that those Syrians are the Arameans, whom he calls *Aramaei*, indicating an extant ethnicity.[^cite_note-84] [Posidonius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posidonius) noted that the people called Syrians by the Greeks refer to themselves as Arameans.[^cite_note-85] [Tacitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacitus) also portrayed Syrians as a distinct ethnic group or race.[^cite_note-\:10-82]&#x20;

In *[The Jewish War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jewish_War)*, [Josephus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephus), a [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrews) historian native to the Levant, mentioned the Syrians as the non-Hebrew, non-Greek indigenous inhabitants of Syria.[^cite_note-86]&#x20;

## History

Main articles: [Arameans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arameans), [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrians), and [Arabs](2185.md)



[Aram-Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aram-Damascus) in 830 BCE.

Syrians are mainly descended from the various [ancient Semitic-speaking peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Semitic-speaking_peoples) of the [ancient Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Near_East).[^cite_note-Haber-87][^cite_note-88][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAndrade2013-89] The Seleucids ruled the indigenous peoples of the Levant, whom they named "Syrians", as a conquered nation; Syrians were not assimilated into Greek communities, and many local peasants were exploited financially as they had to pay rent for Greek landlords. Outside [Greek colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_colonies), the Syrians lived in districts governed by local temples that did not use the Greek civic system of *[poleis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poleis)* and colonies.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAndrade20137-90] The situation changed after the Roman conquest in 64 BC; Semitic-speaking Syrians obtained the citizenship of Greek *poleis*, and the line separating the Greeks and the natives blurred. The idioms Syrian and Greek were used by Rome to denote civic societies instead of separate ethnic groups.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAndrade20138-91]&#x20;

Ancient Syria of the first millennium BC was dominated by the Aramaeans;[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJoseph2000\[httpsbooksgooglecombooksidltnzDQAAQBAJpgPA30_30\]-92] they originated in the Northern Levant as a continuum of the [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age) populations of Syria.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENovak2016123-93] The Aramaeans assimilated most of the earlier Levantine populations through their language.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJoseph2000\[httpsbooksgooglecombooksidltnzDQAAQBAJpgPA30_30\]-92] With the adoption of a common religion, [Christianity](5211.md), most of the inhabitants turned into Syrians (Aramaeans). Islam and the Arabic language had a similar effect where the Aramaeans themselves became Arabs regardless of their ethnic origin following the [Muslim conquest of the Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_the_Levant).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJoseph2000\[httpsbooksgooglecombooksidltnzDQAAQBAJpgPA30_30\]-92] The presence of Arabs in Syria is recorded since the 9th century BC,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGraf2003319-94] and Roman period historians, such as [Strabo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strabo), [Pliny the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder), and [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy), reported that Arabs inhabited many parts of Syria,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMacdonald2009a298-95] which according to modern historians indicate either an ethnic group or a nomadic way of life.[^cite_note-98][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMacdonald2009a319-96][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGraf2003320-97] The *[urheimat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urheimat)* of the Arab ethnos is unclear; the traditional 19th century theory locates this in the Arabian Peninsula,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGraf2003322-99] while some modern scholars, such as David Frank Graf, note that the epigraphic and archaeological evidence render the traditional theory inadequate to explain the Arabs' appearance in Syria.[^cite_note-104][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGraf2003334-101] The Arabs mentioned in Syria by Greco-Roman writers were assimilated into the newly formed "Greco–Aramaean culture" that dominated the region, and the texts they produced were written in Greek and Aramaic.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHoyland200169-105] [Old Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Arabic), the precursor of [Classical Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Arabic), was not a literary language; its speakers used Aramaic for writing purposes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMacdonald2003305-106]&#x20;

### Linguistic Arabization



Umayyad [fresco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresco) from [Qasr al-Hayr al-Gharbî](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qasr_al-Hayr_al-Gharb%C3%AE), built in the early 7th century.

On the eve of the [Rashidun Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_Caliphate) conquest of the Levant, 634 AD, Syria's population mainly spoke Aramaic as the [Lingua franca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_franca),[^cite_note-107] while Greek was the language of administration. [Arabization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabization#The_Levant) and [Islamization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamization) of Syria began in the 7th century, and it took several centuries for Islam, the Arab identity, and language to spread;[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEal-Hassan2001\[httpsbooksgooglecombooksidFUPnSxbUREgCpgPA59_59\]-108] the Arabs of the caliphate did not attempt to spread their language or religion in the early periods of the conquest, and formed an isolated aristocracy.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchulze201019-109] The Arabs of the caliphate accommodated many new tribes in isolated areas to avoid conflict with the locals; caliph [Uthman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uthman) ordered his governor, [Muawiyah I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muawiyah_I), to settle the new tribes away from the original population.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKennedy1992292-110] Syrians who belonged to [Monophysitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monophysitism) denominations welcomed the Muslim Arabs as liberators.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarker1966\[httpsbooksgooglecombooksidLiJljEXvwAoCpgPA244_244\]-111]&#x20;

&#x20;The [Abbasids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate) in the eighth and ninth centuries sought to integrate the peoples under their authority, and the Arabization of the administration was one of their methods.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBraida2012183-112] Arabization gained momentum with the increasing numbers of Muslim converts from [Christianity](5211.md);[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEal-Hassan2001\[httpsbooksgooglecombooksidFUPnSxbUREgCpgPA59_59\]-108] the ascendancy of Arabic as the formal language of the state prompted the cultural and linguistic assimilation of Syrian converts.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPeters2003\[httpsbooksgooglecombooksidHYJ2c9E9IM8CpgPA191_191\]-113] Some of those who remained Christian also became Arabized, while others stayed Aramean,[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBraida2012183-112] it was probably during the Abbasid period in the ninth century that Christians adopted Arabic as their first language; the first translation of the gospels into Arabic took place in this century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBraida2012182-115] Many historians, such as [Claude Cahen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Cahen) and Bernard Hamilton, proposed that the Arabization of Christians was completed before the [First Crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Crusade).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEllenblum2006\[httpsbooksgooglecombooksidoiYgyRyL97oCpgPA53_53\]-116] By the thirteenth century, the Arabic language achieved complete dominance in the region, with many of its speakers having become Arabs.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEal-Hassan2001\[httpsbooksgooglecombooksidFUPnSxbUREgCpgPA59_59\]-108]



Garshuni sample

Those who retained the Aramaic language are divided among two groups:&#x20;

- The [Eastern Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Aramaic_languages) [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language)-speaking group, followers of the [West Syriac Rite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Syriac_Rite) of the [Syriac Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox_Church) and the [Syrian Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Catholic_Church); kept the pre-Islamic [Syrian (Syriac)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terms_for_Syriac_Christians#Syriac_identity) identity throughout the ages, asserting their culture in face of the Arab dominance. Linguists, such as [Carl Brockelmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Brockelmann) and [François Lenormant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Lenormant), suggested that the rise of the [Garshuni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garshuni) writing (using the [Syriac alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_alphabet) to write Arabic) was an attempt by the Syriac Orthodox to assert their identity.

### Revival of the designation "Syrian"



Portrait of a woman with the national [flag of Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Syria), also known as "The Independence flag".

The Arabs in Arabia called the [region of Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria_\(region\)) region *[al-Sham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilad_al-Sham)* ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): بِـلَاد الـشَّـام, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Arabic): *Bilād al-Šām*, [lit.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation) 'the country of Sham') which became the dominant name of the Levant under the [Rashidun Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_Caliphate) and its successors. The geographic designation "Syria" returned in 1864 when [Ottoman Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Syria#1864) was reorganized and the name was used for a vilayet encompassing generally the southern Levant.[^cite_note-124] The use of the national designation "Syrian" however has its origin in the tense relationship between the Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians of the Levant, where Christians wanted to distance themselves from the Muslims.[^cite_note-Groiss-125] Already in the 1830s, the Lebanese traveler As'ad Khayyat identified with the term Syria, but it took till the 1880s for the name to begin to be widely used by the inhabitants to refer to themselves.[^cite_note-Groiss-125] Both Muslims and Christians agreed that the Muslims were not Syrians because they belonged to the Arabs while the Christians retained the Syrianism of antiquity.[^cite_note-Groiss-125] The spread of the Syrian "idea" amongst the Muslims can be traced to the efforts of [Rashid Rida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashid_Rida) who contributed to the formulation of the Syrian Union Party's manifesto in 1918, demanding that Syria, in the aftermath of World War I and the Ottoman withdrawal from the region, become an independent state and not part of larger Arab one ruled by the [Hashemites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hashemites) of the [Kingdom of Hejaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hejaz).[^cite_note-Zisser-126] Rida did not reject the Arab identity but recognized a Syrian uniqueness and advocated the idea of a Syrian state.[^cite_note-Zisser-126] In the end, Syria did become a separate state but under the Hashemite king [Faisal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faisal_I_of_Iraq). He entered Damascus in 1918 in the aftermath of the Ottomans' evacuation of the Levant at the end of World War I. His entry ignited the Syrian national consciousness after he declared an Arab government in the Levant centred in Damascus with him as prince.[^cite_note-Salhi-127] In June 1919, the [Syrian National Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_National_Congress), which included representatives from Palestine and Lebanon, demanded the full independence of Syria, within borders that encompass more or less the Levant; this helped to further strengthen the development of the Syrian national consciousness.[^cite_note-Salhi-127] Initially, most inhabitants were against the establishment of Syria as they considered this a step against Arab unity, but gradually, Faisal's Syria, which was declared an independent kingdom in 1920, prompted the Syrians to begin exploring the notion of Syrianism instead of pan-Arabism.[^cite_note-129][^cite_note-130][^cite_note-131] Faisal was deposed by the French who established a [mandate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_for_Syria_and_the_Lebanon) in 1920, but the formation of a Syrian consciousness amongst the members of the Syrian Arab national movement solidified and spread amongst the Muslims as well as the Christians.[^cite_note-132]&#x20;

## Genetics

See also: [Genetic history of the Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_history_of_the_Middle_East) and [Genetic studies on Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_studies_on_Arabs)

&#x20; Levantine ancestral component\
&#x20; Other ancestral components



Syrian Dr. Khaldoun Al-Hassani Al-Jazairi in his home library.

Genetic tests on Syrians were included in many genetic studies.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBadroDouaihyHaberYouhanna2013-69][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHaberPlattBadroXue2011-133][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEl-SibaiPlattHaberXue2009-70] The [genetic marker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_marker) which identifies descendants of the ancient [Levantines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant) is found in Syrians in high proportion.[^cite_note-134] Modern Syrians exhibit "high affinity to the Levant" based on studies comparing modern and ancient DNA samples.[^cite_note-135] Syrians cluster closely with ancient Levantine populations of the Neolithic and Bronze Ages.[^cite_note-136] A Levantine ancestral genetic component was identified; it is estimated that the Levantine, the Arabian and East African ancestral components diverged 23,700–15,500 years ago, while the divergence between the Levantine and European components happened 15,900–9,100 years ago.[^cite_note-137] The Levantine ancestral component is the most recurrent in Levantines (42–68%); the Peninsular Arabian and East African ancestral components represent around 25% of Syrian genetic make-up.[^cite_note-138][^cite_note-139] Syrians, among other Levantine groups, were found to derive 81–87% of their ancestry from Bronze age Levantines, relating to [Canaanites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canaanites) as well as [Kura–Araxes culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kura%E2%80%93Araxes_culture) impact from before 2400 BCE; 8–12% from an [East African](260987.md) source and 5–10% from Bronze age [Europeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europeans).[^cite_note-140] Compared to the Lebanese, Bedouins and Palestinians, the Syrians have noticeably more Northern European component, estimated at 7%.[^cite_note-141]&#x20;

The paternal Y-DNA haplogroup J1, which reaches its highest frequencies in Yemen 72.6% and Qatar 58.3%, accounted for 33.6% of Syrians.[^cite_note-142] The J2 group accounted for 20.8% of Syrians; other Y-DNA haplogroups include the [E1B1B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_E-M215_\(Y-DNA\)) 12.0%, [I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_I-M170) 5.0%, [R1a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_R-M420) 10.0% and [R1b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_R1b_\(Y-DNA\)) 15.0%.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEl-SibaiPlattHaberXue2009-70][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESeminoPassarinoOefnerLin2000-143] The Syrians are closest to other Levantine populations: the [Lebanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_people), the [Palestinians](23267.md) and [Jordanians](15717.md);[^cite_note-144] this closeness can be explained by the common Canaanite ancestry and geographical unity which was broken only in the twentieth century with the advent of British and French mandates.[^cite_note-145] Regarding the genetic relation between the Syrians and the Lebanese based on Y-DNA, Muslims from Lebanon show closer relations to Syrians than their Christian compatriots.[^cite_note-146] The people of Western Syria show close relations with the people of Northern Lebanon.[^cite_note-147]&#x20;

[Mitochondrial DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondrial_DNA) (mtDNA) analysis indicates that modern Syrian populations share a close genetic affinity with Europe, particularly through the prevalence of maternal haplogroups [H](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_H_\(mtDNA\)) and [R](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_R_\(mtDNA\)).[^cite_note-148] Based on Mitochondrial DNA, the Syrians, Palestinians, Lebanese and Jordanians form a close cluster.[^cite_note-149] A study of mitochondrial DNA variation found that the Syrians have \~10%–15% of lineages of [sub-Saharan African](27067.md) origin, consistent with female migration from eastern Africa into Near Eastern communities within the last few thousand years. It is likely this ancestry traces back to [women brought from Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Syria) as part of the [Arab slave trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_slave_trade).[^cite_note-150] Regarding the [HLA alleles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_leukocyte_antigen), Syrians, and other Levantine populations, exhibit "key differences" from other [Arab](2185.md) populations;[^cite_note-151] based on [HLA-DRB1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HLA-DRB1) alleles, Syrians were close to eastern Mediterranean populations, such as the [Cretans](6591.md) and [Lebanese Armenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians_in_Lebanon).[^cite_note-152] Studying the genetic relation between [Jews](25955086.md) and Syrians showed that the two populations share a close affinity.[^cite_note-153] Apparently, the cultural influence of Arabian expansion in the [Eastern Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Mediterranean) in the seventh century was more prominent than the genetic influx.[^cite_note-154] However, the expansion of Islam did leave an impact on Levantine genes; religion drove Levantine Muslims to mix with other Muslim populations, who were close culturally despite the geographic distance, and this produced genetic similarities between Levantine Muslims and Moroccan and Yemeni populations. Christians and [Druze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze) became a genetic isolate in the predominantly Islamic world.[^cite_note-155]&#x20;

[Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) is the mother tongue of the majority of Syrians[^cite_note-CIA_-_TWF-167] as well as the official state language. The Syrian variety of [Levantine Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levantine_Arabic) differs from [Modern Standard Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Standard_Arabic). [Western Neo-Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Neo-Aramaic), the only surviving [Western Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Aramaic_languages) dialect, is still spoken in three villages ([Maaloula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maaloula), [Bakh'a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Sarkha_\(Bakhah\)) and [Jubb'adin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jubb%27adin)) in the Anti-Lebanon Mountains by both Muslim and Christian [Arameans (Syriacs)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terms_for_Syriac_Christians#Aramean_identity). [Syriacs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian-Assyrians) in the northeast of the country are mainly [Turoyo-Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turoyo_language) speakers but there are also some speakers of [Suret-Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suret_language), especially in the [Khabour Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khabur_\(Euphrates\)). [Classical Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language) is also used as a liturgical language by [Syriac Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Christianity). English, and to a lesser extent French, is widely understood and used in interactions with tourists and other foreigners.&#x20;

## Religion and minority groups

Main articles: [Freedom of religion in Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_religion_in_Syria), [Religion in Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Syria), [Islam in Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Syria), and [Christianity in Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Syria)



Syrian descendants in [São Paulo](390875.md)

[Clip](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Development_Cooperation_Handbook/Interviews/Paolo_Dall%27Oglio) – Interview with [Paolo Dall'Oglio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paolo_Dall%27Oglio), The Syrian tradition of coexistence and the present scenario of confrontation

Religious differences in Syria have historically been tolerated,[^cite_note-168][^cite_note-169] and religious minorities tend to retain distinct cultural, and religious identities. [Sunni Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) is the religion of 74% of Syrians. Shia Muslims are mostly represented by the [Ismailis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Syria#Ismailis), forming 3% of Syria's population. A small group of Shia Muslims (<2% of population) used to live in Damascus in [Sayyidah Zaynab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyidah_Zaynab) as well as in [Idlib](2528679.md) governorate around al-Fu'ah and Kafriya. The [Alawites](436616.md), a group distinct from [Shia Islam](26961.md), make up 12% of the population and mostly live in and around [Tartus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartus) and [Latakia](195594.md). [Christians](18337522.md) make up 10% of the country. Most [Syrian Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Syria) adhere to the [Byzantine Rite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Rite), the two largest groups being the [Antiochian Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiochian_Orthodox_Church) and the [Melkite Greek Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melkite_Greek_Catholic_Church); the rest belong mainly to [Oriental Orthodoxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Orthodox_Churches), namely the [Syriac Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox_Church) and the [Armenian Apostolic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Apostolic_Church).[^cite_note-170][^cite_note-171] The [Druze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze) are a mountainous people who reside in [Jabal al-Druze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabal_al-Druze) who helped spark the [Great Syrian Revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Syrian_Revolt). Many Armenian and Assyrian Christians fled [Turkey](11125639.md) during the [Armenian genocide](140376.md) and the [Assyrian genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_genocide) and settled in Syria. There are also roughly 500,000 [Palestinians](23267.md), who are mostly descendants of refugees from the 1948 Israeli-Arab War. The community of [Syrian Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Jews) inside Syria once numbered 30,000 in 1947, but has only 200 today.[^cite_note-172]&#x20;

The Syrian people's beliefs and outlooks, similar to those of most Arabs and people of the wider Middle-East, are a mosaic of West and East. Conservative and liberally minded people will live right next to each other. Like the other countries in the region, religion permeates life; the government registers every Syrian's religious affiliation.&#x20;

## Cuisine



Tabbouleh

Further information: [Syrian cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_cuisine)

Syrian cuisine is dominated by ingredients native to the region. [Olive oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive_oil), [garlic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garlic), [olives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive), [spearmint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spearmint), and [sesame oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesame_oil) are some of the ingredients that are used in many traditional meals. Traditional Syrian dishes enjoyed by Syrians include, [tabbouleh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabbouleh), [labaneh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strained_yogurt), [shanklish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanklish), [wara' 'enab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolma), [makdous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makdous), [kebab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kebab), [Kibbeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kibbeh), [sfiha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sfiha), [moutabal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baba_ghanoush), [hummus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hummus), [mana'eesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manakish), [bameh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okra), and [fattoush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fattoush).&#x20;

A typical Syrian breakfast is a [meze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meze). It is an assortment platter of foods with cheeses, meats, pickles, olives, and spreads. Meze is usually served with Arab-style tea – highly concentrated black tea, which is often highly sweetened and served in small glass cups. Another popular drink, especially with Christians and non-practicing Muslims, is the [arak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arak_\(drink\)), a liquor produced from grapes or dates and flavored with [anise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anise) that can have an alcohol content of over 90% ABV (however, most commercial Syrian arak brands are about 40–60% ABV).&#x20;

[^cite_note-104]: &#x20;Regarding the urheimat of the Arab ethnos: the traditional theory, which dates to the 19th century and became dominant in the middle of the 20th century, holds that Arabs were a Semitic wave from the Arabian peninsula who infiltrated Syria. The traditional theory does not explain the early presence of the Arabs in the Levant as it lacks the evidence for when and how they allegedly arrived from Arabia.<sup>[\[97\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGraf2003322-99)</sup> Macdonald noted that there is no evidence proving that the inhabitants of the Arabian Peninsula, especially modern Yemen, in the early Hellenistic period (fourth century BC), used the designation "Arab", and that it took several centuries for this ethnic name to be adopted by the majority of the peninsula's inhabitants.<sup>[\[98\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMacdonald2009b2-100)</sup> The historian David Frank Graf considered the traditional theory inadequate for explaining the Arab presence in the Near East. Graf noted the 4th century BC evidence from [Edom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edom), south of the historical region of Syria, represented in a collection of ostraca, which show that the population was either "Arabized Edmoites" or "Edomite Arabs", and that this population was an integral part of the demography of southern Palestine and not a recent infiltration.<sup>[\[99\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGraf2003334-101)</sup> The historian Robert Hoyland, noting the earliest attestation of Arabs in Assyrian sources in the Syrian desert in the 9th century, followed by their earliest attestation in Southern Arabian inscriptions in the seventh/sixth century BC, suggested that north and central Arabia was the homeland of Arabs.<sup>[\[100\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHoyland2001230-102)</sup> Macdonald refused the paradigm of infiltration from Arabia, and considered the Syria/Arabia division a Western concept that would have been unrecognizable for Arabs who were supposedly migrating.<sup>[\[101\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMacdonald2003317-103)</sup>
