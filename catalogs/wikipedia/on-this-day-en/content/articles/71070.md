**Derbyshire** ([/ˈdɑːrbiʃɪər, -ʃər/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*DAR-bee-sheer, -⁠shər*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key))[^cite_note-4] is a [ceremonial county](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceremonial_county) in the [East Midlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Midlands) of England. It borders [Greater Manchester](79819.md) to the north-west, [West Yorkshire](149139.md) to the north, [South Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Yorkshire) to the north and north-east, [Nottinghamshire](68049.md) to the east, [Leicestershire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leicestershire) to the south-east, [Staffordshire](58733.md) to the south and west, and [Cheshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheshire) to the west. The city of [Derby](18727355.md) is the largest settlement.

The county has an area of 2,625 km<sup>2</sup> (1,014 sq mi) and had an estimated population of 1,096,526 in 2024. The eastern half of the county is the more densely populated and contains its largest settlements: [Chesterfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chesterfield,_Derbyshire) in the north-east, Derby in the south-east, and [Swadlincote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swadlincote) in the south. The spa town of [Buxton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buxton) and [Glossop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossop) are located in the north-west, and [Matlock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matlock,_Derbyshire) near the centre. For local government purposes Derbyshire comprises a [non-metropolitan county](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-metropolitan_county), with eight districts, and the Derby [unitary authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_authorities_of_England) area. The [East Midlands Combined County Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Midlands_Combined_County_Authority) includes [Derbyshire County Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derbyshire_County_Council) and [Derby City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derby_City_Council).

The north and centre of Derbyshire have uplands containing the majority of the [Peak District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_District), which has been designated a [national park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_parks_of_the_United_Kingdom). They include [Kinder Scout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinder_Scout), at 636 m (2,087 ft) the highest point in the county. The [River Derwent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Derwent,_Derbyshire) is the longest in the county, at 66 miles (106 km), and flows south until it meets the [River Trent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Trent) just south of Derby. Church Flatts Farm at [Coton in the Elms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coton_in_the_Elms), near Swadlincote, is the furthest point from the sea in the UK.[^cite_note-BBC-5]

## History

See also: [History of Derbyshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Derbyshire)

The area that is now Derbyshire was first visited, probably briefly, by humans 200,000 years ago during the Aveley [interglacial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interglacial), as shown by a [Middle Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Paleolithic) [Acheulean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acheulean) hand axe found near [Hopton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopton,_Derbyshire).[^cite_note-6] Further occupation came with the [Upper Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Paleolithic) and [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic) periods of the Stone Age when [Mesolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesolithic) hunter-gatherers roamed the hilly tundra.[^cite_note-7] Evidence of these nomadic tribes has been found in [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone) caves located on the Nottinghamshire border. Deposits left in the caves date the occupancy at around 12,000 to 7,000 [BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era).[^cite_note-pevsner22-8]



The henge monument at [Arbor Low](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbor_Low)

Burial mounds of Neolithic settlers are also situated throughout the county. These [chambered tombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamber_tomb) were designed for collective burial and are mostly located in the central Derbyshire region.[^cite_note-pevsner22-8] There are tombs at [Minninglow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minninglow) and [Five Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Wells) that date back to between 2000 and 2500 BCE.[^cite_note-9] Five kilometres west of [Youlgreave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Youlgreave) lies the Neolithic [henge monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henge) of [Arbor Low](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbor_Low), which has been dated to 2500 BCE. It is not until the [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age) that real signs of agriculture and settlement are found in the county. In the moors of the Peak District signs of clearance, arable fields and [hut circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hut_circle) were found after archaeological investigation. However this area and another settlement at [Swarkestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swarkestone) are all that have been found.[^cite_note-smith8-10]



Hand-drawn map of Derbyshire by Christopher Saxton in 1577

During the [Roman conquest of Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_conquest_of_Britain), the invaders were attracted to Derbyshire for [its lead ore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derbyshire_lead_mining_history) in the limestone hills of the area. They settled throughout the county, with forts built near [Brough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brough_and_Shatton) in the [Hope Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hope_Valley,_Derbyshire) and at [Ardotalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ardotalia) near Glossop. Later they settled round [Buxton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buxton), famed for its warm springs, and set up [Derventio Coritanorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derventio_Coritanorum) fort near modern-day [Derby](18727355.md) in an area now known as [Little Chester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Chester).[^cite_note-smith8-10]

Several kings of [Mercia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercia) are buried in the [Repton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repton) area.[^cite_note-11]

Following the [Norman Conquest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Conquest), much of the county was subject to the [forest laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_forest). To the northwest was the [Forest of High Peak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_of_High_Peak) under the custodianship of [William Peverel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Peverel) and his descendants. The rest of the county was bestowed upon [Henry de Ferrers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_de_Ferrers), a part of it becoming [Duffield Frith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duffield_Frith). In time the whole area was given to the [Duchy of Lancaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Lancaster). Meanwhile, the [Forest of East Derbyshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_of_East_Derbyshire) covered the whole county to the east of the [River Derwent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Derwent,_Derbyshire) from the reign of [Henry II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_II_of_England) to that of [Edward I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_I_of_England).[^cite_note-12]

## Geography

Derbyshire mostly consists of hilly terrain, with uplands to the north and centre of the county, and lowlands to the south and east. The southern foothills and uplands of the [Pennines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennines) extend from the north of the [Trent Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Trent)[^cite_note-13] and into the north of the county. The [Peak District National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_District) covers the majority of this area and [Kinder Scout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinder_Scout) is the county's highest point, at 636 m (2,087 ft).[^cite_note-14] The terrain is relatively low-lying across the lower [Dove Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Dove,_Central_England), from the Trent Valley and southwards, the far south of the [Derwent Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Derwent,_Derbyshire) and near its eastern borders with [Nottinghamshire](68049.md) and [Leicestershire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leicestershire).

The main rivers in the county are the River Derwent and the River Dove which both join the River Trent in the south. The River Derwent is the longest river in the county, at 66 miles (106 km), and rises in the moorland of [Bleaklow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bleaklow), flowing throughout the Peak District and county for the majority of its course. The River Dove rises in [Axe Edge Moor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axe_Edge_Moor) and forms a boundary between Derbyshire and [Staffordshire](58733.md) for most of its length. The county also contains Church Flatts Farm at [Coton in the Elms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coton_in_the_Elms), near Swadlincote, which is the furthest point from the sea in the UK.[^cite_note-BBC-5]

### Landscape character

The varied landscapes within Derbyshire have been formed mainly as a consequence of the underlying geology, but also by the way the land has been managed and shaped by human activity. The county contains 11 discrete landscape types, known as [National Character Areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Character_Area), which have been described in detail by Natural England[^cite_note-15] and further refined, mapped and described by Derbyshire County Council[^cite_note-16] and the Peak District National Park.[^cite_note-17]

The 11 National Character Areas found within Derbyshire are:

- [Dark Peak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_Peak)

- [White Peak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Peak)

- South West Peak

- Derbyshire Peak Fringe and Lower Derwent

- Nottinghamshire, Derbyshire and Yorkshire Coalfield

- Southern Magnesian Limestone

- Needwood and South Derbyshire Claylands

- Trent Valley Washlands

- Melbourne Parklands

- [Leicestershire & South Derbyshire Coalfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leicestershire_&_South_Derbyshire_Coalfield)

- Mease/Sence Lowlands

### Geology

Derbyshire's solid geology can be split into two very different halves. The oldest rocks occur in the northern, more upland half of the county, and are mostly of [Carboniferous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboniferous) age, comprising [limestones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone), [gritstones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gritstone), [sandstones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandstone) and [shales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shale). In its north-east corner to the east of [Bolsover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolsover), there are also [Magnesian Limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesian_Limestone) rocks of Permian age. In contrast, the southern and more lowland half of Derbyshire contains much softer rocks, mainly [mudstones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mudstone) and sandstones of Permo-Triassic age, which create gentler, more rolling landscapes with few rock outcrops. Across both regions can be found drift deposits of [Quaternary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternary) age – mainly terrace and river gravel deposits and boulder clays. Landslip features are found on unstable layers of sandstones and shales, with [Mam Tor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mam_Tor) and [Alport Castles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alport_Castles) being the best-known. Cemented screes and [tufa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tufa) deposits occur very rarely in the limestone dales and rivers, whilst cave systems have been created naturally in the limestone since Pleistocene times. A recently discovered cave chamber near [Castleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castleton,_Derbyshire), named [Titan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titan_\(cave\)), is the deepest shaft and biggest chamber of any cave in Britain.[^cite_note-bbc-18]

The oldest rocks are Lower Carboniferous limestones of [Dinantian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinantian) age, which form the core of the White Peak within the Peak District National Park. Because northern Derbyshire is effectively an uplifted dome of rock layers that have subsequently eroded to expose older rocks in the centre of the [Derbyshire Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derbyshire_Dome), these are encircled by progressively younger limestone rocks, until they in turn give way on three sides to Upper Carboniferous shales, gritstones and sandstones of [Namurian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namurian) age.



A cross-section of northern Derbyshire, from west to east, showing the approximate structure of an eroded dome, with younger Coal Measure rocks to the east, and older limestone exposed in the centre

Younger still are the sandstones, shales and coal deposits found on the eastern flank of Derbyshire, forming the coal measures, which are of [Westphalian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westphalian_\(stage\)) age. All these rock layers disappear south of a line drawn between Ashbourne and Derby under layers of clays and sandstones ([Mercia Mudstone Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercia_Mudstone_Group) and [Sherwood Sandstones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherwood_Sandstone_Group)) of Permo-Triassic age. Small amounts of carboniferous limestones, gritstones and coal measures reappear in the far south of Derbyshire from [Ticknall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ticknall) (limestone) to [Swadlincote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swadlincote) (coal measures).[^cite_note-19]

Some areas of the White Peak exhibit contemporaneous basalt flows (e.g. Ravens Tor at Millers Dale), as well as subsequent dolerite sill intrusion at a much later stage (e.g. near Tideswell Dale),[^cite_note-20] whilst mineralisation of the carboniferous limestone in a subsequent period created extensive lead and fluorite deposits which have formed a significant part of Derbyshire's economy, as did coal mining. Lead mining has been important here since Roman Times.[^cite_note-Geology-21] The more recent river gravels of the Trent valley remain a significant extractive industry today in south Derbyshire, as does the mining of limestone rock in central and northern parts of the county.[^cite_note-22] Coarse sandstones were once extensively quarried both for local building materials and for the production of gritstone grinding wheels for use in mills, and both former industries have left their mark on the Derbyshire landscape.

## Ecology

Because of its central location in England and altitude range from 27 metres in the south to 636 metres in the north,[^cite_note-willmotmoyes-24]<sup>: 1 </sup> Derbyshire contains many species at the edge of their UK distribution ranges. Some species with a predominantly northern British distribution are at the southern limit of their range, whilst others with a more southern distribution are at their northern limit in Derbyshire. As climate change progresses, a number of sensitive species are now being seen to be either expanding or contracting their range as a result.[^cite_note-willmotmoyes-24]<sup>: 314 </sup> For the purposes of protecting and recording the county's most important habitats, Derbyshire has been split into two regions, each with its own [Biodiversity Action Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity_Action_Plan) (BAP), based around National Character Areas. The Peak District BAP includes all of Derbyshire's uplands of the Dark Peak, South-West Peak and White Peak, including an area of limestone beyond the national park boundary.[^cite_note-25] The remaining areas are monitored and recorded in the Lowland Derbyshire Biodiversity Action Plan, which subdivides the landscape into eight smaller Action Areas.[^cite_note-26]

The Derbyshire Biological Records Centre was formerly based at Derby Museum and Art Gallery, but since 2011 has been managed by Derbyshire Wildlife Trust.[^cite_note-27] Two of the 48 English [Local Nature Partnerships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Nature_Partnership) (LNP) also cover Derbyshire; these are the Peak District LNP and the Lowland Derbyshire & Nottinghamshire LNP.[^cite_note-28]

In October 2025, Derbyshire County Council approved Derbyshire's Local Nature Recovery Strategy, which outlines how nature recovery will be supported and implemented across the county.[^cite_note-29]

### Botany

Since 2002, the [county flower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_flower) for Derbyshire has been [Jacob's-ladder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polemonium_caeruleum) (*Polemonium caeruleum*), a relatively rare species, and characteristic of certain limestone dales in the White Peak.[^cite_note-willmotmoyes-24]<sup>: 187 </sup> Derbyshire is known to have contained 1,919 separate taxa of vascular plants (including species, hybrids and micro-species) since modern recording began,[^cite_note-willmotmoyes-24]<sup>: 409 </sup> of which 1,133 are known to be either native or [archaeophyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeophyte), the remainder being non-native species. These comprise 336 established species, 433 casuals and 17 unassigned. It is known that 34 species of plants once native here have been lost from Derbyshire (i.e. become locally extinct) since modern plant recording began in the 17th century.[^cite_note-willmotmoyes-24]<sup>: 410 </sup> Derbyshire contains two [endemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endemism) vascular plants, found nowhere else in the world: *[Rubus durescens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubus_durescens)*, a [bramble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bramble) occurring in central Derbyshire,[^cite_note-willmotmoyes-24]<sup>: 89 </sup> and Derby hawkweed (*[Hieracium naviense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hieracium_naviense)*), still known only from [Winnats Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winnats_Pass).[^cite_note-willmotmoyes-24]<sup>: 263 </sup> One endemic species of moss, [Derbyshire Feather Moss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thamnobryum_angustifolium), occurs in one small 3-metre patch in just one Derbyshire limestone dale, its sole world location intentionally kept confidential.[^cite_note-30]

The distribution and status of vascular plants in Derbyshire have been recorded over the last 120 years in a series of four major botanical works, each by different authors between 1889 and 2015, all entitled *The Flora of Derbyshire*. Plant recording is mainly undertaken locally by volunteers from the Derbyshire Flora Group,[^cite_note-willmotmoyes-24]<sup>: 406 </sup> and by staff at [Derbyshire Wildlife Trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derbyshire_Wildlife_Trust) and the Peak District National Park.

The Dark Peak is marked by heathlands, bogs, gritstone edges and acid grasslands containing relatively few species, with plants such as [heather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calluna) (*Calluna vulgaris*), [crowberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empetrum_nigrum) (*Empetrum nigrum*), [bilberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaccinium_myrtillus) (*Vaccinium myrtillus*) and [hare's-tail cotton grass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eriophorum_vaginatum) (*Eriophorum vaginatum*) being dominant on the high moors.[^cite_note-willmotmoyes-24]<sup>: 6 </sup> The dales of the White Peak are known for habitats such as [calcareous grassland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcareous_grassland), ash woodlands and rock outcrops in all of which a much greater richness of lime-loving species occurs than elsewhere in the county.[^cite_note-willmotmoyes-24]<sup>: 4 </sup> These include various orchids (such as [early purple orchid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orchis_mascula) (*Orchis mascula*), [dark-red helleborine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epipactis_atrorubens) (*Epipactis atrorubens*) and [fly orchid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ophrys_insectifera) (*Ophrys insectifera*)), [common rockrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helianthemum_nummularium) (*Helianthemum nummularium*), [spring cinquefoil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helianthemum_nummularium) (*Helianthemum nummularium*) and [grass of parnassus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parnassia) (*Parnassia palustris*). Specialised communities of plants occur on former lead workings, where typical [metallophyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallophyte) species include [spring sandwort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minuartia) (*Minuartia verna*), [alpine penny-cress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thlaspi_caerulescens) (*Thlaspi caerulescens*) (both known locally in Derbyshire as Leadwort), as well as [mountain pansy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viola_lutea) (*Viola lutea*) and [moonwort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botrychium_lunaria) (*Botrychium lunaria*).[^cite_note-willmotmoyes-24]<sup>: 6 </sup>[^cite_note-Lead-31]

In 2015, Derbyshire contained 304 vascular plant species now designated as of international, national or local conservation concern, for their rarity or recent declines, and collectively listed as Derbyshire Red Data plants.[^cite_note-willmotmoyes-24]<sup>: 418 </sup> Work on recording and publishing a bryophyte flora for Derbyshire still continues: by 2012 a total of 518 bryophyte species had been recorded for the county.[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33]

Botanical recording in the UK predominantly uses the unchanging [vice-county](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vice-county) boundary system, which results in a slightly different map of Derbyshire from the modern geographic county.[^cite_note-willmotmoyes-24]<sup>: 20 </sup>

### Zoology

A number of specialist organisations protect, promote and monitor records of individual animal groups across Derbyshire. The main ones are Derbyshire Ornithological Society; Derbyshire Mammal Group; Derbyshire Bat Group, Derbyshire Amphibian and Reptile Group, and the Derbyshire & Nottingham Entomological Society. All maintain databases of wildlife sightings, whilst some such as the Derbyshire Ornithological Society provide alerts of rare sightings on their websites or social media pages and also publish major works describing the status and distribution of species.[^cite_note-34]

## Economy



The rugged moorland edge of the southern Pennines at Kinder Downfall

Derbyshire has a mixture of a rural economy in the west, with a former coal-mining economy in the north-east (Bolsover district), the Erewash Valley around Ilkeston and in the south around Swadlincote. The rural landscape varies from arable farmland in the flatlands to the south of Derby, to [upland pasture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upland_pasture) and [moorland](553383.md) in the high [gritstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gritstone) uplands of the southern Pennines.[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36]

Derbyshire is rich in natural mineral resources such as lead, iron, [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleasley_Colliery), and limestone, which have been exploited over a long period. Lead, for example, has been mined since [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Britain) times. The limestone outcrops in the central area led to the establishment of large [quarries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarry) to supply the industries of surrounding towns with [lime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lime_\(material\)) for building and [steelmaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steelmaking), and latterly in the 20th-century cement manufacture.[^cite_note-37] The Industrial Revolution also increased demand for [building stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masonry), and in the late 19th and early 20th-century, the arrival of the railways led to a large number of stone quarries being established.[^cite_note-38] This industry has left its mark on the countryside, but is still a major industry: a lot of the stone is supplied as [crushed stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crushed_stone) for [road building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road#Construction) and concrete manufacture, and is moved by rail.[^cite_note-39]



The ruins of the [Magpie Mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magpie_Mine) near [Sheldon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheldon,_Derbyshire)

Derbyshire's relative remoteness in the late 18th century and an abundance of fast-flowing streams led to a proliferation of the use of [hydropower](14073.md) at the beginning of the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution), following the mills pioneered by [Richard Arkwright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Arkwright).[^cite_note-40] Derbyshire has been said to be the home of the Industrial Revolution, and part of the [Derwent Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derwent_Valley_Mills) has been given [World Heritage status](44940.md) in acknowledgement of this historic importance.[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42]

Nationally famous companies in Derbyshire include [Rolls-Royce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolls-Royce_Holdings), one of the world's leading aerospace companies, based since before World War I in Derby, [Thorntons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorntons) just south of Alfreton and [Toyota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyota_Manufacturing_UK), who have one of the UK's largest car manufacturing plants at [Burnaston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnaston).[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44] Ashbourne Water used to be bottled in Buxton by [Nestlé Waters UK](160227.md) until 2006 and Buxton Water still is.[^cite_note-45]

Derbyshire is one of only three counties permitted to make cheese that is labelled as [Stilton cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stilton_cheese). The others are [Leicestershire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leicestershire) and [Nottinghamshire](68049.md).[^cite_note-46] The smallest of six companies making this product is Hartington Creamery at [Pikehall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pikehall). As of March 2021, Hartington Stilton was marketing within the UK but also exporting to the US, EU and Canada. The company director told the BBC that they had "a surge in interest and consumer sales from the US".[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48]

County Hall, Matlock

Derbyshire has a three-tier local government since the [local government reorganisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Government_Act_1972) in 1974. It has a [county council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derbyshire_County_Council) based in [Matlock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matlock,_Derbyshire) and eight [district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-metropolitan_district) councils and since 1997, a [unitary authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_authority) area of the City of Derby. Derby remains part of Derbyshire only for ceremonial purposes.

Derbyshire has become fractionally smaller during government reorganisation over the years. The Sheffield suburbs Woodseats, Beauchief, Handsworth, Woodhouse, Norton, [Mosborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosborough_\(ward\)), [Totley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totley), [Bradway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradway) and [Dore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dore,_South_Yorkshire) were previously parts of the county, but were lost to [Sheffield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheffield) between 1900 and 1933; [Mosborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosborough) was transferred in 1967. However, Derbyshire gained part of the [Longdendale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longdendale) valley and [Tintwistle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tintwistle) from Cheshire in 1974. The current area of the geographic/ceremonial county of Derbyshire is only 4.7 square kilometres less than it was over 100 years ago.[^cite_note-willmotmoyes-24]<sup>: 1 </sup>[^cite_note-willmotmoyes-24]<sup>: 20 </sup>

At the third tier are the [parish councils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parish_councils_in_England), which do not cover all areas. The eight district councils in Derbyshire and the unitary authority of Derby are shown in the map above.

These district councils are responsible for local planning and building control, local roads, [council housing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_house), [environmental health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_health), markets and fairs, refuse collection and recycling, cemeteries and crematoria, leisure services, parks, and tourism.[^cite_note-lc-50] Education, social services, libraries, main roads, public transport, policing and fire services, [trading standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trading_standards), waste disposal and strategic planning are the responsibility of the County Council.[^cite_note-lc-50]



One of many Victorian village schools in Derbyshire

Although Derbyshire is in the [East Midlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Midlands), some parts, such as [High Peak](345600.md) (which incorporated former areas of [Cheshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheshire) after boundary changes in 1974), the [Borough of Chesterfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_of_Chesterfield) and [North East Derbyshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_East_Derbyshire) are closer to the northern cities of [Manchester](20206.md) and [Sheffield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheffield) and these receive services more affiliated with northern England; for example, the [North West Ambulance Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_West_Ambulance_Service), [Granada Television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITV_Granada), [BBC Look North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Look_North) and [United Utilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Utilities).Outside the main city of Derby, the largest town in the county is [Chesterfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chesterfield,_Derbyshire).

Derbyshire is also part of multiple [combined authorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combined_authorities). The Erewash, Amber Valley and Derby districts are part of the [D2N2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Derby,_Derbyshire,_Nottingham_and_Nottinghamshire_Local_Enterprise_Partnership) partnership with neighbouring Nottinghamshire. The Derbyshire Dales, Bolsover, North East Derbyshire and Chesterfield districts are part of the [South Yorkshire Mayoral Combined Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Yorkshire_Mayoral_Combined_Authority) (as non constituent members).

Derbyshire County Council and Derby City Council are part of the [East Midlands Combined Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Midlands_Combined_County_Authority), and elected the first Mayor of the East Midlands in the [2024 East Midlands Mayoral Election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_East_Midlands_mayoral_election).

### Future

Main article: [Upcoming structural changes to local government in England § Derbyshire and Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upcoming_structural_changes_to_local_government_in_England#Derbyshire_and_Derby)

In July 2026, the [UK Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_Government) announced that it intends to reorganise Derbyshire into two [unitary authority areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_authority_areas) by April 2028. The county will be divided north–south, with [Derbyshire North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derbyshire_North) combining the current districts of Bolsover, Chesterfield, Derbyshire Dales, High Peak, North East Derbyshire, and the northern part of Amber Valley, and [Derbyshire South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derbyshire_South) combining Derby, Erewash, South Derbyshire, and the southern part of Amber Valley. Elections to the new unitary councils will take place in May 2027.[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52]

## Sport

Derbyshire has two [Football League](14419588.md) teams, [Derby County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derby_County_F.C.), who play in the [EFL Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EFL_Championship), the second tier of English football. The next highest-placed team is [Chesterfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chesterfield_F.C.), who participate in [EFL League Two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EFL_League_Two), the fourth tier of English football. There are also many non-league teams playing throughout the county, most notably [Alfreton Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfreton_Town_F.C.), who play in the [Northern Premier League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Premier_League).[^cite_note-alfretontown-80] The county is currently home to the world's oldest football club, [Sheffield F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheffield_F.C.), who play in Dronfield in north-east Derbyshire.[^cite_note-sheffieldfc-81] [Glossop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossop) was the smallest town in the country to have a football team in the top tier of English football, [Glossop North End](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossop_North_End_A.F.C.).[^cite_note-glossopnorthend-82]



County Cricket Ground, in Derby

Derbyshire has a [cricket](25675557.md) team based at the County Cricket Ground. [Derbyshire County Cricket Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derbyshire_County_Cricket_Club) currently play in Division Two of the [County Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Championship). There are also [rugby league](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_league) clubs based in the north of the county, the North Derbyshire Chargers and in Derby (Derby City RLFC). The county has numerous rugby union clubs, including Derby, Chesterfield Panthers, Matlock, Ilkeston, Ashbourne, Bakewell and Amber Valley.

The county is a popular area for a variety of recreational sports such as [rock climbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_climbing_in_the_Peak_District), [hill walking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walking_in_the_United_Kingdom#Hillwalking), [hang gliding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hang_gliding), [caving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caving), sailing on its many reservoirs, and cycling along the many miles of disused rail tracks that have been turned into cycle trails, such as the [Monsal Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monsal_Trail) and [High Peak Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Peak_Trail).

The town of [Ashbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashbourne,_Derbyshire) in Derbyshire is known for its [Royal Shrovetide Football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Shrovetide_Football), described as a "medieval football game", played annually on [Shrove Tuesday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrove_Tuesday) and [Ash Wednesday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ash_Wednesday).

Derbyshire is host to one of the only community [Muggle quidditch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muggle_quidditch) teams in the country, known as Derby Union Quidditch Club. The Club recruits players from the age of 16 upwards from all over Derby, and has representatives from most local sixth forms and the University of Derby. The team has competed against both the Leeds Griffins and the Leicester Lovegoods in the past and is part of the vibrant UK quidditch scene. It is also an official [International Quidditch Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Quidditch_Association) team.

## Local attractions



The 'twisted spire' on [Chesterfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chesterfield,_Derbyshire) parish church

The county of Derbyshire has many attractions for tourists and local people. It offers [Peak District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_District) scenery such as [Mam Tor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mam_Tor) and [Kinder Scout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinder_Scout), and more urban attractions such as [Bakewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakewell), [Buxton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buxton) and [Derby](18727355.md). Such places include [Bolsover Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolsover_Castle), [Castleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castleton,_Derbyshire), [Chatsworth House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chatsworth_House), [Hopton Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopton_Hall), [National Tramway Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Tramway_Museum) at Crich, [Peak Rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_Rail) steam railway, [Midland Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midland_Railway) steam railway, [Dovedale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dovedale), [Haddon Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haddon_Hall), the [Heights of Abraham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heights_of_Abraham) and [Matlock Bath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matlock_Bath).[^cite_note-83]

In the north of the county, three large reservoirs, [Howden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howden_Reservoir), [Derwent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derwent_Reservoir_\(Derbyshire\)) and [Ladybower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ladybower_Reservoir), were built in the early part of the 20th century to supply the rapidly growing populations of Sheffield, Derby and [Leicester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leicester) with drinking water. The moorland catchment area around these is part of the Peak District National Park and extensively used for leisure pursuits such as walking and cycling.

There are many properties and lands in the care of the [National Trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Trust) that are open to the public, such as [Calke Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calke_Abbey), [Hardwick Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardwick_Hall), [High Peak Estate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Peak_Estate), [Ilam Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilam_Park), [Kedleston Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kedleston_Hall), [Longshaw Estate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longshaw_Estate) near [Hathersage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hathersage), and [Sudbury Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudbury_Hall) on the [Staffordshire](58733.md) border.

Notable gardens in Derbyshire include the formal ones in 17th–18th-century French style at [Melbourne Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melbourne_Hall) south of Derby, the [listed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Register_of_Historic_Parks_and_Gardens) garden at [Renishaw Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renishaw_Hall) near [Eckington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eckington,_Derbyshire), Lea Rhododendron Gardens near [Matlock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matlock,_Derbyshire), the [Royal Horticultural Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Horticultural_Society) recommended [Bluebell Arboretum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluebell_Arboretum) near [Swadlincote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swadlincote), and the extensive gardens at Chatsworth House.

[Ardotalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ardotalia), also known as Melandra, or Melandra Castle, is an ancient Roman fort built in the north-west of the county. The ruins and foundations are open free of charge to the public.

## County emblems



The flag of the historic county of Derbyshire

As part of a [2002 marketing campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_flowers_of_the_United_Kingdom), the plant conservation charity [Plantlife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantlife) chose the [Jacob's-ladder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polemonium_caeruleum) as the [county flower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_flower).

In September 2006, a proposal for a [Derbyshire county flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Derbyshire) was introduced, largely on the initiative of [BBC Radio Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_Derby).[^cite_note-flag-84] It consists of a white-bordered dark green cross encompassing a golden [Tudor rose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_rose) (a historical symbol of the county) all set in a blue field. The blue field represents the many waters of the county, its rivers and reservoirs, while the cross is green to mark the great areas of countryside. The flag was subsequently registered with the [Flag Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_Institute) in September 2008.[^cite_note-FI-register-85]

In 2015, [BBC Radio Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_Derby) commissioned a Derbyshire anthem entitled "Our Derbyshire", including lyrics suggested by its listeners. It received its first performance on 17 September 2015 at [Derby Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derby_Cathedral).

In 1801 the population was 147,481[^cite_note-population-87][^cite_note-derby-88] This had grown to 272,217 inhabitants by the time of the 1841 census.[^cite_note-89] According to the [UK Census 2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_Census_2001) there were 956,301 people spread over the county's 254,615 hectares.[^cite_note-90] This was estimated to have risen to 990,400 in 2006.[^cite_note-91]

The county's population grew by 3.0 per cent from 1991 to 2001 which is around 21,100 people. This figure is higher than the national average of 2.65 per cent, but lower than the East Midlands average of 4.0 per cent. The county as a whole has an average population density of 2.9 people per hectare, making it less densely populated than England as a whole.[^cite_note-Key_Derbyshire_Stats-92] The density varies throughout the county, with the lowest being in the region of [Derbyshire Dales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derbyshire_Dales) at 0.88 per hectare, and the highest outside the main cities in the region of [Erewash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_of_Erewash), which has 10.04 people per hectare.[^cite_note-Derbyshire_2001-93]

The 1969 film *[Women in Love](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_Love_\(film\))* by [Ken Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Russell) had scenes filmed in and around [Elvaston Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elvaston_Castle), notably the Greco-Roman wrestling scene, which was filmed in the castle's Great Hall.[^cite_note-94]

The 1988 film *[The Lair of the White Worm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lair_of_the_White_Worm_\(film\))* by Ken Russell, (starring [Amanda Donohoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amanda_Donohoe) and [Hugh Grant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Grant)) was filmed largely in Derbyshire.[^cite_note-95] *Lair's* opening title sequence and primary story locations occur in and around [Thor's Cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thor's_Cave), part of the [Peak District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_District), [River Manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Manifold) valley.[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-97]

The 2008 film *[The Duchess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Duchess_\(film\))* includes scenes filmed at [Chatsworth House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chatsworth_House) and at [Kedleston Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kedleston_Hall).[^cite_note-duchess-98]

The 1993–2002 TV series *[Peak Practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_Practice)* was set in [Crich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crich) and [Fritchley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritchley), except for the twelfth and final series, and originally starred [Kevin Whately](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Whately) and [Amanda Burton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amanda_Burton).[^cite_note-99] In 2003, an unrelated and less successful medical TV drama, *[Sweet Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweet_Medicine)*, was mostly filmed in the historic market town of Wirksworth.

Other Derbyshire locations in which British TV scenes have been filmed include:[^cite_note-100]

- [Alderwasley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alderwasley): *[Stig of the Dump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stig_of_the_Dump)*

- [Ashbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashbourne,_Derbyshire) and Vernon Street in [Derby](18727355.md): *[Nanny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanny_\(TV_series\))*

- [Chesterfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chesterfield,_Derbyshire): The twisted spire of [Church of St Mary and All Saints, Chesterfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_St_Mary_and_All_Saints,_Chesterfield), was made famous by its use in the opening credits of the 1966–1971 ecclesiastical BBC TV sitcom *[All Gas and Gaiters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Gas_and_Gaiters)*, featuring [Derek Nimmo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek_Nimmo).

- [Hadfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadfield,_Derbyshire): *[The League of Gentlemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_League_of_Gentlemen)*

- [The Peak District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Peak_District) is the scene of a series of four crime novels of the 21st century by [Sarah Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Ward_\(novelist\)): *Bitter Chill* (2015), *The Shrouded Path* (2020), *A Deadly Thaw* and *A Patient Fury*.

- [Repton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repton) and especially [Repton School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repton_School): *[Goodbye, Mr. Chips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodbye,_Mr._Chips)* (in both [1939](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodbye,_Mr._Chips_\(1939_film\)) and 1983 versions)
