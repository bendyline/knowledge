**Netflix, Inc.** is an American [media company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_conglomerate) founded on August 29, 1997, by [Reed Hastings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reed_Hastings) and [Marc Randolph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc_Randolph) in [Scotts Valley, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotts_Valley,_California), and currently based in [Los Gatos, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Gatos,_California), with production offices and stages at the [Los Angeles](18110.md)–based [Hollywood studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netflix_Hollywood_Studios) (formerly old [Warner Brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warner_Brothers) studios) and the [Albuquerque Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netflix_Albuquerque_Studios) (formerly ABQ studios). It owns and operates an [eponymous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netflix) [over-the-top](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Over-the-top_media_service) [subscription video on-demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_on_demand#Subscription_models) service, which showcases acquired and [original programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Netflix_original_programming) as well as third-party content licensed from other [production companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production_company) and distributors. Netflix is also the first [streaming media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media) company to be a member of the [Motion Picture Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_Picture_Association).

Netflix initially both sold and rented DVDs [by mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DVD-by-mail), but the sales were eliminated within a year to focus on the DVD rental business.[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-Netflixed-4] In 2007, Netflix introduced streaming media and [video on demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_on_demand). The company expanded to Canada in 2010, followed by Latin America and the Caribbean. In 2011, the service began to acquire and produce original content, beginning with the crime drama *[Lilyhammer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lilyhammer)*.

The company has been listed on [Nasdaq](21559.md) since 2002 and is ranked 117th on the [*Fortune* 500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_500)[^cite_note-5] and 219th on the [*Forbes* Global 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forbes_Global_2000).[^cite_note-6] It is the largest entertainment/media company by [market capitalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_capitalization) as of 2026 estimated at \$450 billion.[^cite_note-7] In 2021, Netflix was ranked as the eighth-most trusted brand globally by [Morning Consult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morning_Consult).[^cite_note-8] During the 2010s, Netflix was the top-performing [stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock) in the [S\&P 500](170992.md) [stock market index](23408957.md), with a total return of 3,693%.[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10]

The company has two CEOs, [Greg Peters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greg_Peters_\(businessman\)) and [Ted Sarandos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Sarandos), who are split between Los Gatos and Los Angeles, respectively.[^cite_note-co-CEO_2020-11][^cite_note-Bloomberg2019-12] It also operates international offices in Asia, Europe and Latin America including in Canada, France, Brazil, the Netherlands, India, Italy, Japan, Poland, South Korea, and the United Kingdom. The company has production hubs in Los Angeles,[^cite_note-13] [Albuquerque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albuquerque,_New_Mexico),[^cite_note-14] [London](17867.md),[^cite_note-15] [Madrid](41188263.md), [Vancouver](32706.md) and [Toronto](64646.md).[^cite_note-16]

### Launch as a mail-based rental business (1997–2006)



Marc Randolph, co-founder of Netflix and the first CEO of the company



Reed Hastings, co-founder and former chairman

Netflix was founded by [Marc Randolph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc_Randolph) and [Reed Hastings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reed_Hastings) on August 29, 1997, in [Scotts Valley, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotts_Valley,_California). Hastings, a computer scientist and mathematician, was a cofounder of [Pure Software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_Software), which was acquired by [Rational Software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_Software) that year for \$750 million, the then biggest acquisition in [Silicon Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_Valley) history.[^cite_note-17] Randolph had worked as a marketing director for [Pure Software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_Software) after Pure Atria acquired a company where Randolph worked. He was previously a co-founder of [MicroWarehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MicroWarehouse), a computer mail-order company as well as vice president of marketing for [Borland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borland).[^cite_note-started-18][^cite_note-19] Hastings and Randolph came up with the idea for Netflix while [carpooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpool) between their homes in [Santa Cruz, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Cruz,_California), and Pure Atria's headquarters in Sunnyvale.[^cite_note-Netflixed-4] [Patty McCord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patty_McCord), later head of [human resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_resources) at Netflix, was also in the carpool group.[^cite_note-20] Randolph admired [Amazon](90451.md) and wanted to find a large category of portable items to sell over the Internet using a similar model. Hastings and Randolph considered and rejected selling and renting [VHS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VHS) as too expensive to stock and too delicate to ship.[^cite_note-started-18] When they heard about DVDs, first introduced in the United States in early 1997, they tested the concept of selling or renting DVDs by mail by mailing a compact disc to Hastings's house in Santa Cruz.[^cite_note-started-18] When the disc arrived intact, they decided to enter the \$16 billion [home video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_video) sales and rental industry.[^cite_note-started-18][^cite_note-Netflixed-4] Hastings is often quoted saying that he decided to start Netflix after being fined \$40 at a Blockbuster store for being late to return a copy of [*Apollo 13*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_13_\(film\)), a claim since repudiated by Randolph.[^cite_note-Netflixed-4] Hastings invested \$2.5 million into Netflix from the sale of Pure Atria.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-Netflixed-4] Netflix launched as the first DVD rental and sales website with 30 employees and 925 titles available—nearly all DVDs published.[^cite_note-Netflixed-4][^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23] Randolph and Hastings met with [Jeff Bezos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeff_Bezos), where [Amazon](90451.md) offered to acquire Netflix for between \$14 and \$16 million. Fearing competition from Amazon, Randolph at first thought the offer was fair, but Hastings, who owned 70% of the company, turned it down on the plane ride home.[^cite_note-chaos-24][^cite_note-25]

Initially, Netflix offered a per-rental model for each DVD but introduced a monthly subscription concept in September 1999.[^cite_note-26] The per-rental model was dropped by early 2000, allowing the company to focus on the business model of flat-fee unlimited rentals without due dates, late fees, shipping and handling fees, or per-title rental fees.[^cite_note-27] In September 2000, during the [dot-com bubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot-com_bubble), while Netflix was suffering losses, Hastings and Randolph offered to sell the company to [Blockbuster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockbuster_\(retailer\)) for \$50 million. [John Antioco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Antioco), CEO of Blockbuster, thought the offer was a joke and declined, saying, "The dot-com hysteria is completely overblown."[^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29] While Netflix experienced fast growth in early 2001, the continued effects of the dot-com bubble collapse and the [September 11 attacks](5058690.md) caused the company to hold off plans for its [initial public offering](63879.md) (IPO) and to lay off one-third of its 120 employees.[^cite_note-30]



Opened Netflix rental envelope containing a DVD copy of *[Coach Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coach_Carter)* (2005)

DVD players were a popular gift for holiday sales in late 2001, and demand for DVD subscription services were "growing like crazy", according to chief talent officer [Patty McCord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patty_McCord).[^cite_note-31] The company went public on [NASDAQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASDAQ) under the ticker symbol NFLX on May 29, 2002, selling 5.5 million shares of [common stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_stock) at US\$15.00 per share.[^cite_note-32] In 2003, Netflix was issued a patent by the [U.S. Patent and Trademark Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Patent_and_Trademark_Office) to cover its subscription rental service and several extensions.[^cite_note-33] Netflix posted its first profit in 2003, earning \$6.5 million on revenues of \$272 million; by 2004, profit had increased to \$49 million on over \$500 million in revenues.[^cite_note-nbcnews_lowersubs_2007-34] In 2005, 35,000 different films were available, and Netflix shipped 1 million DVDs out every day.[^cite_note-35]

In 2004, Blockbuster introduced a DVD rental service, which not only allowed users to check out titles through online sites but allowed for them to return them at [brick and-mortar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brick_and_mortar) stores.[^cite_note-cnbc_blockbuster_war-36] By 2006, Blockbuster's service reached two million users, and while trailing Netflix's subscriber count, was drawing business away from Netflix. Netflix lowered fees in 2007.[^cite_note-nbcnews_lowersubs_2007-34] While it was an [urban legend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_legend) that Netflix ultimately "killed" Blockbuster in the DVD rental market, Blockbuster's debt load and internal disagreements hurt the company.[^cite_note-cnbc_blockbuster_war-36]

On April 4, 2006, Netflix filed a [patent infringement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patent_infringement) lawsuit in which it demanded a [jury trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jury_trial) in the [U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._District_Court_for_the_Northern_District_of_California), alleging that [Blockbuster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockbuster_\(retailer\))'s [online DVD rental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DVD-by-mail) subscription program violated two patents held by Netflix. The first [cause of action](3221417.md) alleged Blockbuster's infringement of copying the "dynamic queue" of DVDs available for each customer, Netflix's method of using the ranked preferences in the queue to send DVDs to subscribers, and Netflix's method permitting the queue to be updated and reordered.[^cite_note-37] The second cause of action alleged infringement of the subscription rental service as well as Netflix's methods of communication and delivery.[^cite_note-38] The companies settled their dispute on June 25, 2007; terms were not disclosed.[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42]

On October 1, 2006, Netflix announced the [Netflix Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netflix_Prize), \$1,000,000 to the first developer of a video [recommendation algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recommendation_algorithm) that could beat its existing algorithm Cinematch, at predicting customer ratings by more than 10%. On September 21, 2009, it awarded the \$1,000,000 prize to team "BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos".[^cite_note-43] Cinematch, launched in 2000, is a recommendation system that recommended movies to its users, many of which they might not ever had heard of before.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45]

Through its division [Red Envelope Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Envelope_Entertainment), Netflix licensed and distributed independent films such as *[Born into Brothels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_into_Brothels)* and *[Sherrybaby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherrybaby)*. In late 2006, [Red Envelope Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Envelope_Entertainment) also expanded into producing original content with filmmakers such as [John Waters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Waters).[^cite_note-46] Netflix closed Red Envelope Entertainment in 2008.[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48]

### Transition to streaming services (2007–2012)

In January 2007, the company launched a [streaming media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media) service, introducing [video on demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_on_demand) via the Internet. However, at that time it only had 1,000 films available for streaming, compared to 70,000 available on DVD.[^cite_note-49] The company had for some time considered offering movies online, but it was only in the mid-2000s that data speeds and bandwidth costs had improved sufficiently to allow customers to download movies from the net. The original idea was a "Netflix box" that could download movies overnight, and be ready to watch the next day. By 2005, Netflix had acquired movie rights and designed the box and service. But after witnessing how popular streaming services such as YouTube were despite the lack of high-definition content, the concept of using a hardware device was scrapped and replaced with a streaming concept.[^cite_note-50]

In February 2007, Netflix delivered its billionth DVD, a copy of *[Babel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babel_\(2006_film\))* to a customer in Texas.[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52] In April 2007, Netflix recruited [ReplayTV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ReplayTV) founder [Anthony Wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Wood_\(businessman\)), to build a "Netflix Player" that would allow streaming content to be played directly on a television rather than a desktop or laptop.[^cite_note-53] Hastings eventually shut down the project to help encourage other hardware manufacturers to include built-in Netflix support, which would be spun off as the [digital media player](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_media_player) product [Roku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roku).[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55][^cite_note-Fastcompany-56]

In January 2008, all rental-disc subscribers became entitled to unlimited streaming at no additional cost. This change came in a response to the introduction of [Hulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulu) and to [Apple](856.md)'s new video-rental services.[^cite_note-57][^cite_note-58] In August 2008, the Netflix database was corrupted and the company was not able to ship DVDs to customers for 3 days, leading the company to move all its data to the [Amazon Web Services](1691376.md) cloud.[^cite_note-59] In November 2008, Netflix began offering subscribers rentals on [Blu-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blu-ray) and discontinued its sale of used DVDs.[^cite_note-60] In 2009, Netflix streams overtook DVD shipments.[^cite_note-61]

On January 6, 2010, Netflix agreed with [Warner Bros.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warner_Bros.) to delay new release rentals 28 days prior to retail, in an attempt to help studios sell physical copies, and similar deals involving [Universal Pictures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Pictures) and [20th Century Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20th_Century_Fox) were reached on April 9.[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64] In July 2010, Netflix signed a deal to stream movies of [Relativity Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativity_Media).[^cite_note-65] In August 2010, Netflix reached a five-year deal worth nearly \$1 billion to stream films from [Paramount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramount_Pictures), [Lionsgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lionsgate_Films) and [Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer). The deal increased Netflix's annual spending fees, adding roughly \$200 million per year. It spent \$117 million in the first six months of 2010 on streaming, up from \$31 million in 2009.[^cite_note-66] On September 22, 2010, Netflix launched in Canada, its first international market.[^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68] In November 2010, Netflix began offering a standalone streaming service separate from DVD rentals.[^cite_note-69]

In 2010, Netflix acquired the rights to *[Breaking Bad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breaking_Bad)*, produced by Sony Pictures Television, after the show's third season, at a point where original broadcaster [AMC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AMC_\(TV_channel\)) had expressed the possibility of cancelling the show. Sony pushed Netflix to release *Breaking Bad* in time for the fourth season, which as a result greatly expanded the show's audience on AMC due to new viewers binging on the Netflix past episodes, and doubling the viewership by the time of the fifth season. *Breaking Bad* is considered the first such show to have this "Netflix effect".[^cite_note-70]

In January 2011, Netflix announced agreements with several manufacturers to include [branded Netflix buttons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netflix_button) on the [remote controls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_control) of devices compatible with the service, such as [Blu-ray players](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blu-ray).[^cite_note-71] By May 2011, Netflix had become the largest source of Internet streaming traffic in North America, accounting for 30% of traffic during peak hours.[^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73][^cite_note-74][^cite_note-75]

On July 12, 2011, Netflix announced that it would separate its existing subscription plans into two separate plans: one covering the streaming and the other DVD rental services.[^cite_note-76][^cite_note-hikes-77] The cost for streaming would be \$7.99 per month, while DVD rental would start at the same price.[^cite_note-fire-78] On September 11, 2011, Netflix expanded to 43 countries in Latin America.[^cite_note-79][^cite_note-80][^cite_note-81] On September 18, 2011, Netflix announced its intentions to rebrand and restructure its DVD home media rental service as an independent subsidiary called Qwikster, separating DVD rental and streaming services.[^cite_note-82][^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84][^cite_note-85][^cite_note-86] On September 26, 2011, Netflix announced a content deal with [DreamWorks Animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DreamWorks_Animation).[^cite_note-87] On October 10, 2011, Netflix announced that it would retain its DVD service under the name Netflix and that its streaming and DVD-rental plans would remain branded together.[^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89]

On January 9, 2012, Netflix started its expansion to Europe, launching in the United Kingdom and Ireland.[^cite_note-90] In February 2012, Netflix reached a multi-year agreement with [The Weinstein Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Weinstein_Company).[^cite_note-91][^cite_note-92] In March 2012, Netflix acquired the [domain name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_name) DVD.com.[^cite_note-93] By 2016, Netflix rebranded its DVD-by-mail service under the name *DVD.com, A Netflix Company*.[^cite_note-94][^cite_note-95] In April 2012, Netflix filed with the [Federal Election Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Election_Commission) (FEC) to form a [political action committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_action_committee) (PAC) called FLIXPAC.[^cite_note-FLIXPAC-96] Netflix spokesperson Joris Evers tweeted that the intent was to "engage on issues like [net neutrality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_neutrality), [bandwidth caps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandwidth_cap), [UBB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usage-based_billing) and [VPPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_Privacy_Protection_Act)".[^cite_note-97][^cite_note-98] In June 2012, Netflix signed a deal with [Open Road Films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Road_Films).[^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100]

On August 23, 2012, Netflix and [The Weinstein Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Weinstein_Company) signed a multi-year output deal for RADiUS-TWC films.[^cite_note-101][^cite_note-102] In August 2010, [Epix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epix) signed a five-year streaming deal with Netflix.[^cite_note-103] For the initial two years of this agreement, first-run and back-catalog content from Epix was exclusive to Netflix. Epix films came to Netflix 90 days after premiering on Epix.[^cite_note-104] These included films from Paramount, Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer and Lionsgate.[^cite_note-105][^cite_note-106]

On October 18, 2012, Netflix launched in Denmark, Finland, Norway and Sweden.[^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108] On December 4, 2012, Netflix and Disney announced an exclusive multi-year agreement for first-run United States subscription television rights to Walt Disney Studios' animated and live-action films, with classics such as *[Dumbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumbo)*, *[Alice in Wonderland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice_in_Wonderland_\(1951_film\))* and *[Pocahontas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pocahontas_\(1995_film\))* available immediately and others available on Netflix beginning in 2016.[^cite_note-109] [Direct-to-video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct-to-video) releases were made available in 2013.[^cite_note-110][^cite_note-111]

On January 14, 2013, Netflix signed an agreement with [Time Warner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WarnerMedia)'s [Turner Broadcasting System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turner_Broadcasting_System) and Warner Bros. Television to distribute [Cartoon Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartoon_Network), [Warner Bros. Animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warner_Bros._Animation), and [Adult Swim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adult_Swim) content, as well as [TNT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TNT_\(American_TV_network\))'s *[Dallas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallas_\(2012_TV_series\))*, beginning in March 2013. The rights to these programs were given to Netflix shortly after deals with [Viacom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viacom_\(2005%E2%80%932019\)) to stream [Nickelodeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickelodeon) and [Nick Jr. Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Jr._Channel) programs expired.[^cite_note-112]

For cost reasons, Netflix stated that it would limit its expansion in 2013,[^cite_note-113] adding only one new market—the Netherlands—in September of that year. This expanded its availability to 40 territories.[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115]

Netflix began efforts to develop original content in 2011; in March, Netflix made a straight-to-series order for the [Kevin Spacey](16768.md)-led political drama *[House of Cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Cards_\(American_TV_series\))* from [MRC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MRC_\(company\)). Beating out other U.S. cable networks, it marked the first first-run television series to be specifically commissioned by the service.[^cite_note-116] In November 2011, Netflix then ordered *[Orange is the New Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_is_the_New_Black)*, a comedy-drama adapted from [Piper Kerman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piper_Kerman)'s [memoir of the same name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_Is_the_New_Black:_My_Year_in_a_Women's_Prison),[^cite_note-117] and a new season of the cancelled Fox sitcom *[Arrested Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrested_Development)*.[^cite_note-netflix_revival-118] Netflix also acquired U.S. rights to the Norwegian drama *[Lilyhammer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lilyhammer)*; following its television premiere on Norway's [NRK1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NRK1) on January 25, 2012, the entirety of its first season was released by Netflix on February 8—a notable departure from the broadcast television model of premiering episodes on a weekly basis.[^cite_note-119][^cite_note-Greene,_Andy-120]

*House of Cards* was released by Netflix on February 1, 2013, marketed as the first "Netflix Original" production.[^cite_note-121] Later that month, Netflix announced an agreement with [DreamWorks Animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DreamWorks_Animation) to commission children's television series based on its properties, beginning with *[Turbo: F.A.S.T.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbo_Fast)*, a spin-off of its film *[Turbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbo_\(2013_film\))*.[^cite_note-122][^cite_note-123] *Orange is the New Black* would premiere in July 2013; Netflix stated that *Orange is the New Black* had been its most-watched original series so far, with all of them having "an audience comparable with successful shows on cable and broadcast TV."[^cite_note-124][^cite_note-125]

On March 13, 2013, Netflix added a Facebook sharing feature, letting United States subscribers access "Watched by your friends" and "Friends' Favorites" by agreeing.[^cite_note-126] This was not legal until the [Video Privacy Protection Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_Privacy_Protection_Act) was modified in early 2013.[^cite_note-127] On August 1, 2013, Netflix reintroduced the "Profiles" feature that permits accounts to accommodate up to five user profiles.[^cite_note-128][^cite_note-129][^cite_note-130][^cite_note-131]

In November 2013, [Marvel Television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marvel_Television) and [ABC Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABC_Signature) announced Netflix had ordered a slate of [four television series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marvel's_Netflix_television_series) based on the [Marvel Comics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marvel_Comics) characters [Daredevil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daredevil_\(Marvel_Comics_character\)), [Jessica Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jessica_Jones), [Iron Fist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Fist_\(character\)) and [Luke Cage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luke_Cage). Each of the four series received an initial order of 13 episodes, and Netflix also ordered a [Defenders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defenders_\(comics\)) miniseries that would tie them together. [*Daredevil*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daredevil_\(TV_series\)) and [*Jessica Jones*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jessica_Jones_\(TV_series\)) premiered in 2015.[^cite_note-132][^cite_note-Netflix-133][^cite_note-134] The *[Luke Cage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luke_Cage_\(TV_series\))* series premiered on September 30, 2016, followed by *[Iron Fist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Fist_\(TV_series\))* on March 17, 2017, and *[The Defenders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Defenders_\(miniseries\))* on August 18, 2017.[^cite_note-135][^cite_note-136] Marvel owner [Disney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney) later entered into other content agreements with Netflix, including acquiring its animated *[Star Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Wars)* series *[Star Wars: The Clone Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Wars:_The_Clone_Wars)*, and a new sixth season.[^cite_note-137]

In February 2014, Netflix began to enter into agreements with U.S. internet service providers, beginning with [Comcast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comcast) (whose customers had repeatedly complained of frequent [buffering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_buffer) when streaming Netflix), to provide the service a direct connection to their networks.[^cite_note-138][^cite_note-139][^cite_note-140] In April 2014, Netflix signed *[Arrested Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrested_Development)* creator [Mitchell Hurwitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitchell_Hurwitz) and his production firm The Hurwitz Company to a multi-year deal to create original projects for the service.[^cite_note-141] In May 2014, Netflix acquired streaming rights to films produced by [Sony Pictures Animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sony_Pictures_Animation).[^cite_note-142] It also quietly began to introduce an updated logo, with a flatter appearance and updated typography.[^cite_note-143]

In September 2014, Netflix expanded into six new European markets, including Austria, Belgium, France, Germany, [Luxembourg](17515.md), and Switzerland.[^cite_note-144] On September 10, 2014, Netflix participated in [Internet Slowdown Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Slowdown_Day) by deliberately slowing down its speed in support of [net neutrality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_neutrality) regulations in the United States.[^cite_note-145] In October 2014, Netflix announced a four-film deal with [Adam Sandler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Sandler) and his [Happy Madison Productions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Happy_Madison_Productions).[^cite_note-146]

In April 2015, following the launch of *[Daredevil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daredevil_\(TV_series\))*, Netflix director of content operations Tracy Wright announced that Netflix had added support for [audio description](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio_description) (a narration track with aural descriptions of key visual elements for the blind or visually impaired), and had begun to work with its partners to add descriptions to its other original series over time.[^cite_note-147][^cite_note-148] The following year, as part of a settlement with the [American Council of the Blind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Council_of_the_Blind), Netflix agreed to provide descriptions for its original series within 30 days of their premiere, and add [screen reader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screen_reader) support and the ability to browse content by availability of descriptions.[^cite_note-149]

In March 2015, Netflix expanded to Australia and New Zealand.[^cite_note-150][^cite_note-151] In September 2015, Netflix launched in Japan, its first country in Asia.[^cite_note-152][^cite_note-153][^cite_note-154] In October 2015, Netflix launched in Italy, Portugal, and Spain.[^cite_note-155]

In January 2016, at the [Consumer Electronics Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_Electronics_Show), Netflix announced a major international expansion of its service into 130 additional countries. ### Attempted acquisition of Warner Bros. (2025–2026)

Further information: [Proposed acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery by Paramount Skydance](81768101.md)

On October 31, 2025, Netflix, Inc. was reported to be actively exploring a bid for the studios and streaming assets of [Warner Bros. Discovery](67824355.md). According to the report, Netflix retained investment bank [Moelis & Co](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moelis_&_Company). to evaluate a potential offer and had been granted access to Warner Bros. Discovery's financial data room as part of its preparatory due-diligence. Netflix stressed that it remains "predominantly focused on growing organically" and is not interested in owning legacy cable networks such as [CNN](62028.md), [TBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TBS_\(American_TV_channel\)) and [TNT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TNT_\(American_TV_network\)).[^cite_note-320][^cite_note-321]

Netflix had previously considered buying other companies such as [20th Century Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20th_Century_Studios) (now 20th Century Studios), [Paramount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramount_Global), [The Walt Disney Company](37398.md), and even [Electronic Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Arts). But the price and the fear of a possible shareholder reaction prevented that.[^cite_note-322]

On December 4, 2025, Netflix won the bidding war for Warner Bros. Discovery, to be followed by exclusive talks to finalize a deal.[^cite_note-323] On December 5, Netflix announced a proposed agreement to acquire Warner Bros. Discovery's "Streaming and Studios" businesses for \$72 billion, with a total asset value of \$82.7 billion. As part of the deal, Netflix would acquire the Warner Bros. division, including its [film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warner_Bros._Motion_Picture_Group), [television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warner_Bros._Television_Studios) and [video game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warner_Bros._Games) studios, [HBO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HBO)/[HBO Max](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HBO_Max) and their respective libraries, [DC Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DC_Entertainment)/[DC Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DC_Studios) and WBD's distribution, publishing and licensing divisions. The acquisition would have excluded the company's "Global Linear Networks" division (aside from [Turner Classic Movies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turner_Classic_Movies), which is aligned with the Warner Bros. film studio),[^cite_note-324] which would have been divested as the separate company Discovery Global.[^cite_note-325]

[Paramount Skydance](79917576.md) entered a hostile bid for the entirety of WBD at \$30 per share, in an all-cash offer higher than Netflix's \$27.75 per share offer for just the studios division; Paramount Skydance assessed its offer as having fewer regulatory hurdles than the Netflix offer.[^cite_note-326] WBD rejected the offer due to concerns over its credibility, believing that it was inferior to Netflix's offer.[^cite_note-327][^cite_note-328] On January 20, 2026, Netflix amended its own offer to an all-cash offer; WBD shareholders were expected to vote on the deal by April.[^cite_note-329] On February 26, 2026, WBD agreed to a competing bid by Paramount Skydance at \$31 per-share, valuing the company at approximately \$111 billion.[^cite_note-NetflixBowsOut-Variety-330][^cite_note-NetflixBacksOut-THR-331] Netflix subsequently backed out, with its co-CEOs issuing a statement that its offer was "no longer financially attractive."[^cite_note-332] Netflix received a \$2.8 billion breakup fee from Paramount Skydance,[^cite_note-333] and later announced that it would add \$25 billion to its share buyback program.[^cite_note-334]

### Corporate culture



Netflix's original Los Gatos headquarters (2006–2022)

[^cite_note-Moss-337]



Netflix's current Los Gatos headquarters (2022–present)

[^cite_note-Moss-337]



Netflix Los Angeles offices on [Sunset Boulevard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunset_Boulevard)

Netflix grants all employees extremely broad discretion with respect to business decisions, expenses, and vacation—but in return expects consistently high performance, as enforced by what is known as the "keeper test".[^cite_note-Allyn-338][^cite_note-Chmielewski-339] All supervisors are expected to constantly ask themselves if they would fight to keep an employee. If the answer is no, then the employee is dismissed.[^cite_note-Economist-340] A [slide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presentation_slide) from an internal presentation on Netflix's corporate culture summed up the test as: "Adequate performance gets a generous [severance package](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severance_package)."[^cite_note-Chmielewski-339] Such packages reportedly range from four months' salary in the United States to as much as six months in the Netherlands.[^cite_note-Economist-340]

The company offers unlimited vacation time for salaried workers and allows employees to take any amount of their paychecks in [stock options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employee_stock_option).[^cite_note-341]

About the culture that results from applying such a demanding test, Hastings has said that "You gotta earn your job every year at Netflix,"[^cite_note-Jarvey-342] and, "There's no question it's a tough place ... There's no question it's not for everyone."[^cite_note-Buddenhagen-343] Hastings has drawn an analogy to [athletics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athletics_\(physical_culture\)): professional athletes lack long-term [job security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Job_security) because an injury could end their career in any particular game, but they learn to put aside their fear of that constant risk and focus on working with great colleagues in the current moment.[^cite_note-Spangler-344]

### Environmental impact

According to a study conducted by the [International Energy Agency](285292.md), streaming a 30-minute show on Netflix in 2019 released around 0.018 kg CO<sub>2</sub>.[^cite_note-345]

In March 2021, Netflix announced that it would work to reach [net zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_zero) greenhouse gas emissions by the end of 2022, while investing in programs to preserve or restore [ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystems). The company stated that it would cut emissions from its operations and electricity use by 45 percent by 2030. Due to the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md) and lack of content production, Netflix had a 14 percent drop in emissions in 2020.[^cite_note-346][^cite_note-347]
