The **Office of Foreign Assets Control** (**OFAC**) is a [financial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_intelligence) and enforcement agency of the [United States Treasury Department](53667.md). It administers and enforces economic and trade [sanctions](411315.md) in support of U.S. [national security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_of_the_United_States) and [foreign policy](7564.md) objectives.[^cite_note-min-2] Under [presidential national emergency powers](210541.md), OFAC carries out its activities against foreign governments, organizations (including terrorist groups and drug cartels), and individuals deemed a threat to U.S. national security.[^cite_note-Treasury's_War_book-3]

Founded in 1950 as the Division of Foreign Assets Control, since 2004 OFAC has operated under the [Office of Terrorism and Financial Intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Terrorism_and_Financial_Intelligence) within the Treasury Department. It is primarily composed of intelligence targeters and lawyers. While many of OFAC's targets are broadly set by the [White House](33057.md), most individual cases are developed as a result of investigations by OFAC's Office of Global Targeting (OGT).[^cite_note-reuters.com-4]

Sometimes described as one of the "most powerful yet unknown" government agencies,[^cite_note-reuters.com-4][^cite_note-5] OFAC has the power to levy significant penalties against entities that defy its directives, including imposing fines, freezing assets, and barring parties from operating in the U.S.

## History

Involvement of the U.S. Department of the Treasury in economic sanctions against foreign states dates to the [War of 1812](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_1812), when Secretary [Albert Gallatin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Gallatin) administered sanctions against the [United Kingdom](31717.md) in retaliation for the [impressment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impressment) of American sailors.[^cite_note-Treasury's_War_book-3][^cite_note-FAQ-6]

Predecessor agencies of the Division of Foreign Assets Control include Foreign Funds Control (FFC), which existed from 1940 to 1947, and the Office of International Finance (1947 to 1950).[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-\:0-8] FFC was established by [Executive Order 8389](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_8389) as a unit of the Office of the Secretary of the Treasury on April 10, 1940. The authority to establish FFC was derived from the [Trading with the Enemy Act 1917](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trading_with_the_Enemy_Act_1917). Among other operations, FFC administered wartime import controls over enemy assets and restrictions on trade with enemy states. It also participated in administering the "Proclaimed List of Certain Blocked Nationals", or the "Black List", and took [censuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censuses) of foreign-owned assets in the US and American-owned assets abroad. FFC was abolished in 1947, with its functions transferred to the newly established Office of International Finance (OIF). In 1948, OIF activities relating to blocked foreign funds were transferred to the Office of Alien Property, an agency within the [Department of Justice](52563.md).[^cite_note-Records-9]

The Division of Foreign Assets Control, OFAC's immediate predecessor, was established in the Office of International Finance by a Treasury Department order in December 1950, following the entry of the [People's Republic of China](5405.md) into the [Korean War](16772.md);[^cite_note-\:0-8] President [Harry S. Truman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_S._Truman) declared a [national emergency](298608.md) and tasked the Division with blocking all Chinese and North Korean assets subject to U.S. jurisdiction. The Division also administered regulations and orders issued under the amended Trading with the Enemy Act.[^cite_note-FAQ-6] On October 15, 1962, by a Treasury Department order, the Division of Foreign Assets Control became the Office of Foreign Assets Control.[^cite_note-Records-9]

## Authority and activities

In addition to the [Trading with the Enemy Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trading_with_the_Enemy_Act_of_1917) and the various [national emergencies](59622928.md) currently in effect, OFAC derives its authority from a variety of [U.S. federal laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._federal_laws), particularly the [International Emergency Economic Powers Act](2434972.md) (IEEPA), regarding [embargoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embargo) and economic sanctions.[^cite_note-10]

In enforcing economic sanctions, OFAC acts to prevent "prohibited transactions", which are described by OFAC as "trade or financial transactions and other dealings in which U.S. persons may not engage unless authorized by OFAC or expressly exempted by statute". OFAC has the authority to grant exemptions to prohibitions on such transactions, either by issuing a general license for certain categories of transactions, or by specific licenses issued on a case-by-case basis.[^cite_note-FAQ-6]

Under the [International Emergency Economic Powers Act](2434972.md) (IEEPA), the [U.S. president](24113.md) can block the removal of foreign assets under U.S. jurisdiction during national emergencies. OFAC executes this mandate by issuing regulations to financial institutions.[^cite_note-11]

As part of its efforts to support the [Iraq sanctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_sanctions), in 2005, OFAC fined [Voices in the Wilderness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voices_in_the_Wilderness_\(organization\)) \$20,000 for gifting medicine and other humanitarian supplies to Iraqis.[^cite_note-12] In a similar case, OFAC imposed and attempted to collect a \$10,000 fine, plus interest, against peace activist Bert Sacks for taking medicine to residents of [Basra](57580.md);[^cite_note-13] charges against Sacks were dismissed by the court in December 2012.[^cite_note-14]

In October 2007, a set of Spanish travel agency websites had their domain name access disabled by [eNom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ENom): the domain names had been on the OFAC blacklist.[^cite_note-Liptak-15][^cite_note-16] When queried, the U.S. Treasury referred to a 2004 press release that claimed the company "had helped Americans evade restrictions on travel to Cuba".[^cite_note-Liptak-15]

In the case of *[United States v. Banki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_v._Banki)*, on June 5, 2010, a U.S. citizen was convicted of violating [sanctions against Iran](9941859.md) by failing to request Iranian currency transfer licenses in advance from OFAC. On August 25, 2010, the Iranian [American Bar Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Bar_Association) announced that it would file an [amicus curiae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amicus_curiae) brief with the [Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Second_Circuit) in *United States v. Banki*.[^cite_note-\:1-17] It has also hired lawyers to request further guidance from OFAC on import of goods from Iran.[^cite_note-\:1-17]

In 2014, OFAC reached a record \$963 million settlement with the French bank [BNP Paribas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BNP_Paribas), which was a portion of an \$8.9 billion penalty imposed in relation to the case as a whole.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19]

Appointment as OFAC director is not subject to [Senate](24909346.md) confirmation.[^cite_note-WSJ01-20]

## Specially Designated Nationals

Main article: [Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specially_Designated_Nationals_and_Blocked_Persons_List)

OFAC publishes a list of Specially Designated Nationals (SDNs), which lists people, organizations, and vessels with whom [U.S. citizens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._citizens) and [permanent residents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permanent_residence_\(United_States\)) are prohibited from doing business.[^cite_note-FAQ-6] This list differs from the list maintained pursuant to Section 314(a) of the [Patriot Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriot_Act).[^cite_note-21]

In August 2009, a federal court ruling in *[KindHearts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KindHearts) v. Treasury* found that Treasury's seizure of KindHearts assets without notice or means of appeal was a violation of the [Fourth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) and [Fifth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) Amendments to the Constitution.[^cite_note-22]

On September 23, 2011, the [Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Ninth_Circuit) upheld a lower court's ruling that procedures used by the Treasury to shut down the Oregon-based [Al Haramain Islamic Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Haramain_Islamic_Foundation) in 2004 were unconstitutional. The court said the Fifth Amendment's guarantee of [due process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Due_process) required the Treasury to give adequate notice of the reasons it puts a group on the terrorist list, as well as a meaningful opportunity to respond. In addition, the court ruled that freezing the group's assets amounts to a seizure under the Fourth Amendment, so that a court order is required.[^cite_note-23]

As of October 7, 2015, the SDN List had more than 15,200 entries from 155 countries. Of those, 178 entries were for aircraft and 575 entries were for ships ("vessels"). The remaining 14,467 entries were for designated individuals and organizations.[^cite_note-24] OFAC creates separate entries in the SDN list for each alias of a designee, so the number of entries does not reflect the number of designees.[^cite_note-Treasury's_War_book-3]

On September 21, 2021, a cryptocurrency exchange was included in the sanctions list for first time for helping launder illicit funds having source from [ransomware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ransomware) attacks. The amounts laundered are more than \$160 million, between 2018 and 2021.[^cite_note-25]

## Sectoral Sanctions Identifications

OFAC publishes a list of Sectoral Sanctions Identifications (SSI), which lists persons, companies, and entities in sectors of the Russian economy (especially energy, finance, and armaments), prohibiting certain types of activity with these individuals or entities by US persons, wherever located.[^cite_note-SSIlist-26] This list is maintained following the issuance of EO [13662](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Executive%20Order%2013662) *Blocking Property of Additional Persons Contributing to the Situation in Ukraine* on March 20, 2014, in accordance with 79 FR 16167.[^cite_note-FR2014-06612-27]

On August 13, 2014, the Treasury Department issued guidance for entities under sectoral sanctions. It increased the number of entities on the sectoral sanctions identifications list by adding subsidiaries of entities under sectoral sanctions that hold 50% or greater ownership by an entity under sectoral sanctions either individually or in the aggregate, either directly or indirectly. Further, US persons cannot use a third party intermediary and must exercise caution during "transactions with a non-blocked entity in which one or more blocked persons has a significant ownership interest that is less than 50% or which one or more blocked persons may control by means other than a majority ownership interest."[^cite_note-US140813-28]

On December 22, 2015, the Treasury Department explicitly listed all entities and their subsidiaries on the sectoral sanctions identifications list using a human readable search.[^cite_note-OFAC22December2015pr-29][^cite_note-OFAC22December2015-30][^cite_note-31]

As of February 20, 2026, OFAC has over 17,000 individuals connected with sanctions.[^cite_note-32]

## Security incidents

Main article: [2024 United States Department of the Treasury hack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_States_Department_of_the_Treasury_hack)

In December 2024, Chinese state-sponsored hackers breached OFAC systems through cybersecurity vendor [BeyondTrust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BeyondTrust), gaining access to unclassified administrative records containing potential sanctions deliberation information and future targets. This incident, described by Treasury officials as "major" and also affecting the Office of the Treasury Secretary and [Office of Financial Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Financial_Research), marked the second known [PRC](5405.md) intrusion into OFAC, following a breach in the early 2000s that compromised general counsel office emails.[^cite_note-90][^cite_note-91] In response, on January 17, 2025, OFAC sanctioned Yin Kecheng, a Shanghai-based hacker affiliated with China's [Ministry of State Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_State_Security_\(China\)) allegedly responsible for the compromise.[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93]
