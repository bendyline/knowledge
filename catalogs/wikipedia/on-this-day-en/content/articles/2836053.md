**Natural uranium** (**NU** or **U<sub>nat</sub>**[^cite_note-WorldNuclear-1]) is [uranium](31743.md) with the same [isotopic ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopic_ratio) as found in nature. It contains 0.711% [uranium-235](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-235), 99.284% [uranium-238](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-238), and a trace of [uranium-234](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-234) by weight (0.0055%). Approximately 2.2% of its [radioactivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_decay) comes from uranium-235, 48.6% from uranium-238, and 49.2% from uranium-234.

Natural uranium can be used to fuel both low- and high-power [nuclear reactors](22151.md). Historically, [graphite-moderated reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphite-moderated_reactor) and [heavy water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_water)-moderated reactors have been fueled with natural uranium in the pure metal (U) or [uranium dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_dioxide) (UO<sub>2</sub>) [ceramic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceramic) forms. However, experimental fuelings with [uranium trioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_trioxide) (UO<sub>3</sub>) and [triuranium octaoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triuranium_octaoxide) (U<sub>3</sub>O<sub>8</sub>) have shown promise.[^cite_note-2]

The 0.72% uranium-235 is not sufficient to produce a self-sustaining critical [chain reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_reaction) in [light water reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_water_reactor) or [nuclear weapons](21785.md); these applications must use [enriched uranium](37555.md). Nuclear weapons take a concentration of 90% uranium-235, and light water reactors require a concentration of roughly 3% uranium-235.[^cite_note-3] Unenriched natural uranium is appropriate fuel for a [heavy-water reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy-water_reactor), like a [CANDU reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CANDU_reactor).

On rare occasions, earlier in geologic history, when uranium-235 was more abundant, uranium ore was found to have naturally engaged in fission, forming [natural nuclear fission reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_nuclear_fission_reactor). Uranium-235 decays at a faster rate ([half-life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-life) of 700 million years) compared to uranium-238, which decays extremely slowly (half-life of 4.5 billion years). Therefore, a billion years ago, there was more than double the uranium-235 compared to now.

During the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project), the name ***Tuballoy*** was used to refer to natural uranium in the refined condition; this term is still in occasional use. Uranium was also codenamed "X-Metal" during World War II. Similarly, enriched uranium was referred to as ***Oralloy*** ([Oak Ridge](38147.md) alloy), and [depleted uranium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depleted_uranium) was referred to as ***Depletalloy*** (depleted alloy).
