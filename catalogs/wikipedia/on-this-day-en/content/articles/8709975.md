# Doré bar



Gold concentrate, dominated by flakes \~0.5–1 mm across (a) and Doré bar produced from the gold concentrate (b).

[^cite_note-\:1-1]

A **doré bar** ([/dɔːˈreɪ bɑːr/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*DAW-ray-BAR*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key), French: [\[dɔʁe\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/French); "gilded" or "golden")[^cite_note-2] is a semi-pure [alloy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alloy) of [gold](12240.md) and [silver](27119.md). It is usually created at the site of a mine and then transported to a [refinery](8921732.md) for further [purification](8921732.md).[^cite_note-\:1-1][^cite_note-\:0-3]

The proportions of silver and gold can vary widely, but refineries usually require gold and silver to account for at least 70 percent of a doré bar’s weight.[^cite_note-\:0-3] The most common impurities that can interfere with the refining process are [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron), [lead](17747.md), [tellurium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tellurium), [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel), and [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper).[^cite_note-\:0-3] Before assay, precious-metal samples may be prepared by melting to make them homogeneous; in doré refining, representative samples from the homogenised melt are used to determine gold, silver and impurity content.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5]

During the 19th century gold rushes, gold nuggets and dust were melted into crude gold bars mistakenly called "[bullion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullion)" by miners. They were, more accurately, doré bars with higher contents of silver and other adulterants than the mints would accept. Mint and private [assayers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallurgical_assay) would subsequently refine the doré bars to an acceptable purity, 999 fine, gold bullion, with the silver and base metals removed. By the time of the [California gold rush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_gold_rush), mints were moving away from the age-old process of [cupellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupellation) to "[part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_parting)" bullion and moving toward the acid refining process developed by chemist [Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Louis_Gay-Lussac) for the French mint. By the time of the [Klondike gold rush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klondike_gold_rush), mints were replacing Gay-Lussac's acid process and introducing electrolysis to refine doré bars into 999.9 purity gold bullion.[^cite_note-6]

## Associated industry codes

According to the [United States Census Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau),[^cite_note-7] the creation of doré bars is conducted at establishments engaged either in "Gold Ore Mining" ([NAICS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAICS) Code 212221),[^cite_note-8] or "Primary Smelting and Refining of Nonferrous Metal (except Copper and Aluminum)" ([NAICS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAICS) Code 331419).[^cite_note-9]
