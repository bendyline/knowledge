# Summary execution



[Nguyễn Ngọc Loan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_Ng%E1%BB%8Dc_Loan) [summarily executes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saigon_Execution) [Viet Cong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viet_Cong) Captain Lê Công Nà in [Saigon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saigon) during the [Tet Offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tet_Offensive) in 1968.

In civil and military jurisprudence, **summary execution** (also called **field execution**) is the putting to death of a person accused of a crime without the benefit of a [free and fair trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_a_fair_trial).[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2] The term results from the legal concept of summary justice to punish a [summary offense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summary_offense), as in the case of a [drumhead court-martial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drumhead_court-martial), but the term usually denotes the *summary execution* of a [sentence of death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence_of_death). Under international law, it is defined as a combatant's refusal to accept an opponent's lawful surrender and the combatant's provision of [no quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_quarter), by killing the surrendering opponents.

Summary executions have been practiced by governments and paramilitary organizations during both peacetime and war, and are usually associated with [war crimes](48626.md) and [political persecution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_persecution).[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4]

## Military jurisdiction



This painting, *[The Third of May 1808](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Third_of_May_1808)* by [Francisco Goya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_Goya), depicts the summary execution of Spaniards by French forces after the [Dos de Mayo Uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dos_de_Mayo_Uprising) in [Madrid](41188263.md).

### Prisoners of war

Major treaties such as the [Geneva Conventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva_Conventions) and [Hague Conventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hague_Conventions_of_1899_and_1907), and [customary international law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customary_international_law) protect the rights of captured [regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_army) or [irregular enemy soldiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irregular_military) and [civilians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilians). [Prisoners-of-war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prisoners-of-war) (POWs) must be treated in carefully defined ways which definitively ban summary execution, as the [Second Additional Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additional_Protocol_II) of the Geneva Conventions (1977) states:

> No sentence shall be passed and no penalty shall be executed on a person found guilty of an offence except pursuant to a conviction pronounced by a court offering the essential guarantees of independence and impartiality.

— Second Protocol of the Geneva Conventions (1977), Article 6.2

### Exceptions to prisoners-of-war status

However, some classes of [combatants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combatants) may not be accorded POW status, but that definition has broadened to cover more classes of combatants over time. In the past, summary execution of [pirates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pirate), [spies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spy), and [francs-tireurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francs-tireurs)[^cite_note-5] have been performed and considered legal under existing international law.[^cite_note-6] Francs-tireurs (a term originating in the [Franco-Prussian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco-Prussian_War)) are enemy civilians or [militia](20622.md) who continue to fight in territory occupied by a warring party and do not wear military uniforms, and may otherwise be known as [guerrillas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guerrilla), [partisans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partisan_\(military\)), [insurgents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insurgents), etc. Though they could be legally jailed or executed by most armies a century ago, the experience of World War II influenced nations occupied by foreign forces to change the law to protect this group. Many of the post-war victors, such as France, Poland, and the USSR, had the experience of resistance fighters being summarily executed by the [Axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_powers) if they were captured. The war also influenced them to make sure that [commandos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commandos) and other special forces who were caught deep behind enemy lines would be protected as POWs, rather than summarily executed as Hitler decreed through his 1942 [Commando Order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commando_Order).



Polish people being executed by a German firing squad in [Kórnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B3rnik), October 1939



The execution of 56 Polish citizens in [Bochnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bochnia), near [Kraków](16815.md), during [German occupation of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Poland_\(1939%E2%80%931945\)), December 18, 1939, in a reprisal for an attack on a German police office two days earlier by the underground organization "White Eagle"

The Commando Order was issued by [Adolf Hitler](2731583.md) on October 18, 1942, stating that all [Allied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II) [commandos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commando) encountered by German forces in Europe and Africa should be killed immediately without trial, even in proper uniforms or if they attempted to surrender. Any commando or small group of commandos or a similar unit, agents and [saboteurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saboteurs) not in proper uniforms who fell into the hands of the German military forces by some means other than direct combat (through the police in occupied territories, for instance) were to be handed over immediately to the [Sicherheitsdienst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicherheitsdienst) (Security Service). The order, which was issued in secret, made it clear that failure to carry out such orders by any commander or officer would be considered to be an act of negligence punishable under German military law.[^cite_note-USGPO_translation-7] This was in fact the second "Commando Order",[^cite_note-8] the first being issued by Generalfeldmarschall [Gerd von Rundstedt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerd_von_Rundstedt) on July 21, 1942, stipulating that parachutists should be handed over to the [Gestapo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestapo).[^cite_note-9] Shortly after World War II, at the [Nuremberg Trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg_Trials), the Commando Order was found to be a direct breach of the [laws of war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_of_war), and German officers who carried out illegal executions under the Commando Order were found guilty of [war crimes](48626.md).

Soldiers who are wearing uniforms of the opposing army *after the start of combat* may be considered illegal combatants and subject to summary execution. Many armies have performed that kind of [false flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_flag) ruse, including both German and US [special forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_forces) during World War II. However, if soldiers remove their disguises and put on proper insignia *before the start of combat* in such an operation, they are legal combatants and must be treated as [prisoners of war (POWs)](25008.md) if captured. That distinction was settled by a military tribunal in the postwar trial of [Otto Skorzeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Skorzeny), who led [Operation Greif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Greif), an infiltration mission in which German commandos wore US uniforms to infiltrate US lines during the [Battle of the Bulge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Bulge).[^cite_note-10]
