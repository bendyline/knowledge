The **United States Department of Homeland Security** (**DHS**) is the U.S. [federal executive department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_federal_executive_departments) responsible for [public security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_security), comparable to [interior ministries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interior_ministry) abroad. Its missions involve [anti-terrorism](83392.md), [civil defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_defense), [immigration](15051.md) and [customs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customs), [border control](247927.md#Border_security), [cybersecurity](7398.md), [transportation security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airport_security), [maritime security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_security_\(USCG\)) and [sea rescue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-sea_rescue), and the mitigation of [weapons of mass destruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weapon_of_mass_destruction).[^cite_note-3]

It began operations on March 1, 2003, after being formed in a restructuring of the [U.S. federal government](195149.md) prescribed by the [Homeland Security Act of 2002](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeland_Security_Act_of_2002), enacted [in response](171761.md) to the [September 11 attacks](5058690.md). With more than 240,000 employees,[^cite_note-dhs.gov-1] DHS is the third-largest [Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_of_the_United_States) department, after the departments of [Defense](7279897.md) and [Veterans Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Veterans_Affairs).[^cite_note-4] [Homeland security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeland_security) policy is coordinated at the [White House](33057.md) by the [Homeland Security Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Homeland_Security_Council). Other agencies with significant homeland security responsibilities include the departments of [Health and Human Services](58250.md), [Justice](52563.md), and [Energy](62866.md).

## History

### Creation

A video released in 2016 by the DHS, detailing its duties and responsibilities

In response to the [September 11 attacks](5058690.md), President [George W. Bush](3414021.md) announced the establishment of the Office of Homeland Security (OHS) to coordinate "[homeland security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeland_security)" efforts. The office was headed by former Pennsylvania governor [Tom Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Ridge), who assumed the title of Assistant to the President for Homeland Security. The official announcement states:

> The mission of the Office will be to develop and coordinate the implementation of a comprehensive national strategy to secure the United States from [terrorist](30636.md) threats or attacks. The Office will coordinate the executive branch's efforts to detect, prepare for, prevent, protect against, respond to, and recover from terrorist attacks within the United States.[^cite_note-hsact2002-5]

Ridge began his duties as OHS director on October 8, 2001.[^cite_note-6] According to *[The Washington Post](102226.md)*, the Bush administration initially had "zero interest" in creating DHS, and Vice President [Dick Cheney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dick_Cheney) thought it would needlesly increase the size of government.[^cite_note-Iati_2019-7] *[Politico](9172097.md)* described Bush as resisting the creation of the cabinet-level department for months, hoping to build a structure similar to the [National Security Council](44026.md) to coordinate interagency work, but political pressure for a reorganization ultimately became "inescapable".[^cite_note-Graff_09112026-8] On November 25, 2002, the [Homeland Security Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeland_Security_Act) established the Department of Homeland Security to consolidate U.S. executive branch organizations related to "homeland security" into a single [Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_of_the_United_States) agency. In January 2003, the office was superseded, but not replaced by the Department of Homeland Security and the White House Homeland Security Council, both of which were created by the Homeland Security Act of 2002. The Homeland Security Council, similar in nature to the National Security Council, retained a policy coordination and advisory role and is led by the assistant to the president for homeland security.[^cite_note-hsact2002-5] The [Gilmore Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilmore_Commission), supported by much of [Congress](31756.md) and [John Bolton](997107.md), helped to solidify further the need for the department. According to [political scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science) [Peter Andreas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Andreas), the creation of DHS constituted the most significant government reorganization since the [Cold War](325329.md)[^cite_note-test-10] and the most substantial reorganization of federal agencies since the [National Security Act of 1947](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Act_of_1947) (which had placed the different [military](32212.md) departments under a [secretary of defense](43998.md) and created the [National Security Council](44026.md) and [Central Intelligence Agency](5183633.md)). Creation of DHS constitutes the most diverse merger ever of federal functions and responsibilities, incorporating 22 government agencies into a single organization.[^cite_note-11]

The founding of the DHS also marked a change in American thought towards threats, reflective of the [culture of anxiety and fear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_influence_of_the_September_11_attacks) that had come about in the wake of the [September 11 attacks](5058690.md). Introducing the term "homeland" centers attention on a population percived to be vulnerable, and which needs to be protected not only against emergencies such as natural disasters but also against hypothetical diffuse threats from individuals who are non-native to the United States. During the signing of the Homeland Security Act, President Bush spoke of "ruthless killers who move and plot in shadows."[^cite_note-12]

Prior to the signing of the bill, controversy about its adoption was focused on whether the [Federal Bureau of Investigation](11127.md) and the Central Intelligence Agency should be incorporated in part or in whole (neither was included). The bill was also controversial for the presence of unrelated "[riders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rider_\(legislation\))", as well as for eliminating certain union-friendly [civil service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_service) and labor protections for department employees. Without these protections, employees could be expeditiously reassigned or dismissed on grounds of security, incompetence or insubordination, and DHS would not be required to notify their union representatives. The plan stripped 180,000 government employees of their union rights.[^cite_note-13] In 2002, Bush officials argued that the September 11 attacks made the proposed elimination of employee protections imperative.[^cite_note-barr-14]

In an August 5, 2002, speech, President Bush said: "We are fighting ... to secure freedom in the homeland."[^cite_note-15] Prior to the creation of DHS, U.S. Presidents had referred to the U.S. as "the nation" or "the republic" and to its internal policies as "domestic".[^cite_note-wolf-16] Also unprecedented was the use, from 2002, of the phrase "the homeland" by White House spokespeople.[^cite_note-wolf-16]



A U.S. Customs and Border Protection officer addresses Vice President [Dick Cheney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dick_Cheney) (center); [Saxby Chambliss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxby_Chambliss) (center right), a [U.S. Senator](24909346.md) from [Georgia](48830.md); and [Michael Chertoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Chertoff) (far right), the second head of the DHS; in 2005

[Congress](31756.md) ultimately passed the Homeland Security Act of 2002, and President Bush signed the bill into law on November 25, 2002. It was the largest U.S. government reorganization in the 50 years since the United States Department of Defense was created.

Tom Ridge was named secretary on January 24, 2003, and began naming his chief deputies. DHS officially began operations on January 24, 2003, but most of the department's component agencies were not transferred into the new department until March 1.[^cite_note-hsact2002-5]



President [George W. Bush](3414021.md) signs the Homeland Security Appropriations Act of 2004 on October 1, 2003.

After establishing the basic structure of DHS and working to integrate its components, Ridge announced his resignation on November 30, 2004, following the re-election of President Bush. Bush initially nominated former [New York City Police Department](255413.md) commissioner [Bernard Kerik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Kerik) as his successor, but on December 10, Kerik withdrew his nomination, citing personal reasons and saying it "would not be in the best interests" of the country for him to pursue the post.

### Changes under Secretary Chertoff

On January 11, 2005, President Bush nominated federal judge [Michael Chertoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Chertoff) to succeed Ridge. Chertoff was confirmed on February 15, 2005, by a vote of 98–0 in the [U.S. Senate](24909346.md) and was sworn in the same day.[^cite_note-hsact2002-5]

In February 2005, DHS and the [Office of Personnel Management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Office_of_Personnel_Management) issued rules relating to employee pay and discipline for a new personnel system named MaxHR. *[The Washington Post](102226.md)* said that the rules would allow DHS "to override any provision in a union contract by issuing a department-wide directive" and would make it "difficult, if not impossible, for unions to negotiate over arrangements for staffing, deployments, technology and other workplace matters".[^cite_note-barr-14] In August 2005, U.S. District judge [Rosemary M. Collyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosemary_M._Collyer) blocked the plan on the grounds that it did not ensure collective-bargaining rights for DHS employees.[^cite_note-barr-14] A federal appeals court ruled against DHS in 2006; pending a final resolution to the litigation, Congress's fiscal year 2008 appropriations bill for DHS provided no funding for the proposed new personnel system.[^cite_note-barr-14] DHS announced in early 2007 that it was retooling its pay and performance system and retiring the name "MaxHR".[^cite_note-hsact2002-5] In a February 2008 court filing, DHS said that it would no longer pursue the new rules, and that it would abide by the existing civil service labor-management procedures. A federal court issued an order closing the case.[^cite_note-barr-14] Chertoff's successor, [Secretary Janet Napolitano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janet_Napolitano) deployed [full body scanners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_body_scanner) to assist the United States Secret Service in 2012.[^cite_note-17]

### Trump and Biden administrations

A 2017 memo by Secretary of Homeland Security [John F. Kelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kelly) directed DHS to disregard "age as a basis for determining when to collect biometrics."[^cite_note-\:1-18] On November 16, 2018, President [Donald Trump](4848272.md) signed the [Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency Act of 2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybersecurity_and_Infrastructure_Security_Agency_Act) into law, which elevated the mission of the former DHS National Protection and Programs Directorate and established the [Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency](59407369.md).[^cite_note-19] In fiscal year 2018, DHS was allocated a net discretionary budget of \$47.716 billion.[^cite_note-Reference_A-2]

In 2021, the Department of Justice began carrying out an investigation into [white supremacy](33178.md) and extremism in the DHS ranks.[^cite_note-20] DHS also halted large-scale immigration raids at job sites, saying in October 2021 that the administration was planning "a new enforcement strategy to more effectively target employers who pay substandard wages and engage in exploitative labor practices."[^cite_note-21] In 2023, the U.S. Customs and Border Patrol began using an app which requires asylum seekers to submit biometric information before they enter the country. In June 2024, John Boyd, the head of the DHS Office of Biometric Identity Management, announced at a conference that the agency "is looking into ways it might use facial recognition technology to track the identities of migrant children." According to Boyd, the initiative is intended to advance the development of facial recognition algorithms. A former DHS official said that every migrant processing center he visited engaged in biometric identity collection, and that children were not separated out during processing. DHS denied collecting the biometric data of children under 14.[^cite_note-\:1-18]

Starting in February 2026, in the [Second presidency of Donald Trump](78298108.md), DHS was subjected to a 76 day [government shutdown](82240292.md), the longest agency shutdown in the history of the United States, as Democrats within Congress demanded reforms to the department.[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23]

## Function

Whereas the Department of Defense is charged with military actions abroad, the Department of Homeland Security works in the civilian sphere to protect the United States within, at, and outside its borders. Its stated goal is to prepare for, prevent, and respond to domestic emergencies, particularly terrorism.[^cite_note-24] On March 1, 2003, the DHS absorbed the U.S. Customs Service and [Immigration and Naturalization Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Immigration_and_Naturalization_Service) (INS) and assumed its duties. In doing so, it divided the enforcement and services functions into two separate and new agencies: [Immigration and Customs Enforcement](216537.md) and [Citizenship and Immigration Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Citizenship_and_Immigration_Services). The investigative divisions and intelligence gathering units of the INS and Customs Service were merged forming [Homeland Security Investigations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Immigration_and_Customs_Enforcement#Homeland_Security_Investigations), the primary investigative arm of DHS. Additionally, the border enforcement functions of the INS, including the [U.S. Border Patrol](567453.md), the [U.S. Customs Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Customs_Service), and the [Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_and_Plant_Health_Inspection_Service) were consolidated into a new agency under DHS: [U.S. Customs and Border Protection](757040.md). The [Federal Protective Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Protective_Service_\(United_States\)) falls under the Management Directorate.[^cite_note-25]

## Organizational structure



DHS Organizational Chart | November 9, 2023

The Department of Homeland Security is headed by the [secretary of homeland security](212926.md) with the assistance of the [deputy secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Deputy_Secretary_of_Homeland_Security). DHS contains operational components, executing specific missions under the purview of the DHS; support components, supporting the mission of the DHS and operational components; and components in the Office of the Secretary, supporting department leadership, DHS components, and the secretary by overseeing and establishing policy.[^cite_note-26]

## National Terrorism Advisory System

In 2011, the Department of Homeland Security phased out the old Homeland Security Advisory System, replacing it with a two-level National Terrorism Advisory System. The system has two types of advisories: alerts and bulletins. NTAS bulletins permit the secretary to communicate critical terrorism information that, while not necessarily indicative of a specific threat against the United States, can reach homeland security partners or the public quickly, thereby allowing recipients to implement necessary protective measures. Alerts are issued when there is specific and credible information of a terrorist threat against the United States. Alerts have two levels: elevated and imminent. An elevated alert is issued when there is credible information about an attack but only general information about timing or a target. An Imminent Alert is issued when the threat is very specific and impending in the very near term.



The [Homeland Security Advisory System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeland_Security_Advisory_System) scale

On March 12, 2002, the [Homeland Security Advisory System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeland_Security_Advisory_System), a color-coded terrorism risk advisory scale, was created as the result of a [presidential directive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidential_Directive) to provide a "comprehensive and effective means to disseminate information regarding the risk of terrorist acts to Federal, State, and local authorities and to the American people". Many procedures at government facilities are tied into the alert level; for example a facility may search all entering vehicles when the alert is above a certain level. Since January 2003, it has been administered in coordination with DHS; it has also been the target of frequent jokes and ridicule on the part of the administration's detractors about its ineffectiveness. After resigning, Tom Ridge said he did not always agree with the threat level adjustments pushed by other government agencies.[^cite_note-54]

## Seal

Main article: [Seal of the United States Department of Homeland Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seal_of_the_United_States_Department_of_Homeland_Security)

The seal was developed with input from senior DHS leadership, employees, and the U.S. Commission on Fine Arts. The [Ad Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ad_Council) – which partners with DHS on its Ready.gov campaign – and the consulting company Landor Associates were responsible for graphic design and maintaining heraldic integrity.

> The seal is symbolic of the Department's mission – to prevent attacks and protect Americans – on the land, in the sea and in the air. In the center of the seal, a graphically styled white American eagle appears in a circular blue field. The eagle's outstretched wings break through an inner red ring into an outer white ring that contains the words "U.S. DEPARTMENT OF" in the top half and "HOMELAND SECURITY" in the bottom half in a circular placement. The eagle's wings break through the inner circle into the outer ring to suggest that the Department of Homeland Security will break through traditional bureaucracy and perform government functions differently. In the tradition of the Great Seal of the United States, the eagle's talon on the left holds an olive branch with 13 leaves and 13 seeds while the eagle's talon on the right grasps 13 arrows. Centered on the eagle's breast is a shield divided into three sections containing elements that represent the American homeland – air, land, and sea. The top element, a dark blue sky, contains 22 stars representing the original 22 entities that have come together to form the department. The left shield element contains white mountains behind a green plain underneath a light blue sky. The right shield element contains four wave shapes representing the oceans alternating light and dark blue separated by white lines.

\- DHS June 6, 2003[^cite_note-55]

## Headquarters



The current headquarters at [St. Elizabeths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Elizabeths_Hospital) West Campus



[Nebraska Avenue Complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebraska_Avenue_Complex), DHS headquarters from its inception until April 2019

Since its inception, the department's temporary headquarters had been in Washington, D.C.'s [Nebraska Avenue Complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebraska_Avenue_Complex), a former naval facility. The 38-acre (15 ha) site, across from [American University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_University), has 32 buildings comprising 566,000 square feet (52,600 m<sup>2</sup>) of administrative space.[^cite_note-56] In early 2007, the department submitted a \$4.1 billion plan to Congress to consolidate its 60-plus Washington-area offices into a single headquarters complex at the [St. Elizabeths Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Elizabeths_Hospital) campus in [Anacostia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anacostia), Southeast Washington, D.C.[^cite_note-57]

In February 2015 the [General Services Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Services_Administration) said that the site would open in 2021.[^cite_note-60] DHS headquarters staff began moving to St. Elizabeths in April 2019 after the completion of the Center Building renovation.[^cite_note-61][^cite_note-62]

## Disaster preparedness and response

### Congressional budgeting effects

During a [Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Senate_Committee_on_Homeland_Security_and_Governmental_Affairs) hearing on the reauthorization of DHS, Deputy Secretary [Elaine Duke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elaine_Duke) said there is a weariness and anxiety within DHS about the repeated congressional efforts to agree to a long-term spending plan, which had resulted in several threats to shut down the federal government. "Shutdowns are disruptive", Duke said. She said the "repeated failure on a longtime spending plan resulting in short-term continuing resolutions (CRs) has caused "angst" among the department's 240,000 employees in the weeks leading up to the CRs."[^cite_note-\:0-63] The uncertainty about funding hampers DHS's ability to pursue major projects and takes away attention and manpower from important priorities. Seventy percent of DHS employees are considered essential and are not furloughed during government shutdowns.[^cite_note-\:0-63]

### Ready.gov



Ready.gov program logo

Soon after formation, the department worked with the [Ad Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ad_Council) to launch the Ready Campaign, a national public service advertising (PSA) campaign to educate and empower Americans to prepare for and respond to emergencies including natural and man-made disasters. With [pro bono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro_bono) creative support from [the Martin Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Martin_Agency) of [Richmond, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richmond,_Virginia), the campaign website "Ready.gov" and materials were conceived in March 2002 and launched in February 2003, just before the launch of the [Iraq War](5043324.md).[^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66] One of the first announcements that garnered widespread public attention to this campaign was one by Tom Ridge in which he stated that in the case of a chemical attack, citizens should use duct tape and plastic sheeting to build a homemade bunker, or "sheltering in place" to protect themselves.[^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68] As a result, the sales of duct tape skyrocketed, and DHS was criticized for being too [alarmist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alarmist).[^cite_note-69]

On March 1, 2003, the [Federal Emergency Management Agency](73797.md) was absorbed into the DHS and in the fall of 2008 took over coordination of the campaign. The Ready Campaign and its Spanish-language version Listo.gov asks individuals to build an emergency supply kit,[^cite_note-70] make a family emergency plan[^cite_note-71] and be informed about the different types of emergencies that can occur and how to respond.[^cite_note-72] The campaign messages have been promoted through television, radio, print, outdoor and web PSAs,[^cite_note-73] as well as brochures, toll-free phone lines and the English and Spanish language websites Ready.gov and Listo.gov.

The general campaign aims to reach all Americans, but targeted resources are also available via "Ready Business" for small- to medium-sized business and "Ready Kids" for parents and teachers of children ages 8–12. In 2015, the campaign also launched a series of PSAs to help the whole community,[^cite_note-74] people with disabilities and others with access and functional needs prepare for emergencies, which included open captioning, a certified deaf interpreter and audio descriptions for viewers who are blind or have low vision.[^cite_note-75]

### National Incident Management System

On March 1, 2004, the [National Incident Management System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Incident_Management_System) (NIMS) was created.[^cite_note-76] The stated purpose was to provide a consistent incident management approach for federal, state, local, and tribal governments. Under Homeland Security Presidential Directive-5, all federal departments were required to adopt the NIMS and to use it in their individual domestic incident management and emergency prevention, preparedness, response, recovery, and mitigation program and activities.[^cite_note-77]

## Cyber-security

See also: [Cyber-security regulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyber-security_regulation)

The DHS [National Cyber Security Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Cyber_Security_Division) (NCSD) is responsible for the response system, risk management program, and requirements for cyber-security in the U.S. The division is home to [US-CERT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Computer_Emergency_Readiness_Team) operations and the [National Cyber Alert System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Cyber_Alert_System).[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-CSRDC-FAQ-94] The DHS Science and Technology Directorate helps government and private end-users transition to new cyber-security capabilities. This directorate also funds the Cyber Security Research and Development Center, which identifies and prioritizes research and development for NCSD.[^cite_note-CSRDC-FAQ-94] The center works on the Internet's routing infrastructure (the SPRI program) and Domain Name System ([DNSSEC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNSSEC)), identity theft and other online criminal activity (ITTC), Internet traffic and networks research (PREDICT datasets and the DETER testbed), Department of Defense and [HSARPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HSARPA) exercises (Livewire and Determined Promise), and wireless security in cooperation with Canada.[^cite_note-95]

The [Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency](59407369.md) (CISA) is responsible for helping the nation to understand, manage, and reduce risks to cyber and physical infrastructure.[^cite_note-96] CISA is in charge of exchanging "cyber defense information and defensive operational collaboration among the federal government, and state, local, tribal, and territorial (SLTT) governments, the private sector, and international partners."[^cite_note-\:4-97] CISA's first operational lead is federal cybersecurity, as well as "protecting and defending the federal civilian executive branch network."[^cite_note-\:4-97] They work closely with the Office of Management and Budget, the Office of the National Cyber Director, and federal agency Chief Information Officers and Chief Information Security Officers.[^cite_note-98] CISA's second operation is to serve as the national coordinator for critical infrastructure security and resilience, where they work closely with various government and industry partners to protect and defend the nation's critical infrastructure.[^cite_note-\:4-97]

On October 30, 2009, DHS opened the [National Cybersecurity and Communications Integration Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Cybersecurity_and_Communications_Integration_Center). The center brings together government organizations responsible for protecting computer networks and networked infrastructure.[^cite_note-99]

In January 2017, DHS officially designated state-run election systems as critical infrastructure. The designation made it easier for state and local election officials to get cybersecurity help from the federal government. In October 2017, DHS convened a Government Coordinating Council (GCC) for the Election Infrastructure Subsection with representatives from various state and federal agencies such as the [Election Assistance Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_Assistance_Commission) and [National Association of Secretaries of State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Association_of_Secretaries_of_State).[^cite_note-100]

On March 8, 2021, DHS, Science and Technology Directorates (S\&T), and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) announced two research and development (R\&D) projects for the Secure and Resilient Mobile Network Infrastructure (SRMNI) project. The project addresses developing solutions that would improve "the government's visibility into mobile device network traffic to identify malware, attacks or attempts to extract data from or through mobile devices."[^cite_note-101]

On May 4, 2026 the DHS inspector general published a report stating The Department of Homeland Security failed to effectively secure smartphones used by staff in its intelligence office, raising the risk of cyberattacks and unauthorized access to sensitive information.[^cite_note-102]

### Excess, waste, and ineffectiveness

The department has been dogged by persistent criticism over excessive [bureaucracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureaucracy), waste, ineffectiveness and lack of transparency. Congress estimates that the department has wasted roughly \$15 billion in failed contracts (as of September 2008).[^cite_note-105] In 2003, the department came under fire after the media revealed that [Laura Callahan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laura_Callahan), Deputy [Chief Information Officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Information_Officer) at DHS with responsibilities for sensitive national security databases, had obtained her bachelor, masters, and doctorate [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) degrees through [Hamilton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton_University), a [diploma mill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diploma_mill) in a small town in [Wyoming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wyoming).[^cite_note-106] The department was blamed for up to \$2 billion of waste and fraud after audits by the [Government Accountability Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Accountability_Office) revealed widespread misuse of government credit cards by DHS employees, with purchases including beer brewing kits, \$70,000 of plastic dog booties that were later deemed unusable, boats purchased at double the retail price (many of which later could not be found), and [iPods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPod) ostensibly for use in "data storage".[^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108][^cite_note-109][^cite_note-110] A 2015 inspection of IT infrastructure found that the department was running over a hundred computer systems whose owners were unknown, including Secret and Top Secret databases, many with out-of-date security or weak passwords. Basic security reviews were absent, and the department had apparently made deliberate attempts to delay publication of information about the flaws.[^cite_note-111] The \$53 billion construction budget allocated to CBP alone is equal to the 2024 defense expenditures of 10 European countries.[^cite_note-Graff_09112026-8]

The [mission creep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission_creep) and breadth of the mandate given to DHS has also been criticized, with *Politico* describing it as "amalgamation of organizational cultures" that has "struggled ever since to coalesce into one organization with a shared mission and culture". It noted Border Patrol's lobbying to keep its traditional green uniforms instead of adopting the blue DHS uniform and infighting between agencies as an example of its organizational incoherence.[^cite_note-Graff_09112026-8] In 2005, *[The Washington Post](102226.md)* argued that:[^cite_note-Glasses_12212005-112]

> "To some extent, the department was set up to fail. It was assigned the awesome responsibility of defending the homeland without the investigative, intelligence and military powers of the FBI, CIA and the Pentagon; it was also repeatedly undermined by the White House that initially opposed its creation."

Critics have argued DHS's "original mission of combating and responding to terrorism seems all-but forgotten in the public eye", and instead has morphed into a presidential "[Praetorian Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praetorian_Guard)" under the first and second Trump administrations. By 2026, DHS accounts for roughly half of all federal law enforcement personnel, and with the passage of the [One Big Beautiful Bill Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Big_Beautiful_Bill_Act), ICE, CBP, and DHS may constitute 55% to 60% of all federal law enforcement personnel.[^cite_note-Graff_09112026-8]

### Data mining

On September 5, 2007, the [Associated Press](18935732.md) reported that the DHS had scrapped an anti-terrorism [data mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_mining) tool called [ADVISE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ADVISE) (Analysis, Dissemination, Visualization, Insight and Semantic Enhancement) after the agency's internal [inspector general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inspector_general) found that [pilot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilot_\(experiment\)) testing of the system had been performed using data on real people without required [privacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privacy) safeguards in place.[^cite_note-113][^cite_note-114] The system, in development at [Lawrence Livermore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Livermore_National_Laboratory) and [Pacific Northwest National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Northwest_National_Laboratory) since 2003, has cost the agency \$42 million to date. Controversy over the program is not new; in March 2007, the Government Accountability Office stated that "the ADVISE tool could misidentify or erroneously associate an individual with undesirable activity such as [fraud](58790.md), crime or terrorism." Homeland Security's Inspector General later said that ADVISE was poorly planned, time-consuming for analysts to use, and lacked adequate justifications.[^cite_note-115]

### Fusion centers

Main article: [Fusion center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fusion_center)

Fusion centers are terrorism prevention and response centers, many of which were created under a joint project between the Department of Homeland Security and the [U.S. Department of Justice](52563.md)'s Office of Justice Programs between 2003 and 2007. The fusion centers gather information from government sources as well as their partners in the private sector.[^cite_note-116][^cite_note-bob-117]

They are designed to promote information sharing at the federal level between agencies such as the CIA, FBI, Department of Justice, U.S. military and state and local level government. As of July 2009, DHS recognized at least seventy-two fusion centers.[^cite_note-118] Fusion centers may also be affiliated with an Emergency Operations Center that responds in the event of a disaster.

There are a number of documented criticisms of fusion centers, including relative ineffectiveness at counterterrorism activities, the potential to be used for secondary purposes unrelated to counterterrorism, and their links to violations of civil liberties of American citizens and others.[^cite_note-119]

David Rittgers of the [Cato Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cato_Institute) notes:

> A long line of fusion center and DHS reports labeling broad swaths of the public as a threat to national security. The North Texas Fusion System labeled [Muslim](6037917.md) lobbyists as a potential threat; a DHS analyst in Wisconsin thought both pro- and anti-abortion activists were worrisome; a Pennsylvania homeland security contractor watched environmental activists, [Tea Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tea_Party_movement) groups, and a [Second Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) rally; the Maryland State Police put anti-death penalty and anti-war activists in a federal terrorism database; a fusion center in Missouri thought that all [third-party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_party_\(United_States\)) voters and [Ron Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ron_Paul) supporters were a threat ...[^cite_note-120]

### Mail interception

In 2006, [MSNBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MSNBC) reported that Grant Goodman, "an 81-year-old retired [University of Kansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Kansas) history professor, received a letter from his friend in the Philippines that had been opened and resealed with a strip of dark green tape bearing the words "by Border Protection" and carrying the official Homeland Security seal."[^cite_note-Mail-121] The letter was sent by a devout Catholic Filipino woman with no history of supporting [Islamic terrorism](566821.md).[^cite_note-Mail-121] A spokesman for U.S. Customs and Border Protection "acknowledged that the agency can, will and does open mail coming to U.S. citizens that originates from a foreign country whenever it's deemed necessary":

> All mail originating outside the United States Customs territory that is to be delivered inside the U.S. Customs territory is subject to Customs examination," says the CBP Web site. That includes personal correspondence. "All mail means 'all mail,'" said John Mohan, a CBP spokesman, emphasizing the point.[^cite_note-Mail-121]

The department declined to outline what criteria are used to determine when a piece of personal correspondence should be opened or to say how often or in what volume [Customs](757040.md) might be opening mail.[^cite_note-Mail-121]

Goodman's story provoked outrage in the [blogosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blogosphere),[^cite_note-122] as well as in the more established media. Reacting to the incident, *[Mother Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mother_Jones_\(magazine\))* remarked "unlike other prying government agencies, Homeland Security wants you to know it is watching you."[^cite_note-123] [CNN](62028.md) observed "on the heels of the [NSA wiretapping controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NSA_warrantless_surveillance_controversy), Goodman's letter raises more concern over the balance between privacy and security."[^cite_note-124]

### Employee morale

In July 2006, the [Office of Personnel Management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Office_of_Personnel_Management) conducted a survey of federal employees in all 36 federal agencies on job satisfaction and how they felt their respective agency was headed. DHS was last or near to last in every category including;

- 33rd on the talent management index

- 35th on the leadership and knowledge management index

- 36th on the job satisfaction index

- 36th on the results-oriented performance culture index

The low scores were attributed to concerns about basic supervision, management and leadership within the agency. Examples from the survey reveal most concerns are about promotion and pay increase based on merit, dealing with poor performance, rewarding creativity and innovation, leadership generating high levels of motivation in the workforce, recognition for doing a good job, lack of satisfaction with various component policies and procedures and lack of information about what is going on with the organization.[^cite_note-125][^cite_note-126]

DHS is the only large federal agency to score below 50% in overall survey rankings. It was last of large federal agencies in 2014 with 44.0% and fell even lower in 2015 at 43.1%, again last place.[^cite_note-127] DHS continued to rank at the bottom in 2019, prompting congressional inquiries into the problem.[^cite_note-128] High work load resulting from chronic staff shortage, particularly in Customs and Border Protection, has contributed to low morale,[^cite_note-129] as have scandals and intense negative public opinion heightened by immigration policies of the Trump administration.[^cite_note-130] Between 2005 and 2024, DHS has ranked last or second-to-last 13 times.[^cite_note-Graff_09112026-8]

DHS has struggled to retain women, who complain of overt and subtle misogyny.[^cite_note-131]

### Calls for abolition

See also: [Abolish ICE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolish_ICE)

From its outset, calls to abolish DHS have come from both conservative and liberal sources. The [Cato Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cato_Institute) proposed abolishing DHS in 2011,[^cite_note-145] the [American Enterprise Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Enterprise_Institute) in 2015, and the [ACLU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACLU) in 2020.[^cite_note-Graff_09112026-8] By 2019, *[The Washington Post](102226.md)* described calls to abolish DHS as being "made year after year, in publications ranging from policy journals to mainstream news outlets". [Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria_Ocasio-Cortez) has suggested abolishing the DHS in light of abuses against detained migrants by the Immigration and Customs Enforcement and Customs and Border Protection agencies.[^cite_note-Iati_2019-7] By 2026, former secretary [Jeh Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeh_Johnson) supported abolishing the agency, arguing it has outgrown its mission, is plagued by infighting, and that the immigration mission has overwhelmed the department's other work.[^cite_note-Graff_09112026-8]

In 2020, the DHS was criticized for detaining [protesters in Portland, Oregon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Floyd_protests_in_Portland,_Oregon). It even drew rebuke from the department's first secretary [Tom Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Ridge) who said, "It would be a cold day in hell before I would consent to an uninvited, unilateral intervention into one of my cities".[^cite_note-146]

On August 10, 2020, in an opinion article for *USA Today* by [Anthony D. Romero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_D._Romero), the [ACLU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACLU) called for the dismantling of DHS over the [deployment of federal forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_deployment_of_federal_forces_in_the_United_States) in July 2020 during the Portland protests.[^cite_note-147]

In January 2026, [Ben Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Rhodes_\(White_House_staffer\)) wrote DHS "helped build the plumbing of a domestic security state" and [Operation Metro Surge](82105033.md) "resembled a counterinsurgency campaign more than a law enforcement operation because that’s what it is — complete with tactics, equipment and legal authorities [derived from](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_boomerang) the war on terror". Rhodes argued "Our obsession with security — aided by politicians determined not to appear “weak” and Supreme Court decisions that empowered the presidency paved the way for the consolidation of power. Now, Minnesota has neither security nor liberty". Rhodes wrote that the [second Trump administration](78298108.md) "should be the period that concludes the [post-9/11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-9/11) era".[^cite_note-148]

In 2026, [Delia Ramirez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delia_Ramirez),[^cite_note-149] and [Fred Kaplan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Kaplan_\(journalist\))[^cite_note-150] called for dismantling DHS.

### ACLU lawsuit

In December 2020, ACLU filed a lawsuit against the DHS, U.S. CBP and U.S. ICE, seeking the release of their records of purchasing cellphone location data. ACLU alleges that these data were used to track U.S. citizens and immigrants and is seeking to discover the full extent of the alleged surveillance.[^cite_note-151]

Documents released by the lawsuit included around 113,654 location points over 3 days which "appears to come from just one area in the Southwestern United States, meaning it is just a small subset of the total volume of people's location information available to the agency."[^cite_note-152][^cite_note-153]

### Surveillance

See also: [Mass surveillance in the United States](40480495.md)

#### ICE

Main article: [United States Immigration and Customs Enforcement § Surveillance and data collection technology](216537.md#Surveillance_and_data_collection_technology)

American Dragnet, a report from the [Center on Privacy and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_on_Privacy_and_Technology), documents the scope of ICE's surveillance capabilities. The report found that ICE has access to the driver's license data of 3 in 4 adults, could locate 3 in 4 adults through their utility records and tracks the movements of drivers in cities home to 3 in 4 adults.[^cite_note-157][^cite_note-158] The report also said "the agency spent approximately \$2.8 billion between 2008 and 2021 on new surveillance, data collection and data-sharing initiatives".[^cite_note-159][^cite_note-160] ICE has also used [data brokers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_broker) to circumvent laws restricting government bodies sharing information with ICE.[^cite_note-161][^cite_note-162][^cite_note-163] ICE has reportedly been a customer of [Paragon Solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paragon_Solutions) and confirmed its use of [Clearview AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clearview_AI).[^cite_note-164][^cite_note-165][^cite_note-166][^cite_note-167] Additionally, ICE has reportedly used [Mobile Fortify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_Fortify), [ShadowDragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ShadowDragon), [Zignal Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zignal_Labs), the network of [Flock Safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flock_Safety), [Magnetic Forensics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grayshift), and products from [Pen-Link](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pen-Link), [LexisNexis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LexisNexis) and [Booz Allen Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booz_Allen_Hamilton).[^cite_note-168][^cite_note-169][^cite_note-170][^cite_note-171]

See also: [Immigration policy of the second Trump administration § Use of data in enforcement](78945778.md#Use_of_data_in_enforcement)

The [second Trump administration](78298108.md) reportedly worked to obtain and centralize data on Americans as outlined in [Executive Order 14243](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_14243) relying heavily on products from [Palantir Technologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palantir_Technologies).[^cite_note-172] This data has been desired to support [expanded deportation efforts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deportation_in_the_second_presidency_of_Donald_Trump) and to [target political opponents and civil society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Targeting_of_political_opponents_and_civil_society_under_the_second_Trump_administration).[^cite_note-173] The administration has sought data from the IRS,[^cite_note-174][^cite_note-175] Medicaid[^cite_note-176] and [Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supplemental_Nutrition_Assistance_Program).[^cite_note-177][^cite_note-178] ICE has also received data from within DHS including from the TSA.[^cite_note-179][^cite_note-180]

#### Office of Intelligence and Analysis

The [Office of Intelligence and Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DHS_Office_of_Intelligence_and_Analysis) (I\&A) has a history of problematic surveillance.[^cite_note-181][^cite_note-182][^cite_note-183] In 2020, the I\&A authorized "collecting and reporting on various activities in the context of elevated threats targeting monuments, memorials, and statues".[^cite_note-184][^cite_note-185] The office surveilled protestors at the [George Floyd protests in Portland, Oregon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Floyd_protests_in_Portland,_Oregon)[^cite_note-186][^cite_note-187] In September 2023, Congress considered revoking some of the agency's collection authorities over concerns about overreach.[^cite_note-188] According to Politico, "a key theme that emerges from internal documents is that in recent years, many people working at I\&A have said they fear they are breaking the law".[^cite_note-189] In 2025, sexual orientation and gender identity were removed from I\&A's list of characteristics that "personnel are prohibited from engaging in intelligence activities based solely on".[^cite_note-190]

### Masking and unidentifiability

ICE has repeatedly acted in ways that are difficult for the public to identify.
