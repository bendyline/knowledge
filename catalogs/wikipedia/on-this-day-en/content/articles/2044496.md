**Radio masts and towers** are typically tall structures designed to support [antennas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antenna_\(radio\)) for [telecommunications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunication) and [broadcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcasting), including [television](29831.md). There are two main types: guyed and self-supporting structures. They are among the tallest human-made structures. Masts are often named after the broadcasting organizations that originally built them or currently use them.

A [mast radiator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mast_radiator) or radiating tower is one in which the metal mast or tower itself is energized and functions as the transmitting antenna.

## Terminology



A radio mast base showing how virtually all lateral support is provided by the guy-wires

The terms "mast" and "tower" are often used interchangeably. However, in structural engineering terms, a **tower** is a self-supporting or [cantilevered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantilever) structure, while a [**mast**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guyed_mast) is held up by stays or [guy-wires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy-wire).[^cite_note-1]

A *mast*

is a [guyed mast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guyed_mast), a thin structure without the sheer strength to stand unsupported, that uses attached [guy lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy_line) for stability. They may be mounted on the ground or on top of buildings. Typical *masts* are of steel lattice or tubular steel construction. Masts tend to be cheaper to build but require an extended area surrounding them to accommodate the guy wires.

A *tower*

is a self-supporting structure, possibly also placed on a rooftop, that accomplishes the same purpose of raising actual radiating antennas to a functional height. Since it does not require land area from which to anchor guy lines, towers are more commonly used in cities where land is in short supply.

[^cite_note-mast_vs_tower_note-2]



The [Tokyo Skytree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Skytree) was, in 2012, the tallest freestanding tower in the world

There are a few borderline designs that are partly free-standing and partly guyed, called *[additionally guyed towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additionally_guyed_tower)*. Examples:

[Gerbrandy tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerbrandy_tower)

consists of a self-supporting tower with a guyed mast on top.

[Blaw-Knox towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaw-Knox_tower)

Those few of the towers still standing do the opposite: They have a guyed lower section surmounted by a freestanding part.

[Zendstation Smilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zendstation_Smilde)

is a tall tower with a guyed mast on top with guys which go to ground.

[Torre de Collserola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torre_de_Collserola)

is a guyed tower with a guyed mast on top where the tower portion is not free-standing.

## History

See also: [History of broadcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_broadcasting)

The first experiments in [radio communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_communication) were conducted by [Guglielmo Marconi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guglielmo_Marconi) beginning in 1894. In 1895–1896 he invented the [vertical monopole or Marconi antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopole_antenna), which was initially a wire suspended from a tall wooden pole. He found that the higher the antenna was suspended, the further he could transmit, the first recognition of the need for height in antennas. Radio began to be used commercially for [radiotelegraphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiotelegraph) communication around 1900.[^cite_note-Laport-1952-3]

The first 20 years of commercial radio were dominated by [radiotelegraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiotelegraph) stations, transmitting over long distances by using very long [wavelengths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength) in the [very low frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Very_low_frequency) band – such long waves that they are nearly unused at present. Because the extreme wavelengths were one to several kilometers long, even the tallest feasible antennas by comparison were still [too short, electrically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_length), and consequently had inherently very low [radiation resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_resistance) (only 5\~25 Ohms). In any antenna, low radiation resistance leads to excessive power losses in its surrounding [ground system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_\(electricity\)), since the low-resistance antenna cannot effectively compete for power with the high-resistance earth. To partially compensate, [radiotelegraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiotelegraph) stations used huge capacitively top-loaded [flattop antennas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-antenna) consisting of horizontal wires strung between multiple 100–300 meters (330–980 ft) steel towers to increase efficiency.[^cite_note-Laport-1952-3]<sup>(pp 77–78)</sup>



Multiwire broadcast [T-antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-antenna) of early AM station [WBZ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WBZ_\(AM\)), [Springfield, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springfield,_Massachusetts), 1925.

[AM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AM_broadcasting) [radio broadcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_broadcasting) began around 1920. The allocation of the [medium wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium_wave) frequencies for broadcasting raised the possibility of using single vertical masts without top loading. The antenna used for broadcasting through the 1920s was the [T-antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-antenna), which consisted of two masts with loading wires on top, strung between them, requiring twice the construction costs and land area of a single mast.[^cite_note-Laport-1952-3]<sup>(pp 77–78)</sup> In 1924 [Stuart Ballantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Ballantine) published two historic papers which led to the development of the single mast antenna.[^cite_note-Laport-1952-3]<sup>(pp 77–78)</sup> In the first he derived the [radiation resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_resistance) of a vertical conductor over a [ground plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_plane).[^cite_note-Ballantine-1924-4]<sup>(pp 833–839)</sup> He found that the radiation resistance increased to a maximum at a length of ⁠ 1 / 2 ⁠ [wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength), so a mast around that length had an [input resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_resistance) that was much higher than the ground resistance, reducing the fraction of transmitter power that was lost in the ground system without assistance from a capacitive top-load. In a second paper the same year he showed that the amount of power radiated horizontally in [ground waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_wave) reached a maximum at a mast height of ⁠ 5 /8⁠ [wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength).[^cite_note-Ballantine-1924-4]<sup>(pp 823–832)</sup>&#x20;



Masts of the [Rugby VLF transmitter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_Radio_Station) near [Rugby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby,_Warwickshire), England

By 1930 the expense of the [T-antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-antenna) led broadcasters to adopt the [mast radiator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mast_radiator) antenna, in which the metal structure of the mast itself functions as the antenna.[^cite_note-Laport-1952-3]<sup>(pp 79–81)</sup> One of the first types used was the diamond cantilever or [Blaw-Knox tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaw-Knox_tower). This had a diamond ([rhombohedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhombohedral)) shape which made it rigid, so only one set of [guy lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy_line) was needed, at its wide waist. The pointed lower end of the antenna ended in a large ceramic insulator in the form of a ball-and-socket joint on a concrete base, relieving bending moments on the structure. The first, a 665 foot (203 m) half-wave mast was installed at radio station [WABC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WCBS_\(AM\))'s 50 [kW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilowatt) transmitter at [Wayne, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayne,_New_Jersey) in 1931.[^cite_note-Radio-Craft-5][^cite_note-Siemens-1931-6] During the 1930s it was found that the diamond shape of the Blaw-Knox tower had an unfavorable current distribution which increased the power emitted at high angles, causing [multipath fading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multipath_fading) in the listening area.[^cite_note-Laport-1952-3]<sup>(pp 79–81)</sup> By the 1940s the AM broadcast industry had abandoned the Blaw-Knox design for the narrow, uniform cross section lattice mast used today, which had a better radiation pattern.

The rise of [FM radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FM_radio) and [television broadcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_broadcasting) in the 1940s–1950s created a need for even taller masts. The earlier AM broadcasting used [LF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_frequency) and [MF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium_frequency) bands, where radio waves propagate as [ground waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_wave) which follow the contour of the Earth. The ground-hugging waves allowed the signals to travel beyond the horizon, out to hundreds of kilometers. However the newer FM and TV transmitters used the [VHF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Very_high_frequency) band, in which radio waves travel by [line-of-sight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line-of-sight_propagation), so they are limited by the [visual horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon). The only way to cover larger areas is to raise the antenna high enough so it has a line-of-sight path to them.

Until 8 August 1991, the [Warsaw radio mast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw_radio_mast) was the world's tallest supported structure on land; its collapse left the [KVLY / KTHI-TV mast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KVLY/KTHI-TV_mast) as the tallest. There are over 50 radio structures in the United States that are 600 m (1968.5 ft) or taller.[^cite_note-7]

## Materials

### Steel lattice



A [3803 KM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3803_KM)-type TV tower located in [Penza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penza)

The steel lattice is the most widespread form of construction. It provides great strength, low weight and wind resistance, and economy in the use of materials. Lattices of triangular cross-section are most common, and square lattices are also widely used. [Guyed masts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guyed_mast) are often used; the supporting [guy lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy_line) carry lateral forces such as wind loads, allowing the mast to be very narrow and simply constructed.

When built as a tower, the structure may be parallel-sided or taper over part or all of its height. When constructed of several sections which taper exponentially with height, in the manner of the [Eiffel Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eiffel_Tower), the tower is said to be an Eiffelized one. The [Crystal Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_Palace_transmitting_station) tower in [London](17867.md) is an example.

### Tubular steel



Typical 200 foot (61 m) triangular guyed lattice mast of an AM radio station in [Mount Vernon, Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Vernon,_Washington), US

[Guyed masts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guyed_mast) are sometimes also constructed out of steel tubes. This construction type has the advantage that cables and other components can be protected from weather inside the tube and consequently the structure may look cleaner. These masts are mainly used for FM-/TV-broadcasting, but sometimes also as mast radiator. The big mast of [Mühlacker transmitting station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%BChlacker_radio_transmitter) is a good example of this. A disadvantage of this mast type is that it is much more affected by winds than masts with open bodies. Several tubular guyed masts have collapsed. In the UK, the [Emley Moor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emley_Moor) and [Waltham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waltham_transmitting_station) TV stations masts collapsed in the 1960s. In Germany the [Bielstein transmitter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sender_Bielstein) collapsed in 1985. Tubular masts were not built in all countries. In Germany, France, UK, Czech Republic, Slovakia, Japan and the Soviet Union, many tubular guyed masts were built, while there are nearly none in Poland or North America.

Several tubular guyed masts were built in cities in Russia and Ukraine. These masts featured horizontal crossbars running from the central mast structure to the guys and were built in the 1960s. The crossbars of these masts are equipped with a gangway that holds smaller antennas, though their main purpose is oscillation damping. The design designation of these masts is [30107 KM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/30107_KM) and they are exclusively used for FM and TV and are between 150–200-metre (490–660 ft) tall with one exception. The exception being the [mast in Vinnytsia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TV_Tower_Vinnytsia) which has height of 354 m (1161 ft) and is currently the tallest guyed tubular mast in the world after the [Belmont transmitting station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belmont_transmitting_station) was reduced in height in 2010.

### Reinforced concrete



[Fernsehturm Stuttgart TV Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernsehturm_Stuttgart) in [Stuttgart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuttgart), Germany: the first reinforced-concrete TV tower.

Reinforced concrete towers are relatively expensive to build but provide a high degree of mechanical rigidity in strong winds. This can be important when antennas with narrow beamwidths are used, such as those used for microwave point-to-point links, and when the structure is to be occupied by people.



Katanga TV tower, a reinforced-concrete tower in [Jabalpur](473219.md), Madhya Pradesh, India.

In the 1950s, AT\&T built numerous concrete towers, more resembling silos than towers, for its first transcontinental microwave route.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9]

In [Germany](11867.md) and the [Netherlands](21148.md) most towers constructed for point-to-point microwave links are built of [reinforced concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforced_concrete), while in the [UK](31717.md) most are [lattice towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_tower).

Concrete towers can form prestigious landmarks, such as the [CN Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CN_Tower) in [Toronto](64646.md), Canada. In addition to accommodating technical staff, these buildings may have public areas such as observation decks or restaurants.

The Katanga TV tower near [Jabalpur](473219.md), Madhya Pradesh, in central India hosts a high-power transmitter for the public broadcasters [Doordarshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doordarshan) and [Prasar Bharati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prasar_Bharati).

The [Stuttgart TV tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernsehturm_Stuttgart) was the first tower in the world to be built in reinforced concrete. It was designed in 1956 by the local civil engineer [Fritz Leonhardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Leonhardt).

### Fiberglass

[Fiberglass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiberglass) poles are occasionally used for low-power non-directional beacons or medium-wave broadcast transmitters.

### Carbon Fiber

Carbon fibre monopoles and towers have traditionally been too expensive but recent developments in the way the carbon fibre tow is spun have resulted in solutions that offer strengths exceeding steel (10 times) for a fraction of the weight (70% less[^cite_note-10]) which has allowed monopoles and towers to be built in locations that were too expensive or difficult to access with the heavy lifting equipment that is needed for a steel structure.

&#x20;Overall a carbon fiber structure is 40 - 50% faster to be erected compared to traditional building materials.



[Kamzík TV Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamz%C3%ADk_TV_Tower), overlooking [Bratislava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bratislava), Slovakia.

### Wood

As of 2022, wood, previously an uncommon material for telecommunications tower construction, has started to become increasingly common. In 2022, a wood telecommunications tower – the first of its kind in Italy – replaced a previously-existing steel structure to blend in with its wooded surroundings.[^cite_note-11] One of the most commonly cited reasons telecom companies opt for wood is because it is the only material in the industry that is [climate positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_Positive_Development_Program).[^cite_note-12] For this reason, some utility pole distributors started to offer wood towers to meet the growing demands of 5G infrastructure. In the United States, for example, wood utility pole distributor Bell Lumber & Pole began developing products for the [telecommunications industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications_industry).[^cite_note-13]

## Other types of antenna supports and structures

### Poles

Shorter masts may consist of a self-supporting or guyed wooden pole, similar to a telegraph pole. Sometimes self-supporting tubular [galvanized steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galvanized_steel) poles are used: these may be termed monopoles.

### Buildings

In some cases, it is possible to install transmitting antennas on the roofs of tall buildings. In [North America](21139.md), for instance, there are transmitting antennas on the [Empire State Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_State_Building), the [Willis Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willis_Tower), [Prudential Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prudential_Tower), [4 Times Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4_Times_Square), and [One World Trade Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_World_Trade_Center). The [North Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tenants_in_1_World_Trade_Center_\(1971%E2%80%932001\)) of the original [World Trade Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Trade_Center_\(1973%E2%80%932001\)) also had a 110-metre (360 ft) telecommunications antenna atop its roof, constructed in 1978–1979, and began transmission in 1980; when it [collapsed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collapse_of_the_World_Trade_Center), several local TV and radio stations were knocked off the air until backup transmitters could be put into service.[^cite_note-14] Such facilities also exist in [Europe](9239.md), particularly for portable radio services and low-power [FM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_modulation) radio stations. In [London](17867.md), the [BBC](19344654.md) erected in 1936 a mast for broadcasting early television on one of the towers of a Victorian building, the [Alexandra Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandra_Palace). It is still in use.



This 100-foot (30 m) tall cross conceals equipment for [T-Mobile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-Mobile_US) at Epiphany Lutheran Church in [Lake Worth, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Worth,_Florida), US. Completed in December 2009.

### Disguised cell-sites

Disguised cell sites sometimes can be introduced into environments that require a low-impact visual outcome, by being made to look like trees, chimneys or other common structures.

Many people view bare [cellphone towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_site) as ugly and an intrusion into their neighbourhoods. Even though people increasingly depend upon cellular communications, they are opposed to the bare towers spoiling otherwise scenic views. Many companies offer to 'hide' cellphone towers in, or as, trees, church towers, flag poles, water tanks and other features.[^cite_note-15] There are many providers that offer these services as part of the normal tower installation and maintenance service. These are generally called "stealth towers" or "stealth installations", or simply [concealed cell sites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_site#Camouflage).



Communications tower, at the horizon on the right, [camouflaged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camouflage) as a tall tree.

The level of detail and realism achieved by disguised cellphone towers is remarkably high; for example, such towers disguised as trees are nearly indistinguishable from the real thing.[^cite_note-16] Such towers can be placed unobtrusively in [national parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_parks) and other such protected places, such as towers disguised as [cacti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cactus) in United States' [Coronado National Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronado_National_Forest).[^cite_note-17]

Even when disguised, however, such towers can create controversy; a tower doubling as a flagpole attracted controversy in 2004 in relation to the [U.S. presidential campaign of that year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Presidential_Election,_2004), and highlighted the sentiment that such disguises serve more to allow the installation of such towers in subterfuge, away from public scrutiny, rather than to serve towards the beautification of the landscape.[^cite_note-18]

### Mast radiators

Main article: [Mast radiator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mast_radiator)

A [mast radiator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mast_radiator) or mast antenna is a radio tower or mast in which the *whole structure* is an antenna. Mast antennas are the transmitting antennas typical for [long](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_wave) or [medium wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium_wave) broadcasting.

&#x20;Structurally, the only difference is that some mast radiators require the mast base to be insulated from the ground. In the case of an insulated tower, there will usually be one insulator supporting each leg. [Some mast antenna designs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folded_unipole_antenna) do not require insulation, however, so base insulation is not an essential feature.&#x20;



Helpterberg Radio Tower

### Telescopic, pump-up and tiltover towers

Main article: [Cell on wheels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_on_wheels)

A special form of the radio tower is the *telescopic mast*. These can be erected very quickly. Telescopic masts are used predominantly in setting up temporary radio links for reporting on major news events, and for temporary communications in emergencies. They are also used in tactical military networks. They can save money by needing to withstand high winds only when raised, and as such are widely used in [amateur radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_radio).

Telescopic masts consist of two or more concentric sections and come in two principal types:

- Pump-up masts are often used on vehicles, and are raised to their full height pneumatically or hydraulically. They are usually only strong enough to support fairly small antennas.

- Telescopic lattice masts are raised by means of a winch, which may be powered by hand or an electric motor. These tend to cater for greater heights and loads than the pump-up type. When retracted, the whole assembly can sometimes be lowered to a horizontal position by means of a second tiltover winch. This enables antennas to be fitted and adjusted at ground level before winching the mast up.

### Balloons and kites

A tethered [balloon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balloon) or a [kite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kite_flying) can serve as a temporary support. It can carry an antenna or a wire (for VLF, LW or MW) up to an appropriate height. Such an arrangement is used occasionally by military agencies or radio amateurs. The American broadcasters [TV Martí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TV_Mart%C3%AD) broadcast a television program to [Cuba](5042481.md) by means of such a balloon.

### Drones

In 2013, interest began in using [unmanned aerial vehicles](58900.md) (drones) for telecom purposes.[^cite_note-19]

### Other special structures

See also: [List of spans § Antenna spans across valleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_spans#Antenna_spans_across_valleys)

For two [VLF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Very_low_frequency) transmitters wire antennas spun across deep valleys are used. The wires are supported by small masts or towers or rock anchors. The same technique was also used at [Criggion radio station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criggion_radio_station).

For ELF transmitters [ground dipole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_dipole) antennas are used. Such structures require no tall masts. They consist of two electrodes buried deep in the ground at least a few dozen kilometres apart. From the transmitter building to the electrodes, overhead feeder lines run. These lines look like power lines of the 10 kV level, and are installed on similar pylons.

## Design features

### Economic and aesthetic considerations



[Felsenegg-Girstel TV-tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felsenegg-Girstel_TV-tower)



[Uetliberg TV-tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uetliberg_TV-tower)

- The cost of a mast or tower is roughly proportional to the square of its height.

- A guyed mast is cheaper to build than a self-supporting tower of equal height.

- A guyed mast needs additional land to accommodate the guys, and is thus best suited to rural locations where land is relatively cheap. An unguyed tower will fit into a much smaller plot.

- A steel lattice tower is cheaper to build than a concrete tower of equal height.

- Two small towers may be less intrusive, visually, than one big one, especially if they look identical.

- Towers look less ugly if they and the antennas mounted on them appear symmetrical.

- Concrete towers can be built with aesthetic design considerations. They are sometimes built in prominent places and include observation decks or restaurants.



A [radio amateur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_amateur)'s [do it yourself](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Do_it_yourself) steel-lattice tower



[Bergwacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bergwacht) antenna with a webcam mounted to aid in [weather forecasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather_forecasting) and observations of the [Großer Feldberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gro%C3%9Fer_Feldberg) plateau.

### Masts for HF/shortwave antennas

For transmissions in the [shortwave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortwave) range, there is little to be gained by raising the antenna more than a half to three quarters of a [wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength) above ground level, and at lower frequencies and longer wavelengths, the height becomes infeasibly great (greater than 85 metres (279 ft)). Shortwave transmitters rarely use masts taller than about 100 metres.

### Access for riggers

Because masts, towers and the antennas mounted on them require maintenance, access to the whole of the structure is necessary. Small structures are typically accessed with a ladder. Larger structures, which tend to require more frequent maintenance, may have stairs and sometimes a lift, also called a service elevator.

### Aircraft warning features

&#x20;Tall structures in excess of certain legislated heights are often equipped with [aircraft warning lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_warning_lights), usually red, to warn pilots of the structure's existence. In the past, ruggedized and under-run filament lamps were used to maximize the bulb life. Alternatively, neon lamps were used. Nowadays such lamps tend to use [LED](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-emitting_diode) arrays.



Radio tower in [Jamshoro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamshoro)

Height requirements vary across states and countries, and may include additional rules such as requiring a white flashing strobe in the daytime and pulsating red fixtures at night. Structures over a certain height may also be required to be painted with [contrasting color schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_warning_paint) such as white and orange or white and red to make them more visible against the sky.

#### Light pollution and nuisance lighting

In some countries where [light pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_pollution) is a concern, tower heights may be restricted so as to reduce or eliminate the need for aircraft warning lights. For example, in the United States the [1996 Telecommunications Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Telecommunications_Act) allows local jurisdictions to set maximum heights for towers, such as limiting tower height to below 200 feet (61 m) and therefore not requiring aircraft illumination under US [Federal Communications Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Communications_Commission) (FCC) rules.

### Wind-induced oscillations

One problem with radio masts is the danger of wind-induced oscillations. This is particularly a concern with steel tube construction. One can reduce this by building cylindrical shock-mounts into the construction. One finds such shock-mounts, which look like cylinders thicker than the mast, for example, at the radio masts of [DHO38](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VLF_transmitter_DHO38) in [Saterland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saterland). There are also constructions, which consist of a free-standing tower, usually from [reinforced concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforced_concrete), onto which a guyed radio mast is installed. One example is the [Gerbrandy Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerbrandy_Tower) in [Lopik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lopik), Netherlands. Further towers of this building method can be found near [Smilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zendstation_Smilde), Netherlands and the [Fernsehturm](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernsehturm%20Waldenburg) in [Waldenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waldenburg,_Baden-W%C3%BCrttemberg), Germany.

### Hazard to birds

Main article: [Towerkill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Towerkill)

Radio, television and cell towers have been documented to pose a hazard to birds. Reports have been issued documenting known bird fatalities and calling for research to find ways to minimize the hazard that communications towers can pose to birds.[^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21]

There have also been instances of rare birds nesting in cell towers and thereby preventing repair work due to legislation intended to protect them.[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23]
