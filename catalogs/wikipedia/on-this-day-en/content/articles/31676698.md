In 2019, the total [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) production in Indonesia is 450.79 million [tonnes of oil equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonne_of_oil_equivalent), with a total primary [energy supply](5260053.md) of 231.14 million tonnes of oil equivalent and electricity final consumption of 263.32 [terawatt-hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TWh).[^cite_note-Indonesia_-_Countries_&_Regions-2] From 2000 to 2021, Indonesia's total energy supply increased by nearly 60%.[^cite_note-\:2-3]<sup>: 15 </sup>

Energy use in Indonesia has been long dominated by [fossil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel) resources. Once a major oil exporter that joined [OPEC](166346.md) in 1962, the country has since become a net oil importer despite still being in OPEC until 2008[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5] and for a short time in 2016,[^cite_note-6] making it the only net oil importer member in the organization.[^cite_note-7] Indonesia is also one of the world's largest coal producers and exporters. In 2024, it was the world's third-largest coal producer, with output increasing to 836 million tonnes.[^cite_note-8] In the same year, Indonesia remained the world's largest coal exporter, exporting 555 million tonnes of coal, primarily thermal coal.[^cite_note-9] Indonesia's coal reserves reached 31.95 billion tonnes in 2024, according to data from the Ministry of Energy and Mineral Resources.[^cite_note-10] In addition, Indonesia has abundant [renewable energy](25784.md) potential of 417,8 gigawatt (GW), including [solar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_energy), [wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power), [hydro](381399.md), [geothermal energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geothermal_energy), [ocean current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_current_energy), and [bioenergy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioenergy), although only 2,5% have been utilized.[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12] Furthermore, Indonesia along with [Malaysia](3607937.md), have two-thirds of ASEAN's gas reserves with a total annual gas production of more than 200 billion cubic meters in 2016.[^cite_note-13]

The [Government of Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Indonesia) has outlined several commitments to increase clean energy use and reduce [greenhouse gas emissions](9528025.md), among other things by issuing the National Energy General Plan (RUEN) in 2017 and joining the [Paris Agreement](30242372.md). In the RUEN, Indonesia targets New and Renewable Energy to reach 23% of the total energy mix by 2025 and 31% by 2050.[^cite_note-14] The country also commits to reduce its greenhouse gas emissions by 29% by 2030 against a business-as-usual baseline scenario, and up to 41% by international support.[^cite_note-15] It also has committed to phasing out coal power by 2040s, although numerous challenges remain [^cite_note-16]

Some major renewable projects in Indonesia include the 75 MW [wind farm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_farm) in [Sidenreng Rappang Regency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidenreng_Rappang_Regency), another 72 MW wind farm in [Jeneponto Regency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeneponto_Regency), and the Cirata Floating Solar Power Plant in West Java with a capacity of 145 MW which will become the largest Floating [Solar Power](13690575.md) Plant in [Southeast Asia](28741.md).[^cite_note-17]

According to the [IEA](285292.md), energy production increased 34% and export 76% from 2004 to 2008 in Indonesia. In 2017, Indonesia had 52,859 MW of installed electrical capacity, 36,892 MW of which were on the [Java–Bali grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bali%E2%80%93Java_Powerline).[^cite_note-19] In 2022, Indonesia had an electrical capacity of 81.2 GW with a projected capacity of 85.1 GW for 2023.[^cite_note-20]

In 2021, Indonesia's total energy supply (TES) comprised 30.3% coal, 28.9% oil, and 14.4% natural gas. Renewable energy sources also added to the mix, with biofuels and waste accounting for 13.8%, wind and solar providing 11.6%, and hydro contributing 0.9%.[^cite_note-21]

## Energy by sources

### Fossil fuel energy sources

#### Coal

This section is an excerpt from [Coal in Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_in_Indonesia).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Coal_in_Indonesia\&action=edit)]

[Coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) supplies a large share of energy in Indonesia. The world's largest coal exporter, the country plays a significant role in the global coal market, primarily serving Asian countries such as [China](5405.md), [India](14533.md) and [Japan](15573.md).

#### Oil



Indonesia used to be a net oil exporter.

Oil is a major sector in the Indonesian economy. During the 1980s, Indonesia was a significant oil-exporting country. Since 2000, domestic consumption has continued to rise while production has been falling, so in recent years Indonesia has begun importing increasing amounts of oil. Within Indonesia, there are considerable amounts of oil in Sumatra, Borneo, Java, and [West Papua Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Papua_\(province\)). - **Minas.** The Minas field, in [Riau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riau), [Sumatra](28679.md), operated by the US-based firm [Chevron](284749.md) Pacific Indonesia, is the largest oil block in Indonesia.

#### Shale

There is potential for [tight oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tight_oil) and [shale gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shale_gas) in northern Sumatra and eastern Kalimantan.[^cite_note-45] There is an estimated 46 trillion ft<sup>3</sup> (1.3 trillion m<sup>3</sup>) of shale gas and 7.9 billion barrels (1.26×10<sup>9</sup> m<sup>3</sup>) of shale oil which could be recovered with existing technologies.[^cite_note-EIAJune2013-46] [Pertamina](4042974.md) has taken the lead in using [hydraulic fracturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic_fracturing) to explore for shale gas in northern Sumatra. Chevron Pacific Indonesia and NuEnergy Gas are also pioneers in using fracking in existing oil fields and in new exploration. Environmental concerns and a government-imposed cap on oil prices present barriers to full development of the substantial shale deposits in the country.[^cite_note-esdm.go.id-47] Sulawesi, Seram, Buru, Papua in eastern Indonesia have shales that were deposited in marine environments which may be more brittle and thus more suitable for fracking than the source rocks in western Indonesia which have higher clay content.[^cite_note-EIAJune2013-46]



This underground coal mine in South Kalimantan is expected to become a major source of CMM emissions.

#### Coal bed methane

With 453 trillion ft<sup>3</sup> (12.8 trillion m<sup>3</sup>) of [coal bed methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_bed_methane) (CBM) reserve mainly in [Kalimantan](165470.md) and [Sumatra](28679.md), Indonesia has potential to redraft its energy charts as United States with its shale gas. With low enthusiasm to develop CBM project, partly in relation to environmental concern regarding emissions of [greenhouse gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gases) and contamination of water in the extraction process, the government targeted 8.9 million ft<sup>3</sup> (250 thousand m<sup>3</sup>) per day at [standard pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_temperature_and_pressure) for 2015.[^cite_note-48]

### Renewable energy sources



Indonesia renewable electricity production by source

Indonesia aims to get 23% and 31% of its energy from renewable sources by 2025 and 2050 respectively.[^cite_note-49] In 2020, renewables has a 11.2% share of the national energy mix, with hydro and geothermal power making up most of this.[^cite_note-esdm.go.id-47] Despite its renewable energy potential, Indonesia is struggling to reach its renewable target. The lack of adequate regulation supports to attract the private sector and the regulation inconsistency are often cited among the main reasons for the lack of progress. One policy requires private investors to transfer their projects to [PLN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perusahaan_Listrik_Negara) (the sole electricity off-taker in the country) at the end of agreement periods, which, combined with the fact that the Minister for Energy and Mineral Resources sets the consumer price of energy, has led to concern about return on investment.

Another issue is related to financing, as to achieve the 23% renewable energy target, it has been estimated that Indonesia needs to invest US\$154 billion. The state is unable to raise so much capital, while there is reluctance from both potential investors and lending banks to get involved.[^cite_note-50] There is also a critical challenge related to cost. Renewable energy projects is still require large up-front investment and as the electricity price has to be below the Region Generation Cost (BPP) (which is already low enough in some major areas), it renders projects economically unattractive. Indonesia also has large coal reserves and is one of the world's largest net exporters of coal, making it less urgent to develop renewable-based power plants compared to countries that depend on coal imports.[^cite_note-51]

The country has been recommended to remove subsidies for fossil fuels, establish a ministry of renewable energy, improve grid management, expand its grid connections with neighboring countries through the [ASEAN power grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASEAN_Power_Grid),[^cite_note-52] mobilize domestic resources to support renewable energy, and facilitate entry for international investors.[^cite_note-\:1-53] Continued reliance on fossil fuels by Indonesia may leave its coal assets stranded and result in significant investments lost as renewable energy is rapidly becoming cost-efficient worldwide.[^cite_note-54]

In February 2020, it was announced that the [People's Consultative Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Consultative_Assembly) is preparing its first renewable energy bill.[^cite_note-\:02-55]

#### Biomass

An estimated 55% of Indonesia's population, 128 million people, primarily rely upon traditional [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass) (mainly wood) for cooking.[^cite_note-56] Reliance on this source of energy has the disadvantage that poor people in rural areas have little alternative but to collect timber from forests, and often cut down trees, to collect wood for cooking.

A pilot project of Palm Oil Mill Effluent (POME) Power Generator with the capacity of 1 Megawatt has been inaugurated in September 2014.[^cite_note-57]

#### Hydroelectricity



[Jatiluhur Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jatiluhur_Dam), Indonesia's first and largest dam, in [Purwakarta Regency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purwakarta_Regency), West Java.

Indonesia has 75 GW of hydro potential, although only around 5 GW has been utilized.[^cite_note-esdm.go.id-47][^cite_note-58] Currently, only 34GW of Indonesia's total hydro potential can feasibly be utilized due to high development costs in certain areas.[^cite_note-59] Indonesia also set a target of 2 GW installed capacity in [hydroelectricity](381399.md), including 0.43 GW [micro-hydro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micro_hydro), by 2025.[^cite_note-60] Indonesia has a potential of around 459.91 MW for [micro hydropower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micro_hydro) developments, with only 4.54% of it being currently exploited.[^cite_note-61]

#### Geothermal energy

Main article: [Geothermal power in Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geothermal_power_in_Indonesia)

Indonesia uses some geothermal energy.[^cite_note-62] According to the Renewable Energy Policy Network's *Renewables 2013 Global Status Report*, Indonesia has the third largest installed generating capacity in the world. With 1.3 GW installed capacity, Indonesia trails only the United States (3.4 GW) and the Philippines (1.9 GW), ahead of Mexico (1.0 GW), Italy (0.9 GW), New Zealand (0.8 GW), Iceland (0.7 GW), and Japan (0.5 GW).[^cite_note-63] The current official policy is to encourage the increased use of geothermal energy for electricity production. Geothermal sites in Indonesia include the [Wayang Windu Geothermal Power Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayang_Windu_Geothermal_Power_Station) and the [Kamojang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamojang) plant, both in [West Java](314356.md).

The development of the sector has been proceeding rather more slowly than hoped. Expansion appears to be held up by a range of technical, economic, and policy issues which have attracted considerable comment in Indonesia. However, it has proved difficult to formulate policies to respond to the problems.[^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66]

Two new plants are slated to open in 2020, at [Dieng Volcanic Complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dieng_Volcanic_Complex) in [Central Java](453584.md) and at [Mount Patuha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Patuha) in [West Java](314356.md).[^cite_note-67]

#### Wind power



Sidrap wind farm, Indonesia's first wind power plant, in [Sidrap Regency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidrap_Regency), [South Sulawesi](389458.md).

On average, low wind speeds mean that for many locations there is limited scope for large-scale energy generation from wind in Indonesia. Only small (<10 kW) and medium (<100 kW) generators are feasible.[^cite_note-68] For Sumba Island in East Nusa Tengarra (NTT), according to NREL, three separate technical assessments have found that "Sumba's wind resources could be strong enough to be economically viable, with the highest estimated wind speeds ranging from 6.5 m/s to 8.2 m/s on an annual average basis."[^cite_note-69] A very small amount of (off-grid) electricity is generated using [wind power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power). For example, a small plant was established at Pandanmino, a small village on the south coast of Java in [Bantul Regency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantul_Regency), Yogyakarta Province, in 2011. However, it was established as an experimental [plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_stations) and it is not clear whether funding for long-term maintenance will be available.[^cite_note-70]

In 2018, Indonesia installed its first [wind farm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_farm), the 75 MW Sidrap, in [Sidenreng Rappang Regency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidenreng_Rappang_Regency), [South Sulawesi](389458.md), which is the biggest wind farm in Southeast Asia.[^cite_note-iesr.or.id-71][^cite_note-72] In 2019, Indonesia installed another wind farm with a capacity of 72 MW, in [Jeneponto Regency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeneponto_Regency), South Sulawesi.[^cite_note-iesr.or.id-71]

#### Solar power

The Indonesian solar PV sector is relatively underdeveloped but has significant potential, up to 207 GW with utilization in the country is less than 1%.[^cite_note-73] However, a lack of consistent and supportive policies, the absence of attractive tariff and incentives, as well as concerns about on-grid readiness pose barriers to the rapid installation of solar power in Indonesia, including in rural areas.[^cite_note-74][^cite_note-\:1-53]

As of early 2023, Indonesia has an installed capacity of 322 MW, and the country has a target of 5 GW by 2030.[^cite_note-\:5-75]

The country's largest solar plant is the [floating solar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_solar) system at the [Cirata Reservoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirata_Dam), with a capacity of 145 MW, inaugurated in November 2023.[^cite_note-\:5-75]

#### Tidal Power

With over 17,000 islands, Indonesia has great potential for [tidal power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_power) development. The Alas Strait, a 50 km stretch of ocean between Lombok and Sumbawa Island, alone could potentially yield 640 GWh of energy annually from tidal power.[^cite_note-76] As of 2023, despite evidence of great potential, no Indonesian tidal power facilities have been developed.

## Use of energy

### Transport sector

Much of the energy in Indonesia is used for domestic transportation. The dominance of private vehicles - mostly cars and motorbikes - in Indonesia has led to an enormous demand for fuel. Energy consumption in the transport sector is growing by about 4.5% every year. There is therefore an urgent need for policy reform and infrastructure investment to enhance the energy efficiency of transport, particularly in urban areas.[^cite_note-77]

There are large opportunities to reduce both the energy consumption from the transport sector, for example through the adoption of higher energy efficiency standards for private cars/motorbikes and expanding mass transit networks. Many of these measures would be more cost-effective than the current transport systems.[^cite_note-78] There is also scope to reduce the carbon intensity of transport energy, particularly through replacing diesel with biodiesel or through electrification. Both would require comprehensive supply chain analysis to ensure that the biofuels and power plants are not having wider environmental impacts such as deforestation or air pollution.[^cite_note-79]

### Electricity sector



Electricity generation in Indonesia in terawatt-hours

The electricity sector in Indonesia, managed primarily by the state-owned enterprise [Perusahaan Listrik Negara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perusahaan_Listrik_Negara) (PLN), faces significant challenges due to the country's [archipelagic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archipelago) nature, which includes over 17,000 islands. By 2020, Indonesia had installed approximately 63.3 gigawatts (GW) of electrical generation capacity, producing around 275 terawatt-hours (TWh) annually, predominantly from [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel) such as coal, natural gas, and oil, alongside contributions from [renewable sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_resource) like hydroelectric and geothermal power to align with the [Paris Climate Agreement](30242372.md)'s goal of increasing renewable energy to at least 23% by 2025. Despite substantial progress in [electrification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrification), with rates increasing from 67% in 2010 to over 99% in 2020, the country still grapples with issues of reliability and service quality, mainly in remote and eastern regions where infrastructure challenges and logistical issues often result in frequent power outages and service disruptions.[^cite_note-80][^cite_note-\:4-30]<sup>: 10 </sup>

## Government policy



Ignasius Jonan, Indonesian Minister of Energy and Mineral Resources

### Climate policies

Indonesia aims to achieve [net zero emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_zero_emissions) by 2060 or sooner as part of its development goal to become an advanced economy by 2045.[^cite_note-82][^cite_note-\:2-3]<sup>: 15 </sup> Indonesia is often classified as a laggard in terms of climate policy.[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84]

### Carbon tax

[Carbon tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_tax) provisions are regulated in Article 13 of Law 7/2021 in which a carbon tax is to be imposed on entities producing carbon emissions that have a negative impact on the environment.[^cite_note-85] Based on Law 7/2021, the imposition of the carbon tax is to be carried out by a combination of two schemes, a carbon tax (cap and tax) and carbon trade (cap and trade).

In the carbon trade scheme, individual or company ("entities") that produce emissions exceeding the cap are required to purchase emission permit certificates ("Sertifikat Izin Emisi"/SIE) from other entities that produce emissions below the cap.

In addition, entities can also purchase emission reduction certificates ("Sertifikat Penurunan Emisi"/SPE). However, if the entity is unable to purchase SIE or SPE in full for the resulting emissions, the cap and tax scheme will apply where entities producing residual emissions that exceed the cap will be subject to carbon tax.

Indonesia implemented a carbon tax in April 2022, initially targeting the power sector with intentions to expand to other sectors by 2025, based on readiness. This tax is part of a broader approach to [decarbonization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decarbonization), regulated under the Law of the Harmonization of Tax Regulations. It complements a domestic [emissions trading system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emissions_trading_system) (ETS) set to become mandatory by 2024. Non-compliant installations under the ETS will incur a carbon tax, linked to the domestic carbon market price.[^cite_note-\:0-86]

## Renewable energy policies

### Electricity generation

In Indonesia's electricity supply plan for 2021-2030 (RUPTL), the state electricity company, [Perusahaan Listrik Negara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perusahaan_Listrik_Negara) (PLN), targets substantial growth in renewable energy, aiming for renewables to account for over half of the capacity additions. The plan includes 10 gigawatts (GW) of hydro and about 3 GW from geothermal, with smaller additions from wind (0.4 GW) and solar photovoltaic (PV) (4.7 GW). Coal is expected to make up one-third of additions (about 14 GW), with no new coal capacity planned post-2030. Natural gas will contribute about 14% to new additions.[^cite_note-87][^cite_note-\:0-86]

### Bioenergy

Indonesia's [Ministry of Energy and Mineral Resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Energy_and_Mineral_Resources) (MEMR) is advancing bioenergy utilization with initiatives like setting up [waste-to-energy plants](1454584.md) in 12 cities, implementing co-firing in coal generators, and increasing the use of liquid biofuels. The rise in [palm oil production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm_oil_production) has led to a higher proportion of biofuel blending in diesel, supported by the National Energy Law of 2007, which established blending mandates and subsidy mechanisms. Since 2015, regulations have raised [biodiesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiesel)'s share in diesel consumption to 30% by January 2020, up from 20% in 2019. The goal for [bioethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioethanol) blending is to reach 20% by 2025, starting from a 10% target in 2020. The government intends to further raise biodiesel blending to 40% (B40) and supports the construction of refineries to transform waste bioenergy into biofuels, including bio-based liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) and [naphtha](184342.md).[^cite_note-\:0-86]
