**Louise Arbour** (French pronunciation: [\[lwiz aʁbuʁ\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/French); born February 10, 1947) is a Canadian jurist who has served as the 31st [governor general of Canada](38152.md) since 2026.

Born in [Montreal](7954681.md), she attended the [Université de Montréal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universit%C3%A9_de_Montr%C3%A9al) and earned a [Licentiate of Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Licentiate_of_Laws) degree. During her career, Arbour has served as the [UN High Commissioner for Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UN_High_Commissioner_for_Human_Rights), a justice of the [Supreme Court of Canada](123468.md) and the [Court of Appeal for Ontario](936907.md) and a chief prosecutor of the [International Criminal Tribunals for the former Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Criminal_Tribunal_for_the_Former_Yugoslavia) and [Rwanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Criminal_Tribunal_for_Rwanda). From 2009 until 2014, she served as president and CEO of the [International Crisis Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Crisis_Group).[^cite_note-1] She made history with the indictment of a sitting head of state, Yugoslavian president [Slobodan Milošević](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slobodan_Milo%C5%A1evi%C4%87), as well as the first prosecution of [sexual assault](218407.md) as a [crime against humanity](61099.md). From March 2017 to December 2018, she was the special representative of the [United Nations Secretary-General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Secretary-General) for international migration.[^cite_note-2] After leaving office, she continued her private law practice in Montreal.[^cite_note-3]

Arbour was appointed Governor General on June 8, 2026 by [King Charles III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Charles_III) under the recommendation of Prime Minister [Mark Carney](13571049.md). Arbour is the oldest person to hold the office in Canadian history.

## Early life and education

Louise Arbour was born in [Montreal](7954681.md), Quebec, to Bernard and Rose (née Ravary) Arbour, the owners of a hotel chain. She attended convent school, during which time her parents divorced. As editor of the school magazine, she earned a reputation for irreverence.[^cite_note-CBC1-4]

In 1967, she graduated from [Collège Regina Assumpta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coll%C3%A8ge_Regina_Assumpta) and proceeded to the [Université de Montréal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universit%C3%A9_de_Montr%C3%A9al) where she completed an [LL.B.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LL.B.) with distinction in 1970. She was a [law clerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_clerk) for Justice [Louis-Philippe Pigeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis-Philippe_Pigeon) of the [Supreme Court of Canada](123468.md) from 1971–1972 while completing graduate studies at the Faculty of Law (Civil Section) of the [University of Ottawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Ottawa). In a 2014 interview, Arbour named the move from Quebec to Ontario as the "biggest hurdle \[she] had to overcome to succeed in \[her] career," as her entire education had been in French.[^cite_note-\:0-5] She was called to the [Bar of Quebec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barreau_du_Qu%C3%A9bec) in 1971 and the [Law Society of Upper Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_Society_of_Upper_Canada) in 1977.[^cite_note-\:1-6]

## Career

### Legal career

#### In Canada

From 1972–73, Arbour served as a research officer for the [Law Reform Commission of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_Commission_of_Canada). She then taught at [Osgoode Hall Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osgoode_Hall_Law_School), [York University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/York_University), first as a Lecturer (1974), then as Assistant Professor (1975), Associate Professor (1977–1987), and finally as Associate Professor and Associate Dean (1987). She was vice-president of the [Canadian Civil Liberties Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Civil_Liberties_Association) until her appointment to the [Supreme Court of Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Ontario) (High Court of Justice) in 1987 and to the [Court of Appeal for Ontario](936907.md) in 1990. In 1995, Arbour was appointed as President of a Commission of Inquiry, under the Inquiries Act, for the purpose of investigating and reporting on events at the Prison for Women in [Kingston, Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingston,_Ontario), following allegations by prisoners of abuse.[^cite_note-7] The inquiry resulted in the publication of the [Arbour Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbour_Report).

#### The Hague

In 1996, at [Richard Goldstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Goldstone)'s recommendation, Arbour [was appointed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council_Resolution_1047) as his replacement as Chief Prosecutor of the [International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Criminal_Tribunal_for_Rwanda) in [Arusha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arusha), and of the [International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Criminal_Tribunal_for_the_former_Yugoslavia) (ICTY) in The Hague. She helped convict [Jean-Paul Akayesu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Paul_Akayesu) of [genocide](12441.md), the first such conviction since [1948 Genocide Convention](143988.md).[^cite_note-8] She also indicted then-Serbian President [Slobodan Milošević](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slobodan_Milo%C5%A1evi%C4%87) for war crimes, the first time a serving head of State was called to account before an international court.[^cite_note-9] Arbour declined to investigate any potential NATO war crimes, stating that "I accept the assurances given by NATO leaders that they intend to conduct their operations in the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia in full compliance with international humanitarian law".[^cite_note-10]

#### Supreme Court of Canada

See also: [Reasons of the Supreme Court of Canada by Justice Arbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reasons_of_the_Supreme_Court_of_Canada_by_Justice_Arbour)

In 1999, Prime Minister [Jean Chrétien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Chr%C3%A9tien) appointed Arbour to the [Supreme Court of Canada](123468.md) on May 26, just one day before the publication of the indictment of Milosevic by the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY).[^cite_note-CBC2-11] In 2004, Arbour retired from the Supreme Court of Canada, having served for just under five years. During her time on the Court, she wrote 68 reasons.[^cite_note-GlobeAndMail1-12][^cite_note-CBC3-13]

### United Nations and later career

After leaving the Supreme Court of Canada, Arbour became the [United Nations High Commissioner of Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_High_Commissioner_of_Human_Rights).[^cite_note-GlobeAndMail1-12] She criticized a number of countries for their human rights records.[^cite_note-CBC4-14] In 2008, she stepped down after serving one four-year term. In 2014, after leaving the [International Crisis Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Crisis_Group) as president and CEO since 2009, Arbour began working for [Borden Ladner Gervais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borden_Ladner_Gervais) as a senior counsel.[^cite_note-CBC4-14][^cite_note-\:2-15] Arbour was also a member of the [Global Commission on Drug Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Commission_on_Drug_Policy), the [International Commission Against the Death Penalty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Commission_Against_the_Death_Penalty),[^cite_note-\:2-15][^cite_note-16] and the [Whitney R. Harris World Law Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitney_R._Harris_World_Law_Institute)'s International Council.[^cite_note-17]



Arbour with [Dimitris Avramopoulos](1068047.md), in 2017

On March 9, 2017, Arbour was appointed by the [U.N. Secretary-General](162415.md), [António Guterres](471806.md), to be his Special Representative for International Migration.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19] In 2018, she described [anti-migration sentiment](6491626.md) in Western countries as "completely self-defeating."[^cite_note-20] In April 2021, [Canadian Minister of National Defence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_National_Defence_\(Canada\)) [Harjit Sajjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harjit_Sajjan) appointed Arbour to lead an independent review of the military’s handling of sexual assault, harassment and other misconduct.[^cite_note-GlobeAndMail2-21] In May 2022, she delivered her final report, which included 48 recommendations, including a proposal for civilian courts to handle sexual assault allegations involving members of the military.[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23]

## Governor General of Canada

On May 5, 2026, Prime Minister [Mark Carney](13571049.md) announced that King [Charles III](125248.md) had approved Arbour's appointment as the 31st governor general of Canada, succeeding [Mary Simon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Simon).[^cite_note-24] Before being installed on June 8, Arbour was received by Charles III in London. During this audience, she was invested as an extraordinary Commander of the [Order of Military Merit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Military_Merit_\(Canada\)), and Commander of the [Order of Merit of the Police Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Merit_of_the_Police_Forces), as is customary for governors general, who are *ex officio* the chancellor of the respective orders.[^cite_note-25] Arbour is the oldest person to hold the office in Canadian history.[^cite_note-26] During her installation ceremony, Arbour warned Canadians to be "vigilant" amid the rise of [artificial intelligence](1164.md) (AI).[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28]

## Personal life

At the [University of Ottawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Ottawa), Arbour met her long-time common-law partner Larry Taman, with whom she lived for 27 years before separating after she moved to [The Hague](30269.md) in 1996.[^cite_note-29][^cite_note-30] Arbour had three children with Taman: Emilie, Patrick and Catherine.[^cite_note-\:1-6] She also has grandchildren.[^cite_note-\:0-5]

Arbour speaks both English and French fluently.[^cite_note-31]

Her daughter, Emilie Taman, is a lawyer and politician affiliated with the [New Democratic Party](19283982.md) (NDP). Emilie was the NDP candidate for [Ottawa—Vanier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottawa%E2%80%94Vanier_\(federal_electoral_district\)) in the [2015 Canadian federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Canadian_federal_election) and the [2017 Ottawa—Vanier federal by-election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Ottawa%E2%80%94Vanier_federal_by-election), and later contested [Ottawa Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottawa_Centre_\(federal_electoral_district\)) in the [2019 Canadian federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Canadian_federal_election).[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34]

Arbour has described herself as a supporter of international justice, human rights and multilateral cooperation, themes that have shaped both her judicial and diplomatic career.[^cite_note-35]

## In the media

Arbour has published works on criminal procedure and criminal law in both French and English. At various times, she has served as an editor for the *Criminal Reports*, the *Canadian Rights Reporter*, and the *Osgoode Hall Law Journal*.[^cite_note-36]

On April 1, 2016, Arbour participated in a [Munk Debate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munk_Debate) concerning what was being called a global refugee crisis at the time, arguing alongside historian [Simon Schama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Schama) in favour of the resolution that Western nations have a [moral imperative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_imperative) to accept a large influx of refugees.[^cite_note-37] Beginning the evening with 77 percent of the audience supporting their side, Arbour and Schama lost the debate to opponents [Mark Steyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Steyn) and [Nigel Farage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Farage), who swung 22 percent of the audience vote against the resolution by the end of the event.[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39]

## Awards and recognition



Exterior of [Louise Arbour Secondary School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louise_Arbour_Secondary_School) in [Brampton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brampton)

In 2005, Arbour was awarded the [Thomas J. Dodd Prize in International Justice and Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_J._Dodd_Prize_in_International_Justice_and_Human_Rights), along with Justice [Richard Goldstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Goldstone), in recognition of her work on the International Criminal Tribunals for the former Yugoslavia and Rwanda.[^cite_note-40] She was the subject of a 2005 fact-based [Canadian](19851291.md)-[German](11867.md) made-for-television movie, *[Hunt for Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunt_for_Justice)*, which follows her quest to indict [Bosnian Serb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnian_Serb) war criminals. Arbour was played by Canadian actress [Wendy Crewson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendy_Crewson).[^cite_note-41] In 2016, she was awarded the [Tang Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_Prize) in rule of law for her contributions to international criminal justice and the protection of human rights.[^cite_note-42] She has multiple schools named after her, including [Louise Arbour Secondary School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louise_Arbour_Secondary_School) in [Brampton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brampton), Ontario.[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45]

Arbour was made a Companion of the [Order of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Canada) in 2007 "for her contributions to the Canadian justice system and for her dedication to the advancement of human rights throughout the world".[^cite_note-46] She was made a Grand Officer of the [National Order of Quebec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Order_of_Quebec) in 2009.[^cite_note-47] She was made a Commander of the [National Order of the Legion of Honour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legion_of_Honour) in 2011. [^cite_note-48] In 2014, she was inducted into [Canada's Walk of Fame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada's_Walk_of_Fame).[^cite_note-49]

Arbour has been awarded numerous [honorary degrees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorary_degree), including Doctor of Civil Laws from the [University of Western Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Western_Ontario) in June 2000,[^cite_note-50] Doctor of Humane Letters from [Mount Saint Vincent University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Saint_Vincent_University) in May 2001,[^cite_note-51] and Doctor of Laws degrees from the [University of New Brunswick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_New_Brunswick) in 1999, from the [University of British Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_British_Columbia) in November 2001, from [Concordia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concordia_University) in 2001,[^cite_note-52] the [University of Waterloo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Waterloo) in October 2006,[^cite_note-53] in June 2009 from the [University of Alberta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Alberta)[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55] and [University of Guelph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Guelph),[^cite_note-56] from [Simon Fraser University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Fraser_University) in October 2009, and from [Vancouver Island University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vancouver_Island_University) in January 2020.[^cite_note-57]

In January 2023, Arbour was awarded the 2023 Sandra Day [O'Connor Justice Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O'Connor_Justice_Prize) from [Arizona State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_State_University), a lifetime achievement award for human rights work as well as upholding the [rule of law](25166191.md) and [judicial independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_independence).[^cite_note-\:22-58]
