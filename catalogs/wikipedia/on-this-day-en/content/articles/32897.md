**William Shakespeare**[^cite_note-1] (c. 23 April 1564[^cite_note-3] – 23 April 1616)[^cite_note-5] was an English playwright, poet and actor. He is widely regarded as the greatest writer in the English language and the world's pre-eminent dramatist. He is often called England's [national poet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_poet) and the "[Bard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bard) of [Avon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Avon,_Warwickshire)" or simply "the Bard". His extant works, including [collaborations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shakespeare's_collaborations), consist of some [39 plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_plays), [154 sonnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_sonnets), 3 long [narrative poems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative_poem) and a few other verses, some of uncertain authorship. His plays [have been translated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_translations_of_works_by_William_Shakespeare) into every major [living language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_language) and are performed more often than those of any other playwright. Shakespeare remains arguably the most influential writer in the English language, and his works continue to be studied and reinterpreted.

Shakespeare was born and raised in [Stratford-upon-Avon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratford-upon-Avon), Warwickshire. At the age of 18, he married [Anne Hathaway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Hathaway_\(wife_of_Shakespeare\)), with whom he had three children: [Susanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susanna_Hall), and twins [Hamnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamnet_Shakespeare) and [Judith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judith_Quiney). Sometime between 1585 and 1592 he began a successful career in London as an actor, writer, and [part-owner](146081.md) ("sharer") of a [playing company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playing_company) called the [Lord Chamberlain's Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Chamberlain's_Men), later known as the [King's Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_Men_\(playing_company\)) after the ascension of [King James VI of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_VI_and_I) to the English throne. At age 49 (around 1613) he appears to have retired to Stratford, where he died three years later. Few records of Shakespeare's private life survive; this has stimulated considerable speculation about such matters as [his physical appearance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portraits_of_Shakespeare), [his sexuality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexuality_of_William_Shakespeare), [his religious beliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_views_of_William_Shakespeare) and even certain fringe theories as to whether the works attributed to him were [written by others](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare_authorship_question).

Shakespeare produced most of his known works between 1589 and 1613. His early plays were primarily [comedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespearean_comedy) and [histories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespearean_history) and are regarded as some of the greatest works produced in these genres. He then wrote mainly [tragedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespearean_tragedy) until 1608, among them *[Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet)*, *[Othello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Othello)*, *[King Lear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Lear)* and *[Macbeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macbeth)*, which are considered to be among the finest works in English. In the last phase of his career, he wrote [tragicomedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragicomedy) (also known as [romances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_late_romances)), such as *[The Winter's Tale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Winter's_Tale)* and *[The Tempest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tempest)*, and collaborated with other playwrights.

Many of Shakespeare's plays were published in editions of varying quality and accuracy during his lifetime. However, in 1623 [John Heminges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Heminges) and [Henry Condell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Condell), two fellow actors and friends of Shakespeare's, published a more definitive text known as the [First Folio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Folio), a posthumous collected edition of Shakespeare's dramatic works that includes 36 of his plays. Its preface includes a prescient poem by [Ben Jonson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Jonson), a rival of Shakespeare, who hailed Shakespeare with the now-famous epithet: "not of an age, but for all time".

## Life

Main article: [Life of William Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_of_William_Shakespeare)

### Early life



[John Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Shakespeare)'s house, believed to be [Shakespeare's birthplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_Birthplace), in [Stratford-upon-Avon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratford-upon-Avon)

William Shakespeare was the son of [John Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Shakespeare), an [alderman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alderman) and a successful glover (glove-maker) originally from [Snitterfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snitterfield) in [Warwickshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warwickshire), and [Mary Arden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Shakespeare), the daughter of an [affluent landowning family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arden_family) that was influential in the [Recusant Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recusancy) community.[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum198714–22-8] He was born in [Stratford-upon-Avon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratford-upon-Avon), where he was [baptised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptism) on 26 April 1564. His date of birth is unknown but is traditionally observed on 23 April, [Saint George's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_George's_Day).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum198724–26-2] This date, which can be traced to [William Oldys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Oldys) and [George Steevens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Steevens), has proved appealing to biographers because Shakespeare died on the same date in 1616.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum198724,_296-9][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan199815–16-10] He was the third of eight children, and the eldest surviving son.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum198723–24-11]

Although no attendance records for the period survive, most biographers agree that Shakespeare was probably educated at the [King's New School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Edward_VI_School,_Stratford-upon-Avon) in Stratford,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum198762–63-12][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd200653-13][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWellsTaylorJowettMontgomery2005xv–xvi-14] a free school chartered in 1553,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaldwin1944464-15] based about a quarter-mile (400 m) from his home in [Stratford's guildhall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratford-upon-Avon_Guildhall).[^cite_note-16] [Grammar schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammar_school) varied in quality during the [Elizabethan era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabethan_era), but grammar school curricula were largely similar: the basic [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) text was standardised by royal decree,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaldwin1944179–180,_183-17][^cite_note-FOOTNOTECressy197528–29-18] and the school would have provided an intensive education in grammar based upon Latin [classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classics) authors.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaldwin1944117-19]

At the age of 18, Shakespeare married 26-year-old [Anne Hathaway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Hathaway_\(Shakespeare's_wife\)). The [consistory court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consistory_court) of the [Diocese of Worcester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Diocese_of_Worcester) issued a marriage licence on 27 November 1582. The next day, two of Hathaway's neighbours posted bonds guaranteeing that no lawful claims impeded the marriage.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum198777–78-20] The ceremony may have been arranged in some haste; the Worcester [chancellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor) allowed the [marriage banns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banns_of_marriage) to be read once instead of the usual three times.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood200384-21][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum198778–79-22] Six months after the marriage, Anne gave birth to a daughter, [Susanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susanna_Hall), baptised 26 May 1583.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage93_93\]-23] Twins, son [Hamnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamnet_Shakespeare) and daughter [Judith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judith_Quiney), followed almost two years later and were baptised 2 February 1585.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage94_94\]-24] Hamnet died of unknown causes at the age of 11 and was buried 11 August 1596.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage224_224\]-25]



[Shakespeare's coat of arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare_coat_of_arms), from the 1602 book *The book of coates and creasts. Promptuarium armorum.* It features spears as a [pun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canting_arms) on the family name.

[^cite_note-26]

After the birth of the twins, Shakespeare left few historical traces until he is mentioned as part of the London theatre scene in 1592. The exception is the appearance of his name in the "complaints bill" of a law case before the Queen's Bench court at Westminster dated [Michaelmas Term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michaelmas_Term) 1588 and 9 October 1589.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBate2008314-27] Scholars refer to the years between 1585 and 1592 as Shakespeare's "lost years".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage95_95\]-28] Biographers attempting to account for this period have reported many [apocryphal](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/apocryphal) stories. [Nicholas Rowe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Rowe_\(writer\)), Shakespeare's first biographer, recounted a Stratford legend that Shakespeare fled the town for London to escape prosecution for deer [poaching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poaching) in the estate of local squire [Thomas Lucy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Lucy). Shakespeare is also supposed to have taken his revenge on Lucy by writing a scurrilous ballad about him.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum198797–108-29][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERowe170916–17-30] Another 18th-century story has Shakespeare starting his theatrical career minding the horses of theatre patrons in London.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987144–145-31] [John Aubrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Aubrey) reported that Shakespeare had been a country schoolmaster.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987110–111-32] Some 20th-century scholars suggested that Shakespeare may have been employed as a schoolmaster by Alexander Hoghton of [Lancashire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancashire), a Catholic landowner who named a certain "William Shakeshafte" in his will.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonigmann19981-33][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWellsTaylorJowettMontgomery2005xvii-34] Little evidence substantiates such stories other than [hearsay](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/hearsay) collected after his death, and Shakeshafte was a common name in the Lancashire area.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonigmann199895–117-35][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood200397–109-36]

### London and theatrical career

It is not known definitively when Shakespeare began writing, but contemporary allusions and records of performances show that several of his plays were on the London stage by 1592.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChambers1988a287,_292-37] By then, he was sufficiently known in London to be attacked in print by the playwright [Robert Greene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Greene_\(dramatist\)) in his *[Groats-Worth of Wit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greene's_Groats-Worth_of_Wit)* from that year:

> ... there is an upstart Crow, beautified with our feathers, that with his *Tiger's heart wrapped in a Player's hide*, supposes he is as well able to bombast out a blank verse as the best of you: and being an absolute *Johannes factotum*, is in his own conceit the only Shake-scene in a country.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenblatt2005213-38]

Scholars differ on the exact meaning of Greene's words,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenblatt2005213-38][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage153_153\]-39] but most agree that Greene was accusing Shakespeare of reaching above his rank in trying to match such university-educated writers as [Christopher Marlowe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Marlowe), [Thomas Nashe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Nashe) and Greene himself (the so-called "[University Wits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_Wits)").[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006176-40] The italicised phrase parodying the line "Oh, tiger's heart wrapped in a woman's hide" from Shakespeare's *[Henry VI, Part 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VI,_Part_3)*, along with the pun "Shake-scene", clearly identify Shakespeare as Greene's target. As used here, *[Johannes Factotum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_of_all_trades,_master_of_none)* ("Jack of all trades") refers to a second-rate tinkerer with the work of others, rather than the more common "universal genius".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenblatt2005213-38][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987151–153-41]

Greene's attack is the earliest surviving mention of Shakespeare's work in the theatre. Biographers suggest that his career may have begun any time from the mid-1580s to just before Greene's remarks.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWells200628-42][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987144–146-43][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChambers1988a59-44] After 1594 Shakespeare's plays were performed at [The Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Theatre), in [Shoreditch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoreditch), only by the [Lord Chamberlain's Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Chamberlain's_Men), a company owned by a group of players, including Shakespeare, that soon became the leading [playing company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playing_company) in London.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage184_184\]-45] After the death of [Queen Elizabeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_I) in 1603, the company was awarded a royal patent by the new king [James I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_VI_and_I), and changed its name to the [King's Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_Men_\(playing_company\)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChambers1923208–209-46]

> All the world's a stage,\
> &#x20;and all the men and women merely players:\
> &#x20;they have their exits and their entrances;\
> &#x20;and one man in his time plays many parts ...

— *[As You Like It](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As_You_Like_It)*, Act II, Scene 7, 139–142

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWellsTaylorJowettMontgomery2005666-47]

In 1599 a partnership of members of the company built their own theatre on the south bank of the [River Thames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Thames), which they named the [Globe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe_Theatre). In 1608 the company also took over the [Blackfriars indoor theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackfriars_Theatre). Extant records of Shakespeare's property purchases and investments indicate that his association with the company made him a wealthy man,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChambers1988b67–71-48] and in 1597 he bought the second-largest house in Stratford, [New Place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Place), and in 1605 invested in a share of the parish [tithes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tithes) in Stratford.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBentley196136-49]

Some of Shakespeare's plays were published in [quarto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarto_\(binding\)) editions, beginning in 1594, and by 1598 his name had become a selling point and began to appear on the [title pages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_page).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage188_188\]-50][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKastan199937-51][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnutson200117-52] Shakespeare continued to act in his own and other plays after his success as a playwright. The 1616 edition of [Ben Jonson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Jonson)'s *Works* names him on the cast lists for *[Every Man in His Humour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Every_Man_in_His_Humour)* (1598) and *[Sejanus His Fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sejanus_His_Fall)* (1603).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAdams1923275-53] The absence of his name from the 1605 cast list for Jonson's *[Volpone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volpone)* is taken by some scholars as a sign that his acting career was nearing its end.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWells200628-42] The [First Folio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Folio) of 1623, however, lists Shakespeare as one of "the Principal Actors in all these Plays", some of which were first staged after *Volpone*, although one cannot know for certain which roles he played.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage200_200\]-54] In 1610, [John Davies of Hereford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Davies_of_Hereford) wrote that "good Will" played "kingly" roles.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987200–201-55] In 1709 Rowe passed down a tradition that Shakespeare played the ghost of Hamlet's father.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERowe170932-56] Later traditions maintain that he also played Adam in *[As You Like It](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As_You_Like_It)*, and the Chorus in *[Henry V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_V_\(play\))*,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006357-57][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWellsTaylorJowettMontgomery2005xxii-58] though scholars doubt the sources of that information.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987202–203-59]

Throughout his career, Shakespeare divided his time between London and Stratford. In 1596, the year before he bought New Place as his family home in Stratford, Shakespeare was living in the parish of [St Helen's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Helen's_Church,_Bishopsgate), [Bishopsgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishopsgate), north of the River Thames.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHales1904401–402-60][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan1998121-61] He moved across the river to [Southwark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwark) by 1599, the same year his company constructed the Globe Theatre there.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHales1904401–402-60][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShapiro2005122-62] By 1604 he had moved north of the river again, to an area north of [St Paul's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Paul's_Cathedral) with many fine houses. There he rented rooms from a French [Huguenot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huguenot) named Christopher Mountjoy, a maker of women's wigs and other headgear.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan1998325-63][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenblatt2005405-64]

### Later years and death



[Shakespeare's funerary monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_funerary_monument) in Stratford-upon-Avon

[Nicholas Rowe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Rowe_\(writer\)) was the first biographer to record the tradition, repeated by [Samuel Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Johnson), that Shakespeare retired to Stratford "some years before his death".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006476-65][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood1806ix–x,_lxxii-66] He was still working as an actor in London in 1608; in an answer to the sharers' petition in 1635, [Cuthbert Burbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuthbert_Burbage) stated that after purchasing the lease of the [Blackfriars Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackfriars_Theatre) in 1608 from [Henry Evans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Evans_\(theatre\)), the King's Men "placed men players" there, "which were [Heminges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Heminges), [Condell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Condell), Shakespeare, etc.".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESmith1964558-67] However, it is perhaps relevant that the [bubonic plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bubonic_plague) raged in London throughout 1609.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006477-68][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarroll1991179–182-69] The London public playhouses were repeatedly closed during extended outbreaks of the plague (a total of over 60 months closure between May 1603 and February 1610),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBate2008354–355-70] which meant there was often no acting work. Retirement from all work was uncommon at that time.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan1998382–383-71] Shakespeare continued to visit London during the years 1611–1614.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006476-65] In 1612 he was called as a witness in *[Bellott v Mountjoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellott_v_Mountjoy)*, a court case concerning the marriage settlement of Mountjoy's daughter, Mary.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan1998326-72][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006462–464-73] In March 1613 he bought a [gatehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gatehouse) in the former [Blackfriars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackfriars,_London) priory;[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987272–274-74] and from November 1614 he was in London for several weeks with his son-in-law, [John Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hall_\(physician\)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan1998387-75] After 1610 Shakespeare wrote fewer plays, and none are attributed to him after 1613.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage279_279\]-76] His last three plays were collaborations, probably with [John Fletcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Fletcher_\(playwright\)),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan1998375–378-77] who succeeded him as the house playwright of the King's Men. He retired in 1613, before the [Globe Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe_Theatre) burned down during the performance of *[Henry VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VIII_\(play\))* on 29 June.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage279_279\]-76]

Shakespeare died on 23 April 1616, at the age of 52.[^cite_note-79] He died within a month of signing his will, a document which he begins by describing himself as being in "perfect health". No extant contemporary source explains how or why he died. Half a century later, [John Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ward_\(vicar\)), the vicar of Stratford, wrote in his notebook: "Shakespeare, Drayton, and Ben Jonson had a merry meeting and, it seems, drank too hard, for Shakespeare died of a fever there contracted",[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1991\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage78_78\]-80][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERowse1963453-81] not an impossible scenario since Shakespeare knew Jonson and [Michael Drayton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Drayton). Of the tributes from fellow authors, one refers to his relatively sudden death: "We wondered, Shakespeare, that thou went'st so soon / From the world's stage to the grave's tiring room."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKinney201211-82][^cite_note-83]



[Holy Trinity Church, Stratford-upon-Avon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_Holy_Trinity,_Stratford-upon-Avon), where Shakespeare was baptised and is buried

He was survived by his wife and two daughters. Susanna had married a physician, John Hall, in 1607,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage287_287\]-84] and Judith had married [Thomas Quiney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Quiney), a [vintner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vintner), two months before Shakespeare's death.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987292–294-85] Shakespeare signed his last will and testament on 25 March 1616; the following day, Thomas Quiney, his new son-in-law, was found guilty of fathering an illegitimate son by Margaret Wheeler, and Margaret and her son both died during childbirth. Thomas was ordered by the church court to do public penance, which would have caused much shame and embarrassment for the Shakespeare family.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987292–294-85]

Shakespeare bequeathed the bulk of his large estate to his elder daughter Susanna[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage304_304\]-86] under stipulations that she pass it down intact to "the first son of her body".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan1998395–396-87] The Quineys had three children, all of whom died without marrying.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChambers1988b8,_11,_104-88][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage296_296\]-89] The Halls had one child, Elizabeth, who married twice but died without children in 1670, ending Shakespeare's direct line.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChambers1988b7,_9,_13-90][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987289,_318–319-91] Shakespeare's will scarcely mentions his wife, Anne, who was probably entitled to one-third of his estate automatically.[^cite_note-93] He did make a point, however, of leaving her "my second best bed", a bequest that has led to much speculation.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006483-94][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFrye200516-95][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenblatt2005145–146-96] Some scholars see the bequest as an insult to Anne, whereas others believe that the second-best bed would have been the matrimonial bed and therefore rich in significance.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987301–303-97]



Shakespeare's grave, next to those of Anne Shakespeare, his wife, and [Thomas Nash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Nash_\(relative_of_Shakespeare\)), the husband of his granddaughter

Shakespeare was buried in the [chancel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancel) of the [Holy Trinity Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Trinity_Church,_Stratford-upon-Avon) two days after his death.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987306–307-98][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWellsTaylorJowettMontgomery2005xviii-99] The epitaph carved into the stone slab covering his grave includes a curse against moving his bones, which was carefully avoided during restoration of the church in 2008:[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBBC_News2008-100]

> Good frend for Iesvs sake forbeare,\
> &#x20;To digg the dvst encloased heare.\
> &#x20;Bleste be yͤ man yͭ spares thes stones,\
> &#x20;And cvrst be he yͭ moves my bones.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage306_306\]-101][^cite_note-102]

Translation:

> Good friend, for Jesus' sake forbear,\
> &#x20;To dig the dust enclosed here.\
> &#x20;Blessed be the man that spares these stones,\
> &#x20;And cursed be he that moves my bones.

Some time before 1623 a [funerary monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_funerary_monument) was erected in his memory on the north wall, with a half-effigy of him in the act of writing. Its plaque compares him to [Nestor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nestor_\(mythology\)), [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates), and [Virgil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987308–310-103] In 1623, in conjunction with the publication of the [First Folio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Folio), the [Droeshout engraving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Droeshout_engraving) was published.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECooper200648-104] Shakespeare has been commemorated in many [statues and memorials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memorials_to_William_Shakespeare) around the world, including funeral monuments in [Southwark Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwark_Cathedral) and [Poets' Corner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poets'_Corner) in [Westminster Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Abbey).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWestminster_Abbeyn.d.-105][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESouthwark_Cathedraln.d.-106]

## Plays

Main articles: [Shakespeare's plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_plays), [William Shakespeare's collaborations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shakespeare's_collaborations), [List of works by William Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_works_by_William_Shakespeare), and [Chronology of Shakespeare's plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronology_of_Shakespeare's_plays)



*Procession of Characters from Shakespeare's Plays* by an unknown 19th-century artist

Most playwrights of the period typically collaborated with others at some point, as critics agree Shakespeare did, mostly early and late in his career.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThomson200349-107]

The first recorded works of Shakespeare are *[Richard III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_III_\(play\))* and the three parts of *[Henry VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VI,_Part_1)*, written in the early 1590s during a vogue for [historical drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_\(theatrical_genre\)). Shakespeare's plays are difficult to date precisely, however,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFrye20059-108][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan1998166-109] and studies of the texts suggest that *[Titus Andronicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titus_Andronicus)*, *[The Comedy of Errors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Comedy_of_Errors)*, *[The Taming of the Shrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Taming_of_the_Shrew),* and *[The Two Gentlemen of Verona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Two_Gentlemen_of_Verona)* may also belong to Shakespeare's earliest period.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987159–161-110][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFrye20059-108] His first [histories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespearean_history), which draw heavily on the 1587 edition of Raphael Holinshed's [*Chronicles of England, Scotland, and Ireland*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holinshed's_Chronicles),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDuttonHoward2003147-111] dramatise the destructive results of weak or corrupt rule and have been interpreted as a justification for the origins of the [Tudor dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_dynasty).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERibner2005154–155-112] The early plays were influenced by the works of other Elizabethan dramatists, especially [Thomas Kyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Kyd) and [Christopher Marlowe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Marlowe), by the traditions of medieval drama, and by the plays of [Seneca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_the_Younger).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFrye2005105-113][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERibner200567-114][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBednarz2004100-115] *The Comedy of Errors* was also based on classical models, but no source for *The Taming of the Shrew* has been found, though it has an identical plot but different wording as another play with a similar name.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan1998136-116][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage166_166\]-117] Like *The Two Gentlemen of Verona*, in which two friends appear to approve of rape,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFrye200591-118][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan1998116–117-119][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWerner200196–100-120] the *Shrew*'s story of the taming of a woman's independent spirit by a man sometimes troubles modern critics, directors, and audiences.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFriedman2006159-121]



*Oberon, Titania and Puck with Fairies Dancing*. By [William Blake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Blake), c. 1786.

Shakespeare's early classical and Italianate comedies, containing tight double plots and precise comic sequences, give way in the mid-1590s to the romantic atmosphere of his most acclaimed comedies.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006235-122] *[A Midsummer Night's Dream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Midsummer_Night's_Dream)* is a witty mixture of romance, fairy magic, and comic lowlife scenes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood2003161–162-123] Shakespeare's next comedy, the equally romantic *[The Merchant of Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Merchant_of_Venice)*, contains a portrayal of the vengeful Jewish moneylender [Shylock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shylock), which reflects dominant Elizabethan views but may appear derogatory to modern audiences.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood2003205–206-124][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan1998258-125] The wit and wordplay of *[Much Ado About Nothing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Much_Ado_About_Nothing)*,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006359-126] the charming rural setting of *[As You Like It](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As_You_Like_It)*, and the lively merrymaking of *[Twelfth Night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelfth_Night)* complete Shakespeare's sequence of great comedies.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006362–383-127] After the lyrical *[Richard II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_II_\(play\))*, written almost entirely in verse, Shakespeare introduced prose comedy into the histories of the late 1590s, *[Henry IV, Part 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_IV,_Part_1)* and *[2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_IV,_Part_2)*, and *[Henry V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_V_\(play\))*. *Henry IV* features [Falstaff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falstaff), rogue, wit and friend of Prince Hal. His characters become more complex and tender as he switches deftly between comic and serious scenes, prose and poetry, and achieves the narrative variety of his mature work.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShapiro2005150-128][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGibbons19931-129][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006356-130] This period begins and ends with two tragedies: *[Romeo and Juliet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romeo_and_Juliet)*, the famous romantic tragedy of sexually charged adolescence, love, and death;[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood2003161-131][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan1998206-132] and *[Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar_\(play\))*—based on Sir [Thomas North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_North)'s 1579 translation of [Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch)'s *[Parallel Lives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_Lives)*—which introduced a new kind of drama.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006353,_358-133][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShapiro2005151–153-134] According to the Shakespearean scholar [James Shapiro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_S._Shapiro), in *Julius Caesar*, "the various strands of politics, character, inwardness, contemporary events, even Shakespeare's own reflections on the act of writing, began to infuse each other".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShapiro2005151-135]



*Hamlet, Horatio, Marcellus, and the Ghost of Hamlet's Father*. [Henry Fuseli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Fuseli), 1780–1785.

In the early-17th century, Shakespeare wrote the so-called "[problem plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespearean_problem_play)" *[Measure for Measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_for_Measure)*, *[Troilus and Cressida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troilus_and_Cressida)*, and *[All's Well That Ends Well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All's_Well_That_Ends_Well)* and a number of his best known [tragedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespearean_tragedy).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBradley199185-136][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMuir200512–16-137] Many critics believe that Shakespeare's tragedies represent the peak of his art. [Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Hamlet) has probably been analysed more than any other Shakespearean character, especially for his famous [soliloquy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soliloquy) which begins "[To be or not to be; that is the question](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_be,_or_not_to_be)".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBradley199194-138] Unlike the introverted Hamlet, whose fatal flaw is hesitation, [Othello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Othello_\(character\)) and Lear are undone by hasty errors of judgement.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBradley199186-139] The plots of Shakespeare's tragedies often hinge on such fatal errors or flaws, which overturn order and destroy the hero and those he loves.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBradley199140,_48-140] In *[Othello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Othello)*, [Iago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iago) stokes Othello's sexual jealousy to the point where he murders the innocent wife who loves him.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBradley199142,_169,_195-141][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenblatt2005304-142] In *[King Lear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Lear)*, the old king commits the tragic error of giving up his powers, initiating the events which led to the torture and blinding of the Earl of Gloucester and the murder of Lear's youngest daughter, [Cordelia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cordelia_\(King_Lear\)). According to the critic [Frank Kermode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Kermode), "the play...offers neither its good characters nor its audience any relief from its cruelty".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBradley1991226-143][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006423-144][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKermode2004141–142-145] In *[Macbeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macbeth)*, the shortest and most compressed of Shakespeare's tragedies,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcDonald200643–46-146] uncontrollable ambition incites Macbeth and his wife, [Lady Macbeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Macbeth), to murder the rightful king and usurp the throne until their own guilt destroys them in turn.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBradley1991306-147] In this play, Shakespeare adds a supernatural element to the tragic structure. His last major tragedies, *[Antony and Cleopatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antony_and_Cleopatra)* and *[Coriolanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriolanus)*, contain some of Shakespeare's finest poetry and were considered his most successful tragedies by the poet and critic [T. S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._S._Eliot).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006444-148][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcDonald200669–70-149][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEliot193459-150] Eliot wrote, "Shakespeare acquired more essential history from Plutarch than most men could from the whole [British Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Museum)."[^cite_note-151]

In his final period, Shakespeare turned to [romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_late_romances) or [tragicomedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragicomedy) and completed three more major plays: *[Cymbeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cymbeline)*, *[The Winter's Tale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Winter's_Tale),* and *[The Tempest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tempest)*, as well as the collaboration, *[Pericles, Prince of Tyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pericles,_Prince_of_Tyre)*. Less bleak than the tragedies, these four plays are graver in tone than the comedies of the 1590s, but they end with reconciliation and the forgiveness of potentially tragic errors.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDowden188157-152] Some commentators have seen this change in mood as evidence of a more serene view of life on Shakespeare's part, but it may merely reflect the theatrical fashion of the day.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDowden188160-153][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFrye2005123-154][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcDonald200615-155] Shakespeare collaborated on two further surviving plays, *[Henry VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VIII_\(play\))* and *[The Two Noble Kinsmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Two_Noble_Kinsmen)*, probably with [John Fletcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Fletcher_\(playwright\)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWellsTaylorJowettMontgomery20051247,_1279-156]

### Classification



*The Plays of William Shakespeare*, a painting containing scenes and characters from several plays of Shakespeare; by [Sir John Gilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gilbert_\(painter\)), c. 1849

Shakespeare's works include the 36 plays printed in the [First Folio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Folio) of 1623, listed according to their folio classification as [comedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespearean_comedy), [histories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespearean_history), and [tragedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespearean_tragedy).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyce199691,_193,_513.-157] Two plays not included in the First Folio,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenblattAbrams20121168-158] *[The Two Noble Kinsmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Two_Noble_Kinsmen)* and *[Pericles, Prince of Tyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pericles,_Prince_of_Tyre)*, are now accepted as part of the canon, with today's scholars agreeing that Shakespeare made major contributions to the writing of both.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKathman2003629-159][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyce199691-160] No Shakespearean poems were included in the First Folio, partly because the collection was compiled by men of theatre.[^cite_note-161]

In the late 19th century the critic [Edward Dowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Dowden) classified four of the late comedies as [romances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_late_romances), and though many scholars prefer to call them *[tragicomedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragicomedy)*, Dowden's term is often used.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEdwards19581–10-162][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESnyderCurren-Aquino2007-163] In 1896 [Frederick S. Boas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_S._Boas) coined the term "[problem plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespearean_problem_play)" to describe four plays: *[All's Well That Ends Well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All's_Well_That_Ends_Well)*, *[Measure for Measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_for_Measure)*, *[Troilus and Cressida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troilus_and_Cressida)* and *[Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet)*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchanzer19631–10-164] "Dramas as singular in theme and temper cannot be strictly called comedies or tragedies", he wrote. "We may, therefore, borrow a convenient phrase from the theatre of today and class them together as Shakespeare's problem plays."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoas1896345-165] The term, much debated and sometimes applied to other plays, remains in use, though *Hamlet* is definitively classed as a tragedy.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchanzer19631-166][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom1999325–380-167][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBerry200537-168]

### Performances

Main article: [Shakespeare in performance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare_in_performance)

It is not clear for which companies Shakespeare wrote his early plays. The title page of the 1594 edition of *Titus Andronicus* reveals that the play had been acted by three different troupes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWellsTaylorJowettMontgomery2005xx-169] After the [plagues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death_in_England) of 1592–93, Shakespeare's plays were performed by his own company at [The Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Theatre) and the [Curtain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtain_Theatre) in [Shoreditch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoreditch), north of the Thames.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWellsTaylorJowettMontgomery2005xxi-170] Londoners flocked there to see the first part of *Henry IV*, [Leonard Digges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Digges_\(writer\)) recording, "Let but Falstaff come, Hal, Poins, the rest ... and you scarce shall have a room".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShapiro200516-171] When the company found themselves in dispute with their landlord, they pulled The Theatre down and used the timbers to construct the [Globe Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe_Theatre), the first playhouse built by actors for actors, on the south bank of the Thames at [Southwark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwark).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFoakes19906-172][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShapiro2005125–131-173] The Globe opened in autumn 1599, with *Julius Caesar* one of the first plays staged. Most of Shakespeare's greatest post-1599 plays were written for the Globe, including *Hamlet*, *Othello*, and *King Lear*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFoakes19906-172][^cite_note-FOOTNOTENagler19587-174][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShapiro2005131–132-175]



The reconstructed [Globe Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe_Theatre) on the south bank of the [River Thames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Thames) in [London](17867.md)

After the Lord Chamberlain's Men were renamed the [King's Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_Men_\(playing_company\)) in 1603, they entered a special relationship with the new [King James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_VI_and_I). Although the performance records are patchy, the King's Men performed seven of Shakespeare's plays at court between 1 November 1604, and 31 October 1605, including two performances of *The Merchant of Venice*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWellsTaylorJowettMontgomery2005xxii-58] After 1608, they performed at the indoor [Blackfriars Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackfriars_Theatre) during the winter and the Globe during the summer.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFoakes199033-176] The indoor setting, combined with the [Jacobean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobean_era) fashion for lavishly staged [masques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masques), allowed Shakespeare to introduce more elaborate stage devices. In *Cymbeline*, for example, [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter_\(mythology\)) descends "in thunder and lightning, sitting upon an eagle: he throws a thunderbolt. The ghosts fall on their knees."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006454-177][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHolland2000xli-178]

The actors in Shakespeare's company included the famous [Richard Burbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Burbage), [William Kempe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Kempe), [Henry Condell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Condell) and [John Heminges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Heminges). Burbage played the leading role in the first performances of many of Shakespeare's plays, including *Richard III*, *Hamlet*, *Othello*, and *King Lear*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERingler1997127-179] The popular comic actor Will Kempe played the servant Peter in *Romeo and Juliet* and [Dogberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogberry) in *Much Ado About Nothing*, among other characters.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage210_210\]-180][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChambers1988a341-181] He was replaced around 1600 by [Robert Armin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Armin), who played roles such as [Touchstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Touchstone_\(As_You_Like_It\)) in *As You Like It* and the fool in *King Lear*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShapiro2005247–249-182] In 1613 Sir [Henry Wotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Wotton) recorded that *Henry VIII* "was set forth with many extraordinary circumstances of pomp and ceremony".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWellsTaylorJowettMontgomery20051247-183] However, on 29 June a cannon set fire to the thatch of the Globe and burned the theatre to the ground, an event that pinpoints the date of a Shakespeare play with rare precision.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWellsTaylorJowettMontgomery20051247-183]

### Textual sources



Title page of the *[First Folio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Folio)*, 1623. Copper engraving of Shakespeare by [Martin Droeshout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Droeshout).

In 1623 [John Heminges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Heminges) and [Henry Condell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Condell), two of Shakespeare's colleagues from the King's Men, published the [First Folio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Folio), a collected edition of Shakespeare's plays. It contained 36 texts, including 18 printed for the first time.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWellsTaylorJowettMontgomery2005xxxvii-184] Most of the others had already appeared in [quarto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_size) versions—flimsy books made from sheets of paper folded twice to make four leaves.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWellsTaylorJowettMontgomery2005xxxiv-185][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMowatWerstine2015xlvii-186] No evidence suggests that Shakespeare approved these editions, which the First Folio describes as "stol'n and surreptitious copies".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPollard1909xi-187]

[Alfred Pollard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_W._Pollard) termed some of the pre-1623 versions as "[bad quartos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_quarto)" because of their adapted, paraphrased or garbled texts, which may in places have been reconstructed from memory.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWellsTaylorJowettMontgomery2005xxxiv-185][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPollard1909xi-187][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMaguire199628-188] Where several versions of a play survive, each [differs from the others](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_plays#Shakespeare_and_the_textual_problem). The differences may stem from copying or [printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letterpress_printing) errors, from notes by actors or audience members, or from Shakespeare's own [papers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foul_papers).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBowers19558–10-189][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWellsTaylorJowettMontgomery2005xxxiv–xxxv-190] In some cases, for example, *Hamlet*, *Troilus and Cressida,* and *Othello*, Shakespeare could have revised the texts between the quarto and folio editions. In the case of *[King Lear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Lear)*, however, while most modern editions do conflate them, the 1623 folio version is so different from the 1608 quarto that the *Oxford Shakespeare* prints them both, arguing that they cannot be conflated without confusion.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWellsTaylorJowettMontgomery2005909,_1153-191]

## Poems

In 1593 and 1594, when the theatres were closed because of [plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bubonic_plague), Shakespeare published two narrative poems on sexual themes, *[Venus and Adonis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus_and_Adonis_\(Shakespeare_poem\))* and *[The Rape of Lucrece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rape_of_Lucrece)*. He dedicated them to [Henry Wriothesley, 3rd Earl of Southampton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Wriothesley,_3rd_Earl_of_Southampton). In *Venus and Adonis*, an innocent [Adonis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adonis) rejects the sexual advances of [Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus_\(mythology\)); while in *The Rape of Lucrece*, the virtuous wife [Lucrece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucretia) is raped by the lustful [Tarquin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sextus_Tarquinius).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERoe200621-192] Influenced by [Ovid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovid)'s *[Metamorphoses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamorphoses)*,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFrye2005288-193] the poems show the guilt and moral confusion that result from uncontrolled lust.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERoe20063,_21-194] Both proved popular and were often reprinted during Shakespeare's lifetime. A third narrative poem, *[A Lover's Complaint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Lover's_Complaint)*, in which a young woman laments her seduction by a persuasive suitor, was printed in the first edition of the *Sonnets* in 1609. Most scholars now accept that Shakespeare wrote *A Lover's Complaint*. Critics consider that its fine qualities are marred by leaden effects.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERoe20061-195][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJackson2004267–294-196][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan1998289-197] *[The Phoenix and the Turtle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Phoenix_and_the_Turtle)*, printed in Robert Chester's 1601 *Love's Martyr*, mourns the deaths of the legendary [phoenix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_\(mythology\)) and his lover, the faithful [turtle dove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_turtle_dove). In 1599, two early drafts of sonnets 138 and 144 appeared in *[The Passionate Pilgrim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Passionate_Pilgrim)*, published under Shakespeare's name but without his permission.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERoe20061-195][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan1998289-197][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage327_327\]-198]

### Sonnets

Main article: [Shakespeare's sonnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_sonnets)



Title page from 1609 edition of *Shake-Speares Sonnets*

Published in 1609, the *[Sonnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_sonnets)* were the last of Shakespeare's non-dramatic works to be printed. Scholars are not certain when each of the 154 sonnets was composed, but evidence suggests that Shakespeare wrote sonnets throughout his career for a private readership.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood2003178-199][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage180_180\]-200] Even before the two unauthorised sonnets appeared in *The Passionate Pilgrim* in 1599, [Francis Meres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Meres) had referred in 1598 to Shakespeare's "sugred Sonnets among his private friends".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan1998180-201] Few analysts believe that the published collection follows Shakespeare's intended sequence.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage268_268\]-202] He seems to have planned two contrasting series: one about uncontrollable lust for a married woman of dark complexion (the "dark lady"), and one about conflicted love for a fair young man (the "fair youth"). It remains unclear if these figures represent real individuals, or if the authorial "I" who addresses them represents Shakespeare himself, although [William Wordsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Wordsworth) believed that with the sonnets "Shakespeare unlocked his heart".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan1998180-201][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987\[httpsarchiveorgdetailswilliamshakespea0000schopage180_180\]-200]

> Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?\
> &#x20;Thou art more lovely and more temperate ...

— Opening lines from Shakespeare's [Sonnet 18](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonnet_18).

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMowatWerstinen.d.-203]

The 1609 edition was dedicated to a "Mr. W\.H.", credited as "the only begetter" of the poems. It is not known whether this was written by Shakespeare himself or by the publisher, [Thomas Thorpe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Thorpe), whose initials appear at the foot of the dedication page; nor is it known who Mr. W\.H. was, despite numerous theories, or whether Shakespeare even authorised the publication.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1987268–269-204] Critics praise the *Sonnets* as a profound meditation on the nature of love, sexual passion, procreation, death, and time.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood2003177-205]

## Style

Main article: [Shakespeare's writing style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_writing_style)

Shakespeare's first plays were written in the conventional style of the day. He wrote them in a stylised language that does not always spring naturally from the needs of the characters or the drama.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEClemen2005a150-206] The poetry depends on extended, sometimes elaborate metaphors and conceits, and the language is often rhetorical—written for actors to declaim rather than speak. The grand speeches in *[Titus Andronicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titus_Andronicus)*, in the view of some critics, often hold up the action, for example; and the verse in *[The Two Gentlemen of Verona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Two_Gentlemen_of_Verona)* has been described as stilted.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFrye2005105,_177-207][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEClemen2005b29-208]



*[Pity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pity_\(William_Blake\))* by [William Blake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Blake), 1795, is an illustration of two similes in *Macbeth*:

"And pity, like a naked new-born babe,\
&#x20;Striding the blast, or heaven's cherubim, hors'd\
&#x20;Upon the sightless couriers of the air."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEde_Sélincourt1909174-209]

However, Shakespeare soon began to adapt the traditional styles to his own purposes. The opening [soliloquy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soliloquy) of *[Richard III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_III_\(play\))* has its roots in the self-declaration of [Vice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Vice) in medieval drama. At the same time, Richard's vivid self-awareness looks forward to the soliloquies of Shakespeare's mature plays.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrooke200469-210][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBradbrook2004195-211] No single play marks a change from the traditional to the freer style. Shakespeare combined the two throughout his career, with *[Romeo and Juliet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romeo_and_Juliet)* perhaps the best example of the mixing of the styles.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEClemen2005b63-212] By the time of *Romeo and Juliet*, *[Richard II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_II_\(play\))* and *[A Midsummer Night's Dream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Midsummer_Night's_Dream)* in the mid-1590s, Shakespeare had begun to write a more natural poetry. He increasingly tuned his metaphors and images to the needs of the drama itself.

Shakespeare's standard poetic form was [blank verse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blank_verse), composed in [iambic pentameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iambic_pentameter). In practice, this meant that his verse was usually unrhymed and consisted of ten syllables to a line, spoken with a stress on every second syllable. The blank verse of his early plays is quite different from that of his later ones; its sentences tend to start, pause, and finish at the [end of lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End-stopping), with the risk of monotony.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFrye2005185-213] Once Shakespeare mastered traditional blank verse, he began to interrupt and vary its flow. This technique releases the new power and flexibility of the poetry in plays such as *[Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar_\(play\))* and *[Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet)*. Shakespeare uses it, for example, to convey the turmoil in Hamlet's mind:[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWright2004868-214]

> Sir, in my heart there was a kind of fighting\
> &#x20;That would not let me sleep. Methought I lay\
> &#x20;Worse than the mutines in the bilboes. Rashly—\
> &#x20;And prais'd be rashness for it—let us know\
> &#x20;Our indiscretion sometimes serves us well ...

— *Hamlet*, Act 5, Scene 2, 4–8[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWright2004868-214]

After *Hamlet*, Shakespeare varied his poetic style further, particularly in the more emotional passages of the late tragedies. The literary critic [A. C. Bradley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._C._Bradley) described this style as "more concentrated, rapid, varied, and, in construction, less regular, not seldom twisted or elliptical".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBradley199191-215] In the last phase of his career, Shakespeare adopted many techniques to achieve these effects. These included [run-on lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enjambment), irregular pauses and stops, and extreme variations in sentence structure and length.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcDonald200642–46-216] In *[Macbeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macbeth)*, for example, the language darts from one unrelated metaphor or simile to another: "was the hope drunk/ Wherein you dressed yourself?" (1.7.35–38); "... pity, like a naked new-born babe/ Striding the blast, or heaven's cherubim, hors'd/ Upon the sightless couriers of the air ..." (1.7.21–25). The listener is challenged to complete the sense.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcDonald200642–46-216] The late romances, with their shifts in time and surprising turns of plot, inspired a last poetic style in which long and short sentences are set against one another, clauses are piled up, subject and object are reversed, and words are omitted, creating an effect of spontaneity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcDonald200636,_39,_75-217]

Shakespeare combined poetic genius with a practical sense of the theatre.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGibbons19934-218] Like all playwrights of the time, he dramatised stories from sources such as [Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch) and [Raphael Holinshed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael_Holinshed).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGibbons19931–4-219] He reshaped each plot to create several centres of interest and to show as many sides of a narrative to the audience as possible. This strength of design ensures that a Shakespeare play can survive translation, cutting, and wide interpretation without loss to its core drama.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGibbons19931–7,_15-220] As Shakespeare's mastery grew, he gave his characters clearer and more varied motivations and distinctive patterns of speech. He preserved aspects of his earlier style in the later plays, however. In the [late romances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_late_romances), he deliberately returned to a more artificial style, which emphasised the illusion of theatre.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcDonald200613-221][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMeagher2003358-222]

## Legacy

### Influence

Main article: [Shakespeare's influence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_influence)



*Macbeth Consulting the Vision of the Armed Head*. By [Henry Fuseli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Fuseli), 1793–1794.

Shakespeare's work has made a significant and lasting impression on later theatre and literature. In particular, he expanded the dramatic potential of [characterisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characterisation), plot, [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language), and genre.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChambers194435-223] Until *Romeo and Juliet*, romance had not been viewed as a worthy topic for tragedy.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELevenson200049–50-224] [Soliloquies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soliloquy) had been used mainly to convey information about characters or events, but Shakespeare used them to explore characters' minds.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEClemen1987179-225] His work heavily influenced later poetry. The [Romantic poets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romantic_poetry) attempted to revive Shakespearean verse drama, though with little success. The critic [George Steiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Steiner) described all English verse dramas from [Samuel Taylor Coleridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Taylor_Coleridge) to [Alfred, Lord Tennyson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred,_Lord_Tennyson), as "feeble variations on Shakespearean themes".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESteiner1996145-226] [John Milton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Milton), considered by many to be the most important English poet after Shakespeare, wrote in tribute: "Thou in our wonder and astonishment/ Hast built thyself a live-long monument."[^cite_note-227]

Shakespeare influenced novelists such as [Thomas Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hardy_\(writer\)), [William Faulkner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Faulkner) and [Charles Dickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens). The American novelist [Herman Melville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Melville)'s soliloquies owe much to Shakespeare; his [Captain Ahab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captain_Ahab) in *[Moby-Dick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moby-Dick)* is a classic [tragic hero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragic_hero), inspired by *King Lear*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBryant199882-228] Scholars have identified 20,000 pieces of music linked to Shakespeare's works, including [Felix Mendelssohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Mendelssohn)'s [overture and incidental music for *A Midsummer Night's Dream*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Midsummer_Night's_Dream_\(Mendelssohn\)) and [Sergei Prokofiev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Prokofiev)'s ballet [*Romeo and Juliet*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romeo_and_Juliet_\(Prokofiev\)). His work has inspired several operas, among them [Giuseppe Verdi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Verdi)'s [*Macbeth*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macbeth_\(Verdi\)), *[Otello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otello)* and [*Falstaff*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falstaff_\(opera\)), whose critical standing compares with that of the source plays.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGross2003641–642-229] Shakespeare has also inspired many painters, including the Romantics and the [Pre-Raphaelites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Raphaelite_Brotherhood), while [William Hogarth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Hogarth)'s 1745 painting of actor [David Garrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Garrick) playing Richard III was decisive in establishing the genre of theatrical portraiture in Britain.[^cite_note-230] Shakespeare has been a rich source for filmmakers; [Akira Kurosawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akira_Kurosawa) adapted *Macbeth* and *King Lear* as *[Throne of Blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Throne_of_Blood)* and [*Ran*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ran_\(film\)). Other examples of Shakespeare on film include [Max Reinhardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Reinhardt)'s [*A Midsummer Night's Dream*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Midsummer_Night's_Dream_\(1935_film\)), [Laurence Olivier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurence_Olivier)'s *[Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet_\(1948_film\))* and [Al Pacino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Pacino)'s documentary *[Looking For Richard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Looking_For_Richard)*.[^cite_note-231] [Orson Welles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orson_Welles), a lifelong lover of Shakespeare, directed and starred in [*Macbeth*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macbeth_\(1948_film\)), [*Othello*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Othello_\(1951_film\)) and *[Chimes at Midnight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chimes_at_Midnight)*. The last, in which he plays [John Falstaff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Falstaff), was Welles's favourite of his films.[^cite_note-232]

In Shakespeare's day English grammar, spelling and pronunciation were less standardised than they are now,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECercignani1981-233] and his use of language helped to shape modern English.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECrystal200155–65,_74-234] [Samuel Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Johnson) quoted him more often than any other author in his *[A Dictionary of the English Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Dictionary_of_the_English_Language)*, the first serious work of its type.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWain1975194-235] Expressions such as "with bated breath" (*Merchant of Venice*) and "a foregone conclusion" (*Othello*) have found their way into everyday English speech.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJohnson200212-236][^cite_note-FOOTNOTECrystal200163-237]

Shakespeare's influence extends far beyond his native England and the English language. His reception in Germany was particularly significant; as early as the 18th century Shakespeare was widely translated and popularised in Germany, notably by the [travelling theatre company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seyler_Theatre_Company) of [Abel Seyler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_Seyler), and gradually became a "classic of the [German Weimar era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar_Classicism);" [Christoph Martin Wieland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christoph_Martin_Wieland) was the first to produce complete translations of Shakespeare's plays in any language.[^cite_note-238][^cite_note-239] The Swiss Romantic artist [Henry Fuseli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Fuseli), a friend of [William Blake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Blake), translated *Macbeth* into German.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEParaisz2006130-240] The psychoanalyst [Sigmund Freud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_Freud) drew on Shakespearean psychology, in particular, that of Hamlet, for his theories of human nature.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom1995346-241] The actor and theatre-director [Simon Callow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Callow) writes, "this master, this titan, this genius, so profoundly British and so effortlessly universal, each different culture – German, Italian, Russian – was obliged to respond to the Shakespearean example; for the most part, they embraced it, and him, with joyous abandon, as the possibilities of language and character in action that he celebrated liberated writers across the continent. Some of the most deeply affecting productions of Shakespeare have been non-English, and non-European. He is that unique writer: he has something for everyone."[^cite_note-242]

According to *[Guinness World Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness_World_Records)*, Shakespeare remains the world's best-selling playwright, with sales of his plays and poetry believed to have achieved in excess of four billion copies in the almost 400 years since his death. He is also the third most translated author in history,[^cite_note-243] with his plays [translated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_translations_of_works_by_William_Shakespeare) into over 80 languages, from major world tongues such as German, Hindi, and Japanese, to constructed languages like [Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto) and [Klingon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klingon_language).[^cite_note-244][^cite_note-245] Major festivals, including the [Globe to Globe Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe_to_Globe_Festival) in London (2012), have staged all 37 plays in 37 different languages, with productions ranging from *Hamlet* in [Lithuanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanian_language) to *The Merchant of Venice* in [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew), performed by [Habima Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habima_Theatre), Israel's national theatre.[^cite_note-246][^cite_note-247]

### Critical reputation

Main articles: [Reputation of William Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reputation_of_William_Shakespeare) and [Timeline of Shakespeare criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_Shakespeare_criticism)

> He was not of an age, but for all time.

— [Ben Jonson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Jonson)

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJonson199610-248]

Shakespeare was not revered in his lifetime, but he received a large amount of praise.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDominik19889-249][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrady2001b267-250] In 1598 the cleric and author [Francis Meres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Meres) singled him out from a group of English playwrights as "the most excellent" in both comedy and tragedy.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrady2001b265-251][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreer19869-252] The authors of [the *Parnassus* plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parnassus_plays) at [St John's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Cambridge), numbered him with [Geoffrey Chaucer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Chaucer), [John Gower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gower) and [Edmund Spenser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Spenser).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrady2001b266-253] In the [First Folio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Folio), [Ben Jonson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Jonson) called Shakespeare the "Soul of the age, the applause, delight, the wonder of our stage", although he had remarked elsewhere that "Shakespeare wanted art" (lacked skill).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJonson199610-248]

Between [the Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Restoration) of the monarchy in 1660 and the end of the 17th century, classical ideas were in vogue. As a result, critics of the time mostly rated Shakespeare below [John Fletcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Fletcher_\(playwright\)) and Ben Jonson.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrady2001b269-254] [Thomas Rymer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Rymer), for example, condemned Shakespeare for mixing the comic with the tragic. Nevertheless, the poet and critic [John Dryden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dryden) rated Shakespeare highly, saying of Jonson, "I admire him, but I love Shakespeare".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDryden200671-255] He also famously remarked that Shakespeare "was naturally learned; he needed not the spectacles of books to read nature; he looked inwards, and found her there."[^cite_note-256] For several decades, Rymer's view held sway but during the 18th century, critics began to respond to Shakespeare on his own terms and, like Dryden, to acclaim what they termed his natural genius. A series of scholarly editions of his work, notably those of [Samuel Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Johnson) in 1765 and [Edmond Malone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmond_Malone) in 1790, added to his growing reputation.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrady2001b270–272-257][^cite_note-FOOTNOTELevin1986217-258] By 1800, he was firmly enshrined as the national poet,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrady2001b270-259] and described as the "[Bard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bard) of Avon" (or simply "the Bard").[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDobson1992185–186-260][^cite_note-262] In the 18th and 19th centuries, his reputation also spread abroad. Among those who championed him were the writers [Voltaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaire), [Johann Wolfgang Von Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_von_Goethe), [Stendhal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stendhal) and [Victor Hugo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Hugo).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrady2001b272–74-263][^cite_note-265]



[William Ordway Partridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Ordway_Partridge)'s garlanded statue of William Shakespeare in [Chicago](6886.md)'s [Lincoln Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Park_\(community_area\),_Chicago), typical of many created in the 19th and early 20th centuries

During the [Romantic era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism) Shakespeare was praised by the poet and literary philosopher [Samuel Taylor Coleridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Taylor_Coleridge), and the critic [August Wilhelm Schlegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Wilhelm_Schlegel) translated his plays in the spirit of [German Romanticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Romanticism).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELevin1986223-266] In the 19th century, critical admiration for Shakespeare's genius often bordered on adulation.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESawyer2003113-267] "This King Shakespeare," the essayist [Thomas Carlyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Carlyle) wrote in 1840, "does not he shine, in crowned sovereignty, over us all, as the noblest, gentlest, yet strongest of rallying signs; indestructible".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECarlyle1841161-268] The [Victorians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_era) produced his plays as lavish spectacles on a grand scale.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoch200258–59-269] The playwright and critic [George Bernard Shaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Bernard_Shaw) mocked the cult of Shakespeare worship as "[bardolatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bardolatry)", claiming that the new [naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_\(theatre\)) of [Henrik Ibsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrik_Ibsen)'s plays had made Shakespeare obsolete.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrady2001b276-270]

The modernist revolution in the arts during the early 20th century, far from discarding Shakespeare, eagerly enlisted his work in the service of the [avant-garde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde). The [Expressionists in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Expressionism_\(cinema\)) and the [Futurists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Futurism) in Moscow mounted productions of his plays. The [Marxist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism) playwright and director [Bertolt Brecht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertolt_Brecht) devised an [epic theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_theatre) under the influence of Shakespeare. The poet and critic [T. S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._S._Eliot) argued against Shaw that Shakespeare's "primitiveness" in fact made him truly modern.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrady2001a22–26-271] Eliot, along with [G. Wilson Knight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._Wilson_Knight) and the school of [New Criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Criticism), led a movement towards a closer reading of Shakespeare's imagery. In the 1950s, a wave of new critical approaches replaced modernism and paved the way for [post-modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodernism) studies of Shakespeare.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrady2001a24-272] [Harold Bloom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Bloom) wrote, "Shakespeare was larger than [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato) and than [St. Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Augustine). He *encloses* us because we *see* with his fundamental perceptions."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom2008xii-273] In the 21st century, his reputation remains pre-eminent. [Emma Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emma_Smith_\(scholar\)), professor of Shakespeare Studies at the [University of Oxford](31797.md), in a study published in 2019, called him "the world's greatest playwright";[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESmith2019cover-274] while [Dennis Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Kennedy_\(author\)), Samuel Beckett Professor of Drama and Theatre (Emeritus) at [Trinity College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin), noted in 2004 that he remains "the most performed playwright in the world".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKennedy20042-275] [Gary Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Taylor_\(scholar\)), co-editor of *[The New Oxford Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Oxford_Shakespeare)* published in 2017, wrote; "most of the most important, the most talented, and best educated writers and readers of the last four centuries have considered Shakespeare the best writer in English, or the best modern writer in any language, or the world's best playwright, or the best Western writer of the last thousand years, or the best writer ever, or the greatest genius of all time".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETaylorBourus20171-276]

## Speculation

### Authorship

Main article: [Shakespeare authorship question](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare_authorship_question)

Around 230 years after Shakespeare's death, doubts began to be expressed about the authorship of the works attributed to him.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShapiro201077–78-277] Proposed alternative candidates include [Francis Bacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Bacon), [Christopher Marlowe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Marlowe) and [Edward de Vere, 17th Earl of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_de_Vere,_17th_Earl_of_Oxford).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGibson200548,_72,_124-278] Several "group theories" have also been proposed.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcMichaelGlenn196256-279] All but a few Shakespeare scholars and literary historians consider it a fringe theory, with only a small minority of academics believing that there is reason to question the traditional attribution,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThe_New_York_Times2007-280] but interest in the subject, particularly the [Oxfordian theory of Shakespeare authorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxfordian_theory_of_Shakespeare_authorship), continues into the 21st century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKathman2003620,_625–626-281][^cite_note-FOOTNOTELove2002194–209-282][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1991430–440-283]

### Religion

Main article: [Religious views of William Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_views_of_William_Shakespeare)

Shakespeare conformed to the official state religion,[^cite_note-285] but his private views on religion have been the subject of debate. [Shakespeare's will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_will) uses a Protestant formula, and he was a confirmed member of the [Church of England](5955.md), where he was married, his children were baptised, and where he is buried.

Some scholars are of the view that members of Shakespeare's family were Catholics, at a time when practising Catholicism in England was against the law.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPritchard19793-286] Shakespeare's mother, [Mary Arden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Shakespeare), certainly came from a pious Catholic family. The strongest evidence might be a Catholic statement of faith signed by his father, [John Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Shakespeare), found in 1757 in the rafters of his former house in Henley Street. However, the document is now lost and scholars differ as to its authenticity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood200375–78-287][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd200622–23-288] In 1591 the authorities reported that John Shakespeare had missed church "for fear of process for debt", a common Catholic excuse.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood200378-289][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006416-290][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum198741–42,_286-291] In 1606 the name of William's daughter Susanna appears on a list of those who failed to attend Easter [communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucharist) in Stratford.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWood200378-289][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAckroyd2006416-290][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum198741–42,_286-291]

Other authors argue that there is a lack of evidence about Shakespeare's religious beliefs. Scholars find evidence both for and against Shakespeare's Catholicism, Protestantism, or lack of belief in his plays, but the truth may be impossible to prove.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson200434-292][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShapiro2005167-293]

In 1934, [Rudyard Kipling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudyard_Kipling) published a short story in *[The Strand Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Strand_Magazine)*, "Proofs of Holy Writ", postulating that Shakespeare had helped to polish the prose of the [King James Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_James_Bible), published in 1611.[^cite_note-294]

### Sexuality

Main article: [Sexuality of William Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexuality_of_William_Shakespeare)



Artistic depiction of the Shakespeare family, late 19th century

Few details of Shakespeare's sexuality are known. At 18 he married 26-year-old [Anne Hathaway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Hathaway_\(wife_of_Shakespeare\)), who was pregnant. Susanna, the first of their three children, was born six months later on 26 May 1583. Over the centuries, some readers have posited that Shakespeare's sonnets are autobiographical,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELee190055-295] and point to them as evidence of his love for a young man. Others read the same passages as the expression of intense friendship rather than romantic love.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECasey1998-296][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPequigney1985-297][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEvans1996132-298] The 26 so-called ["Dark Lady"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_Lady_\(Shakespeare\)) sonnets, addressed to a married woman, are taken as evidence of heterosexual liaisons.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFort1927406–414-299]

### Portraiture

Main article: [Portraits of Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portraits_of_Shakespeare)

No written contemporary description of Shakespeare's physical appearance survives, and no evidence suggests that he ever commissioned a portrait. From the 18th century, the desire for authentic Shakespeare portraits fuelled claims that various surviving pictures depicted Shakespeare.[^cite_note-300] That demand also led to the production of several fake portraits, as well as misattributions, re-paintings, and relabelling of portraits of other people.[^cite_note-301][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoenbaum1981190-302]

Some scholars suggest that the [Droeshout portrait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Droeshout_portrait), which [Ben Jonson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Jonson) approved of as a good likeness,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECooper200648,_57-303] and his [Stratford monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_funerary_monument) provide perhaps the best evidence of his appearance.[^cite_note-304] Of the claimed paintings, the art historian [Tarnya Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarnya_Cooper) concluded that the [Chandos portrait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandos_portrait) had "the strongest claim of any of the known contenders to be a true portrait of Shakespeare". After a three-year study supported by the [National Portrait Gallery, London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Portrait_Gallery,_London), the portrait's owners, Cooper contended that its composition date, contemporary with Shakespeare, its subsequent provenance, and the sitter's attire, all supported the attribution.[^cite_note-305]
