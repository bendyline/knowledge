# Tower block

"High-rise" and "Tower Block" redirect here. For other uses, see [High Rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Rise_\(disambiguation\)) and [Tower Block (film)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_Block_\(film\)).

The first [high-rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-rise) in [Jakarta](16275.md), [Indonesia](14579.md) was [Wisma 46](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisma_46) (262 m), which held the longest record, of 20 years, as the highest building in Jakarta.

A **tower block**, **high-rise**, **apartment tower**, **residential tower**, **apartment block**, **block of flats**, or **office tower** is a tall [building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building), as opposed to a [low-rise building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-rise_building); it is defined differently in terms of height depending on the [jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction). It is used as a [residential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apartment_building) or [office building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_building), or has other functions, including [hotel](14276.md), [retail](183515.md), or with multiple purposes combined. Residential high-rise buildings are also known in some varieties of English, such as [British English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English), as **tower blocks** and may be referred to as **MDUs**, standing for **multi-dwelling units**.[^cite_note-1] A very tall high-rise building is referred to as a [skyscraper](29485.md).

High-rise buildings became possible to construct with the invention of the [elevator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevator) (lift) and with less expensive, more abundant building materials. The materials used for the [structural system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_system) of high-rise buildings are [reinforced concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforced_concrete) and [steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel). Most [North American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American)–style [skyscrapers](29485.md) have a [steel frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel_frame), while residential blocks are usually constructed of concrete. There is no clear difference between a tower block and a skyscraper, although a building with forty or more stories and taller than 150 metres (490 ft) is generally considered a skyscraper.[^cite_note-2]

High-rise structures pose particular design challenges for [structural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_engineering) and [geotechnical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geotechnical_engineering) engineers, particularly if situated in a [seismically active](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismic_activity) region or if the underlying soils have geotechnical risk factors such as high [compressibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compressibility) or [bay mud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_mud). They also pose serious challenges to firefighters during emergencies in high-rise structures. New and old building design, building systems such as the building [standpipe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standpipe_\(firefighting\)) system, [HVAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HVAC) systems (heating, ventilation and air conditioning), [fire sprinkler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_sprinkler) systems, and other things such as stairwell and [elevator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevator) evacuations pose significant problems. Studies are often required to ensure that pedestrian [wind comfort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_engineering#Wind_comfort) and wind danger concerns are addressed. In order to allow less wind exposure, to transmit more daylight to the ground and to appear more slender, many high-rises have a design with [setbacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Setback_\(architecture\)).

[Apartment buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apartment_building) have technical and economic advantages in areas of high [population density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_density), and have become a distinctive feature of housing accommodation in virtually all densely populated [urban areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_area) around the world. In contrast with [low-rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-rise) and single-family houses, apartment blocks accommodate more inhabitants per unit of area of land and decrease the cost of [municipal infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipal_infrastructure).

## Definition

Various bodies have defined "high-rise":

- [Emporis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emporis) defines a high-rise as "A multi-story structure between 35–100 metres (115–328 ft) tall, or a building of unknown height from 12–39 floors."

  [^cite_note-3]

- The *New Shorter Oxford English Dictionary* defines a high-rise as "a building having many storeys".

- The International Conference on Fire Safety in High-Rise Buildings defined a high-rise as "any structure where the height can have a serious impact on [evacuation](1536572.md)"

  [^cite_note-4]

- In the U.S., the [National Fire Protection Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Fire_Protection_Association) defines a high-rise as being higher than 75 feet (23 m), or about seven stories.

  [^cite_note-5]

- Most building engineers, inspectors, architects and similar professionals define a high-rise as a building that is at least 75 feet (23 m) tall.

## History



Sliding ladder for firefighters in 1904

High-rise apartment buildings had already appeared in [antiquity](252905.md): the *[insulae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insula_\(building\))* in [Ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) and several other cities in the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire), some of which might have reached up to ten or more stories,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAldrete200479f.-6] one reportedly having 200 stairs.[^cite_note-7] Because of the destruction caused by poorly built high-rise *insulae* collapsing,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAldrete200478-8] several [Roman emperors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_emperor), beginning with [Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus) (r. 30 BC – 14 AD), set limits of 20–25 metres (66–82 ft) for multi-story buildings, but met with limited success,[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10] as these limits were often ignored despite the likelihood of taller *insulae* collapsing.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAldrete200478–9-11] The lower [floors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storey) were typically occupied by either shops or wealthy families, while the upper stories were rented out to the lower classes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAldrete200479_ff.-12] Surviving [Oxyrhynchus Papyri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxyrhynchus_Papyri) indicate that seven-story buildings even existed in [provincial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_province) towns, such as in third century AD [Hermopolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermopolis) in [Roman Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Egypt).[^cite_note-PapyrusOxyrhynchus-13]

In [Arab Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt_in_the_Middle_Ages), the initial capital city of [Fustat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fustat) housed many high-rise residential buildings, some seven stories tall that could reportedly accommodate hundreds of people. [Al-Muqaddasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Muqaddasi), in the 10th century, described them as resembling [minarets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minaret), while [Nasir Khusraw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasir_Khusraw), in the early 11th century, described some of them rising up to 14 stories, with [roof gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roof_garden) on the top story complete with ox-drawn [water wheels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_wheel) for [irrigating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation) them.[^cite_note-Behrens-Abouseif_1992_6-14][^cite_note-Joan_D._Barghusen,_Bob_Moulder_2001_11-15] By the 16th century, [Cairo](6293.md) also had high-rise apartment buildings where the two lower floors were for commercial and storage purposes and the multiple stories above them were [rented](342324.md) out to [tenants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leasehold_estate).[^cite_note-Mortada_2003_viii-16]

The skyline of many important [medieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) cities was dominated by large numbers of high-rising urban towers, which fulfilled defensive but also representative purposes. The residential [Towers of Bologna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Towers_of_Bologna) numbered between 80 and 100 at a time, the largest of which still rise to 97.2 m (319 ft). In [Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence), a law of 1251 decreed that all urban buildings should be reduced to a height of less than 26 m (85 ft), the regulation immediately put into effect.[^cite_note-Werner_Müller_345-17] Even medium-sized towns such as [San Gimignano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Gimignano) are known to have featured 72 towers up to 51 m (167 ft) in height.[^cite_note-Werner_Müller_345-17]

The [Hakka people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakka_people) in [southern China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_China) have adopted [communal living](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communal_living) structures designed to be easily defensible in the forms of *weilongwu* (围龙屋), as well as *[(yuanzhai) tulou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujian_tulou)* \[(園寨)土楼], often abbreviated to *yuanlou* \[園楼][^cite_note-18]; the latter are large, enclosed and fortified earth buildings, between two (though usually at least three) and five stories high[^cite_note-19] and housing up to 80[^cite_note-20] families. The oldest tulou still standing dates from the 14th century.

High-rises were built in the [Yemeni](350939.md) city of [Shibam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibam) in the 16th century. The houses of Shibam are all made of [mud bricks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mudbrick), but about five hundred of them are [tower houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_house), which rise five to sixteen stories high,[^cite_note-Helfritz-21] with each floor having one or two [apartments](265567.md).[^cite_note-Jerome-22][^cite_note-UNESCO-23] This technique of building was implemented to protect residents from Bedouin attacks. While Shibam has existed for around two thousand years, most of the city's houses date from the 16th century. The city has the tallest mud buildings in the world, some more than 30 [meters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meter) (100 [feet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot_\(unit\))) high.[^cite_note-Shipman_154–62-24] Shibam has been called "one of the oldest and best examples of urban planning based on the principle of vertical construction" or "[Manhattan](45470.md) of the desert".[^cite_note-UNESCO-23]

The engineer's definition of high-rise buildings comes from the development of [fire trucks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_truck) in the late 19th century. [Magirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magirus) had shown the first [cogwheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cogwheel) sliding ladder in 1864. The first [turntable ladder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turntable_ladder) drawn by horses was developed in 1892 which had a length of 25 meters (82 ft). The [extension ladder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_ladder) was motorized by Magirus in 1904. A limit of 22 meters (72 ft) for the highest floor was common in building regulations at that time and it still remains so in Germany. The common height for turntable ladders later increased to 32 meters (105 feet), so that 30 meters (98 ft) is a common limit in some building regulations, for example in Switzerland. Any building that exceeds the height of the usual turntable ladders in a city must install additional fire safety equipment, so that these high-rise buildings have a different section in the building regulations in the world.



A residential block in [Steinfurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steinfurt), Westphalia, Germany, forming a "Y"

The residential tower block with its typical [concrete construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete_construction) is a familiar feature of [Modernist architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernist_architecture). Influential examples include [Le Corbusier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Corbusier)'s "housing unit", his [Unité d'Habitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit%C3%A9_d'Habitation), repeated in various European cities starting with his [Cité radieuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ville_Radieuse) in Marseille (1947–52), constructed of *[béton brut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%A9ton_brut)*, rough-cast [concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete), as steel for framework was unavailable in post-war France. Residential tower blocks became standard in housing urban populations displaced by [slum clearances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slum_clearance) and "[urban renewal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_renewal)". High-rise projects after [World War II](32927.md) typically rejected the classical designs of the [early skyscrapers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_skyscrapers), instead embracing the uniform [international style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Style_\(architecture\)); many older skyscrapers were redesigned to suit contemporary tastes or even got demolished - such as New York's [Singer Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singer_Building), once the world's tallest skyscraper. However, with the movements of [Postmodernism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodern_architecture), [New Urbanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Urbanism), and [New Classical Architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Classical_Architecture), that established since the 1980s, a more classical approach came back to global skyscraper design, that is popular today.

Other contemporary styles and movements in high-rise design include [organic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_architecture), [sustainable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_architecture), [neo-futurist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Futurism), [structuralist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structuralism_\(architecture\)), [high-tech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-tech_architecture), [deconstructivist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deconstructivism), [blob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blobitecture), [digital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_architecture), [streamline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streamline_Moderne), [novelty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novelty_architecture), [critical regionalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_regionalism), [vernacular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernacular_architecture), [Art Deco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Deco) (or *Art Deco Nouveau*), and [neohistorist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neohistorist), also known as [revivalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revivalism_\(architecture\)).

Currently, the tallest high-rise apartment building in the world is the [Central Park Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Park_Tower) on [Billionaires' Row](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billionaires'_Row) in [Midtown Manhattan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midtown_Manhattan), towering at 1,550 feet (470 m).

### Streets in the sky

"Streets in the sky" redirects here. For the third studio album by UK rock band The Enemy, see [Streets in the Sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streets_in_the_Sky).



"Street in the sky" at Park Hill

Streets in the sky is a style of [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture) that emerged in Britain in the 1960s and 1970s.[^cite_note-SIS-25] Generally built to replace run-down [terraced housing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terraced_house), the new designs included not only modern improvements such as inside [toilets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toilet), but also shops and other community facilities within high-rise blocks.[^cite_note-intute-26] Examples of the buildings and developments are [Trellick Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trellick_Tower), [Balfron Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balfron_Tower), [Broadwater Farm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadwater_Farm), [Robin Hood Gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Hood_Gardens) and [Keeling House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keeling_House) in London, [Hunslet Grange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunslet_Grange) in [Leeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeds) and [Park Hill, Sheffield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park_Hill,_Sheffield), and Castlefields and [Southgate Estate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southgate_Estate), [Runcorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runcorn). These were an attempt to develop a new architecture, differentiated from earlier large housing estates, such as [Quarry Hill flats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarry_Hill,_Leeds) in Leeds.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28] [Alison and Peter Smithson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alison_and_Peter_Smithson) were the architects of [Robin Hood Gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Hood_Gardens).[^cite_note-29] As another large example, in 2005 it was decided to carry out a 20-year process of demolition and replacement of dwellings with modern houses in the [Aylesbury Estate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aylesbury_Estate) in south London, built in 1970.[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31] The [Hulme Crescents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulme_Crescents) in [Manchester](20206.md) were the largest social housing scheme in Europe when built in 1972 but lasted just 22 years. The Crescents had one of the worst reputations of any British social housing schemes and were marred by numerous design and practical problems.[^cite_note-32]

The ideal of Streets in the Sky worked rarely in practice. Unlike an actual city street, these walkways were not [thoroughfares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoroughfare), and often came to a dead end multiple storeys above the ground. They lacked a regular flow of passers-by, and the walkways and especially the stairwells could not be seen by anyone elsewhere, so there was no deterrent to crime and disorder. There were no "eyes on the street" as advocated by [Jane Jacobs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Jacobs) in her book *[The Death and Life of Great American Cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Death_and_Life_of_Great_American_Cities)*. The [Unité d'Habitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit%C3%A9_d'Habitation) in [Marseille](40888948.md) provides a more successful example of the concept, with the fifth floor walkway including a shop and café.[^cite_note-33]

### Towers in the park and microdistricts



[Debney Meadows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debney_Estate) (Flemington Estate) (1962–1965) in [Melbourne](17306237.md).



A panel housing [microdistrict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microdistrict) in [Chertanovo Severnoye District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chertanovo_Severnoye_District) of Moscow is built with a similar idea in mind

[Towers in the park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Towers_in_the_park) is a [morphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphology_\(architecture_and_engineering\)) of [modernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernist)[^cite_note-GLOBE-34] high-rise apartment buildings characterized by a high-rise building surrounded by a swath of landscaped land; e.g., the tower does not directly front the street.

It is based on an ideology popularised by [Le Corbusier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Corbusier) with the [Plan Voisin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plan_Voisin), an expansion of the [Garden city movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_city_movement) aimed at reducing the problem of urban congestion. It was introduced in several large cities across the world, notably in [North America](21139.md),[^cite_note-GLOBE-34] [Europe](9239.md)[^cite_note-35] and [Australia](4689264.md)[^cite_note-36] as a solution for housing, especially for [public housing](177912.md), reaching a peak of popularity in the 1960s with the introduction of [prefabrication technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefabricated_building).

By the early 1970s, opposition to this style of towers mounted, with many, including urban planners, now referring to them as "[ghettos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghetto)".[^cite_note-37] Neighbourhoods like [St. James Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._James_Town) were originally designed to house young "swinging single" middle class residents, but the apartments lacked appeal and the area quickly became much poorer. From its early days of implementation the concept was criticised for making residents feel unsafe, including large empty common areas dominated by [gang](80408.md) culture and crime. The layout was criticised for normalising anti-social behaviour and hampering the efforts of essential services, particularly for [law enforcement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_enforcement).[^cite_note-38]

The history of [microdistricts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microdistrict) as an urban planning concept dates back to the 1920s, when the Soviet Union underwent rapid [urbanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanization). Under the [Soviet urban planning ideologies of the 1920s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_urban_planning_ideologies_of_the_1920s), residential complexes—compact territories with residential dwellings, schools, shops, entertainment facilities, and [green spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_belt)—started to prevail in urban planning practice, as they allowed for more careful and efficient planning of the rapid urban expansion. These complexes were seen as an opportunity to build a collective society,[^cite_note-van_Dijk-39] an environment suitable and necessary for the new way of life.[^cite_note-Gentile-40]

## Developments by region

### Asia



High-rise buildings, [Hong Kong](13404.md)



Modern towers of [Vladivostok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladivostok), Russia



Road in front, skyline in background ([Abu Dhabi](18950756.md), Middle East)



[Panoramic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panorama) view of the skyline in [Hyderabad](37534.md), India, with several high-rise residential buildings

Residential tower complexes are common in Asian countries such as China, India, Bangladesh, Indonesia, Taiwan, Singapore, Japan, Pakistan, Iran and South Korea, as urban densities are very high. In [Singapore](27318.md) and urban [Hong Kong](13404.md), land prices are so high that a large portion of the population lives in high-rise apartments. In fact, over 60% of Hong Kong residents live in apartments, many of them [condominiums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condominium_\(living_space\)). Of them in 2020, 2,112,138 were identified residents of public housing,[^cite_note-41] which is 28% of the total population.

[Sarah Williams Goldhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Williams_Goldhagen) (2012) celebrated the work of innovative architecture firms such as WOHA (based in Singapore), Mass Studies (based in Seoul), Amateur Architecture Studio (based in [Hangzhou, China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangzhou,_China)), and the New York City-based Steven Holl in the transformation of residential towers into "vertical communities" or "vertical cities in the sky" providing aesthetic, unusually designed silhouettes on the skyline, comfortable private spaces and attractive public spaces. None of these "functional, handsome, and humane high-rise residential buildings" are [affordable housing](3531662.md).[^cite_note-Goldhagen-42][^cite_note-Meinhold-43]

#### China



A modern *[xiaoqu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiaoqu)* in the city of [Qingdao](250171.md)

The 2012 Pritzker Prize was awarded to Chinese architect Wang Shu. Among his winning designs is the Vertical Courtyard Apartments, six 26-story towers by his architectural firm Amateur Architecture Studio built in Hangzhou.[^cite_note-Meinhold-43] "These towers were designed to house two-story apartments, in which every inhabitant would enjoy "the illusion of living on the second floor", accomplished by folding concrete floor planes (like "bamboo mats," claims the firm), so that every third story opens into a private courtyard. In the larger towers, the two-story units are stacked slightly askew, adding to the visual interest of the variegated façades (Goldhagen 2012)."[^cite_note-Goldhagen-42]

#### Japan



[Okayama prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okayama_prefecture) "mansions"

[Housing in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing_in_Japan) includes various traits coming from different eras. The word *[danchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danchi)* now either means an employer-provided housing or has a meaning similar to "[projects](177912.md)". For modern hi-rises, there are two [borrowed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasei-eigo) words to make a distinction:

- "*Apaato*" (アパート）is used to describe a rather small apartment, initially made to be rented;

- a large, modern apartment would be a "*mansion*" (マンション). The "mansion" nickname is used for both residential towers and for individual condominium apartments (for being roomy, spacey enough to compare to detached houses).

#### South Korea

In South Korea, the tower blocks are called Apartment Complex (아파트 단지). The first residential towers began to be built after the [Korean War](16772.md). The South Korean government needed to build many apartment complexes in the cities to be able to accommodate the citizens. In the 60 years since, as the population increased considerably, tower blocks have become more common. This time, however, the new tower blocks integrated shopping malls, parking systems, and other convenient facilities.

[Samsung Tower Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samsung_Tower_Palace) in [Seoul](19159283.md), South Korea, is the tallest apartment complex in Asia.

In Seoul, approximately 80 percent of its residents live in apartment complexes which comprise 98 percent of recent residential construction.[^cite_note-44] Seoul proper is noted for its [population density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_density), eight times greater than [Rome](25458.md), though less than [Manhattan](45470.md) and Paris. Its metropolitan area is the densest in the [OECD](33853117.md).[^cite_note-45]

#### India

Several metropolitan cities in [India](14533.md) have witnessed a surge in vertical development in the 21st-century.[^cite_note-\:0-46] Most skyscrapers in cities like [Mumbai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_Mumbai), [Hyderabad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_Hyderabad), and [Delhi-NCR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_Delhi-NCR) consist of residential units which are commonly referred to as "apartment complexes", "apartment societies", or "gated societies".[^cite_note-47] These residential high-rise buildings are typically located in affluent neighborhoods and include recreational amenities for their residents. The tallest apartment complex in India is the [Palais Royale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palais_Royale,_Mumbai) in Mumbai.[^cite_note-48]

Several commercial high-rise buildings also exist and are predominantly used as office spaces, often with multiple organizations sharing the various floors within the high-rise building.[^cite_note-\:0-46]

Renovated apartment building from 1963 in [Bucharest, Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bucharest,_Romania). With the 2010s, renovation of older apartment buildings in Eastern Europe has become common, especially in countries which get [EU funds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_Funds_and_Cohesion_Fund).

Although some [Central](5188.md) and [Eastern European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe) countries during the [interwar period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interwar_period), such as the [Second Polish Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Polish_Republic), already started building housing estates that were considered to be of a high standard for their time, many of these structures perished during the Second World War.

In the [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc), tower blocks were constructed in great numbers to produce plenty of cheap accommodation for the growing postwar populations of the [USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) and [its satellite states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_state#Soviet_satellite_states). This took place mostly in the 1950s, 1960s and 1970s, though in the [People's Republic of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Republic_of_Poland) this process started [even earlier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-Year_Plan) due to the severe damages that Polish cities sustained during World War II. Throughout the former Eastern Bloc countries, tower blocks built during the Soviet years make up much of the current housing estates and most of them were built in the specific [socialist realist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_realism) style of architecture that was dominant in the territories east of the [Iron Curtain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Curtain): blocky buildings of that era are colloquially known as [Khrushchyovka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khrushchyovka). However, there were also larger and more ambitious projects built in Eastern Europe at the time, which have since become recognisable examples of post-war [modernism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernist_architecture); such as the largest *[falowiec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falowiec)* building in the [Przymorze Wielkie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Przymorze_Wielkie) district of [Gdańsk](12099.md), with a length of 860 m (2,821.52 ft) and 1,792 flats, it is the second longest housing block in Europe.[^cite_note-zupgra-49]

In [Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Republic_of_Romania), the mass construction of standardised housing blocks began in the 1950s and 1960s with the outskirts of the cities, some of which were made up of slums.[^cite_note-Elleh2014-50] Construction continued in the 1970s and 1980s, under the [systematisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematization_\(Romania\)) programme of [Nicolae Ceaușescu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolae_Ceau%C8%99escu), which consisted largely of the demolition and reconstruction of existing villages, towns, and cities, in whole or in part, in order to build blocks of flats (*blocuri*), as a result of increasing urbanisation following an accelerated industrialisation process.[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52] In [Czechoslovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechoslovak_Socialist_Republic) (now the [Czech Republic](5321.md) and [Slovakia](26830.md)), [panelák](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panel%C3%A1k) building under [Marxism–Leninism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism%E2%80%93Leninism) resulted from two main factors: the postwar housing shortage and the ideology of the [ruling party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_Czechoslovakia).

In Eastern European countries, opinions about these buildings vary greatly, with some deeming them as eyesores on their city's landscape while others glorify them as relics of a bygone age and historical examples of unique architectural styles (such as socialist realism, [brutalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brutalism), etc.).[^cite_note-zupgra-49] Since the [dissolution of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union), and especially in the late 1990s and early 2000s, many of the former Eastern Bloc countries have begun construction of new, more expensive and modern housing. The [Śródmieście](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Ar%C3%B3dmie%C5%9Bcie,_Warsaw) borough of [Warsaw](32908.md), the capital of Poland, has seen the development of an array of skyscrapers. Russia is also currently undergoing a dramatic buildout, growing a commercially shaped skyline. Moreover, the ongoing changes made to postwar housing estates since the 2000s in former communist countries vary – ranging from simply applying a new coat of paint to the previously grey exterior to thorough modernisation of entire buildings.[^cite_note-zupgra-49]

In the [European Union](9317.md), among former [Warsaw Pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw_Pact) states, a majority of the population lives in flats in Latvia (65.1%), Estonia (61.3%), Lithuania (58.9%), the Czech Republic (51.0%), Slovakia (46.3%) and Bulgaria (46.2%) (as of 2025, data from [Eurostat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostat)).[^cite_note-53] However, not all flat dwellers in Eastern Europe live in Cold War-era blocks of flats; many live in buildings constructed after the fall of the Berlin Wall, and some in buildings that survived World War II.

In Western Europe, there are fewer high-rise buildings because of the historic city centres. In the 1960s, developers began demolishing older buildings to replace them with modern high-rise buildings.In the European Union, among the remaining EU countries, the majority of the population lives in apartments in Malta (65.2%), Spain (64.7%), Greece (59.8%), Germany (58.6%), Italy (57.8%), Portugal (49.6%), Austria (49.2%) and Sweden (48.4%) (as of 2025, Eurostat data).[^cite_note-54] However, not all residents of these countries live in Cold War-era apartment buildings; many live in buildings constructed after the fall of the Berlin Wall, and some in buildings that survived World War II.

##### Belgium

In [Brussels](3708.md), there are numerous modern high-rise buildings in the [Northern Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Quarter_\(Brussels\)) business district (also called *Little Manhattan*).[^cite_note-55][^cite_note-56]

##### France



[La Défense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_D%C3%A9fense) at night

There are some tall residential buildings in *La Défense* district, such as [Tour Défense 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tour_D%C3%A9fense_2000), even though the district is mainly "commercial". This allows the residents to walk to the nearby office buildings without using vehicles.

##### Great Britain

Main article: [Tower blocks in Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_blocks_in_Great_Britain)



[Argyle Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argyle_Building,_Glasgow) in [Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow)

Tower blocks were first built in the United Kingdom after the [Second World War](32927.md), and were seen as a cheap way to replace 19th-century urban slums and war-damaged buildings. They were originally seen as desirable, but quickly fell out of favour as tower blocks attracted rising crime and social disorder, particularly after the collapse of [Ronan Point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronan_Point) in 1968.[^cite_note-57]

Although Tower blocks are controversial and numerous examples have been demolished, many still remain in large cities. Due to lack of proper regulation, some tower blocks present a significant fire risk and even though there have been efforts to make them more safe,[^cite_note-58] modern safety precautions can be prohibitively expensive to retrofit. The [Grenfell Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grenfell_Tower_fire) fire in 2017 was partly caused by council ignorance, as a local action group complained to the council about the fire hazards of the tower several years before the incident, yet remedial work had not been carried out.[^cite_note-guardiansafety-59] This fire further made tower blocks less desirable to British residents.

There are old high-rise buildings built in the 1960s and 1970s in areas of [London](17867.md) such as [Tower Hamlets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Borough_of_Tower_Hamlets), [Newham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Borough_of_Newham), [Hackney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hackney,_London), and virtually any area in London with [council housing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_house). Some new high-rises are being built in areas such as [Central London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_London), [Southwark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwark), and [Nine Elms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nine_Elms). In east London, some old high-rises are being gentrified, in addition to new high-rises being built in areas such as [Stratford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratford,_London) and [Canary Wharf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canary_Wharf).

##### Ireland



[Capital Dock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_Dock), 22-story "mixed use" building in [Dublin](8504.md), Ireland

###### Republic of Ireland

The majority of residential high-rise buildings in the Republic of Ireland were concentrated in the suburb of [Ballymun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballymun), Dublin. The [Ballymun Flats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballymun_Flats) were built between 1966 and 1969: seven 15-story towers, nineteen 8-story blocks and ten 4-story blocks.[^cite_note-60] These were the "seven towers" referred to in the [U2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U2) song "Running to Stand Still". They have since been demolished.&#x20;

Inner Dublin flat complexes, typically of four to five storeys include Sheriff Street (demolished), [Fatima Mansions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatima_Mansions_\(housing\)) (demolished and redeveloped), St Joseph's Gardens (demolished; replaced by Killarney Court flat complex), St Teresa's Gardens, Dolphin House, Liberty House, St Michael's Estate (eight storeys) and O'Devaney Gardens and a lot more mainly throughout the north and south inner city of Dublin. Suburban flat complexes were built exclusively on the northside of the city in [Ballymun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballymun), [Coolock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coolock) and [Kilbarrack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilbarrack). These flats were badly affected by a heroin epidemic that hit working-class areas of Dublin in the 1980s and early 1990s.

Residential tower blocks were previously uncommon outside of Dublin, but during the era of the [Celtic Tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Tiger) the largest cities such as Dublin, [Cork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cork_\(city\)), [Limerick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limerick) and [Galway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galway) witnessed new large apartment building, although their heights have generally been restricted. Some large towns such as [Navan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navan), [Drogheda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drogheda), [Dundalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dundalk) and [Mullingar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mullingar) have also witnessed the construction of many modern apartment blocks.

###### Northern Ireland

Tower blocks in Northern Ireland were never built to the frequency as in cities on the island of [Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain), but taller high-rises are generally more common than in the Republic of Ireland. Most tower blocks and flat complexes are found in [Belfast](5046.md), although many of these have been demolished since the 1990s and replaced with traditional [public housing units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_housing). The mid-rise Divis flats complex in west Belfast was built between 1968 and 1972. It was demolished in the early 1990s after the residents demanded new houses due to mounting problems with their flats. [Divis Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divis_Tower), built separately in 1966, still stands, however; and in 2007 work began to convert the former British Army base at the top two floors into new dwellings. Divis Tower was for several decades Ireland's tallest residential building, having since being surpassed by the privately owned [Obel Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obel_Tower) in the city centre. In the north of the city, the iconic seven-tower complex in the [New Lodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Lodge,_Belfast) remains, although so too the problems that residents face, such as poor piping and limited sanitation. Farther north, the four tower blocks in [Rathcoole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rathcoole_\(Belfast\)) dominate the local skyline, while in south Belfast, the tower blocks in Seymour Hill, Belvoir and [Finaghy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finaghy) remain standing.

Most of the aforementioned high-rise flats in the city were built by the [Northern Ireland Housing Trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Housing_Trust) (NIHT) as part of overspill housing schemes, the first such development being the pair of point blocks in East Belfast's [Cregagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cregagh) estate. These eleven-story towers were completed in 1961 and were the first tall council housing blocks on the island of Ireland.[^cite_note-61] The NIHT also designed the inner city Divis Flats complex. The six-to-eight-storey deck-access flats that comprised most of the Divis estate were of poor build quality and were all demolished by the early 1990s.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGlendinningMuthesius1994367-62] Similar slab blocks were built by the NIHT in East Belfast (Tullycarnet) and Derry's [Bogside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogside) area, all four of which have been demolished.

[Belfast Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belfast_Corporation) constructed seven tower blocks on the former Victoria Barracks site in the New Lodge district. While the Corporation built some [mid-rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-rise) flats as part of [slum clearance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slum_clearance_in_the_United_Kingdom) schemes (most notably the now demolished Unity Flats and the Weetabix Flats in the Shankill area), New Lodge was its only high-rise project in the inner city; there were three more in outlying areas of the city during the 1960s, two being in Mount Vernon in North Belfast and one being in the [Clarawood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarawood) estate, East Belfast. The [Royal Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Victoria_Hospital,_Belfast) built three thirteen-story towers for use as staff accommodation, prominently located adjacent to the M2 Motorway at Broadway. Belfast City Hospital also constructed a high-rise slab block which since privatisation has been named Bradbury Court, formerly known as Erskine House. Queens University Belfast built several eleven storey towers at its Queens Elms student accommodation. Of the three sixteen-story point blocks of [Larne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larne) Borough Council in the late 1960s, only one remains.[^cite_note-63]

### North America

#### Canada

See also: [List of tallest buildings in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_Canada)



High-rise towers in [New Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Westminster)

In Canada, large [multi-family buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-family_buildings) are usually known as *apartment buildings* or *apartment blocks* if they are rented from one common landowner, or *condominiums* or *condo towers* if each dwelling unit is individually owned; they may be called *low-rise* (or *walk-up*), *mid-rise*, *high-rise*, or *skyscraper* depending on their height. Tall residential towers are a staple building type in all large cities. Their relative prominence in Canadian cities varies substantially, however. In general, more populated cities have more high-rises than smaller cities, due to a relative scarcity of land and a greater demand for housing.

However, some cities such as [Quebec City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_City) and [Halifax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halifax,_Nova_Scotia) have fewer high-rise buildings due to several factors: a focus on [historic preservation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historic_preservation), height restrictions, and lower growth rates. In middle-sized cities with a relatively low population density, such as [Calgary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calgary), [Edmonton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonton), [Winnipeg](100730.md), or [Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton,_Ontario) there are more apartment towers but they are greatly outnumbered by [single-family houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_house). Most of the largest residential towers in Canada are found in [Montreal](7954681.md), [Toronto](64646.md), and [Vancouver](32706.md); the country's most densely populated cities.

Toronto contains the second largest concentration of high-rise apartment buildings in North America (after New York). In Canada, like in other New World countries, but unlike Western Europe, most high-rise towers are located in the city centre (or "downtown"), where smaller, older buildings were demolished to make way in [redevelopment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redevelopment) schemes.

[Central Park Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Park_Tower) in [Manhattan](45470.md), New York City, the tallest residential high-rise tower in the world, December 2020

In the United States, tower blocks are commonly referred to as "midrise" or "highrise apartment buildings", depending on their height, while buildings that house fewer flats (apartments), or are not as tall as the tower blocks, are called "lowrise apartment buildings". Specifically, "midrise" buildings are as tall as the streets are wide, allowing five hours of sunlight on the street.[^cite_note-64]

Some of the first residential towers were the [Castle Village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle_Village,_Manhattan) towers in Manhattan, New York City, completed in 1939. Their cross-shaped design was copied in towers in [Parkchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkchester,_Bronx) and [Stuyvesant Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuyvesant_Town) residential developments.

The government's experiments in the 1960s and 1970s to use high-rise apartments as a means of providing the housing solution for the poor broadly resulted in failure. Made in the [tower in the park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_in_the_park) style, all but a few high-rise [housing projects](177912.md) in the nation's largest [cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cities), such as [Cabrini–Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabrini%E2%80%93Green) and [Robert Taylor Homes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Taylor_Homes) in Chicago, [Penn South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penn_South) in Manhattan, and the [Desire Projects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desire_Projects) in [New Orleans](53842.md), fell victim to the "[ghettofication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghetto)" and are now being torn down, renovated, or replaced. Another example is the former [Pruitt–Igoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pruitt%E2%80%93Igoe) complex in [St. Louis](27687.md), torn down in the 1970s.

In contrast to their [public housing](177912.md) counterparts, commercially developed high-rise apartment buildings continue to flourish in cities around the country largely due to high land prices and the housing boom of the 2000s. The [Upper East Side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_East_Side) in New York City, featuring high-rise apartments, is the wealthiest urban neighborhood in the United States.

Currently, the tallest residential building in the world is [Central Park Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Park_Tower) located in [Midtown Manhattan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midtown_Manhattan), having a height of 1,550 feet (470 m) with the highest occupied floor at 1,417 feet (432 m).[^cite_note-65]

### Oceania

See also: [List of tallest buildings in Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_Australia) and [List of tallest buildings in Sydney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_Sydney)



Housing commission towers in Waterloo, Sydney, Australia

High-rise living in Australia was limited to the [Sydney CBD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_CBD) until the 1960s, when a short-lived fashion saw public housing tenants located in new high-rise developments, especially in Sydney and Melbourne. The buildings pictured along with four other 16-story blocks were constructed on behalf of the [Royal Australian Navy](26327.md) and were available to sailors and their families for accommodation. Due to social problems within these blocks the Navy left and the [Department of Housing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Housing) took charge and flats were let to low income and immigrant families. During the 1980s many people escaping communism in Eastern bloc countries were housed in these buildings. Developers have enthusiastically adopted the term "apartment" for these new high-rise blocks, perhaps to avoid the stigma still attached to housing commission flats.

## Deck access

Deck access is a type of flat that is accessed from a walkway that is open to the elements, as opposed to flats that are accessed from fully enclosed internal corridors. Deck-access blocks of flats are usually fairly low-rise structures. The decks can vary from simple walkways, which may be covered or uncovered, to decks wide enough for small vehicles. The best-known example of deck-access flats in the UK is [Park Hill, Sheffield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park_Hill,_Sheffield), where the decks are wide enough to allow electric vehicles; however, the design is inspired by French Modernist architect [Le Corbusier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Corbusier), particularly his [Unité d'habitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit%C3%A9_d'habitation) in [Marseille](40888948.md).[^cite_note-66]

An apartment building with a [pergola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pergola) and [solar panels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_panel) in [the Bronx](3338.md), New York City
