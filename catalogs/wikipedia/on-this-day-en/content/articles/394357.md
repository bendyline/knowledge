The terms **international waters** or **transboundary waters** apply where any of the following types of bodies of water (or their [drainage basins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin)) transcend international boundaries: oceans, [large marine ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_marine_ecosystem), enclosed or semi-enclosed regional seas and [estuaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estuaries), rivers, lakes, [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater) systems ([aquifers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquifer)), and [wetlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wetland).[^cite_note-1]

"International waters" is not a defined term in international law. It is an informal term, which sometimes refers to waters beyond the "territorial sea" of any country.[^cite_note-buchanan-2] In other words, "international waters" is sometimes used as an informal synonym for the more formal term "**high seas**", which under the doctrine of *mare liberum* ([Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) for 'freedom of the seas'), do not belong to any state's jurisdiction. As such, states have the right to fishing, navigation, overflight, laying cables and pipelines, and scientific research.

The [Convention on the High Seas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_on_the_High_Seas), signed in 1958, which has 63 signatories, defined "high seas" to mean "all parts of the sea that are not included in the [territorial sea](496983.md#Territorial_sea) or in the [internal waters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_waters) of a State" and where "no State may validly purport to subject any part of them to its sovereignty."[^cite_note-3] The Convention on the High Seas was used as a foundation for the [United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea](31940.md) (UNCLOS), signed in 1982, which recognized [exclusive economic zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclusive_economic_zone) extending 200 nautical miles (230 mi; 370 km) from the [baseline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseline_\(sea\)), where coastal states have sovereign rights to the water column and sea floor as well as the natural resources found there.[^cite_note-4]

The high seas make up 50% of the surface area of the planet and cover over two-thirds of the ocean.[^cite_note-5]

Ships sailing the high seas are generally under the jurisdiction of the [flag state](6162348.md) (if there is one);[^cite_note-6] however, when a ship is involved in certain criminal acts, such as [piracy](50715.md),[^cite_note-7] any nation can exercise jurisdiction under the doctrine of [universal jurisdiction](48640.md). International waters can be contrasted with [internal waters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_waters), [territorial waters](496983.md) and [exclusive economic zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclusive_economic_zones).

UNCLOS also contains, in its part XII, special provisions for the protection of the marine environment, which, in certain cases, allow [port States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_State) to exercise extraterritorial jurisdiction over foreign ships on the high seas if they violate international environmental rules (adopted by the [IMO](14986.md)), such as the [MARPOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MARPOL_73/78) Convention.[^cite_note-8]

## Underground transboundary waters

When an underground water crosses international boundaries, the term transboundary aquifer applies. The term transboundariness can then be applied, which is a concept, measure and approach first introduced in 2017 [^cite_note-9] when talking about underground transboundary waters.&#x20;

The importance of this approach is that the physical properties of aquifers become merely additional variables within the broad spectrum of the transboundary nature of an aquifer: social (population); economic (groundwater efficiency); political (cross-border); existing research or data; water quality and quantity; other issues that drive the agenda (security, trade, immigration, etc.).

The criteria proposed through this approach attempt to encompass and quantify all potential variables that play a role in defining the transboundary nature and multidimensional boundaries of an underground transboundary water.

## International waterways



The [Río de la Plata basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%ADo_de_la_Plata_Basin) gives sea access to landlocked [Paraguay](23420.md) and [Bolivia](3462.md), and navigation is free for all international commercial ships.



[Komárno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kom%C3%A1rno), in Slovakia, is an [inland port](1341936.md) on the [Danube River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danube_River), which is an important international [waterway](174851.md).

Several international treaties have established [freedom of navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_the_seas) on semi-enclosed seas.

- The [Copenhagen Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_Dues#Copenhagen_Convention) of 1857 opened access to the [Baltic Sea](3335.md) by abolishing the [Sound Dues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_Dues) and making the [Danish Straits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_Straits) an international waterway free to all commercial shipping. Separately, the Royal Ordinance of 1999 regulates the access of foreign warships to Danish waters.

  [^cite_note-10]

  [^cite_note-11]

  [^cite_note-12]

- Several conventions have opened the [Bosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosphorus) and [Dardanelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dardanelles) to shipping. The latest, the [Montreux Convention Regarding the Regime of the Turkish Straits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreux_Convention_Regarding_the_Regime_of_the_Turkish_Straits), maintains the straits' status as an international waterway.

Other international treaties have opened up rivers, which are not traditionally international waterways.

- The [Río de la Plata basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%ADo_de_la_Plata_Basin), including the rivers [Paraná](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paran%C3%A1_River), [Uruguay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruguay_River) and [Paraguay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraguay_River), is legally open for all international commercial ships without restriction, it notably gives sea access to landlocked [Paraguay](23420.md) and [Bolivia](3462.md).

- The [Danube River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danube_River) is an international waterway so that [Germany](11867.md) and [Croatia](5573.md), as well as landlocked [Austria](26964606.md), [Slovakia](26830.md), [Hungary](13275.md), [Serbia](29265.md) and [Moldova](19260.md) can have secure access to the [Black Sea](3386.md).

## Disputes over international waters

See also: [Territorial claims in the Arctic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_claims_in_the_Arctic), [South China Sea dispute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_China_Sea_dispute), and [Australian Antarctic Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Antarctic_Territory)



The Atlantic Ocean has the busiest ocean trade routes in the world.

Current unresolved disputes over whether particular waters are "International waters" include:

- [Arctic Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_Ocean): While Canada, Denmark, Russia and Norway all regard parts of the Arctic seas as *national waters* or *[internal waters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_waters)*, most [European Union](9317.md) countries and the United States officially regard the whole region as international waters. The [Northwest Passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Passage#International_waters_dispute) through the [Arctic Archipelago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_Archipelago) is one of the more prominent examples, with Canada claiming it as [internal waters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_waters), while the United States and the European Union considers it an [international strait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_strait).

  [^cite_note-13]

- [Southern Ocean](20611428.md): Australian claim to an [exclusive economic zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclusive_economic_zone) (EEZ) in Antarctica is challenged by Japan.

- [Okinotorishima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okinotorishima#EEZ_dispute): Japanese claim to an EEZ around Okinotorishima is challenged by China, South Korea, and Taiwan.

- [South China Sea](74209.md): Disputes exist between Brunei, China, Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Taiwan, and Vietnam.
