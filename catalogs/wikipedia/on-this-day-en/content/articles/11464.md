# Frigate

This article is about the warship. For the bird, see [Frigatebird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frigatebird). For other uses, see [Frigate (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frigate_\(disambiguation\)).



The [Italian Navy](1126939.md) frigate [*Carlo Bergamini*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_frigate_Carlo_Bergamini_\(F_590\)) in 2011



The [French Imperial Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Imperial_Navy) frigate [*Pénélope*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_frigate_P%C3%A9n%C3%A9lope_\(1806\))

A **frigate** ([/ˈfrɪɡɪt/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English)) is a type of [warship](263960.md).&#x20;

In different eras, the roles and capabilities of ships classified as frigates have varied. In the 17th to early 18th centuries the term "frigate" was loosely given to any [full-rigged ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full-rigged_ship) built for speed and manoeuvrability and intended for scouting, escort or patrol. By the second quarter of the 18th century, what is regarded as the "true frigate" was developed in France and subsequently copied by other navies. This type of vessel was characterised by a long sleek design with a single [gun deck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gun_deck) of 28 and 36 cannons, and an unarmed deck below used for berthing the crew.&#x20;

In the mid-nineteenth century the definition of *frigate* was expanded to include the early [ironclad warships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironclad_warship), which also had a single gun deck. However, later developments in ironclad ships rendered the designation obsolete and the term fell out of favour in navies worldwide.

The term was reintroduced in the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War) to describe a seagoing [escort ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_escort) of intermediate size between a [corvette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corvette) and a [destroyer](8779.md). After the war, this definition has gradually expanded to include many different kinds of vessels, from large ocean-going [anti-submarine warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-submarine_warfare) ships to corvettes, destroyers, and nuclear-powered guided-missile [cruisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruiser).[^cite_note-1]

## Etymology

The etymology of the word (Italian: *fregata*; Dutch: *fregat*; Spanish/Catalan/Portuguese/Sicilian: *fragata*; French: *frégate*; German: *Fregatte*) remains uncertain, although it may have originated as a [corruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corruption_\(linguistic\)) of *[aphractus](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/aphractus)*, a [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) word for an open vessel with no lower deck. *Aphractus*, in turn, derived from the [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) phrase ἄφρακτος ναῦς (*aphraktos naus*) – "undefended ship".[^cite_note-2]

## Age of sail

### Origins

The term *frigate* originated in the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean) in the late 16th century, referring to a lighter [galley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galley)-type warship with oars, sails and a light armament, built for speed and manoeuvrability.[^cite_note-Henderson-3] The first recorded usage of the term seems to date from 1562, when [Alonso Pérez, Duke of Medina Sidonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alonso_de_Guzm%C3%A1n_y_Sotomayor,_7th_Duke_of_Medina_Sidonia) armed a galley fleet complemented with vessels called *fragatas* which were claimed to be invented by him. These frigates proved impractical and were retired soon.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERodríguez_González2017316-317-4] Other sources state Medina Sidonia actually credited [Álvaro de Bazán, Marquis of Santa Cruz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%81lvaro_de_Baz%C3%A1n,_Marquis_of_Santa_Cruz) with their invention.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAltolaguirre_y_Duvale1888147-148-5] The term was later used as an alternate name for the *galizabra*, a kind of [galleass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galleass) built by Álvaro's brother [Alonso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alonso_de_Baz%C3%A1n) in 1584 as a cross between a galley and a [zabra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zabra).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERodríguez_González2017322-6]



Light frigate, circa 1675–1680

In 1583, during the [Eighty Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eighty_Years'_War) of 1568–1648, [Habsburg Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburg_Spain) recovered the [southern Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Netherlands) from the Protestant rebels. This soon resulted in the use of the occupied ports as bases for [privateers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privateers), the "[Dunkirkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunkirker)", to attack the shipping of the Dutch and their allies. To achieve this the Dunkirkers developed small, manoeuvrable, sailing vessels that came to be referred to as frigates. The success of these Dunkirker vessels influenced the ship design of other navies contending with them, but because most regular navies required ships of greater endurance than the Dunkirker frigates could provide, the term soon came to apply less exclusively to any relatively fast and elegant sail-only warship. In French, the term *frigate* gave rise to a verb – *frégater*, meaning 'to build long and low', and to an adjective, adding more confusion. Even the huge English [*Sovereign of the Seas*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_ship_Sovereign_of_the_Seas) could be described as "a delicate frigate" by a contemporary after her upper decks were reduced in 1651.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERodger2004216-7]

The navy of the [Dutch Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Republic) became the first navy to build the larger ocean-going frigates. The Dutch navy had three principal tasks in the struggle against Spain: to protect Dutch merchant ships at sea, to blockade the ports of Spanish-held [Flanders](10878.md) to damage trade and halt enemy [privateering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privateering), and to fight the Spanish fleet and prevent troop landings. The first two tasks required speed, shallowness of draft for the shallow waters around the Netherlands, and the ability to carry sufficient supplies to maintain a blockade. The third task required heavy armament, sufficient to stand up to the Spanish fleet. The first of the larger battle-capable frigates were built around 1600 at [Hoorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoorn) in [Holland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_Holland).[^cite_note-8] By the later stages of the [Eighty Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eighty_Years'_War) (1568–1648) the Dutch had switched entirely from the heavier ships still used by the English and Spanish to the lighter frigates, carrying around 40 guns and weighing around 300 tons. In the 17th century, the term *fregat* in the [Dutch Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Navy) described any oceangoing warship carrying fewer than 40 guns.[^cite_note-allan2023-9] The effectiveness of the Dutch frigates became most evident in the [Battle of the Downs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Downs) in 1639, encouraging most other navies, especially the English, to adopt similar designs.[^cite_note-10]

The fleets built by the [Commonwealth of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_England) in the 1650s generally consisted of ships described as "frigates", the largest of which were [two-decker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-decker) "great frigates" of the [third rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third-rate). Carrying 60 guns, these vessels were as big and capable as "great ships" of the time; however, most other frigates at the time were used as "[cruisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruiser)": independent fast ships. The term "frigate" implied a long [hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hull_\(watercraft\))-design, which relates directly to speed (see [hull speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hull_speed)) and which also, in turn, helped the development of the [broadside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadside_\(naval\)) tactic in naval warfare. In the 17th century the term 'frigate' was sometimes applied very loosely and could be used for any warship. The more precise description of the time was the rating of the ship, frigates generally were ships of the fourth to sixth rates (in 1675, fourth rate up to 60 guns, fifth up to 40, sixth up to 20)[^cite_note-11]



[*Boudeuse*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_frigate_Boudeuse), of [Louis Antoine de Bougainville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Antoine_de_Bougainville)

At this time, a further design evolved, reintroducing oars and resulting in galley frigates such as [HMS *Charles Galley*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Charles_Galley) of 1676, which was rated as a 32-gun [fifth-rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth-rate) but also had a bank of 40 oars set below the upper deck that could propel the ship in the absence of a favourable wind. In Danish, the word "fregat" often applied to warships carrying as few as 16 guns, such as [HMS *Falcon*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Falcon_\(1801\)), which the British classified as a sloop. Under the [rating system of the Royal Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rating_system_of_the_Royal_Navy), by the middle of the 18th century, the term "frigate" was technically restricted to single-decked ships of the [fifth rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth-rate), though small 28-gun frigates were classed as [sixth rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixth-rate).[^cite_note-Henderson-3]

### Classic design



A [*Magicienne*-class frigate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magicienne-class_frigate)



Gun deck of the [*Pallas*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pallas-class_frigate_\(1808\)) frigate [*Méduse*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_frigate_M%C3%A9duse_\(1810\))

The classic sailing frigate, or "true frigate", well-known today for its role in the [Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_wars), can be traced back to French developments in the second quarter of the 18th century. The French-built [*Médée*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_frigate_M%C3%A9d%C3%A9e_\(1741\)) of 1740 is often regarded as the first example of this type. These ships were [square-rigged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_rig) and carried all their main guns on a single continuous upper deck. The lower deck, known as the "gun deck", now carried no armament, and functioned as a "berth deck" where the crew lived, and was in fact placed below the [waterline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterline) of the new frigates. The typical earlier cruiser had a partially armed lower deck, from which it was known as a "half-battery" or *demi-batterie* ship. Removing the guns from this deck allowed the height of the hull upperworks to be lowered, giving the resulting "true-frigate" much improved sailing qualities. The unarmed deck meant that the frigate's guns were carried comparatively high above the waterline; as a result, when seas were too rough for two-deckers to open their lower deck [gunports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gun_port), frigates were still able to fight with all their guns (see the [action of 13 January 1797](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_of_13_January_1797), for an example when this was decisive).[^cite_note-Breen-12][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGardiner199236–37-13]

The [Royal Navy](26061.md) captured a number of the new French frigates, including *Médée*, during the [War of the Austrian Succession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Austrian_Succession) (1740–1748) and the British were impressed by them, particularly for their inshore handling capabilities. They soon built copies (ordered in 1747), based on a French privateer named *Tygre*, and started to adapt the type to their own needs, setting the standard for other frigates as the leading naval power. The first British frigates carried 28 guns including an upper deck battery of twenty-four 9-pounder guns (the remaining four smaller guns were carried on the [quarterdeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarterdeck)) but soon developed into fifth-rate ships of 32 or 36 guns including an upper deck battery of twenty-six 12-pounder guns, with the remaining six or ten smaller guns carried on the quarterdeck and forecastle.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGardiner199237-14] Technically, "unrated ships" with fewer than 28 guns could not be classed as frigates but as "[post ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post_ship)"; however, in common parlance most post ships were sometimes described as "frigates", the same casual misuse of the term being extended to smaller two-decked ships that were too small to stand in the line of battle. However, most ships of this size were called "ship sloops".[^cite_note-15] A total of fifty-nine French sailing frigates were built between 1777 and 1790, with a standard design averaging a hull length of 135 ft (41 m) and an average draught of 13 ft (4.0 m). The new frigates recorded sailing speeds of up to 14 knots (26 km/h; 16 mph), significantly faster than their predecessor vessels.[^cite_note-Breen-12]

### Heavy frigate



[HMS *Trincomalee*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Trincomalee) (1817) a restored British 18-pounder, 38-gun heavy frigate

In 1778, the British Admiralty introduced a larger "heavy" frigate, with a main battery of twenty-six or twenty-eight 18-pounder guns (with smaller guns carried on the quarterdeck and forecastle). This move may reflect the naval conditions at the time; with both France and Spain as enemies, the usual British preponderance in ship numbers was no longer the case and there was pressure on the British to produce cruisers of individually greater force. In reply, the first French 18-pounder frigates were laid down in 1781. The 18-pounder frigate eventually became the standard frigate of the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars. The British produced larger, 38-gun, and slightly smaller, 36-gun, versions and also a 32-gun design that can be considered an 'economy version'. The 32-gun frigates also had the advantage that they could be built by the many smaller, less-specialised shipbuilders.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGardiner199239-16][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGardiner200019-17]

Frigates could (and usually did) additionally carry smaller carriage-mounted guns on their quarterdecks and [forecastles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forecastle) (the superstructures above the upper deck). In 1778 the [Carron Iron Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carron_Company) of Scotland produced a naval gun which would revolutionise the armament of smaller naval vessels, including the frigate. The [carronade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carronade) was a large calibre, short-barrelled naval cannon which was light, quick to reload and needed a smaller crew than a conventional long gun. Due to its lightness it could be mounted on the forecastle and quarterdeck of frigates. It greatly increased the firepower, measured in weight of metal (the combined weight of all projectiles fired in one broadside), of these vessels. The disadvantages of the carronade were that it had a much shorter range and was less accurate than a long gun. The British quickly saw the advantages of the new weapon and soon employed it on a wide scale. The US Navy also copied the design soon after its appearance. The French and other nations eventually adopted variations of the weapon in succeeding decades. The typical heavy frigate had a main armament of 18-pounder long guns, plus 32-pounder carronades mounted on its upper decks.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGardiner1992153-18]

### Super-heavy frigates



[USS *Constitution*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Constitution)

The first "super-heavy frigates", armed with 24-pounder long guns, were built by the naval architect [F H Chapman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fredrik_Henrik_af_Chapman) for the Swedish navy in 1782. Because of a shortage of ships-of-the-line, the Swedes wanted these frigates, the *Bellona* class, to be able to stand in the battle line in an emergency. In the 1790s the French built a small number of large 24-pounder frigates, such as [*Forte*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_frigate_Forte_\(1794\)) and [*Egyptienne*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_frigate_%C3%89gyptienne), they also cut-down (reduced the height of the hull to give only one continuous gun deck) a number of older ships-of-the-line (including [*Diadème*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_ship_Diad%C3%A8me_\(1756\))) to produce super-heavy frigates; the resulting ship was known as a [*rasée*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Razee). It is not known whether the French were seeking to produce very potent cruisers or merely to address stability problems in old ships. The British, alarmed by the prospect of these powerful heavy frigates, responded by rasée-ing three of their smaller 64-gun battleships, including [*Indefatigable*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Indefatigable_\(1784\)), which went on to have a very successful career as a frigate. At this time the British also built a few 24-pounder-armed large frigates, the most successful of which was [HMS *Endymion*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Endymion_\(1797\)) (1,277 tons).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGardiner200040–42-19][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGardiner199240-20]

In 1797, three of the [United States Navy](20518076.md)'s [first six major ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Original_six_frigates_of_the_United_States_Navy) were rated as 44-gun frigates, which operationally carried fifty-six to sixty [24-pounder long guns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/24-pounder_long_gun) and 32-pounder or 42-pounder carronades on two decks; they were exceptionally powerful. These ships were so large, at around 1,500 tons, and well-armed that they were often regarded as equal to ships of the line, and after a series of losses at the outbreak of the [War of 1812](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_1812), secret [Admiralty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiralty_\(United_Kingdom\)) instructions ordered British frigates (usually rated at 38 guns or less) to never engage the large American frigates at any less than a 2\:1 advantage. [USS *Constitution*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Constitution), preserved as a [museum ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_ship) by the US Navy, is the oldest commissioned warship afloat, and is a surviving example of a frigate from the [Age of Sail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Sail). *Constitution* and her sister ships [*President*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_President_\(1800\)) and [*United States*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_United_States_\(1797\)) were created in a response to deal with the Barbary Coast pirates and in conjunction with the [Naval Act of 1794](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Act_of_1794). [Joshua Humphreys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua_Humphreys) proposed that only [live oak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_oak), a tree that grew only in America, should be used to build these ships.[^cite_note-Archibald,_Roger_1997-21]

The Admiralty, concerned by repeated defeats in single-ship actions, responded to the success of the American 44s in three ways. They built a class of conventional 40-gun, 24-pounder armed frigates on the lines of *Endymion*. They cut down three old 74-gun Ships-of-the-Line into *rasées*, producing frigates with a 32-pounder main armament, supplemented by 42-pounder carronades. These had an armament that far exceeded the power of the American ships. Finally, [*Leander*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Leander_\(1813\)) and [*Newcastle*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Newcastle_\(1813\)), 1,500-ton spar-decked frigates (with an enclosed waist, giving a continuous line of guns from bow to stern at the level of the quarterdeck/forecastle), were built, which were an almost exact match in size and firepower to the American 44-gun frigates.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGardiner200048–56-22]

### Role



[HMS *Warrior*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Warrior_\(1860\)), the first iron-hulled armoured steam frigate – the hull survived as an oil terminal dock and was restored to its original appearance in the late 20th century

Frigates were perhaps the hardest-worked of warship types during the [Age of Sail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Sail). While smaller than a [ship-of-the-line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_of_the_line), they were formidable opponents for the large numbers of [sloops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloop-of-war) and [gunboats](274471.md), not to mention [privateers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privateer) or merchantmen. Able to carry six months' stores, they had very long range; and vessels larger than frigates were considered too valuable to operate independently.

Frigates scouted for the fleet, went on commerce-raiding missions and patrols, and conveyed messages and dignitaries. Usually, frigates would fight in small numbers or singly against other frigates. They would avoid contact with ships-of-the-line; even in the midst of a fleet engagement it was bad etiquette for a ship of the line to fire on an enemy frigate which had not fired first.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELavery198949,_298–300-23] Frigates were involved in fleet battles, often as "repeating frigates". In the smoke and confusion of battle, [signals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_flag_signalling) made by the fleet commander, whose [flagship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flagship) might be in the thick of the fighting, might be missed by the other ships of the fleet.[^cite_note-EB-24] Frigates were therefore stationed to windward or leeward of the main [line of battle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_of_battle), and had to maintain a clear line of sight to the commander's flagship. Signals from the flagship were then repeated by the frigates, which themselves standing out of the line and clear from the smoke and disorder of battle, could be more easily seen by the other ships of the fleet.[^cite_note-EB-24] If damage or loss of masts prevented the flagship from making clear conventional signals, the repeating frigates could interpret them and hoist their own in the correct manner, passing on the commander's instructions clearly.[^cite_note-EB-24] For officers in the Royal Navy, a frigate was a desirable posting. Frigates often saw action, which meant a greater chance of glory, promotion, and [prize money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prize_money).

Unlike larger ships that were placed [in ordinary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_Fleet), frigates were kept in service in peacetime as a cost-saving measure and to provide experience to frigate captains and officers which would be useful in wartime. Frigates could also carry [marines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_\(military\)) for boarding enemy ships or for operations on shore; in 1832, the frigate [USS *Potomac*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Potomac_\(1822\)) landed a party of 282 sailors and Marines ashore in the US Navy's [first Sumatran expedition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Sumatran_expedition). Frigates remained a crucial element of navies until the mid-19th century. The first [ironclads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironclad_warship) were classified as "frigates" because of the number of guns they carried. However, terminology changed as iron and steam became the norm, and the role of the frigate was assumed first by the [protected cruiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protected_cruiser) and then by the [light cruiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_cruiser).

Frigates are often the vessel of choice in historical naval novels due to their relative freedom compared to ships-of-the-line (kept for fleet actions) and smaller vessels (generally assigned to a home port and less widely ranging). For example, the [Patrick O'Brian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_O'Brian) [Aubrey–Maturin series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aubrey%E2%80%93Maturin_series), [C. S. Forester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._S._Forester)'s [Horatio Hornblower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horatio_Hornblower) series and [Alexander Kent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Reeman)'s [Richard Bolitho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Bolitho) series. The 2003 film *[Master and Commander: The Far Side of the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_and_Commander:_The_Far_Side_of_the_World)* features a reconstructed historic frigate, [HMS *Rose*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Surprise_\(replica_ship\)), to depict Aubrey's frigate HMS *Surprise*.

## Age of steam

Main article: [Steam frigate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_frigate)



French [paddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paddle_steamer) frigate [*Descartes*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_frigate_Descartes)

Vessels classed as frigates continued to play a great role in navies with the adoption of steam power in the 19th century. In the 1830s, navies experimented with large [paddle steamers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paddle_steamer) equipped with large guns mounted on one deck, which were termed "paddle frigates".[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26]

From the mid-1840s on, frigates which more closely resembled the traditional sailing frigate were built with steam engines and screw [propellers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propeller). These "[screw frigates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_frigate#Screw_frigates)", built first of wood and later of [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrought_iron), continued to perform the traditional role of the frigate until late in the 19th century.

### Armoured frigate

From 1859, armour was added to ships based on existing frigate and [ship of the line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_of_the_line) designs. The additional weight of the armour on these first [ironclad warships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironclad_warship) meant that they could have only one gun deck, and they were technically frigates, even though they were more powerful than existing ships-of-the-line and occupied the same strategic role. The phrase "armoured frigate" remained in use for some time to denote a sail-equipped, broadside-firing type of ironclad. The first such ship was the revolutionary Marine Nationale wooden-hulled [*Gloire*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_ironclad_Gloire), protected by 12 cm-thick (4.7 in) armour plates. The British response was [HMS *Warrior*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Warrior_\(1860\)) of the Warrior-class ironclads, launched in 1860. With her iron hull, steam engines propelling the 9,137 ton vessel to speeds of up to 14 knots and rifled breechloading 110-pdr guns, *Warrior* is the ancestor of all modern warships.

During the 1880s, as warship design shifted from iron to steel and cruising warships without sails started to appear, the term "frigate" fell out of use. Vessels with armoured sides were designated as "[battleships](4054.md)" or "[armoured cruisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armored_cruiser)", while "[protected cruisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protected_cruiser)" only possessed an armoured deck, and unarmoured vessels, including frigates and sloops, were classified as "[unprotected cruisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unprotected_cruiser)".

## Modern era

### World War II

See also: [List of frigates of World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_frigates_of_World_War_II)



A [Loch-class frigate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loch-class_frigate)



The U.S. Navy [*Tacoma*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacoma-class_frigate) patrol frigate [USS *Gallup*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Gallup_\(PF-47\)) at [San Pedro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Pedro,_California), [California](5407.md), on 30 May 1944

Modern frigates are related to earlier frigates only by name. The term "frigate" was readopted during the [Second World War](32927.md) by the British [Royal Navy](26061.md) to describe an [anti-submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-submarine_warfare) escort vessel that was larger than a [corvette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corvette#World_War_II) (based on a mercantile design), while smaller than a [destroyer](8779.md). The vessels were originally to be termed "twin screw corvettes" until the Royal Canadian Navy suggested to the British re-introducing the term "frigate" for the significantly enlarged vessels. Frigates, termed [destroyer escorts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destroyer_escort) by the US at the time, are less expensive to build and maintain than larger destroyers and [cruisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruiser).[^cite_note-27] Small anti-submarine escorts specifically designed for naval use had previously been classified as [sloops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloop-of-war) by the Royal Navy, and the [*Black Swan*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Swan-class_sloop) sloops of 1939–1945 (propelled by steam turbines as opposed to cheaper triple-expansion steam engines) were as large as the new types of frigate, and more heavily armed. 22 of these were reclassified as frigates after the war, as were the remaining 24 smaller [Castle-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle-class_corvette) corvettes.

The frigate was introduced to remedy some of the shortcomings inherent in the [Flower-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower-class_corvette) corvette design: limited armament, a hull form not suited to open-ocean work, a single [shaft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Driveshaft) which limited speed and manoeuvrability, and a lack of range. The frigate was designed and built to the same mercantile construction standards ([scantlings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scantling)) as the corvette, allowing manufacture by yards unused to warship construction. The first frigates of the [River class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River-class_frigate) (1941) were essentially two sets of corvette machinery in one larger hull, armed with the latest [Hedgehog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedgehog_\(weapon\)) anti-submarine weapon.

The frigate possessed less offensive firepower and speed than a destroyer, including an escort destroyer, but such qualities were not required for anti-submarine warfare. Submarines were slow while submerged, and [ASDIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASDIC) sets did not operate effectively at speeds of over 20 [knots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_\(unit\)) (23 [mph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miles_per_hour); 37 [km/h](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilometres_per_hour)). Rather, the frigate was an austere and weatherly vessel suitable for mass-construction and fitted with the latest innovations in anti-submarine warfare. As the frigate was intended purely for convoy duties, and not to deploy with the fleet, it had limited range and speed.

It was not until the Royal Navy's [Bay class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay-class_frigate) of 1944 that a British design classified as a "frigate" was produced for fleet use, although it still suffered from limited speed. These [anti-aircraft](146640.md) frigates, built on incomplete [Loch-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loch-class_frigate) frigate hulls, were similar to the [United States Navy](20518076.md)'s destroyer escorts (DE), although the latter had greater speed and offensive armament to better suit them to fleet deployments. The destroyer escort concept came from design studies by the [General Board of the United States Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Board_of_the_United_States_Navy) in 1940, as modified by requirements established by a British commission in 1941[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGardiner1980149-28] prior to the American entry into the war, for deep-water escorts. The American-built destroyer escorts serving in the Royal Navy were rated as [Captain-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captain-class_frigate) frigates. The U.S. Navy's two Canadian-built [*Asheville*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asheville-class_frigate) and 96 British-influenced, American-built [*Tacoma*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacoma-class_frigate) frigates that followed originally were classified as "patrol [gunboats](274471.md)" (PG) in the U.S. Navy but on 15 April 1943 were all reclassified as **patrol frigates (PF)**.

[BRP *Miguel Malvar*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BRP_Miguel_Malvar_\(FFG-06\)) (FFG-06), the lead ship of [her class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_Malvar-class_frigate) of guided-missile frigates of the [Philippine Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine_Navy) during sea trials off the coast of Ulsan, South Korea

The introduction of the [surface-to-air missile](182664.md) after World War II made relatively small ships effective for anti-aircraft warfare: the "guided-missile frigate". In the US navy, these vessels were called "[ocean escorts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_escort)" and [designated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hull_classification_symbol) "DE" or "DEG" until 1975 – a holdover from the World War II destroyer escort or "DE". While the Royal Navy used similar designations for their warships built in the 1950s, they maintained the use of the term "frigate"; in the 1990s the RCN re-introduced the frigate designation. Likewise, the French Navy refers to missile-equipped ships, up to cruiser-sized ships ([*Suffren*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffren-class_frigate), [*Tourville*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourville-class_frigate), and [Horizon classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon-class_frigate)), by the name of "frégate", while smaller units are named *[aviso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviso)*. The Soviet Navy used the term "guard-ship" (*сторожевой корабль*).

From the 1950s to the 1970s, the United States Navy commissioned ships classed as guided-missile frigates ([hull classification symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hull_classification_symbol) DLG or DLGN, literally meaning guided-missile [destroyer leaders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destroyer_leader)), which were actually anti-aircraft warfare cruisers built on destroyer-style hulls. These had one or two twin launchers per ship for the [RIM-2 Terrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RIM-2_Terrier) missile, upgraded to the [RIM-67 Standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RIM-67_Standard) ER missile in the 1980s. This type of ship was intended primarily to defend aircraft carriers against anti-ship [cruise missiles](6590.md), augmenting and eventually replacing converted World War II cruisers (CAG/CLG/CG) in this role. The guided-missile frigates also had an anti-submarine capability that most of the World War II cruiser conversions lacked. Some of these ships – [*Bainbridge*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Bainbridge_\(CGN-25\)) and [*Truxtun*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Truxtun_\(CGN-35\)) along with the [*California*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California-class_cruiser) and [*Virginia* classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia-class_cruiser) – were [nuclear-powered (DLGN)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_naval_reactors).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBauerRoberts1991215–217-29] These "frigates" were roughly mid-way in size between cruisers and destroyers. This was similar to the use of the term "frigate" during the age of sail during which it referred to a medium-sized warship, but it was inconsistent with conventions used by other contemporary navies which regarded frigates as being smaller than destroyers. During the [1975 ship reclassification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Navy_1975_ship_reclassification), the large American frigates were redesignated as guided-missile cruisers or destroyers (CG/CGN/DDG), while ocean escorts (the American classification for ships smaller than destroyers, with hull symbol DE/DEG (destroyer escort)) such as the *[Knox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knox-class_frigate)*-class were reclassified as frigates (FF/FFG), sometimes called "fast frigates". In the late 1970s, as a gradual successor to the *Knox* frigates, the US Navy introduced the 51-ship [*Oliver Hazard Perry*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Hazard_Perry-class_frigate) guided-missile frigates (FFG), the last of which was decommissioned in 2015, although some serve in other navies.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBauerRoberts1991251–252-30] By 1995 the older guided-missile cruisers and destroyers were replaced by the [*Ticonderoga*-class cruisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ticonderoga-class_cruiser) and [*Arleigh Burke*-class destroyers](197341.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGardinerChumbley1995580–585-31]

One of the most successful post-1945 designs was the British [*Leander*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leander-class_frigate) frigate, which was used by several navies. Laid down in 1959, the *Leander* class was based on the previous [Type 12 anti-submarine frigate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitby-class_frigate) but equipped for anti-aircraft use as well. They were used by the UK into the 1990s, at which point some were sold on to other navies. The *Leander* design, or improved versions of it, were licence-built for other navies as well. Nearly all modern frigates are equipped with some form of offensive or defensive missiles, and as such are rated as guided-missile frigates (FFG). Improvements in surface-to-air missiles (e.g., the [Eurosam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurosam) [Aster 15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MBDA_Aster)) allow modern guided-missile frigates to form the core of many modern navies and to be used as a fleet defence platform, without the need for specialised anti-air warfare frigates.



[*Nilgiri*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilgiri-class_frigate_\(2019\)) class frigates of the [Indian Navy](604522.md)

Modern destroyers and frigates have sufficient endurance and seaworthiness for long voyages and so are considered [blue water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue-water_navy) vessels, while [corvettes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corvette) (even the largest ones capable of carrying an [anti-submarine warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-submarine_warfare) helicopter) are typically deployed in coastal or [littoral zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Littoral_zone), so are regarded as [green-water vessels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green-water_navy).[^cite_note-32] According to Sidharth Kaushal of the Royal United Services Institute for Defence and Security Studies, describing the difference between 21st century destroyers and frigates, the larger "destroyers can more easily carry and generate the power for more powerful high-resolution radar and a larger number of vertical launch cells. They can thus provide theatre wide air and missile defence for forces such as a carrier battle group and typically serve this function". By contrast the smaller "frigates are thus usually used as escort vessels to protect sea lines of communication or as an auxiliary component of a strike group". The largest and most powerful destroyers, such as the [*Ticonderoga*-class cruisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ticonderoga-class_cruiser), are often classified as cruisers due to their extra armament and facilities to serve as fleet flagships.[^cite_note-33]

#### Other uses

The Royal Navy [Type 61 (*Salisbury* class)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salisbury-class_frigate) were ["air direction"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_picket) frigates equipped to track aircraft. To this end they had less armament than the [Type 41 (*Leopard*-class)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopard-class_frigate) air-defence frigates built on the same hull. Multi-role frigates such as the [MEKO 200](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MEKO_200), [*Anzac*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anzac-class_frigate) and [*Halifax* classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halifax-class_frigate) are designed for navies needing warships deployed in a variety of situations that a general frigate class would not be able to fulfil, and not requiring destroyers.

#### Anti-submarine role

See also: [anti-submarine warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-submarine_warfare)



[HMS *Somerset*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Somerset_\(F82\)) of the [Royal Navy](26061.md). [Type 23 frigates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_23_frigate) were built for [anti-submarine warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-submarine_warfare) but are capable multi-purpose ships.

[^cite_note-34]

At the opposite end of the spectrum, some frigates are specialised for [anti-submarine warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-submarine_warfare). Increasing submarine speeds towards the end of World War II (see [German Type XXI submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Type_XXI_submarine)) greatly reduced the margin of speed superiority of frigate over submarine. The frigate could no longer be slow and powered by mercantile machinery, and consequently postwar frigates such as the [*Whitby* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitby-class_frigate) were faster.

Such ships carry improved [sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar) equipment, such as the [variable depth sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_depth_sonar) or [towed array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Towed_array_sonar), and specialised weapons such as [torpedoes](31424.md), forward-throwing weapons such as [Limbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limbo_\(weapon\)) and missile-carried anti-submarine torpedoes such as [ASROC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASROC) or [Ikara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikara_\(missile\)). The Royal Navy's original [Type 22 frigate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_22_frigate) is an example of a specialised anti-submarine warfare frigate, though it also has [Sea Wolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_Wolf_\(missile\)) surface-to-air missiles for point defence plus [Exocet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exocet) surface-to-surface missiles for limited offensive capability.

Especially for anti-submarine warfare, most modern frigates have a [landing deck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helipad) and [hangar](1011776.md) aft to operate [helicopters](8286923.md), eliminating the need for the frigate to close with unknown sub-surface threats, and using fast helicopters to attack nuclear submarines which may be faster than surface warships. For this task the helicopter is equipped with sensors such as [sonobuoys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonobuoy), wire-mounted dipping sonar and [magnetic anomaly detectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_anomaly_detector) to identify possible threats, and torpedoes or [depth-charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depth-charge) to attack them.

With their onboard radar helicopters can also be used to reconnoitre over-the-horizon targets and, if equipped with [anti-ship missiles](140725.md) such as [Penguin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penguin_missile) or [Sea Skua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_Skua), to attack them. The helicopter is also invaluable for [search and rescue](172599.md) operation and has largely replaced the use of [small boats](135131.md) or the [jackstay rig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underway_replenishment) for such duties as transferring personnel, mail and cargo between ships or to shore. With helicopters these tasks can be accomplished faster and less dangerously, and without the need for the frigate to slow down or change course.

#### Air defence role



[*Iver Huitfeldt* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iver_Huitfeldt-class_frigate)

Frigates designed in the 1960s and 1970s, such as the US Navy's [*Knox*-class frigate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knox-class_frigate), West Germany's [*Bremen*-class frigate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bremen-class_frigate), and Royal Navy's Type 22 frigate were equipped with a small number of short-ranged surface-to-air missiles ([Sea Sparrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIM-7_Sparrow#Sea_Sparrow) or Sea Wolf) for point defence only.

By contrast newer frigates starting with the [*Oliver Hazard Perry*-class frigate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Hazard_Perry-class_frigate) are specialised for "zone-defence" [air defence](146640.md), because of major developments in [fighter jets](10929.md) and [cruise missiles](6590.md). Recent examples include the [*De Zeven Provinciën*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Zeven_Provinci%C3%ABn-class_frigate) air defence and command frigate of the [Royal Netherlands Navy](198478.md). These ships are armed with [VL Standard Missile 2 Block IIIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RIM-66_Standard), one or two [Goalkeeper CIWS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goalkeeper_CIWS) systems, ([HNLMS *Evertsen*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HNLMS_Evertsen_\(F805\)) has two Goalkeepers, the rest of the ships have the capacity for another one.) [VL Evolved Sea Sparrow Missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RIM-162_ESSM), a special [SMART-L](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMART-L) radar and a Thales [Active Phased Array Radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_Phased_Array_Radar) (APAR), all of which are for air defence. Another example is the [*Iver Huitfeldt* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iver_Huitfeldt-class_frigate) of the [Royal Danish Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Danish_Navy).[^cite_note-35]

#### Littoral combat ship (LCS)



[USS *Indianapolis*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Indianapolis_\(LCS-17\)), a [*Freedom*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom-class_littoral_combat_ship) [littoral combat ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Littoral_combat_ship) of the United States Navy

Some new classes of ships similar to corvettes are optimized for high-speed deployment and combat with small craft rather than combat between equal opponents; an example is the U.S. [littoral combat ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Littoral_combat_ship) (LCS). As of 2015, all [*Oliver Hazard Perry*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Hazard_Perry-class_frigate) frigates in the United States Navy have been decommissioned, and their role partially assumed by the new LCS. While the LCS class ships are smaller than the frigate class they will replace, they offer a similar degree of weaponry while requiring less than half the crew complement and offering a top speed of over 40 knots (74 km/h; 46 mph). A major advantage for the LCS ships is that they are designed around specific mission modules allowing them to fulfil a variety of roles. The modular system also allows for most upgrades to be performed ashore and installed later into the ship, keeping the ships available for deployment for the maximum time.

The latest U.S. deactivation plans make this the first time that the U.S. Navy has been without a frigate class of ships since 1943 (technically [USS *Constitution*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Constitution) is rated as a frigate and is still in commission, but does not count towards Navy force levels).[^cite_note-36] The remaining 20 LCSs to be acquired from 2019 and onwards that will be enhanced will be designated as frigates, and existing ships given modifications may also have their classification changed to *FF* as well.[^cite_note-37] However, the United States Navy selected a [FREMM multipurpose frigate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FREMM_multipurpose_frigate) variant for a new [*Constellation* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constellation-class_frigate) of 20 frigates, to be built by Fincantieri beginning in 2024.[^cite_note-38]

[*Baden-Württemberg*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_frigate_Baden-W%C3%BCrttemberg), the lead ship of [her class of frigates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baden-W%C3%BCrttemberg-class_frigate) in the [German Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundesmarine), is currently the largest frigate in the world.

[Stealth technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stealth_technology) has been introduced in modern frigate design by the French [*La Fayette* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Fayette-class_frigate) design.[^cite_note-39] Frigate shapes are designed to offer a minimal [radar cross section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_cross_section), which also lends them good air penetration; the manoeuvrability of these frigates has been compared to that of sailing ships. Examples are the Italian and French [Horizon class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon-class_frigate) with the [Aster 15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aster_15) and [Aster 30](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aster_30) missile for anti-missile capabilities, the German [F125](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F125-class_frigate) and [*Sachsen*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sachsen-class_frigate) frigates, the Japanese [Mogami-class frigate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mogami-class_frigate), the Russian [Admiral Gorshkov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiral_Gorshkov-class_frigate) classes with the [Zircon missile](53627779.md), the Indian [*Shivalik*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shivalik-class_frigate), [*Talwar*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talwar-class_frigate) and [*Nilgiri*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilgiri-class_frigate_\(2019\)) classes with the [Brahmos missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmos_missile), and the Malaysian [*Maharaja Lela* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maharaja_Lela-class_frigate) with the [Naval Strike Missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Strike_Missile).

The modern [French Navy](383256.md) applies the term first-class frigate and second-class frigate to both destroyers and frigates in service. Pennant numbers remain divided between F-series numbers for those ships internationally recognised as frigates and D-series pennant numbers for those more traditionally recognised as destroyers. This can result in some confusion as certain classes are referred to as frigates in French service while similar ships in other navies are referred to as destroyers. This also results in some recent classes of French ships such as the [Horizon class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon-class_frigate) being among the largest in the world to carry the rating of frigate. The *Frégates de Taille Intermédiaire* (FTI), which means frigates of intermediate size, is a French military program to design and create a planned class of frigates to be used by the French Navy. At the moment, the program consists of five ships, with commissioning [planned from 2023](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_of_the_French_Navy).

In the [German Navy](12960.md), frigates were used to replace ageing destroyers; however in size and role the new German frigates exceed the former class of destroyers. The current German F125 [Baden-Württemberg-class frigates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baden-W%C3%BCrttemberg-class_frigate) are the largest class of frigates worldwide with a displacement of more than 7,200 tons . The same was done in the [Spanish Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Navy), which went ahead with the deployment of the first [Aegis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegis_combat_system) frigates, the [*Álvaro de Bazán*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%81lvaro_de_Baz%C3%A1n-class_frigate) frigates. The [Myanmar Navy](9502915.md) is producing modern frigates with a reduced [radar cross section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_cross_section) known as the [Kyan Sittha-class frigate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyan_Sittha-class_frigate). Before the Kyan Sittha class, the Myanmar Navy also produced an [*Aung Zeya*-class frigate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aung_Zeya-class_frigate). Although the size of the Myanmar Navy is quite small, it is producing modern guided-missile frigates with the help of Russia, China, and India. However, the fleets of the Myanmar Navy are still expanding with several on-going shipbuilding programmes, including one 135 m (442 ft 11 in), 4,000-tonne frigate with the [vertical missile launch systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_launching_system).[^cite_note-myanmar-40] The four planned [*Tamandaré*-class frigates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamandar%C3%A9-class_frigate) of the [Brazilian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazilian_Navy) will be responsible for introducing ships with stealth technology in the national navy and the Latin American region, with the first boat expected to be launched in 2024.[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42]

### Disputed classes

These ships are classified by their respective nations as frigates, but are considered destroyers internationally due to size, armament, and role.

-  [Egyptian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_Navy) operates the [ENS *Tahya Misr*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ENS_Tahya_Misr). This is one of the *Aquitaine*-class variants of the FREMM Multipurpose frigates purchased from France, which is classified as a destroyer by France.

-  [German Navy](12960.md) operates three [*Sachsen*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sachsen-class_frigate) frigates and four [*Baden-Württemberg*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baden-W%C3%BCrttemberg-class_frigate) frigates. These ships are officially classified as frigates by Germany, but regarded as destroyers internationally due to size and capability.

-  [Royal Moroccan Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Moroccan_Navy) operates the [*Mohammed VI*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moroccan_frigate_Mohammed_VI). This is one of the *Aquitaine*-class variants of the FREMM Multipurpose frigates purchased from France, which is classified as a destroyer by France.

-  [Royal Netherlands Navy](198478.md) operates four [*De Zeven Provinciën*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Zeven_Provinci%C3%ABn-class_frigate) frigates. These ships are classified as frigates by the Netherlands, but regarded as destroyers internationally due to size and capability.

  [^cite_note-47]

-  [Royal Norwegian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Norwegian_Navy) operates four [*Fridtjof Nansen*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fridtjof_Nansen-class_frigate) frigates. These ships are subclasses of Spain's *Alvaro de Bazan*-class, and classified as frigates, but are regarded as destroyers due to their size and armament.

  [^cite_note-48]

-  [Romanian Naval Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_Naval_Forces) operates the [Romanian frigate *Mărășești*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_frigate_M%C4%83r%C4%83%C8%99e%C8%99ti), classified as a destroyer until 2001. No official reason was given for this and there was no change in armament or capability, thus remaining in the destroyer type.

-  [Spanish Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Navy) operates five [*Álvaro de Bazán*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%81lvaro_de_Baz%C3%A1n-class_frigate) frigates. These ships are officially classified as a frigates by Spain, but due to their size and capabilities are regarded internationally as destroyers. They also served as the basis for Australia's *Hobart*-class destroyers.

## Former operators

-  [Cuban Revolutionary Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Revolutionary_Navy) decommissioned its last true frigates, the [Koni-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koni-class_frigate) frigate in 1998.

-  [Dominican Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominican_Navy) decommissioned its last [River-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River-class_frigate) frigate in 1998.

-  [Islamic Republic of Iran Navy](2160969.md) lost their entire frigate force, comprising four [*Moudge*-class](10946960.md) frigates and three [*Alvand*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvand-class_frigate) frigates in the [2026 Iran war](82533582.md).

-  [Iraqi Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_Navy) lost its only operational frigate [*Ibn Khaldoum*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_frigate_Ibn_Khaldun) which was sunk in 2003.
