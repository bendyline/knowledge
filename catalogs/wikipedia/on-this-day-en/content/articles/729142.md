The **Beechcraft King Air** is a line of American [utility aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_aircraft) produced by [Beechcraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beechcraft). The King Air line comprises a number of twin-[turboprop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turboprop) models that have been divided into two families. The Model 90 and 100 series developed in the 1960s are known as King Airs, while the later [T-tail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-tail) Model 200 and 300 series were originally [marketed](59252.md) as [Super King Airs](9023483.md), with the name "Super" being dropped by Beechcraft in 1996 (although it is still often used to differentiate the 200 and 300 series King Airs from their smaller stablemates).

The King Air was the first aircraft in its class and was produced continuously from 1964 to 2021. It outsold all of its turboprop competitors combined. It has recently faced competition from light jet aircraft such as the [Embraer Phenom 100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embraer_Phenom_100), [Honda HA-420 HondaJet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honda_HA-420_HondaJet) and [Cessna Citation Mustang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cessna_Citation_Mustang); as well as from newer turboprop aircraft including the [Piaggio P180 Avanti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piaggio_P180_Avanti), and single-engine [Piper Malibu Meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piper_Malibu_Meridian), [Pilatus PC-12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilatus_PC-12), and [Socata TBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socata_TBM).

### Model 90 series



King Air B90 at [Piedmont Triad International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piedmont_Triad_International_Airport)

The Model 90 King Air was conceived as the **Model 120** in 1961. In its original planned configuration, the Model 120 was to have been powered by two 917 shp (684 kW) [Turbomeca Bastan VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbomeca_Bastan) engines.[^cite_note-4] On May 15, 1963, Beechcraft began test flights of the proof-of-concept [Model 87](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beechcraft_U-21_Ute), a modified [Queen Air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beechcraft_Queen_Air) with [Pratt & Whitney Canada PT6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_Canada_PT6)A-6 engines. On July 14, Beech announced a new type, and a month later began accepting orders for the "King Air", with deliveries to commence in Autumn 1964.[^cite_note-5] On January 24, 1964, the first definitive prototype, by now designated **Model 65-90** and also fitted with PT6A-6 engines,[^cite_note-6] flew for the first time. After 10 months of test flying, in 1964 the Model 87 was delivered to the [United States Army](32087.md) as the NU-8F. The first production aircraft was delivered on October 8, and by the end of the month, 152 aircraft had been ordered;[^cite_note-7] by year's end, seven had been built.[^cite_note-HBPL-8]

In 1966, after 112 65-90s were completed,[^cite_note-HBPL-8] production switched to the **Model 65-A90** with PT6A-20 engines. As a measure of the type's popularity, 206 65-A90s were built in less than two years when production switched to the **Model B90**, the first of these rolling off the production line in 1968.[^cite_note-HBPL-8] Unpressurised military models based on the Model 87 were also built under the designation [U-21 Ute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beechcraft_U-21_Ute).[^cite_note-9]

A total of 184 B90 models were produced before the **Model C90** was introduced in 1971,[^cite_note-HBPL-8] with wingspan increased over earlier models by 4 ft 11 in (1.50 m) to 50 ft 3 in (15.32 m), Maximum Take-Off Weight (MTOW) increased by 350 lb (160 kg) to 9,650 lb (4,378 kg), and PT6A-20A engines. The broadly similar **Model E90** was introduced the following year, with PT6A-28 engines; the two were produced in parallel. Further refinement of the 90 series resulted in the **Model F90** and follow-on **Model F90-1**. The F-models featured the [T-tail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-tail) of the [Model 200](9023483.md#Super_King_Air_200) mated to the fuselage and wings of the E90, with PT6A-135 engines of 750 shp (560 kW) driving four-bladed propellers. The F90 prototype flew on January 16, 1978, and 203 production versions followed between 1979 and 1983, when the F90 was superseded by the F90-1.[^cite_note-HBPL-8] The F90 prototype was re-engined with [Garrett AiResearch TPE-331](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrett_AiResearch_TPE-331) engines to test the feasibility of a **Model G90**, but this model was not put into production.

The **Model C90-1** entered production in 1982 after 507 C90s and 347 E90s had been built,[^cite_note-HBPL-8] and featured PT6A-21 engines and improvements to the pressurization system. 54 were built.[^cite_note-HBPL-8][^cite_note-10] The following year the F90-1 was put into production with redesigned engine cowlings, upgraded PT6A-135A engines, hydraulic landing gear, and triple-fed electrical bus; only 33 were built by the time production terminated in 1985.[^cite_note-HBPL-8] The C90-1 was soon followed by the **Model C90A**, which featured the redesigned engine cowlings of the F90-1. The C90A received an increase in MTOW in 1987, being certified to 10,100 lb (4,580 kg). The C90A model was in production until 1992, by which time 235 had been built, all but 74 with the increased MTOW.

Only two C90As were built in 1992, the **Model C90B** followed that year with airframe improvements, four-bladed propellers, and propeller synchrophasing,[^cite_note-11] all in an effort to reduce cabin noise. This model also had PT6A-21s; the first production C90B was fitted with the 10,000th PT6 engine delivered to Beechcraft. In 1994 a cheaper version was introduced as the **C90SE** (Special Edition), with three-bladed propellers, standardised interior and mechanical instruments instead of the [Electronic Flight Instrument System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Flight_Instrument_System) (EFIS) fitted to the C90B.[^cite_note-NZ-12] A total of 456 C90Bs and C90SEs were delivered by the time production of these models ended in late 2005.[^cite_note-HBPL-8]

In July 2005, during the [Oshkosh Airshow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oshkosh_Airshow), Beechcraft introduced the **C90GT**. The C90GT was fitted with 750 shp (560 kW) PT6A-135As, [flat rated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_rated) to the same 550 shp (410 kW) as the earlier King Airs. This engine change increased performance due to lower operating temperatures, improving both cruise speed and climb rate. With a 275 kt (509 km/h, 316 mph) cruise speed, the C90GT was highly competitive with the new generation of [Very Light Jets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Very_Light_Jet) over short to medium distances, while providing a larger and more luxurious cabin. C90GT deliveries commenced at the beginning of 2006.[^cite_note-HBPL-8] On May 21, 2007, during the 7th Annual European Business Aviation Convention & Exhibition in Geneva, Beechcraft announced the **Model C90GTi** updated version of the C90GT,[^cite_note-13] featuring the [Rockwell Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockwell_Collins) Proline 21 [avionics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avionics) package previously only offered for the B200 and B300 King Airs. Deliveries commenced in 2008 after 97 C90GTs were delivered to customers over the previous two years.[^cite_note-HBPL-8] In 2015, the **C90GTx** was introduced with additional upgrades. In 2019, the C90 unit cost was US\$2.75M, and \$4.2M (\~\$5.07 million in 2024) for the C90GTi.[^cite_note-BCA-2019-14]

In March 2021, Beechcraft discontinued the C90GTx, thus ending the Model 90 production run. [Textron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textron), Beechcraft's parent company, stated that it intends to support the existing 90 series fleet indefinitely given the large number of aircraft being actively operated.[^cite_note-15]

### Model 100 series



A Model B100 King Air with Garrett engines



[Japan Maritime Self-Defense Force](2370744.md) TC-90

The **Model 100** is a stretched derivative of the Model 90 featuring five cabin windows instead of the Model 90's three; MTOW increased by 1,300 lb (590 kg) over the 90, to 10,600 lb (4,810 kg). The 100 used the wings, tail, and engines (two PT6A-28 engines, although rated at 680 shp)[^cite_note-16] from the [Model 99](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beechcraft_Model_99) airliner, itself a development of the Queen Air (as was the Model 90).

The Model 100 was flown for the first time on March 17, 1969, and unveiled to the public in May. A total of 89 Model 100s were built before it was superseded by the **Model A100** in 1972, with a further increase in MTOW to 11,500 lb (5,220 kg), fuel capacity increased by 94 US gallons (78 imp gal; 360 L), and four-bladed propellers.[^cite_note-17] A total of 157 A100s were built by the time production of this model ceased in 1979. The next in the series was the **B100**, which featured 715 shp (533 kW) Garrett AiResearch TPE-331 engines as an alternative to the Pratt & Whitneys offered on other King Airs, and another increase in MTOW to 11,800 lb (5,350 kg). The B100 was introduced in 1976 and was produced concurrently with the A100 for several years; manufacture ceased in 1983 after 137 were built. The [Model 200 Super King Air](9023483.md) was developed from the Model 100, with the same fuselage design being used for both models (with some differences, mainly associated with the different tails). The Model 200 had different wings and a T-tail and entered service in 1974.

### Military King Air versions

#### Japan

The [Japan Maritime Self-Defense Force](2370744.md) (JMSDF) has operated a total of 40 C90 and C90A King Airs, with deliveries beginning in 1973. These have been given various designations by the JMSDF and consist of 34 **TC-90** trainers, five **LC-90** transports, and a single **UC-90** which is configured for photographic aerial survey. The TC-90s and the UC-90 are operated by the [202nd Naval Air Training Squadron (JMSDF)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/202nd_Naval_Air_Training_Squadron_\(JMSDF\)) based at [Tokushima Air Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokushima_Airport), while the LC-90s are attached to various Lockheed P-3 *Kokutai* (Squadrons) and [Air Transport Squadron 61](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Transport_Squadron_61_\(JMSDF\))[^cite_note-18] as liaison aircraft. In late 2005, the JMSDF marked 500,000 accident-free flying hours of the TC-90 trainer fleet. Philippine maintenance staff will also be trained.[^cite_note-19]

The JMSDF made plans to lease at least five TC-90 aircraft to the Philippines to conduct maritime patrols.[^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23] Two aircraft were transferred free of charge in March 2017.[^cite_note-flyteam-24] From November 2016 to November 2017, six [Philippine Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine_Navy) pilots were trained to fly the aircraft at [Tokushima Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokushima_Airport). Maintenance staff are also being trained.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26][^cite_note-27] There are plans to transfer three more aircraft.[^cite_note-flyteam-24]

#### United States



US Navy T-44A Pegasus



A [US Army](32087.md) U-21 Ute

The U.S. military has used King Air 90s in various roles, primarily VIP and liaison transport, with designations including the **VC-6A**, the **T-44 Pegasus**, and the [U-21 Ute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beechcraft_U-21_Ute). The U-21 Ute used by the US Army was the most common version.

The T-44A Pegasus is a trainer version, designated the **Model H90** by Beechcraft, used to train [United States Navy](20518076.md), [Marine Corps](17349325.md), [Coast Guard](32223.md), and [Air Force](32090.md) (USAF) pilots to fly multi-engine aircraft. A total of 61 were delivered to the US Navy between 1977 and 1980. In August 2006, the Navy announced that after 29 years of operation, the T-44A fleet would be upgraded with modernized avionics systems, and redesignated T-44Cs.[^cite_note-28]

Two VC-6A aircraft were operated by the US military. One was a Model 65-A90 operated by the US Army and serialled 66-15361,[^cite_note-29] the other a B90, designated as a VC-6A (66-7943), was operated by the USAF and used by [President](24113.md) [Lyndon Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_Johnson).

##### Air Force One



VC-6A used as Air Force One

During the administration of Lyndon Johnson, the USAF acquired a [commercial off-the-shelf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_off-the-shelf) Model B90 King Air. With the military designation **VC-6A**, the aircraft, serialled 66-7943, was used to transport President Johnson between [Bergstrom Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bergstrom_Air_Force_Base) (near Austin) and the Johnson family ranch near [Johnson City, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnson_City,_Texas). When Johnson was aboard, the aircraft used the callsign [Air Force One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Force_One). After Johnson left office, the aircraft continued to serve in the [89th Military Airlift Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/89th_Airlift_Wing) as a VIP transport until its retirement in 1985. This aircraft is now on display, with other presidential aircraft, at the [National Museum of the United States Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_the_United_States_Air_Force) at [Wright Patterson Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wright_Patterson_Air_Force_Base) near Dayton.[^cite_note-AF1NMUSAF-30]

### Modification and upgrade programs



A B100 King Air modified with five-bladed propellers

A number of aftermarket modifications and upgrades are available for 90 and 100 Series King Airs.[^cite_note-CAT-31] An engine upgrade involves earlier-build 90 Series aircraft being re-engined with the PT6A-135A engines of the C90GT.[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33] A more radical re-engining program involves the replacement of the PT6s in C90 and E90 King Airs with TPE-331s.



A King Air 90 with the full CargoLiner Conversion

Among the numerous airframe modifications available: a cargo conversion for the 90 model, the CargoLiner, which replaces the rear door with a large pallet accessible cargo door, a heavy duty floor structure and cabin cargo liner, also a crew hatch for cockpit access for the crew in the 90, 100, and 200;[^cite_note-34] a Wing Front Spar Reinforcement Kit for both 90 and 100 Series aircraft,[^cite_note-CAT-31] a modification for the entire King Air line that entails reworking and extending the nose[^cite_note-35] to house a baggage compartment as well as the avionics normally found in the noses of King Air aircraft. Modifications available for the King Air 100 include a belly cargo pod similar to those fitted to the Beech 99 and the Model 1300 version of the King Air 200 series.[^cite_note-36]

## Operators

Main article: [List of Beechcraft King Air operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Beechcraft_King_Air_operators)

In addition to its use by military and government users, the King Air is also used by many non-governmental organizations, as well as by corporate and private users. This includes commercial use by air-taxi and air charter companies.[^cite_note-37]

The [Royal Flying Doctor Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Flying_Doctor_Service) of Australia previously operated a large number of 90 Series King Airs, but retired the last example in 2006, standardizing on the King Air 200 Series and the [Pilatus PC-12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilatus_PC-12) for its fleet requirements.
