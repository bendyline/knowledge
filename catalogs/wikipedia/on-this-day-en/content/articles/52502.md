The **Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine**[^cite_note-2] is awarded yearly by the [Nobel Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Assembly_at_the_Karolinska_Institute) at the [Karolinska Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karolinska_Institute) for outstanding discoveries in [physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology) or [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine). It is one of the five [Nobel Prizes](21201.md) prizes that, in accordance with [Alfred Nobel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Nobel)'s 1895 will, are awarded "to those who, during the preceding year, have conferred the greatest benefit to humankind". Nobel Prizes are awarded in the fields of [Physics](52497.md), Medicine or Physiology, [Chemistry](25416893.md), [Literature](23385442.md), and [Peace](26230922.md). A sixth prize, for [Economic Sciences](23535306.md), was established in 1968 by Sveriges Riksbank (Sweden's central bank) in memory of Alfred Nobel.&#x20;

The Nobel Prize is presented annually on the anniversary of Alfred Nobel's death, 10 December. As of 2024, 116 Nobel Prizes in Physiology or Medicine have been awarded to 232 [ laureates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Nobel_laureates_in_Physiology_or_Medicine), 218 men and 14 women. The first one was awarded in 1901 to the German [physiologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiologist), [Emil von Behring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_von_Behring), for his work on [serum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_plasma) therapy and the development of a [vaccine](32653.md) against [diphtheria](58937.md). The first woman to receive the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, [Gerty Cori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerty_Cori), received it in 1947 for her role in elucidating the [metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism) of [glucose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose), important in many aspects of medicine, including treatment of [diabetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diabetes). The most recent Nobel prize was announced by the Nobel Assembly at [Karolinska Institutet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karolinska_Institute) on 6 October 2025, and has been awarded to Americans [Mary E. Brunkow](81273907.md) and [Fred Ramsdell](60424610.md), and Japanese scientist [Shimon Sakaguchi](48010182.md), for their discoveries concerning peripheral immune tolerance and regulatory T cells.[^cite_note-3]&#x20;

The prize consists of [a medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_medal) along with a diploma and a certificate for the monetary award. The [front side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obverse) of the medal displays the same profile of [Alfred Nobel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Nobel) depicted on the medals for Physics, Chemistry, and Literature; the reverse side is unique to this medal.&#x20;

Some awards have been controversial. This includes one to [António Egas Moniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ant%C3%B3nio_Egas_Moniz) in 1949 for the [prefrontal lobotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobotomy), bestowed despite protests from the medical establishment. Other controversies resulted from disagreements over who was included in the award. The 1952 prize to [Selman Waksman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selman_Waksman) was litigated in court, and half the patent rights were awarded to his co-discoverer [Albert Schatz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Schatz_\(scientist\)) who was not recognised by the prize. Nobel prizes cannot be awarded [posthumously](21021837.md). Also, no more than three recipients can receive a Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, a limitation that is sometimes discussed because of an increasing trend for larger teams to conduct important scientific projects.&#x20;

## Background



Nobel was interested in experimental physiology and set up his own laboratories.

Main article: [Nobel Prize](21201.md)

[Alfred Nobel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Nobel) was born on 21 October 1833 in [Stockholm](26741.md), Sweden, into a family of engineers.[^cite_note-Levinovitz5-4] He was a chemist, engineer and inventor who amassed a fortune during his lifetime, most of it from his 355 inventions, of which [dynamite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamite) is the most famous.[^cite_note-Levinovitz11-5] He was interested in experimental physiology and set up his own labs in France and Italy to conduct experiments in blood transfusions. Keeping abreast of scientific findings, he was generous in his donations to [Ivan Pavlov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Pavlov)'s laboratory in Russia and was optimistic about the progress resulting from scientific discoveries made in laboratories.[^cite_note-Feldman237-238-6]&#x20;

In 1888, Nobel was surprised to read his own obituary, titled "The Merchant of death Is Dead", in a French newspaper. As it happened, it was Nobel's brother [Ludvig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludvig_Nobel) who had died, but Nobel, unhappy with the content of the obituary and concerned that his legacy would reflect poorly on him, was inspired to change his will.[^cite_note-Time-7] In his last will, Nobel requested that his money be used to create a series of prizes for those who confer the "greatest benefit on mankind" in [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry), [peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace), [physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology) or medicine, and literature.[^cite_note-BBCHistory-8] Though Nobel wrote several wills during his lifetime, the last was written a little over a year before he died in 1896 at the age of 63.[^cite_note-9] Because his will was contested, it was not approved by the [Storting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_Norway) (Norwegian Parliament) until 26 April 1897.[^cite_note-Levinovitz13-10]&#x20;

After Nobel's death, the [Nobel Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Foundation) was set up to manage the assets of the bequest.[^cite_note-nobelfoundation-11] In 1900, the Nobel Foundation's newly created [statutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute) were promulgated by Swedish King [Oscar II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_II_of_Sweden).[^cite_note-thelocal1-12][^cite_note-Levinovitz14-13] According to Nobel's will, the [Karolinska Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karolinska_Institute) in Sweden, a medical school and research centre, is responsible for the Prize in Physiology or Medicine.[^cite_note-14] Today, the prize is commonly referred to as the Nobel Prize in Medicine.[^cite_note-Levinovitz112-15]&#x20;

## Nomination and selection



The reverse side of the Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine

It was important to Nobel that the prize be awarded for a "discovery" and that it be of "greatest benefit on mankind".[^cite_note-Lindsten-16] Per the provisions of the will, only select persons are eligible to nominate individuals for the award. These include members of academies around the world, professors of medicine in Sweden, Denmark, Norway, Iceland, and Finland, as well as professors of selected universities and research institutions in other countries. Past Nobel laureates may also nominate.[^cite_note-17] Until 1977, all professors of Karolinska Institute together decided on the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. That year, changes in Swedish law forced the institute to make public any documents pertaining to the Nobel Prize, and it was considered necessary to establish a legally independent body for the Prize work. Therefore, the Nobel Assembly was constituted, consisting of 50 professors at Karolinska Institute. It elects the [Nobel Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Committee) with five members who evaluate the nominees, the Secretary who is in charge of the organisation, and each year ten adjunct members to assist in the evaluation of candidates. In 1968, a provision was added that no more than three persons may share a Nobel prize.[^cite_note-Levinovitz17-18]&#x20;

True to its mandate, the committee has chosen researchers working in the basic sciences over those who have made [applied science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_science) contributions. [Harvey Cushing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvey_Cushing), a pioneering American [neurosurgeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurosurgeon) who identified [Cushing's syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cushing%27s_syndrome), was not awarded the prize, nor was [Sigmund Freud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_Freud), as his [psychoanalysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychoanalysis) lacks hypotheses that can be experimentally confirmed.[^cite_note-Feldman238-19] The public expected [Jonas Salk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonas_Salk) or [Albert Sabin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Sabin) to receive the prize for their development of the [polio vaccines](192198.md), but instead the award went to [John Enders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Enders), [Thomas Weller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Huckle_Weller), and [Frederick Robbins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Robbins) whose basic discovery that the polio virus could reproduce in monkey cells in laboratory preparations made the vaccines possible.[^cite_note-bishop-20]&#x20;

Through the 1930s, there were frequent prize laureates in classical [physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology), but after that, the field began fragmenting into specialities. The last classical physiology laureates were [John Eccles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Eccles_\(neurophysiologist\)), [Alan Hodgkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Hodgkin), and [Andrew Huxley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Huxley) in 1963 for their findings regarding "unitary electrical events in the central and peripheral nervous system."[^cite_note-Feldman239-21]&#x20;

## Prizes

A Medicine or Physiology Nobel Prize laureate earns a [gold medal](526488.md), a diploma bearing a [citation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citation), and a sum of money.[^cite_note-22] These are awarded during the prize ceremony at the [Stockholm Concert Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_Concert_Hall).&#x20;

### Medals

Main article: [Nobel Prize medal § Physiology and Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_medal#Physiology_and_Medicine)



[Alexander Fleming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Fleming)'s 1945 [Nobel Prize medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_medal) for Physiology or Medicine on display at the [National Museum of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Scotland), Edinburgh.

The Physiology or Medicine medal has a portrait of [Alfred Nobel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Nobel) in left profile on the [obverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obverse_and_reverse).[^cite_note-NobelUnique-23] The medal was designed by [Erik Lindberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erik_Lindberg).[^cite_note-NobelUnique-23] The reverse of the medal depicts the 'Genius of Medicine holding an open book in her lap, collecting the water pouring out from a rock in order to quench a sick girl's thirst'.[^cite_note-NobelMedicine-24] It is inscribed "*Inventas vitam iuvat excoluisse per artes*" ("It is beneficial to have improved (human) life through discovered arts") an adaptation of "*inventas aut qui vitam excoluere per artes*" from line 663 from book 6 of the *[Aeneid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid)* by the Roman poet [Virgil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil).[^cite_note-NobelMedicine-24] A plate below the figures is inscribed with the name of the recipient. The text "REG. UNIVERSITAS MED. CHIR. CAROL." denoting the [Karolinska Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karolinska_Institute) is also inscribed on the reverse.[^cite_note-NobelMedicine-24]&#x20;

Between 1902 and 2010 the Nobel Prize medals were struck by the [Myntverket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myntverket), the Swedish royal [mint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mint_\(facility\)), located in [Eskilstuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eskilstuna). In 2011 the medals were made by the Det Norske Myntverket in [Kongsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kongsberg). The medals have been made by Svenska Medalj in [Eskilstuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eskilstuna) since 2012.[^cite_note-NobelUnique-23]&#x20;

### Diplomas

Nobel laureates receive a diploma directly from the [King of Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_of_Sweden). Each diploma is uniquely designed by the prize-awarding institutions for the laureate who receives it. In the case of the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, that is the Nobel Assembly at Karolinska Institute. Well-known artists and calligraphers from Sweden are commissioned to create it.[^cite_note-nobelprize5-25] The diploma contains a picture and text which states the name of the laureate and a citation as to why they received the prize.[^cite_note-nobelprize5-25]&#x20;

### Award money

At the awards ceremony, the laureate is given a document indicating the award sum. The amount of the cash award may differ from year to year, based on the funding available from the [Nobel Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Foundation). For example, in 2009 the total cash awarded was 10 million SEK (US\$1.4 million),[^cite_note-nobelprize2-26] but in 2012, the amount was 8 million Swedish Krona, or US\$1.1 million.[^cite_note-27] If there are two laureates in a particular category, the award grant is divided equally between the recipients, but if there are three, the awarding committee may opt to divide the grant equally, or award half to one recipient and a quarter to each of the two others.[^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29][^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31]&#x20;

### Ceremony and banquet

The awards are bestowed at a gala ceremony followed by a banquet.[^cite_note-local-32] The Nobel Banquet is an extravagant affair with the menu, planned months ahead of time, kept secret until the day of the event. The Nobel Foundation chooses the menu after tasting and testing selections submitted by selected chefs of international repute. Currently, it is a three-course dinner, although it was originally six courses in 1901. Each Nobel Prize laureate may bring up to 16 guests. Sweden's royal family attends, and typically the Prime Minister and other members of the government attend as well as representatives of the Nobel family.[^cite_note-33]&#x20;

## Laureates

For a more comprehensive list, see [List of Nobel laureates in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Nobel_laureates_in_Physiology_or_Medicine).



[Emil von Behring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_von_Behring) received the first Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1901.

[^cite_note-Feldman242-34]

The first Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded in 1901 to the German physiologist [Emil Adolf von Behring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Adolf_von_Behring).[^cite_note-Feldman242-34] Behring's discovery of [serum therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serum_therapy) in the development of the diphtheria and tetanus vaccines put "in the hands of the physician a victorious weapon against illness and deaths".[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-Behring-36] In 1902, the award went to [Ronald Ross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Ross) for his work on malaria, "by which he has shown how it enters the organism and thereby has laid the foundation for successful research on this disease and methods of combating it".[^cite_note-37] He identified the mosquito as the transmitter of malaria, and worked tirelessly on measures to prevent malaria worldwide.[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39] The 1903 prize was awarded to [Niels Ryberg Finsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Ryberg_Finsen), the first Faroese laureate, "in recognition of his contribution to the treatment of diseases, especially [lupus vulgaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lupus_vulgaris), with [concentrated light radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phototherapy), whereby he has opened a new avenue for medical science".[^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41] He died within a year after receiving the prize at the age of 43.[^cite_note-finsen-42] [Ivan Pavlov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Pavlov), whose work Nobel admired and supported, received the prize in 1904 for his work on the physiology of [digestion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digestion).[^cite_note-43]&#x20;



[Nikolaas Tinbergen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolaas_Tinbergen) (left) and [Konrad Lorenz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konrad_Lorenz) (right) were awarded (with [Karl von Frisch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_von_Frisch)) for their discoveries concerning animal behaviour.

[^cite_note-nobel-1973-44]

Subsequently, those selecting the recipients have exercised wide latitude in determining what falls under the umbrella of Physiology or Medicine. The awarding of the prize in 1973 to [Nikolaas Tinbergen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolaas_Tinbergen), [Konrad Lorenz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konrad_Lorenz), and [Karl von Frisch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_von_Frisch) for their observations of animal behavioural patterns could be considered a prize in the [behavioural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioural_sciences) rather than medicine or physiology.[^cite_note-Levinovitz112-15] Tinbergen expressed surprise in his Nobel Prize acceptance speech at "the unconventional decision of the Nobel Foundation to award this year's prize 'for Physiology or Medicine' to three men who had until recently been regarded as 'mere animal watchers'".[^cite_note-Tinbergen-45]&#x20;



In 1947, [Gerty Cori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerty_Cori) was the first woman to be awarded the Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Laureates have been awarded the Nobel Prize in a wide range of fields that relate to physiology or medicine. Prizes have recognized significant discoveries in areas as diverse as [signal transduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_transduction) through [G proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G_protein), [neurobiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurobiology), and [intermediary metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermediary_metabolism). As of 2025, a total of 116 Nobel Prizes in Physiology or Medicine have been awarded to 232 individuals since 1901.[^cite_note-nervesignaling-46][^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48]&#x20;

Thirteen women have received the prize: [Gerty Cori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerty_Cori) (1947), [Rosalyn Yalow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosalyn_Yalow) (1977), [Barbara McClintock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_McClintock) (1983), [Rita Levi-Montalcini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rita_Levi-Montalcini) (1986), [Gertrude B. Elion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gertrude_B._Elion) (1988), [Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christiane_N%C3%BCsslein-Volhard) (1995), [Linda B. Buck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linda_B._Buck) (2004), [Françoise Barré-Sinoussi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7oise_Barr%C3%A9-Sinoussi) (2008), [Elizabeth H. Blackburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_H._Blackburn) (2009), [Carol W. Greider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_W._Greider) (2009), [May-Britt Moser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May-Britt_Moser) (2014), [Tu Youyou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tu_Youyou) (2015), [Katalin Karikó](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katalin_Karik%C3%B3) (2023) and [Mary E. [Mario Capecchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Capecchi), [Martin Evans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Evans), and [Oliver Smithies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Smithies) were awarded the prize in 2007 for the discovery of a [gene targeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_targeting) procedure (a type of [genetic recombination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_recombination)) for introducing [homologous recombination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homologous_recombination) in mice, employing [embryonic stem cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embryonic_stem_cell) through the development of the [knockout mouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knockout_mouse).[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-Hansson-53] In 2009, the Nobel Prize was awarded to [Elizabeth Blackburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Blackburn), [Carol W. Through 2025, the prize has been awarded to a single individual 40 times, shared by two laureates 35 times, and shared by three laureates 41 times (the maximum allowed).&#x20;

### Time factor and death



[Ralph M. Steinman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_M._Steinman) was an inadvertent posthumous recipient of the Prize.

Because of the length of time that may pass before the significance of a discovery becomes apparent, some prizes are awarded many years after the initial discovery. [Barbara McClintock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_McClintock) made her discoveries in 1944, before the structure of the DNA molecule was known; she was not awarded the prize until 1983. Similarly, in 1916 [Peyton Rous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peyton_Rous) discovered the role of tumor viruses in chickens, but was not awarded the prize until 50 years later, in 1966.[^cite_note-Levinovitz114-56] Nobel laureate [Carol Greider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Greider)'s research leading to the prize was conducted over 20 years before. She noted that the passage of time is an advantage in the medical sciences, as it may take many years for the significance of a discovery to become apparent.[^cite_note-Dreifus-57]&#x20;

In 2011, Canadian immunologist [Ralph M. Steinman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_M._Steinman) was awarded the prize; however, unbeknownst to the committee, he had died three days before the announcement. The committee decided that since the prize was awarded "in good faith," it would be allowed to stand.&#x20;

### Controversial inclusions and exclusions

Main article: [Nobel Prize controversies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_controversies)

Some of the awards have been controversial. The person who was deserving of the 1923 prize for the discovery of [insulin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insulin) as a central hormone for controlling diabetes (awarded only a year after its discovery)[^cite_note-Judson291-58] has been heatedly debated. It was shared between [Frederick Banting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Banting) and [John Macleod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Macleod_\(physiologist\)); this infuriated Banting who regarded Macleod's involvement as minimal. Macleod was the department head at the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto) but otherwise was not directly involved in the findings. Banting thought his laboratory partner [Charles Best](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Best_\(medical_scientist\)), who had shared in the laboratory work of discovery, should have shared the prize with him as well. In fairness, he decided to give half of his prize money to Best. Macleod on his part felt the biochemist [James Collip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Collip), who joined the laboratory team later, deserved to be included in the award and shared his prize money with him.[^cite_note-Judson291-58] Some maintain that [Nicolae Paulescu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolae_Paulescu), a Romanian professor of physiology at the [University of Medicine and Pharmacy in Bucharest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Davila_University_of_Medicine_and_Pharmacy), was the first to isolate insulin, in 1916, although his pancrein was an impure aqueous extract unfit for human treatment similar to the one used previously by [Israel Kleiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_Kleiner_\(biochemist\)).[^cite_note-J._Nutrition_92-59][^cite_note-nrjs-60][^cite_note-nrps-61] When Banting published the paper that brought him the Nobel,[^cite_note-nrks-62] Paulescu already held a patent for his discovery (10 April 1922, patent no. 6254 (8322) "Pancreina şi procedeul fabricaţiei ei"/"Pancrein and the process of making it", from the Romanian Ministry of Industry and Trade).[^cite_note-nrms-63][^cite_note-nrns-64][^cite_note-nrqs-65]&#x20;

The Spanish neurophysiologist Fernando de Castro (1896–1967) was the first to describe arterial [chemoreceptors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemoreceptors) and circumscribe them to the carotid body for the respiratory reflexes in 1926–1928. For many experts, this direct disciple of Santiago Ramón y Cajal deserved to share the Nobel Prize 1938 with the awarded Corneille Heymans, but at that time Spain was immersed in the Spanish Civil War and it seems that the Nobel Board even doubted if he was alive or not, being at the front since almost the beginning of the conflict. Heymans himself recognised the merits of De Castro for the Nobel Prize in different occasions, including a famous talk in Montevideo (Uruguay).[^cite_note-66]&#x20;



Scandal and controversy resulted from the 2008 award to [Harald zur Hausen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_zur_Hausen) for the discovery of [HPV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_papillomavirus_infection), and to [Françoise Barré-Sinoussi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7oise_Barr%C3%A9-Sinoussi) and [Luc Montagnier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luc_Montagnier) for discovering [HIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_immunodeficiency_virus).

In 1949, despite protests from the medical establishment, the Portuguese neurologist [António Egas Moniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ant%C3%B3nio_Egas_Moniz) received the Physiology or Medicine Prize for his development of the [prefrontal leucotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefrontal_leucotomy), which he promoted by declaring the procedure's success just 10 days postoperative. Due largely to the publicity surrounding the award, it was prescribed without regard for modern [medical ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_ethics). Favourable results were reported by such publications as *[The New York Times](30680.md)*. It is estimated that around 40,000 lobotomies were performed in the United States before the procedure's popularity faded.[^cite_note-67] [Rosemary Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosemary_Kennedy), the sister of [John F. Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy), was subjected to the procedure by [their father](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Kennedy); it incapacitated her to the extent that she needed to be institutionalised for the rest of her life.[^cite_note-Feldman287-68][^cite_note-69]&#x20;

The 1952 prize, awarded solely to [Selman Waksman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selman_Waksman) for his discovery of [streptomycin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streptomycin), omitted the recognition some felt due to his co-discoverer [Albert Schatz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Schatz_\(scientist\)).[^cite_note-pharmj-70][^cite_note-Wainwright-71] There was litigation brought by Schatz against Waksman over the details and credit of the streptomycin discovery; Schatz was awarded a substantial settlement, and, together with Waksman, Schatz was to be officially recognised as a co-discoverer of streptomycin as concerned patent rights. He is not a Nobel Prize laureate.[^cite_note-pharmj-70]&#x20;

The 1962 Prize awarded to [James D. Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_D._Watson), [Francis Crick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crick), and [Maurice Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Wilkins)—for their work on [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA) structure and properties—did not recognise contributing work from others, such as [Alec Stokes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alec_Stokes) and [Herbert Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Wilson). In addition, [Erwin Chargaff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Chargaff), [Oswald Avery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Avery), and [Rosalind Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosalind_Franklin) (whose key DNA [x-ray crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography) work was the most detailed yet least acknowledged among the three)[^cite_note-franklin-72] contributed directly to the ability of Watson and Crick to solve the structure of the DNA molecule. Avery died in 1955, Franklin died in 1958 and [posthumous nominations](21201.md#Posthumous_nominations) for the Nobel Prize are not permitted. Files of Nobel Prize nominations show Franklin was not nominated when she was alive.[^cite_note-Fredholm-73] As a result of Watson's misrepresentations of Franklin and her role in the discovery of the double helix in his book *[The Double Helix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Double_Helix)*, Franklin has come to be portrayed as a classic victim of sexism in science.[^cite_note-Holt-74][^cite_note-75] Chargaff, for his part, was not quiet about his exclusion from the prize, bitterly writing to other scientists about his disillusionment regarding the field of molecular biology.[^cite_note-nytimesnobel-76]&#x20;

The 2008 award went to [Harald zur Hausen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_zur_Hausen) in recognition of his discovery that [human papillomavirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_papillomavirus_infection) (HPV) can cause [cervical cancer](53338.md), and to [Françoise Barré-Sinoussi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7oise_Barr%C3%A9-Sinoussi) and [Luc Montagnier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luc_Montagnier) for discovering the [human immunodeficiency virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_immunodeficiency_virus) (HIV).[^cite_note-77] Whether [Robert Gallo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Gallo) or Luc Montagnier deserved more credit for the discovery of the virus that causes AIDS has been a matter of [considerable controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Gallo#HIV/AIDS_research). As it was, Gallo was left out and not awarded a prize.[^cite_note-science_nobel-78][^cite_note-79] Additionally, there was a scandal when it was learned that Harald zur Hausen was being investigated for having a financial interest in vaccines for the cervical cancer that HPV can cause. [AstraZeneca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AstraZeneca), which had a stake in two lucrative HPV vaccines could benefit financially from the prize, had agreed to sponsor Nobel Media and Nobel Web. According to Times Online, two senior figures in the selection process that chose zur Hausen also had strong links with AstraZeneca.[^cite_note-AstraZeneca-80]&#x20;

### Limits on number of awardees

The provision that restricts the maximum number of nominees to three for any one prize, introduced in 1968, has caused considerable controversy.[^cite_note-Levinovitz17-18][^cite_note-Levinovitz61-81] From the 1950s onward, there has been an increasing trend to award the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine to more than one person. There were 59 people who received the prize in the first 50 years of the last century, while 113 individuals received it between 1951 and 2000. This increase could be attributed to the rise of the international scientific community after World War II, resulting in more persons being responsible for the discovery, and nominated for, a particular prize. Also, current biomedical research is more often carried out by teams rather than by scientists working alone, making it unlikely that any one scientist, or even a few, is primarily responsible for a discovery;[^cite_note-bishop-20] this has meant that a prize nomination that would have to include more than three contributors is automatically excluded from consideration.[^cite_note-Levinovitz114-56] Also, deserving contributors may not be nominated at all because the restriction results in a cut-off point of three nominees per prize, leading to controversial exclusions.[^cite_note-Lindsten-16]&#x20;

## Years without awards

There have been nine years in which the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was not awarded (1915–1918, 1921, 1925, 1940–1942). Most of these occurred during either [World War I](4764461.md) (1914–1918) or [World War II](32927.md) (1939–1945).[^cite_note-prizefacts-55] In 1939, [Nazi Germany](21212.md) forbade [Gerhard Domagk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerhard_Domagk) from accepting his prize.[^cite_note-Levinovitz23-82] He was later able to receive the diploma and medal but not the money.[^cite_note-prizefacts-55][^cite_note-83]&#x20;
