# Prune a grapevine on a two-wire trellis

Bailey's rule underlies all grape pruning: fruit grows "in a few clusters near the base of the growing shoots of the season," which spring from last year's canes. Rockwell adds that an unpruned vine sets three or four times the bunches it can ripen. Winter pruning is therefore mostly thinning down to a few good canes. The Kniffin system trains them along two wires and needs almost no summer tying.

## What you need

- One- or two-year-old vines of a strong American variety such as Concord
- Sturdy posts of a rot-resistant wood (cedar, locust or oak), standing 6–6½ feet out of the ground
- No. 10 wire for the top run and No. 12 for the lower run, with staples
- Sharp one-hand pruning shears
- Soft twine for tying canes

## Steps

1. Plant strong-growing vines 8–10 feet apart in the row, in sunny, well-drained, rich soil. Shorten the roots by about a third and spread them out.
2. Cut the new vine back to two or three buds and let it grow freely the first summer.
3. In the first winter, remove all canes but the strongest one and cut that back to two or three buds. The vine looks no bigger, but its roots are established.
4. In the second season, train one strong cane up a stake. Build the trellis:
   - Set a post between every second vine and brace the end posts.
   - String the lower wire about 3½ feet up and the top wire about 5 feet 9 inches up.
5. When the cane is strong enough, usually in the second or third season, run it straight up as the trunk and tie it to each wire.
6. Prune each year between November and late February. Bailey stops about six weeks before the buds swell, because spring cuts "bleed" heavily.
7. Choose four canes, one going each way along each wire. Prefer canes that grew close to the trunk, which keeps the bearing wood near the head of the vine.
8. Cut every other cane away, including all the wood that fruited last summer.
9. Shorten the four canes. For strong varieties, Bailey leaves about ten buds on each upper cane and five on each lower one, about thirty buds in all.
10. Make each cut an inch or two beyond the last bud you keep, so the drying end does not harm it.
11. Leave a short stub with a bud or two near each kept cane where you can, to grow next year's replacement close to the trunk.
12. Before the buds swell in spring, stretch each cane out along its wire and tie it with one or two ties.
13. Let the new shoots hang down freely. In early summer, break off weak shoots from the base of the canes and any from the old trunk. Clip shoot tips only where they trail on the ground.

## From the source books

- Kniffin himself cut all four canes to about six buds; Bailey reports that later growers left the upper canes longer.
- Bailey puts a fair crop on a strong Concord at 30–60 clusters, or 12–15 pounds.
- Rockwell's simpler home method:
  - In the second winter, cut two arms back to eight to ten buds each, for a first crop of about forty bunches.
  - In later winters, keep only the three or four fruited canes nearest the trunk, each cut to eight to ten buds.
  - Each May, rub off any sprouts on the trunk.
- If the bunches ripen slowly and unevenly, Rockwell says the vine is overloaded and "being damaged for the following year"; cut off some bunches. On an arbor, he cuts the side shoots back to two or three buds each year.
- Rockwell protects bunches from black rot by tying a paper bag over each one once the bunches are well formed, which also speeds ripening.

## Safety and modern practice

- Trellis wire under tension can whip back when cut or tightened. Wear eye protection and gloves, and brace the end posts well before tensioning.
- Call your local utility-locating service before driving or digging posts.
- Avoid old creosote-soaked timbers as posts in a food garden.
- European wine grapes and many modern hybrids are often spur-pruned on permanent arms instead; check your variety's needs.
