# Cleft-graft an apple tree

Cleft grafting is the standard way to change an established apple, pear or plum tree to a better variety. A short twig of the wanted variety (the scion, or "cion" in the old books) is wedged into a split branch stub, where it grows once its growing layer knits with the stock's. Bailey says such scions "should begin to bear when three to four years old."

## What you need

- Dormant scion wood: last season's shoots from the variety you want, cut in late fall or winter
- Damp sand, moss or sawdust and a cool cellar or refrigerator to store the scions
- A fine-toothed pruning saw
- A sharp knife for shaping the scions
- A cleft-grafting knife: a heavy, curved splitting blade with a wedge on the other end. A sturdy chisel and a hardwood wedge will do.
- A wooden mallet or short club
- Grafting wax. Bailey's hand wax is 4 parts resin, 2 parts beeswax and 1 part tallow by weight.
- Tallow or grease for your hands if you apply wax by hand

## Steps

1. Collect scions while fully dormant, choosing well-formed shoots of the previous year with plump buds.
2. Keep the scions cool and slightly moist until spring so they stay dormant.
3. Graft in spring. Bailey's Nursery-Book says the best time is as the leaves are pushing out, and his Manual says just before the buds swell. Scions set very late make short growth that may die over winter.
4. Choose branches no more than about 1½ inches thick, spaced around the tree at roughly equal distances from the center.
5. Saw each branch off squarely and smoothly to make a stub.
6. Split the stub down the middle 1½–2 inches deep with the grafting knife.
7. Drive the wedge into the split and leave it there to hold the cleft open.
8. Cut each scion to three buds, with the lowest bud just above where the wedge will start.
9. Shape the base into a long, smooth wedge with one clean draw cut on each side. Leave the outer edge slightly thicker than the inner so the stub grips it.
10. Push a scion into each side of the cleft, down to the first bud.
11. Line up the scion's bark-and-wood boundary with the stub's. Tilt the top of the scion slightly outward so the two growing layers are sure to cross.
12. Pull out the wedge so the stub clamps both scions tightly. Bailey stresses that a tight fit is more important than perfect alignment.
13. Seal everything airtight with wax: both sides of the split, the end of the stub, around each scion, and the scion tips.
14. Through summer, rub off water sprouts that crowd the grafts.
15. Over the next few years, cut away the remaining old top gradually, a part each year, until the new variety has replaced it.

## From the source books

- Bailey prefers two scions per stub, which "double the chances of success" and heal the stub faster.
- Bailey says the old top should be changed over in three to five years, following the tree's existing shape:
  - Graft some small side branches as well, so the new head fills out.
  - Do not graft one horizontal branch directly above another.
  - Do not remove so much in the first year that the inner limbs sunburn.
- To make hand wax, Bailey melts the ingredients together, pours them into cold water, and pulls the wax until it "gets a grain."
- **Young trees:** For stocks one or two years old, Bailey uses the whip graft instead:
  - Cut both stock and scion diagonally for 1–2 inches and make a matching split in each.
  - Lock the two tongues together and bind them with five or six turns of waxed string.
  - Wax over the joint if it is above ground.

## Safety and modern practice

- Cut away from your hands, and keep the hand that holds the scion clear of the blade's path. A splitting knife struck with a mallet can glance off a hard stub.
- Work from a stable ladder on firm ground, with both feet planted before you strike.
- Melt wax gently in a double boiler outdoors, away from open flame. Resin, turpentine and the alcohol in "liquid" waxes are flammable, and hot wax burns.
- Ready-made grafting compound or grafting tape is a simpler modern alternative. Avoid coal tar and pitch dressings.
- Get scion wood only from healthy trees, so you do not carry virus or fire blight into the tree you are grafting.
