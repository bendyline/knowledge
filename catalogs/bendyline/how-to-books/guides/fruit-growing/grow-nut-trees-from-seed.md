# Grow nut trees from seed

Walnuts, butternuts, hickories, chestnuts and hazels grow readily from nuts. The two real enemies are drying out, especially for chestnuts, and rodents that dig up and eat the nuts. Fuller cautions that a seedling reproduces the species, not the parent tree: "there is nothing certain about seedling nut trees." Plant the best nuts you can find, and expect named varieties to need grafting.

## What you need

- Freshly fallen, sound nuts
- Screens or shallow boxes for sorting and curing
- For chestnuts, acorns and beechnuts: boxes or cans with drainage holes, plus moist sharp sand or damp peat moss
- Galvanized hardware cloth with ½-inch mesh, and rot-resistant boards for a seedbed frame
- Light, sandy or loamy soil, with leaf mold or very old manure
- A spade and a tamping stick

## Steps

1. Gather nuts as soon as they fall. Fuller spreads chestnuts in the shade and stirs them daily for two or three weeks.
2. Throw out shrunken nuts and any with grub holes; Fuller says they rot and spoil the rest.
3. Cure walnuts, butternuts and hickories until the hulls are mostly dry. Store them in burlap bags hung from a rope or wire in an unheated cellar, out of reach of mice. Husk filberts before drying them.
4. Never let chestnuts, beechnuts or acorns dry out. Layer them with damp material in a box or can with drainage holes:
   - Weschcke dampens peat, wrings it out and mixes in half as much dry peat.
   - Lay down 1½ inches of the peat, then a layer of nuts not touching each other, then an inch of peat, and so on.
   - Finish with 2 inches of peat on top.
5. Store the container at 32–40°F, cold but not frozen solid, and check it now and then.
6. Fuller's outdoor alternative uses moist sharp sand in a mouse-proof box. He buries the box on a dry knoll, or banks a foot of soil over it in the shade of a building.
7. Build a seedbed in a sunny, well-drained spot. Weschcke's is 4 by 12 feet:
   - Sink 2-foot-wide hardware cloth 18 inches into the ground around the edge to stop burrowing rodents.
   - Fit a wooden frame inside the wire.
   - Make a removable top frame, also screened, to keep out squirrels.
8. Fill the bed with good, weed-free garden soil. Keep any manure or leaf mold near the surface so the seedlings make side roots rather than one deep root.
9. In spring, plant shallowly. Weschcke says the soil over a nut should "never exceed the thickness of the nut," and leaves big walnuts slightly exposed. Fuller covers chestnuts about 2 inches deep in drills 2–3 feet apart.
10. Plant already-sprouted nuts with the sprout pointing down, barely covered.
11. Tamp the soil lightly to remove air pockets. Keep the bed weeded, and always put the cover back afterward.
12. After one or two seasons, dig the seedlings with care and keep their roots out of sun and wind. Cut long taproots back by about half to force side roots, then set the trees in nursery rows.
13. Move the trees to their permanent places two or three years later. Space them at least 40 feet apart, near the upper part of a slope rather than in a frost pocket.

## From the source books

- **Transplanting bare roots:** Weschcke soaks taproots for several hours, then coats them in clay slurry "the consistency of thick paint." He plants at nursery depth, tamps the soil very firm, and re-tamps it two or three times in the first summer.
- **Watering:** In drought, Weschcke digs a hole a foot from the trunk, fills it with water three times, about 5 gallons in all, and repeats weekly. He says "sprinkling with a hose does not suffice."
- **Pruning:** Fuller prunes walnuts in summer or early winter, never in spring when the sap is running, because the cuts bleed. He thinks no cultivated tree "requires less pruning than walnuts."

## Safety and modern practice

- Wear gloves when hulling black walnuts and butternuts; the hulls stain skin dark for days. Discard any nuts that are moldy inside.
- Black walnut roots release a compound (juglone) that stunts tomatoes, potatoes and many other plants. Site the trees well away from vegetable gardens.
- Use wire guards and fencing, not poison baits, against rodents.
