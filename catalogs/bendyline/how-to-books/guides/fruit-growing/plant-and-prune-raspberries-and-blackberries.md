# Plant and prune raspberries and blackberries

Raspberries and blackberries grow from perennial roots, but each cane lives only two summers. It grows the first year, fruits the second, then dies. Pruning comes down to three jobs: limit the number of new canes, shape them so they bear well, and remove the old ones after harvest. A well-kept patch can last ten years or more.

## What you need

- Dormant young plants (suckers or root-cutting plants)
- Well-rotted manure or compost
- A spade, a hoe and an iron rake
- Long-handled pruning shears and heavy gloves
- Stakes about 5 feet long and 2–3 inches thick, or posts with wire, and soft twine
- Straw, old hay or leaves for mulch

## Steps

1. Choose the site:
   - Raspberries, especially red ones, like moist, rather heavy soil and tolerate partial shade.
   - Blackberries want lighter, warm, well-drained ground in full sun.
2. Prepare the soil deeply, since the planting will stand for many years. Roe warns that plants set on hard subsoil "will suffer from every drought."
3. Plant while the plants are dormant. Roe prefers late October and November, before the buds that form next year's canes start growing and turn brittle. Early spring is safer in the far North.
4. Space red raspberries about 3 by 5 feet and black raspberries (blackcaps) 3 by 6–7 feet. Rockwell gives large blackberries up to 6 by 8 feet.
5. Set the plants firmly, an inch or so deeper than before on heavy soil and 2–3 inches deeper on light soil.
6. Cut each cane back to about 6 inches so the roots put their strength into new canes rather than a first-year crop.
7. If you plant in fall, put a shovelful of fine compost on each plant and mound soil over it for winter. Level the mound in spring.
8. Keep the ground level, clean and loose with shallow cultivation, or mulch it in hot weather.
9. Each spring, keep four to six strong new canes per plant. Roe says to treat all other suckers "as weeds" and cut them while small.
10. In early summer, pinch the soft tip off new blackcap canes at about 2–2½ feet and new blackberry canes at about 3 feet. This forces low side branches and makes stocky, self-supporting bushes.
11. As soon as harvest ends, cut every cane that has fruited down to the ground and remove it.
12. In late winter or early spring, before buds open, prune the side branches of blackcaps and blackberries back by one-third to one-half. Rockwell keeps blackberry side branches to no more than 18 inches.
13. Tie canes snugly to a stake along their whole length, or between two wires run on either side of the row.
14. Top-dress each plant with a shovelful or two of compost in late fall.

## From the source books

- **Red raspberries:** Roe's rule is to shorten them by about a third in November or March. He found canes winter-killed to 18 inches still gave "as many and far finer berries," only later. He suggests cutting late varieties back by half for bigger fruit and a longer season.
- **Winter protection:** North of New York City, Roe lays tender raspberries down for winter:
  - Throw a shovelful of soil beside each plant.
  - Bend all the canes the same way over that little mound, so they curve rather than snap.
  - Cover them with earth, and uncover them in early April once hard frosts are over.
  - Rockwell pins the tips with soil and adds rough mulch only when freezing weather arrives.
- **Feeding:** Roe warns that vigorous red kinds should not be over-fed, while weaker growers need plenty of manure. Rockwell says over-rich soil keeps blackberry canes from ripening their wood before winter.
- **Diseased canes:** Rockwell pulls up plants with orange rust at once and cuts out canes hollowed by borers.

## Safety and modern practice

- Wear thick gloves, long sleeves and eye protection. Canes whip back when cut.
- Rockwell burns diseased canes. Check local burn rules, or bag and dispose of them instead.
- Buy certified virus-free plants, and keep new plantings away from wild brambles, which carry orange rust and viruses.
- Modern fall-bearing (everbearing) raspberries fruit on first-year canes. They are often simply mowed to the ground in late winter.
