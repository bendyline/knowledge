# Plant a strawberry bed

A strawberry bed set out in spring bears a full crop the following June and will go on for a second year. Roe writes that "so much depends on a right start in life, even in a strawberry bed." Most failures come from careless planting, with crowns buried or roots bunched and left near the surface.

## What you need

- Young strawberry plants from a reliable grower
- Plenty of well-rotted stable manure. Kellogg's rate works out to just under a pound per square foot.
- A spade or garden fork, a rake and a trowel
- Water, plus old berry baskets, inverted flowerpots or shingles for shade
- Straw, marsh hay or leaves for winter mulch

## Steps

1. Choose any soil that grows good vegetables. Kellogg warns against freshly turned old timothy sod, which harbors white grubs.
2. If you can, dig or plow the bed the fall before and spread manure over it. Dig again in spring, as early as the ground can be worked.
3. Rake the soil fine and mixed to its full depth.
4. Plant in early spring; Kellogg's season runs from late March to mid-May.
5. If the plants arrive before you are ready, heel them in. Set them roots-down in a V-shaped trench about 8 inches deep, out of sun and wind, cover the roots but not the crowns, and keep the soil wet.
6. Plant only into moist, freshly stirred soil. Never plant when the ground is wet and sticky.
7. Lay out rows 2 feet apart with plants 1 foot apart in the row. Both Roe and Kellogg use this garden spacing.
8. Just before planting, trim about a third off the roots of spring plants. Roe says this sets off a quick growth of new feeding roots.
9. With a trowel, make a hole deep enough to take the roots straight down, spread like a fan.
10. Hold the plant in one hand. With the other, half-fill the hole with fine, rich soil and press it against the roots.
11. Fill the hole, then press down hard with your thumbs and fingers on both sides of the plant.
12. Finish with the crown, where the leaves start, exactly level with the surface. Roe's test is that you should not be able to pull the plant up by its leaves.
13. Keep the soil moist "night and day" until the plants are growing. In hot weather, shade them through the middle of the day.
14. Hoe or cultivate shallowly, 2–3 inches deep, every week or ten days all season, but never while the soil is wet.
15. Pinch off every blossom the first season, and remove runners as your growing system calls for.
16. In early winter, soon after the first hard freeze, cover plants and soil with about 3 inches of straw, marsh hay or leaves.
17. When the frost leaves the ground in spring, rake the mulch off the plants into the aisles, where it keeps the berries clean.

## From the source books

- **Training runners:** Kellogg grows plants in hills (15 inches apart in 30-inch rows, all runners removed) or in hedgerows, where each plant may root two or six runners.
- **Pollination:** Kellogg distinguishes "perfect" (bisexual) varieties from "pistillate" ones, which need pollen from a perfect variety. Kellogg plants three or four pistillate rows between rows of a perfect kind.
- **Feeding:** Roe warns that many people "kill their plants with kindness," burning new plants with guano or fresh manure. He wants "coolness, moisture, and shade" instead.
- **Picking:** Kellogg picks with about an inch of stem left on, and never while plants are wet with dew or rain.
- **Renewing the bed:** Kellogg lets a bed fruit only two years. After the first crop he mows off the leaves, narrows the rows and top-dresses with manure. After the second he turns the bed under and plants something else for at least a year.
- **Everbearers:** Kellogg removes everbearers' blossoms only until late June or early July, then lets them fruit until frost.

## Safety and modern practice

- Kellogg's spray program uses arsenate of lead, which is toxic and long banned for food crops. Rely instead on certified disease-free plants, rotation, mulch and removing badly diseased plants.
- Most varieties sold today have perfect flowers, so pairing pistillate and perfect kinds is rarely needed. Check the catalog description.
- Rinse berries just before eating, especially where manure was used on the bed.
