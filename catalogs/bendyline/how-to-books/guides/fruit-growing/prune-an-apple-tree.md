# Prune an apple tree

Pruning lets light into the tree, keeps the fruit within reach, and removes dead and diseased wood before it spreads rot. Burritt warns that "haphazard cutting and sawing without a definite purpose" is worse than none. Decide on the shape you want and prune toward it a little every year.

## What you need

- One-hand pruning shears for twigs and young trees
- Two-handled loppers for heading back branch ends
- A sharp, rigid pruning saw with teeth on one edge only. Burritt's is no more than 30 inches long.
- A stable orchard ladder
- Gloves and eye protection

## Steps

1. Prune when the tree is dormant, ideally in March or April. Burritt notes that dormant pruning works anywhere from November to June, and that cuts heal fastest as growth begins in spring.
2. Where harsh winter winds dry the wood, wait until late winter.
3. Cut out dead, diseased or broken wood first, whatever the season. Cut well back into sound wood.
4. **On a young tree,** choose three to five main limbs spaced around the trunk to form the head, and remove the rest. Rockwell warns against two limbs leaving the trunk at directly opposite points, which makes a crotch that splits.
5. Avoid crotches where two branches of equal size fork with a deep split. Favor limbs that leave the trunk at a wide angle.
6. Set the lowest limbs between 1 and 4 feet from the ground. Burritt heads upright varieties at about 3–3½ feet and spreading ones at about 4 feet.
7. **On any tree,** remove branches that cross, rub or fill the same space as another.
8. Take out upright leaders in the middle to keep the center open to sun, which colors and ripens the fruit.
9. Cut back the tallest limbs so that all the fruit can be picked from a ladder.
10. When shortening a small branch, cut just above a bud. Choose an outward-facing bud on upright varieties to spread them, and an inward-facing bud on sprawling ones.
11. Remove a side branch completely at its "shoulder," where it joins the parent limb. Burritt explains that a cut behind the shoulder will not heal, and that a stub rots back into the heart of the tree.
12. Thin out some small branches near the ends of larger limbs. This also thins the coming crop.

## From the source books

- **Old, neglected trees:**
  - Burritt cuts high, sprawling limbs back 5–10 feet, each to a strong side branch. He spreads the work over at least two years, because heavy cutting at once weakens the tree and "causes an excessive growth of suckers."
  - The next spring, Burritt keeps a few well-placed suckers to refill the center and removes the rest. Rockwell likewise cuts suckers off close but saves the most promising ones to grow into new limbs.
- **Fruit thinning:** Burritt thins apples by hand just after the "June drop," leaving fruits 4–6 inches apart and never in clusters. He says the larger, evenly colored fruit repays the labor.
- **Other fruits:** Rockwell notes that apples need the most pruning. Pears and cherries need only their limb ends shortened and an occasional branch removed for light.
- **Winter versus summer:** Burritt explains that heavy winter pruning stimulates wood growth. He calls summer pruning "a last resort" for checking growth, because the tree may not mature its foliage afterward.

## Safety and modern practice

- Burritt painted large wounds with lead paint or gas tar. Do not use either one: lead is toxic, and current research finds that wound dressings do not prevent decay. A clean cut just outside the branch collar, the swollen ring at the base of a limb, seals best.
- To remove a heavy limb, first make an undercut a foot out from the trunk. Cut through from above just beyond it, then remove the stub at the collar so the bark does not tear.
- Set ladders on firm, level ground and keep three points of contact. Do not saw above shoulder height from a ladder.
- Leave limbs near power lines, and large limbs over buildings, to a certified arborist.
- Burritt and Rockwell also recommend arsenical and lime-sulfur sprays. Those are outside this guide; clean pruning and good sanitation do much of the same work.
