# Plant a bare-root fruit tree

Young apple, pear, plum and peach trees are usually sold dormant with bare roots. Rockwell says a well-planted tree "will give more fruit in the first ten years than three trees carelessly put in." The essentials are keeping the roots from drying out, spreading them in loose, rich soil, firming them in hard, and pruning the top to match the roots lost in digging.

## What you need

- A healthy nursery tree. Burritt prefers a well-grown tree two years from the bud; a one-year whip at least 5 feet tall also works.
- A spade, and a sharp knife or pruning shears
- A barrowful of old, fine manure or compost for each hole
- Water and a pail
- A stake, with rye straw, raffia or strips of cloth for ties (never string or wire)
- Straw or coarse litter for mulch, and wire netting against rabbits

## Steps

1. Pick a slightly raised or sloping site with deep loam that drains perfectly. Rockwell calls good drainage "imperative."
2. Plant in spring in the North. Rockwell puts the line at Philadelphia and St. Louis, with fall planting usually better farther south.
3. If you cannot plant the day the tree arrives, heel it in. Dig a narrow trench in moist, shaded ground, lay the tree in at a 45-degree angle, and cover the roots with soil.
4. Dig a hole 3–6 feet across and 12–18 inches deep. Make it larger in hard soil.
5. Mix the manure thoroughly into the soil you dug out, then return most of it to the hole. Leave room in the center for the roots to spread without bending.
6. With a sharp knife, cut each broken or bruised root back cleanly past the damage. Shorten any long, straggling roots by a third to a half.
7. Set the tree straight. Rockwell plants it 1–3 inches deeper than it grew in the nursery; Bailey keeps the same depth unless the soil is loose and will settle.
8. Work fine topsoil in among the roots with your fingers. Bailey lifts the tree up and down slightly as he fills so soil settles under the crown and no air pockets remain.
9. If the ground is dry, pour in plenty of water when the hole is half full. Never water only on the surface, which crusts.
10. Fill the rest of the hole and firm it by standing on the soil all around the trunk.
11. Stake the tree if the trunk is crooked, tying it with soft material.
12. Cut back the top:
    - Head back the leader by about half.
    - On a branched tree, keep three to five well-spaced branches.
    - Shorten the season's growth on those branches by half to two-thirds, or to one or two buds each.
13. Mulch with straw or litter if a hot, dry spell is likely, but watch for mice nesting in it.
14. Keep grass and crops away from a circle around the tree, at least 2 feet out the first year and wider each year after. Stir the soil there through early summer.

## From the source books

- Burritt finds fall planting fine for hardy apples on well-drained soil once the buds have matured, after about early October near New York. He waits until spring to prune fall-planted trees.
- In cold districts, Rockwell leaves a mound of earth 6–12 inches high around fall-set trees and levels it in spring. Burritt banks every young tree with soil each fall against mice, and adds fine wire netting where rabbits are common.
- The books disagree about manure in the hole. Rockwell enriches each hole generously, while Bailey says manure there should be "of small amount and very thoroughly mixed with the earth," or it dries the soil.
- Rockwell spaces standard apples 30–45 feet apart, and Burritt averages 35–40 feet. For small yards, Rockwell suggests dwarf trees, which bear two or three years sooner.

## Safety and modern practice

- On grafted trees, especially dwarfs, keep the graft union, the knob low on the trunk, 2 inches or more above the final soil line. If it is buried, the top variety can root and lose its dwarfing rootstock. This is the main exception to Rockwell's "plant deeper" advice.
- Call your local utility-locating service before digging large holes.
- Use plastic spiral guards or hardware-cloth collars rather than poison baits to protect trunks from mice and rabbits.
