# Fish a trout stream with worms

A small worm drifted naturally down a clear stream is one of the most dependable ways to catch trout. Done well, it takes as much skill as fly fishing. You fish upstream so the trout, which face into the current, do not see you. You cast lightly into the shallow edges where feeding fish lie, and you keep the bait moving at the speed of the water.

## What you need

- A light rod of 10½ to 12 feet. Edmonds and Lee use their 10½-foot fly rod so they can switch between fly and worm.
- A fly reel and line, with a tapered leader about 3 yards long ending in a very fine tip
- A two-hook rig (the "Pennell"): two small hooks tied an inch apart on the leader. A single hook with the worm threaded over the shank also works.
- A few small split shot, used only when needed
- Small, pale pinkish worms about 2 inches long, well scoured (cleaned out), kept in damp moss
- Waders or wading shoes
- A fishing license

## Steps

1. Keep the worms in a can or bag of damp moss in a cool place. Edmonds and Lee note that well-scoured worms are much less likely to fly off the hook when you cast.
2. Bait the two-hook rig. Put the upper hook through the worm a quarter to half an inch below the head, give the worm one twist around the leader, and put the lower hook through the lower body.
3. Enter the water quietly at the very tail of a run. Stay low and wade slowly so you do not push a wave ahead of you.
4. Make your first cast straight upstream, 25 to 30 feet ahead and only 6 to 12 inches from the bank.
5. Cast with a slow, smooth swing rather than a flick, or the worm will tear off. As the worm comes within a foot of the target, push your arm forward and lower the rod tip so the worm drops in quietly.
6. Let the current carry the worm back toward you. Raise the rod tip slowly to stay in touch, but never fast enough to drag the bait.
7. Watch the line. When it stops, wait. When it starts to move away, set the hook with a quick turn of the wrist.
8. Cover the water in a fan pattern, each cast a little farther from the bank, then move a yard upstream and repeat.
9. Drop the worm into eddies behind tufts of grass, beside boulders, and along sunken logs. Fish the deep center channel last.
10. Add a small split shot about 18 inches above the hooks only when wind or very fast water keeps the worm from settling.
11. If bites are hard to see, grease the line so it floats, or tie a small tuft of oiled wool where the leader joins the line as a float you can watch.

## From the source books

- Edmonds and Lee keep the worm for the low, clear water of high summer, a span of eight to ten weeks starting around June 10, when trout stop rising to the fly. They find the best fish often feed in surprisingly thin water at the edges.
- Sears gives the opposite season. Until the young maple leaves are half grown, trout lie quietly in deep pools and take well-cleaned worms or white grubs better than flies. He rated white grubs the best bait he had tried.
- Sears honed his hooks to a perfect point and filed half the barb away. He said a well-sunk hook holds without a large barb.
- Sears also tells of finding big trout in a cold spring-hole in hot weather and scattering them with a bushy pole. When they came back, they took bait readily.

## Safety and modern practice

- Fishing requires a license almost everywhere. Some trout waters ban bait or are fly-only or artificial-lure-only, and seasons and limits vary, so read the local regulations.
- Trout often swallow worms deeply, which kills many released fish. To release fish, set the hook promptly and use barbless hooks. If a fish is hooked deep, cut the line close rather than pulling the hook.
- Keep released fish in the water, handle them with wet hands, and let them swim away on their own.
- Never dump leftover worms on the ground. Non-native earthworms damage forest soils, so put them in the trash.
- Clean, drain and dry boots and waders between waters to avoid spreading invasive species. Some states ban felt-soled waders for this reason.
- Wade carefully, use a staff in current, and stay out of fast or deep water.
