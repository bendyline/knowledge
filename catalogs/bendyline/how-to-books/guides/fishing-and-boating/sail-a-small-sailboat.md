# Sail a small sailboat

A sailboat can be steered to almost any point except straight into the wind. The sail is let out or pulled in (trimmed) with a rope called the sheet, and the boat is steered with the tiller. Learn in a boat with one sail, in a light, steady breeze, ideally with an experienced sailor aboard. Master each maneuver before trying the next.

## What you need

- A small boat with a single sail and a centerboard or keel
- Oars and oarlocks, a bailer, an anchor and plenty of line
- Neatly coiled lines that can run freely
- A life jacket for everyone aboard
- Knowing how to swim

## Steps

1. Before leaving, check that everything is aboard and that every rope is coiled clear so it can run without tangling.
2. Test the boat's balance in open water. With the sail set and the tiller let go, a well-balanced boat should swing up into the wind on its own and stop.
3. Hoist the sail with the sheet slack enough to let it swing, but not loose enough to let the boom fly out.
4. Begin with the wind coming over the side (a reach). Let the sail out until its front edge starts to flutter, then pull it in just enough to stop the flutter. Always keep it out as far as it will go without fluttering.
5. Keep the sheet in your hand. Never tie or cleat it while sailing, so you can let it go instantly in a gust.
6. To sail toward the wind, pull the sail in nearly flat and steer closer to the wind until the front of the sail just begins to lift, then bear away a little. A good boat sails at about 45 degrees to the wind.
7. To tack (turn through the wind), first ease off slightly to gain speed. Call "Ready about," then "Hard-a-lee," and push the tiller toward the sail. Everyone ducks the boom and moves to the new high side. Trim the sheet as the sail fills on the new side, ease it to build speed, then head up again.
8. To reach a point upwind, zigzag in tacks. Verrill recommends a long leg on the boat's better tack and a short leg on the other, rather than many short tacks.
9. Keep the centerboard down when sailing toward the wind, and raise it partly or fully when running before the wind.
10. To change sides when the wind is behind you, do not swing the stern through the wind (a jibe). Instead pull the sail in and turn up into the wind and around, which Verrill calls wearing.
11. If a jibe cannot be avoided, haul the sheet in fast. As the boom crosses the middle, let the sheet run out smoothly so the sail does not slam across.
12. Sit on the high (windward) side in a breeze to keep the boat upright, and trim it so the stern sits a little deeper than the bow.

## From the source books

- Verrill's first rule is "NEVER TAKE CHANCES." Shorten sail or head for shelter early rather than late, and ignore anyone who laughs at your caution.
- Verrill tells of three men who capsized miles from shore. Two tried to swim to a nearby schooner and drowned. The one who stayed on the overturned hull was picked up. A swamped boat, he says, will float and support people for hours.
- Day has the beginner sit low in the cockpit and watch the sail near the top of its front edge (the throat), which lifts first when you steer too close. He says experienced sailors steer by the jib or by the feel of the wind on their face.
- In rough water, Day says to ease the sheet and keep the boat moving, because every wave "is a hill that the boat has to climb over." He marks good sheet positions with bits of colored yarn.

## Safety and modern practice

- Wear a life jacket. Take lessons from a community sailing program or a certified instructor before sailing alone.
- Check the forecast. Avoid offshore winds, which can blow you away from land, and thunderstorms, and get off the water at the first sign of lightning.
- Look up before you launch or raise a mast. Aluminum masts touching overhead power lines at ramps and in boatyards have killed many sailors.
- If you capsize, stay with the boat. Cold water and fatigue are the main dangers, so dress for the water temperature.
- Learn the basic navigation rules, such as which boat gives way, and keep clear of large vessels in channels.
