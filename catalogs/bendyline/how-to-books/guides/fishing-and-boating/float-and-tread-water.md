# Float and tread water

Floating and treading water are the two skills that keep you alive in deep water. They let you rest, breathe and wait for help without swimming anywhere. The old teachers began with one idea: your body naturally floats. Panic, not weight, is what pulls most beginners under.

## What you need

- Calm water that deepens gradually over a firm bottom of gravel or smooth stones, with no weeds, holes or current
- A capable swimmer or lifeguard close by
- Optionally, a float aid that can be removed bit by bit as you gain confidence

## Steps

1. Check the place first. Know the depth, make sure the bottom is firm and free of weeds and holes, and stay where you can stand.
2. Build confidence the way Benjamin Franklin suggested (Walker quotes him). Wade in to chest depth, face the shore, and toss a sinking object (Franklin used an egg) between you and the shore. Dive for it with your eyes open, and notice how the water pushes you back up.
3. Practice an upright float. Lean your head back until your chin is higher than your forehead. Fill your chest, hollow your back and keep your hands under water. Spread your arms to the sides, with one leg forward and one back, for balance.
4. For a back float, stretch your arms above your head with the thumbs locked and your back arched. Lean slowly back until the backs of your head and hands rest in the water, then give a gentle push with your feet so your legs rise.
5. Keep your lungs well filled and breathe deeply and regularly through your mouth. Keep your head well back and your body limp.
6. If your legs sink, draw your heels up with your knees bent and spread apart. Stretching your arms out under the surface beyond your head also balances the legs.
7. If you start to roll, spread your arms wider or make a small stroke on the side that is rolling.
8. To stand up from a back float, bring your arms forward and scoop with your hands. At the same time pull your head and upper body forward, and your legs will drop to the bottom.
9. To tread water, stay upright and pump your legs up and down "like running up-stairs," while pressing your palms down against the surface.
10. Practice each skill in shallow water first, then near a helper in water just over your head.
11. Get out as soon as you feel tired, chilled or numb.

## From the source books

- Walker notes that the body is lighter than water overall, but the head is heavy. Beginners who throw their arms up and reach for the air push their heads under, which is why he stresses keeping the arms under water.
- Dalton and Sterrett both say people float more easily in salt water than in fresh, that women and stout people float more easily than thin men, and that floating may take long practice even for good swimmers.
- Walker gives two ways to tread water. One is a quick, short version of the breaststroke kick. The other is an alternating circular kick from a half-sitting position. He notes that both are useful when helping someone in trouble.
- For a cramp in the leg, Walker says to stretch the leg hard with the heel pushed down and toes pulled up, or to turn on your back and paddle ashore with your hands.
- Walker also repeats the old warning against swimming before digestion is finished.

## Safety and modern practice

- Never swim alone. Choose beaches and pools with lifeguards when you can, and take lessons from a qualified instructor such as the Red Cross or a YMCA.
- Enter unknown water feet first. Diving into shallow or unfamiliar water causes many spinal injuries.
- If a rip current carries you out, float and swim parallel to the shore until you are free of it, then angle back in.
- Sudden immersion in cold water makes you gasp and breathe fast. Float on your back for the first minute until your breathing calms down.
- Wear a life jacket on boats and in open water if you are not a strong swimmer. Watch children within arm's reach.
- The old rule against swimming after a meal is no longer stressed. Alcohol is a much greater drowning risk.
- To help someone in trouble, reach to them with a pole or throw them something that floats. Do not go in after them unless you are trained.
