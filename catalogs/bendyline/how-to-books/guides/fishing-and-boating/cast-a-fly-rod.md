# Cast a fly rod

In fly fishing you cast the line, not the fly. The weight of the line, flexing the springy rod, carries the light fly out. A basic overhead cast has two motions, a back cast and a forward cast, with a short pause between them. Learn it with a short line first. Distance comes on its own once timing and accuracy are right.

## What you need

- A fly rod and reel. Camp fits the reel underneath the rod with its handle on the right for a right-handed caster.
- A tapered fly line and a leader, softened by soaking if it is gut
- A fly
- Open water with nothing tall close behind you, ideally a gentle current

## Steps

1. Put the rod together with the reel underneath. Thread the line through the guides and tie on the leader and fly.
2. Pull out 15 to 20 feet of line. For your first practice, cast downstream, because the current helps straighten the line.
3. Grip the handle firmly with your thumb along the top. Keep your elbow low and let the wrist do most of the work, so the rod bends and springs.
4. Hold the slack line in your free hand at about waist height. Strip line in or let it slide out through your fingers as needed.
5. For the back cast, lift the line off the water quickly and neatly. Swing the rod smartly up to just past vertical, tilted slightly to the right. Throw the line high so it cannot touch the water behind you.
6. Do not carry the rod far back, because that loses control of the line.
7. Start the forward cast when you feel the line begin to pull back on the rod tip.
8. Bring the rod forward and down with increasing speed, and stop it when it is about level with the water. Do not start the forward cast too hard, because that whips and weakens the leader.
9. To land a fly softly, aim at a point two or three feet above the spot where you want it to fall.
10. Strip in any slack before the next back cast.
11. To fish a floating fly, use a sidearm cast instead, with the rod moving nearly parallel to the water and the back of your hand toward it. Camp says this lands the fly upright more often.
12. Cast dry flies upstream and let them drift back toward you as naturally as a real insect. Between casts, dry the fly with four or five gentle false casts in the air.

## From the source books

- Seth Green, writing in Orvis and Cheney's collection, gives three principles: "quick out of the water; second, give the line time to straighten behind you; third, throw." He said he threw seventy feet of line against a strong wind that way.
- Camp disagrees about the pause. He says instant photography had shown the line still has a loop behind the caster when the forward cast begins, and that you should start forward when the line first pulls on the tip.
- Sears kept his outfit simple: one serviceable four-ounce rod, a plain click reel, twenty yards of tapered waterproof line, a six-foot leader and two flies. He preferred dark flies in bright water and clear weather, and brighter flies toward evening.
- In a piece reprinted in the Orvis collection, Sears (writing as "Nessmuk") describes putting back every trout under eight inches and stopping once he had enough. That was an early form of catch-and-release.

## Safety and modern practice

- Wear glasses or sunglasses and a hat. A hook moving at casting speed can badly hurt an eye.
- Before every cast, look behind you for people, branches and overhead power lines. Carbon and graphite rods conduct electricity.
- Get a fishing license and learn the local seasons, size and bag limits, and any fly-only or catch-and-release rules.
- To release fish well, pinch hook barbs flat, play fish quickly, wet your hands before touching a fish, and keep it in the water while you unhook it.
- Wade with care. Shuffle your feet, use a wading staff in current, and do not wade deeper than your knees in fast water.
