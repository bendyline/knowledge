# Row a boat with two oars

Rowing a small boat with a pair of oars (sculling, in the old books) is mostly rhythm and balance. You face the stern, push your feet against the footrest, and use your body as well as your arms. Learn on smooth, sheltered water in calm weather. Walker suggests a boat that is not too light, because a heavier boat is steadier.

## What you need

- A rowboat with a seat across the middle and a footrest (stretcher)
- A matched pair of oars and oarlocks
- A boat hook, or an oar, to push off with
- A bow line for tying up
- A life jacket for each person aboard

## Steps

1. Push the boat off with the boat hook, heading the bow into the current or tide.
2. Sit in the middle of the seat so the boat floats level. Brace your feet on the middle of the footrest with your legs slightly bent.
3. Set the oars in the oarlocks with the hollow (concave) face of each blade toward the stern.
4. Reach: lean forward until your head is over your knees and stretch your arms toward the stern, so the blades swing toward the bow. Do not reach so far that the oars jam in the locks.
5. Catch: dip both blades in together, square to the water. Do not rush or jerk the start of the stroke.
6. Pull: bend your arms and lean back together. Keep the blades just covered, not buried deep.
7. Finish: bring your elbows to your hips and your hands toward your chest, with your body leaning back only as far as you can recover quickly.
8. Feather: as the blades leave the water, let your elbows sink and bend your wrists back so the blades turn flat.
9. Recover: swing the oars back with the blades 2 or 3 inches above calm water, or higher in waves. Turn them square again just before the next catch.
10. Keep your hands from knocking together by carrying one slightly ahead of or above the other. Hold each handle with your thumb capping the end so the oar stays seated in its lock.
11. Steer mostly by watching the stern and what you see past it out of the corners of your eyes. When you must look ahead, glance over your shoulder near the end of the recovery, just after your hands pass your knees.
12. To turn, push one oar backward through the water ("back water") while you pull hard on the other.
13. To land, point the bow into the current, lift the oars in and lay them in the boat with the blades forward. Step ashore with the bow line and tie up with two half hitches.

## From the source books

- Walker advises long strokes in a heavy boat and shorter, quicker strokes in a light one. Against a current, he says, keep near the bank where the current is weakest. With the current, a learner should keep to the middle.
- Walker's cure for knocking hands is to sit 3 or 4 inches to the right of center with your feet still centered. That puts the right hand slightly ahead. A London waterman told him that the hand he carried uppermost depended on which shoulder he was looking over toward the shore.
- Walker's old river rule: when two boats meet, the one going with the tide gives way. A boat passing another takes the outside.
- Guy Nickalls, writing on sculling in Lehmann's book, says balance comes first. He also says it makes little difference which hand goes over the other, so long as both blades enter and leave the water together.

## Safety and modern practice

- Wear a properly fitted life jacket. U.S. law requires a Coast Guard-approved one for each person aboard, and most people who drown in small-boat accidents were not wearing one.
- Do not overload the boat. Follow its capacity plate, keep weight low and centered, and do not stand up.
- Check the wind and weather, and dress for the water temperature, not the air. Cold water can disable a swimmer within minutes.
- If you capsize, stay with the boat. Most small boats float even when full of water.
- Keep clear of larger vessels, which cannot stop or turn quickly. Carry a white light if you may be out after dark, and learn the local boating rules.
