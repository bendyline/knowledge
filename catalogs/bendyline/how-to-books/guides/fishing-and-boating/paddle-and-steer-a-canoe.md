# Paddle and steer a canoe

A canoe tips easily if you sit high or move carelessly, but handled well it is steady. The keys are a low, balanced load, a kneeling position, short and lively strokes, and a stern paddler who corrects the course on every stroke instead of dragging the paddle like a rudder.

## What you need

- A canoe. Taylor recommends a 16-foot canvas-covered canoe with a 32-inch beam, about 60 pounds, for two people and 350 to 400 pounds of gear.
- Paddles: the usual rule is a paddle as long as you are tall. Taylor suggests about 5½ feet for the stern and 5 feet for the bow, shorter for smaller paddlers. Bring a spare.
- About 20 feet of soft rope tied at the bow and at the stern
- Kneeling pads
- A rope to lash the gear in
- Life jackets for everyone

## Steps

1. Load the heaviest bags low and in the middle so the canoe floats level. Keep everything below the gunwales, and when paddling alone, put enough weight forward to balance you in the stern.
2. Lash the gear to the thwarts with a rope run through the bag handles, so it stays with the canoe if you upset.
3. Kneel on the bottom with your knees spread and your back resting against a thwart. This is steadier than sitting and lets you use your back and legs.
4. Hold the top grip with one hand and the shaft with the other, a little above the blade.
5. Forward stroke: reach forward, dip the blade fully, and pull it back past your hip while keeping your body still. Put most of the effort into the first part of the stroke and finish smartly. Lift the blade out and swing it forward edgewise.
6. Use short, quick strokes. Taylor puts a good cruising pace at about 45 strokes a minute, against 26 to 30 for a lazy summer paddler.
7. Bow paddler: dip the blade a little out from the side rather than tight against the hull, so you do not push the bow off course. Watch for rocks and snags. To move the bow sideways, reach straight out and pull the water toward the canoe (a "draw").
8. Stern paddler: you steer. End each stroke with an outward push and a turn of the wrist to stop the bow swinging away from your paddling side. Do not trail the paddle behind as a rudder, except at slow speeds.
9. In rough water, keep kneeling and do not drive the canoe hard into the waves. Rather than paddling straight into heavy seas, take them at an angle on the quarter, with the stern paddler turning the bow into the biggest waves. The bow paddler should keep paddling and should not lean to dodge the spray.
10. Paddling alone, kneel near the middle with the load spread fore and aft, or sit backward on the bow seat so your weight is nearer the center.
11. To carry the canoe, lash two paddle blades across the middle thwart with the handles to the next thwart. Pad your shoulders with a sweater and carry it upside down.

## From the source books

- Taylor writes that more canoe upsets are caused by a bow paddler leaning or shifting than by a stern paddler's steering mistakes.
- Taylor says running rapids is only for experienced canoeists. The canoe must move faster than the current and stay level, with its bow kept into the current.
- White ties his paddle to the nearest thwart with a light line. He once had to swim more than a mile to catch an empty canoe blown along by the wind.
- Wallace's warning about lashing gear comes from experience. He and a companion upset in a rapid far from help, lost most of their food and outfit, and spent three weeks reaching a trading post in near-zero weather.

## Safety and modern practice

- Wear your life jacket, not just carry it. Most canoeists who drown were not wearing one.
- Keep your weight low and centered. Hold both gunwales when you step in and out, stepping into the middle.
- If you capsize, stay with the canoe, which will float, and work it toward shore. Cold water is dangerous, so dress for the water temperature.
- Scout or carry around rapids you are not trained for, and never run low-head dams. In current, avoid any line that could wrap around you.
- Old repair kits used white lead, which is toxic. Use a modern canoe repair kit instead.
- On big lakes, stay near shore and get off the water before afternoon winds build.
