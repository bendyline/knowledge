# Hammer a copper bowl

Copper is soft and tough enough to be beaten into a bowl from one flat sheet, with no seams or solder. The work alternates between hammering, which hardens the metal, and annealing, which softens it again. Charles Sleffel's beginner method uses a hollow in a hardwood block as the form.

## What you need

- A sheet of 20-gauge copper. Rose says 18 to 24 gauge suits most work, and copper is "the best material for the beginner" in raised work
- A hardwood block with a shallow round hollow carved in one face
- A round-headed hammer for shaping, and a planishing hammer with a polished, slightly broader face
- A round-top steel stake held in a bench vise, and a wooden mallet
- Dividers, metal shears, a smooth file and emery cloth
- A gas torch or burner, tongs, and a pail of cold water
- Powdered pumice and a woolen cloth

## Steps

1. Draw the bowl full size in profile. To find the disc size, lay a string along the drawn curve from the center of the base to the rim. That length is the radius of the disc.
2. Mark the circle on the copper with dividers, a little larger than the bowl's outline, and cut it out with the shears. Keep all marks light.
3. Anneal the disc. Heat it with the flame until it is red all over, then plunge it into cold water and dry it. Rose notes that sheet copper usually comes from the mill partly hardened.
4. Lay the disc over the hollow in the block. With the rounded end of the hammer, beat it down into the hollow, turning the disc steadily with your left hand as you strike.
5. Watch the edge. Wrinkles form as the rim gets smaller. Long, even wrinkles hammer out safely, but short, sharp ones can split the metal, so flatten them as you go.
6. Anneal again as soon as the copper feels hard and springy. Repeat the hammering and annealing until the bowl matches your drawing.
7. Clamp the round-top stake in the vise. Set the bowl over it and go over the whole surface with the planishing hammer, striking just hard enough to flatten the bumps. Done well, the surface is covered with "brilliant facets."
8. Mark an even rim height and trim the ragged edge with shears.
9. File the rim until it is perfectly true. Sleffel checks it upside down on a sheet of glass: if light shows under any part of the edge, keep filing.
10. Fold emery cloth over the edge and rub until the file marks disappear and the rim is slightly rounded.
11. To flatten the base, find the center with dividers, draw a circle about 2 inches across, and tap the bottom down with the mallet from the outside. If it dents inward, hammer it back from the inside.
12. Polish with a paste of powdered pumice and water rubbed on with a wet woolen cloth.

## From the source books

- Rose's more advanced method is "raising" over a steel anvil. You strike in rings from the base outward with a raising hammer, then planish with a polished hammer to remove irregularities. He warns not to stretch the metal more than necessary, "as the more it is stretched the thinner it becomes."
- To level the rim, Rose stands the bowl on a flat surface plate and scribes a line around it with a surface gauge.
- Rose lets annealed work cool gradually or quenches it in cold water, then dries it in sawdust.
- For shallow trays, Sleffel nails a copper square over a carved hollow and drives the center down with a rounded hardwood peg. A peg mars the surface less than a steel hammer.

## Safety and modern practice

- Wear safety glasses and hearing protection while hammering. Freshly cut copper edges are razor sharp, so file them before handling the disc much.
- Anneal on a fireproof brick or tray, away from anything flammable, with a fire extinguisher at hand. Hot copper looks the same as cold copper, so always pick it up with tongs.
- Rose cleans work in dilute sulfuric acid "pickle." A citric acid or commercial jewelers' pickle is far safer for home use. If you use any acid, wear goggles and gloves and always add acid to water, never water to acid.
- Do not use bare copper bowls for acidic foods, which can dissolve copper.
