# Weave a small round reed basket

Round reed, made from the pith of rattan, is the usual material for a first basket. A few stiff "spokes" are crossed to make the frame, and thinner reed is woven over and under them from the center out. Anna Gill's beginner basket, a little container for buttons, about 3 inches across the base, takes one sitting and teaches every basic step.

## What you need

From Gill's "Basket for Mother's Buttons":

- 8 spokes of No. 4 round reed, each 16 inches long
- 1 short spoke of No. 4 reed, 9 inches long
- No. 2 round reed for weavers. Gill uses two coils of tan and two of natural for bands of color
- 1 strand of raffia
- A bucket or tub of water, pruning shears or a sharp knife, flat-nose pliers, an awl and a ruler

## Steps

1. Soak the reed until it bends without cracking. Gill gives 10 minutes for No. 2 reed and 15 to 20 minutes for No. 4. Hot water works faster than cold.
2. Make a short slit through the center of 4 of the long spokes, keeping it no longer than needed.
3. Push the other 4 long spokes and the short spoke through the slits to form a cross. The short spoke makes an odd number of spoke ends, so plain over-and-under weaving alternates by itself on each round.
4. Wet the raffia. Weave it once around the groups of four: behind one group, over the next, and so on around the cross.
5. Weave two rounds over and under pairs of spokes, then begin taking each spoke singly so they spread evenly like the spokes of a wheel.
6. Tuck the end of a No. 2 weaver behind a spoke and weave over one spoke and under the next, round and round. When a weaver runs out, cross the old and new ends behind a spoke.
7. Continue until the base is 3 inches across, then weave two rows with the tan reed.
8. Wet the spokes well, press each one hard with the pliers at the edge of the base, and turn them all up.
9. Weave up the sides: seven rows of tan, twelve of natural, then nine of tan. Hold the spokes firmly and straight as you go.
10. Soak the spoke ends again. For Gill's "Open Border No. 2," take each spoke behind the next two spokes to the right and push it down into the weaving beside a spoke. Use the awl to open space where the weaving is tight.
11. Make the loops even. Turner checks them by turning the basket upside down on a flat surface so every loop touches.
12. Trim any ends inside the basket, always cutting on a slant so the reed does not split.

## From the source books

- Turner explains that the weaver's tension shapes the basket. Pulling the weaver snug draws the stakes together. For straight sides, use only enough tension to hold the stakes upright, then none at all. For a flaring basket, ease off in the first rows. For a curved shape, start with the stakes flaring and keep an even pull.
- Turner keeps stakes no more than 1/2 inch apart on bases under 4 inches across. For larger baskets he cuts short spokes for the base only and inserts a new stake beside each spoke, doubling their number.
- Gill's simplest border, "Open Border No. 1," takes each spoke behind just one neighbor. Her mats use the same center with 32 rows of weaving for a mat about 5 3/4 inches across.
- Gill lists other weaves for later baskets. Pairing uses two weavers crossed between spokes. The triple twist, or "wale," uses three weavers and is used to start sides and give borders and handles a strong foundation.
- Gill says reed can be dyed before or after weaving, and that the dyer needs "rubber gloves and large earthen pots."

## Safety and modern practice

- Cut away from your hand with knives and shears, and keep blades sharp so they do not slip.
- Soak only as much reed as you will use in an hour or so. Reed left wet for days can turn gray or mildew. Dry finished baskets in open air.
- If you stain or dye baskets, use products labeled for wicker or natural fibers, wear gloves, and work with ventilation.
