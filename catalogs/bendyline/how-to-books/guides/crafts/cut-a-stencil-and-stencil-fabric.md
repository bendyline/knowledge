# Cut a stencil and stencil fabric

A stencil is a sheet with a design cut out of it. Color brushed through the openings repeats the same crisp shape as often as you like. It is a quick way to decorate curtains, runners, pillow covers and bags, and, as the Beard sisters say, "you need not be an artist" to do it.

## What you need

- Heavy, smooth manila wrapping paper for the stencil, or bought stencil board
- Tissue paper and a very soft pencil for tracing
- Sharp scissors, or a sharp penknife for stencil board
- White shellac and a brush to seal the stencil
- Fabric. Cheesecloth, linen, crash, burlap and monk's cloth all work, and a closer weave is easier
- Blotting paper, a flat board and small pins or thumbtacks
- Stiff round or flat bristle brushes, one for each color, and a soft rag
- Color: oil paints thinned with turpentine for heavy fabric, or dyes dissolved in boiling water for washable cloth

## Steps

1. Choose or draw a simple, bold design. Separate the petals from one another and widen thin stems.
2. Wherever lines would join or run a long way, leave small "bridges" of uncut paper. These hold the stencil together. Short sections that seem to fit into one another carry the eye across the gaps.
3. Trace the design onto tissue paper with the soft pencil. Lay the tracing pencil side down on the manila paper and go over the lines to transfer them. Flip the tracing to make mirror-image repeats.
4. Cut out the parts of the design, staying just inside the lines. As the Beards put it, "a snip beyond will let in color in the wrong place."
5. Coat the stencil with shellac on both sides, including the cut edges, and let it dry completely before use.
6. Hem the fabric first, so the hem gives you a straight line to work from.
7. Lay blotting paper on the board and the fabric on top. Work out how many times the motif fits, leaving a small space between repeats, and mark the center of each with a pin.
8. Pin the stencil down flat. Put pins in the center of each flower, at petal and leaf points, in the bridges, and anywhere the paper tends to lift.
9. Mix the color. For oil paint, thin it with turpentine to a thick cream. For dye, dissolve about a teaspoonful in two tablespoons of boiling water, then add more water to the shade you want. Mix enough for the whole piece at once.
10. Dip the brush, then rub it on the rag until it is nearly dry. A wet brush blurs the outline.
11. Hold the brush upright and scrub the color into the openings, starting at the left. Use a separate brush for each color.
12. Pull the pins, move the stencil to the next mark, and repeat until the border is done.

## From the source books

- Archer's *Needlecraft* says stenciling is "more of a rubbing in one than painting." To make oil-paint stenciling washable, she steams it over a kettle or presses it with a wet cloth and hot iron. Watercolor or crayon work should not be treated this way.
- Archer notes that five tubes of paint (blue, yellow, red, black and white) will mix almost any shade. A matching second batch is very hard to mix, which is why you should make enough at the start.
- Kindergarten children made symmetrical stencils by folding paper and cutting half a design on the fold.
- The Beards stencil cheesecloth curtains in pink and green dye, adding single flowers scattered over the body with a small stencil. They wash stenciled curtains after soaking them in strong salt water, use pure white soap, and dry them in the shade.

## Safety and modern practice

- Shellac, turpentine and naphtha (which Archer uses to clean stencils) give off flammable vapors. Use them only with good ventilation and well away from flames. Water-based acrylic fabric paints and stencil brushes are a safer modern choice. Heat-set them as the label directs.
- Clear plastic stencil film is durable and lets you see the fabric beneath. Cut it with a craft knife on a cutting mat, keeping fingers behind the blade.
- Wear gloves when handling dyes, and keep dye cups away from food.
