# Build a coil pot by hand

Coil building raises a pot from ropes of clay laid one on another. It needs no wheel and suits larger pieces than pinching a single lump. Mary White calls it "the strong and simple Indian process," and both she and Charles Binns recommend a plain cylinder, such as a flower jar, as the first piece.

## What you need

- Pottery clay. White notes that ordinary modeling clay will not stand the heat of the kiln
- A taut wire for cutting clay, and a plaster slab or tile to build on
- A bowl of slip (clay thinned with water to thick cream), a small brush and a pitcher of water
- Stiff cardboard for a profile template
- A pointed modeling tool and, ideally, an oval steel scraper with one saw-toothed and one smooth edge
- A sponge, fine sandpaper, and access to a kiln

## Steps

1. Wedge the clay. Knead a lump of about ten pounds, cut it in half with the wire, and press the two outer surfaces together. Repeat until no air holes show in the cut faces.
2. Draw the outline of your jar full size on cardboard and cut it out as a template for the outside of the wall.
3. Pat out a flat round of clay about 5 inches across and 1/2 inch thick on the plaster tile. Mark a circle 2 3/4 inches across in its center.
4. Roll a coil on the table with the base of your palms, turning it fully with each push, into an even rope about 1 inch thick. Roll coils as needed so they do not dry out.
5. Crosshatch the marked circle with a tool and brush a little slip over it.
6. Flatten the coil slightly, cut one end to a long point and stand it on edge on the circle. Support it between thumb and middle finger and press it down with your forefinger, keeping the wall vertical.
7. When you get around, cut the end to a point that fits the start, and press the joint together with your thumbnail. Weld the coil to the base inside and out with short, firm strokes.
8. Brush slip on the top edge and add a second coil. Binns advises starting each new ring at a different place so the joints do not line up.
9. Let the piece stiffen for about half an hour, then smooth the inside of the bottom and walls while you can still reach.
10. Add two more coils, checking the shape against the template all around. Rest the piece again, and continue until the jar is as tall as the drawing.
11. Let it dry overnight. Scrape the surface with the saw-toothed tool to level the bumps, fill hollows with slip and clay of the same softness, and then smooth with the plain edge until the walls are about 1/4 inch thick.
12. Polish the surface with a damp sponge and your fingers. True the rim by rubbing the jar upside down on a wet sheet of glass.
13. Turn the jar over, carve a shallow foot ring into the bottom and cut your mark. Let it dry completely and slowly, then have it fired.

## From the source books

- White warns never to add wet clay to a much drier piece. The wet clay shrinks as it dries and cracks the drier clay around it.
- White mends cracks in partly dry clay with slip and clay of matching softness. Cracks from cooling too fast after firing cannot be fixed: "the piece is spoiled." Clay that has once frozen is also useless.
- Binns smooths the coils together with as little water as possible. Water is tempting, but it "will inevitably make the work sloppy."
- Binns says a too-thick bottom will crack in drying. Make it thinner in the center than at the edges.
- White prefers two firings for a large jar: first unglazed (the "biscuit" firing), then again after glazing.

## Safety and modern practice

- Dry clay dust contains fine silica, which harms the lungs over time. Clean up with a damp sponge or mop rather than sweeping, keep food and drink out of the work area, and wear a well-fitted dust mask when sanding.
- Use only lead-free glazes that are labeled food-safe for anything that will hold food or drink. Older books sometimes describe lead glazes.
- Kilns reach well over 1,800°F. Have a community studio or experienced potter fire your work. Home kilns need proper wiring, clearance and ventilation.
