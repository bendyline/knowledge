# Darn a hole in a sock

Darning rebuilds a worn or holed patch of knitting by weaving a small piece of new fabric right into it. It is the standard repair for heels, toes and the balls of socks, and it works on sweaters and gloves too. You lay one set of threads across the hole, then weave a second set over and under them, like a tiny loom.

## What you need

- Darning cotton or yarn close in color and weight to the sock, used single or doubled depending on how fine the sock is
- A darning needle with a large eye
- Small sharp scissors
- The sock, turned wrong side out

## Steps

1. Darn as soon as a hole appears. Kinne and Cooley point out that small holes grow quickly and are far easier to mend.
2. Turn the sock wrong side out and neatly trim away ragged, loose bits around the hole.
3. Thread the needle without tying a knot. Start on the wrong side in sound fabric, about 1/4 inch to the right of the hole.
4. Work fine running stitches in rows that follow the knitted loops, running up and down the sock. Keep the rows close together.
5. Leave a tiny loop at every turn. Knitted fabric stretches, and darning thread shrinks in washing, so a tight turn would pucker the darn.
6. At the hole, pass the needle through a loop on the edge, carry the thread straight across to the loop opposite, and keep running stitches on the far side.
7. Shape the darn roughly like a diamond, so no single row of knitted loops takes all the strain. Include any thin, worn fabric around the hole, and cover close-together small holes with one darn.
8. When the hole is covered with lengthwise threads, start the crosswise rows a little below the hole, again with running stitches through sound fabric.
9. Across the hole, weave over one laid thread and under the next. On each following row, go over the threads you went under on the row before.
10. Continue until the weaving reaches a little above the hole, so the cross rows extend past the damage just as the first set did. Clip the thread close.

## From the source books

- Kerzman, in *Knitting*, calls this crosswise darning the usual method. She prefers darning that imitates the knitted stitch, because when well done it "cannot be told from the knitting, except on the wrong side." For that version you unravel the damaged area to two even rows of open loops above and below, cut it out, and fold the halves inside. Then you lay foundation threads across the gap and work loops over them to copy the knit stitch. She admits it is more tedious.
- Kinne and Cooley explain why socks are darned rather than patched: a cloth patch under a sock hole would be uncomfortable, so a woven piece is built into it instead. For fine work, or for sweaters and gloves, they say to catch every loop at the edge of the hole. For everyday sock darning you can skip some.
- The same lesson suggests overhanding small rips in sock seams on the wrong side, taking up only the very edges so no ridge forms. Narrow ladders (runs) can be overhanded together the same way.
- Klickmann's *Needlework Economies* recommends "preventive mending." Darn the toes and heels of children's stockings before they are first worn, using crochet silk or mercerized thread because it is less bulky than wool.
- Dillmont, writing about darning woven cloth, also insists on loops at each turn so the darn will not pull together when the thread shrinks.

## Safety and modern practice

- Keep darns flat and smooth on soles and heels, and avoid knots underfoot. Lumps can rub blisters, especially for anyone with reduced feeling in their feet.
- Match fiber to fiber where you can, such as wool or wool-nylon darning yarn on wool socks, so the patch shrinks and wears at the same rate as the sock.
- Store needles and scissors away from small children.
