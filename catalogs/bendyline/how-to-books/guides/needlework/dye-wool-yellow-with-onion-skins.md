# Dye wool yellow with onion skins

Onion skins are one of the easiest plant dyes: free, always at hand, and strongly yellow on wool mordanted with alum. Ethel Mairet explains that most plant dyes are "adjective": the fiber must first be treated with a mordant (from the French *mordre*, "to bite") that fixes the color, or the dye will be pale and fade.

## What you need

- Undyed wool yarn or fleece, weighed dry
- Alum, 1/4 lb per pound of wool
- Cream of tartar, 1 oz per pound of wool, which Mairet says brightens the final color
- A generous quantity of dry onion skins. Mairet gives no weight, so more skins give a deeper shade
- Clean soft water, ideally rainwater
- A large pot kept only for dyeing, a stirring stick, a strainer and a cloth bag
- Mild soap for washing the wool

## Steps

1. Scour the wool. Wash out all grease and oil in warm water no hotter than 140°F, with every bath at about the same temperature. Squeeze as little as possible so it does not felt. Mairet says scouring must be done "very thoroughly or the wool will not take the colour."
2. If your water is hard, boil it for 20 to 30 minutes first. Mairet says hard water dulls colors and prevents some from dyeing at all.
3. Dissolve the alum and cream of tartar in the pot of water.
4. When the water is warm, add the scoured wool. Bring it up to a boil slowly and keep it there for about one hour.
5. Take the pot off the heat and let the wool cool in the bath overnight.
6. Wring the wool out without rinsing it. Store it in a linen or cotton bag in a cool place for 4 or 5 days.
7. Wash the mordanted wool thoroughly before dyeing.
8. Boil the onion skins in water for about 30 minutes, then strain out the skins.
9. Put the wet, mordanted wool into the dye liquid and heat it for half an hour to an hour, stirring gently now and then.
10. Let the wool sit in the warm bath a while longer if you want a deeper color. Then wring it out and wash it.
11. Dry the wool in the shade.

## From the source books

- Mairet lists onion skins twice. Under yellow dyes, alum-mordanted wool boiled with the skins "gives a good yellow." Under brown dyes, she mordants with alum and dries the wool two or three times in between, which she says "makes the colour more durable." She then boils the cooled onion-skin liquid with the wool for 1/2 to 1 hour and keeps it warm for a while for a browner shade.
- Her method for heather tips gives a gentler choice that suits onion skins too. Pour the strained, boiling dye liquid over the mordanted wool, cover it closely and leave it until morning.
- She notes that "the longer time that can be given to each process, the more satisfactory will be the result." A mordant that only coats the surface gives uneven, fading color.
- Silk is mordanted like wool but at lower temperatures. Cotton and linen need a tannin treatment first.
- Mairet's book also uses iron (copperas), tin and chrome mordants. Iron darkens colors toward brown and must have its own pot, because even a trace dulls reds and yellows. She adds tin to make onion yellow more orange. **Do not use tin or chrome at home** (see below).

## Safety and modern practice

- Use the dye pot, spoons and strainer only for dyeing, never again for food. Work with good ventilation, wear rubber gloves and eye protection, and avoid breathing alum or other powders.
- Skip the tin (stannous chloride, often used with oxalic acid) and chrome (potassium dichromate) recipes in older dye books. Chrome is toxic and a known carcinogen, and tin salts and oxalic acid are corrosive. Alum with cream of tartar is the standard safer mordant.
- Mairet scours wool with ammonia and washing soda. A mild wool wash or pH-neutral detergent works and is gentler. If you do use ammonia, ventilate well and never mix it with bleach.
- Many modern dyers keep wool at a simmer rather than a hard boil to protect its softness. They also avoid sudden temperature changes and rough handling, which cause felting.
- Let baths cool before disposal, and check local rules for pouring spent mordant down the drain.
