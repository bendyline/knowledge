# Embroider a flower with basic stitches

A single flower uses most of the stitches a beginner needs. Grace Christie describes just such a flower: a back-stitch outline, petals filled with chain stitch, a center that is "a mass of French knots," and a stem in stem stitch.

## What you need

- Plain linen or cotton fabric, with a margin around the design
- Crewel wool or embroidery silk or floss in two or three colors
- A crewel (embroidery) needle large enough to pass through without forcing
- Tracing or tissue paper, a soft pencil, and sewing cotton for tacking
- Small sharp scissors, and optionally a small two-hoop frame

## Steps

1. Draw the flower, stem and leaves on tissue paper.
2. Transfer the design. On thin fabric, lay the fabric over the drawing and trace straight onto it. On opaque fabric, tack the tissue to the cloth with running stitches along every line, then tear the paper away so the stitched outline remains.
3. Thread a short needleful, since long lengths wear out before they are used. Start without a knot by running a few small stitches on the right side where later stitching will cover them.
4. Hold the fabric curved over the fingers of your left hand so the thread lies looser than the ground. This prevents puckering.
5. **Stem:** work stem stitch from the bottom up. Take a long stitch forward on the surface and a shorter one back underneath, always keeping the thread on the same side of the needle.
6. **Petal outlines:** use back stitch. Bring the needle up 1/16 inch from the end of the line, go down at the very start, then come up 1/16 inch beyond where the thread first emerged.
7. **Petal filling:** work chain stitch in curved rows from the outside inward. Bring the needle up and hold the thread down to the left with your thumb. Go back in exactly where it came out and bring the needle up 1/16 inch farther along, over the held loop. Do not pull tight.
8. For small petals or leaves, use satin stitch instead: straight stitches laid close and parallel, usually on a slant.
9. **Center:** bring the needle up where a French knot should sit and wind the thread once or twice around it. Put the point back in right beside where it came up, holding the thread with your left thumb as you draw the needle through. Higgin says good knots "should look like beads."
10. Finish each thread on the front, as Higgin advises, under stitching or where later stitches will cover it, with no knots or loose ends on the back.
11. If the work has puckered, lay it right side up on damp blotting paper on a board. Pin the edges at short intervals while easing it back into shape, then cover it and weight it flat overnight.

## From the source books

- Higgin, writing for the Royal School of Art-Needlework, calls stem stitch the first stitch taught. For a leaf outline she starts at the stalk and goes up the right side with the needle left of the thread. Coming down the left side she reverses so the points of the stitches stay on the outside edge.
- Christie suggests keeping a sampler of favorite stitches for reference, and notes that lines running along a stem suggest growth better than lines across it.
- Christie thinks it best to draw the design straight onto the cloth with a brush. Failing that, she uses "pouncing": prick holes along a tracing, rub powdered charcoal through them, then paint over the line with light red oil paint thinned with turpentine. She calls carbon paper "not a good medium though it may be an expeditious one."
- For washing, Christie uses a mild special soap and dries the work pinned flat. If ironing is necessary, iron only on the wrong side.

## Safety and modern practice

- Modern water-soluble or heat-erasable fabric pens and printed transfer sheets replace pouncing and oil paint. If you use the historical paint method, work with ventilation and keep turpentine away from flames. Rags with oil paint on them can heat up on their own and catch fire, so dry them flat outdoors before throwing them away.
- Test any marking pen on a scrap first. Some heat-erasable inks reappear in cold or set permanently under an iron.
