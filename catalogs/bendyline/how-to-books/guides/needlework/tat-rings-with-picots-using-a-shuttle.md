# Tat rings with picots using a shuttle

Tatting is a lace of knots worked with a small shuttle. Its basic unit is a ring of "double stitches" with little loops called picots, and rings joined at their picots make edgings and insertions. Mrs. Beeton notes that it was valued for trimming underlinen "on account of its strength."

## What you need

- A tatting shuttle. Dillmont recommends one no more than about 7 cm (2 3/4 inches) long, with ends that close tightly and a hole in the center post for the thread
- Strongly twisted cotton thread. Dillmont found it makes "the best shaped knots and picots"
- A fine crochet hook or a pin for joining
- Small scissors and a sewing needle for finishing

## Steps

1. Wind thread onto the shuttle, but not too much, or the blades will gape at the ends and the thread will get soiled.
2. Hold the thread end between your left thumb and forefinger. Pass the thread around your spread middle fingers and back to the thumb to make a loop around the hand. Hold the shuttle in your right hand.
3. **First half of the double stitch:** pass the shuttle through the loop on your hand, between the fingers, and pull the shuttle thread out tight to the right. Then hold your right hand still. Spread the fingers of your left hand to tighten the loop and slide the new half-knot up against your thumb. The knot must form from the loop thread, not the shuttle thread.
4. **Second half:** pass the shuttle through the loop in the opposite direction, pull the shuttle thread taut again, and draw this half up beside the first. The two halves make one double stitch.
5. Test your work. The shuttle thread should slide freely through the stitches. If it will not, the shuttle thread has been knotted, and the ring cannot be closed.
6. **Picot:** before the next double stitch, leave a short gap of thread, about 1/6 inch as Beeton suggests. When you push the next stitch up against the others, the extra thread stands out as a small loop. Keep all the gaps the same length.
7. Follow Dillmont's simple ring: 4 double, picot, 3 double, picot, 2 double, picot, 2 double, picot, 3 double, picot, 4 double.
8. **Close the ring:** pinch the stitches between your left thumb and forefinger and pull the shuttle thread until the first stitch touches the last.
9. Leave enough bare thread that the rings will not overlap, then start the next ring.
10. **Join:** after the first 4 double stitches of the new ring, put the hook down through the last picot of the previous ring. Catch the loop thread from your hand and draw it through, pass the shuttle through that small loop, and pull it up snug like a stitch.
11. Finish the ring and repeat for the length you need. Fasten off by tying the threads and securing the ends with a few stitches on the wrong side.

## From the source books

- Dillmont says that when the shuttle has passed through the loop, the right hand "must stop with a sudden jerk" and hold the thread tight while the left hand draws up the knot. Beginners can rest the right hand on the table while practicing this.
- Dillmont counts the stitch that closes a picot as the first of the next group, so "1 picot, 3 knots" means three knots in all after the picot.
- *The Bath Tatting Book* explains the two halves as two single stitches: one with the thread over the back of the hand, shuttle pushed away, and one with the thread over the palm, shuttle pulled toward you. Working them in the opposite order gives a "reversed double," used to give work a consistent right and wrong side.
- A series of first halves only makes a twisted "Josephine picot," in Dillmont's terms.
- A second shuttle or a thread from the reel, which Beeton calls the "straight" thread, lets you make chains (half rings) between rings. Beeton credits Mlle. Riego with joining work as you go instead of sewing the parts together afterward.

## Safety and modern practice

- Today's tatters often call the double stitch the "double knot," and the key move of letting the stitch form from the hand thread the "flip" or "transfer." Patterns use abbreviations such as "ds" for double stitch and "p" for picot.
- Fine thread under tension can cut skin. Wrap the fingers that take the strain if they get sore, and rest your hands and eyes often during long sessions with fine work.
