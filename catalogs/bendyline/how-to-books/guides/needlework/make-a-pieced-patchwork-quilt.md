# Make a pieced patchwork quilt

A quilt has three layers: a decorative top, a filling (batting) and a plain back, held together by many lines of stitching. In a pieced quilt the top is sewn from small pieces joined into blocks. Marie Webster notes that only "the simplest form of sewing" is needed, but accurate cutting is essential.

## What you need

- Fabric for the top, all of one kind (all cotton, for example), as Klickmann advises
- Backing fabric the same size as the finished top, usually plain lightweight white cotton
- Filling: cotton batting (wadding) or wool. Use it thin for close, fancy quilting, or thicker for warmth with quilting lines spaced farther apart
- Sewing thread for piecing, and fine quilting thread. Webster gives "about number seventy" cotton for fine work
- Needles, pins, scissors, a ruler, pencil or chalk, and bias strips for binding
- A quilting frame, or a large table if you will baste the layers instead

## Steps

1. Decide the size. Klickmann gives 6 by 5 feet for a double bed, 5 by 4 feet for a single bed and 4 by 3 feet for a crib. A bedspread that hangs over the sides runs about 72 to 90 by 90 to 100 inches.
2. Choose colors that suit the room. Webster finds scrap quilts prettiest when the pieces are blended shades of one color.
3. Cut the pieces (squares, triangles, diamonds or hexagons) so that every piece of a shape is exactly the same size. Webster stresses that for a perfect star "all the seams must be the same width."
4. Sew the pieces together with a running stitch, making a narrow seam on the wrong side, to form blocks.
5. Join the finished blocks into rows and the rows into the top, again with narrow seams on the wrong side. Klickmann suggests making the quilt in sections and then uniting them.
6. Add a border in harmonizing colors if you like.
7. To use a frame, sew the edges of the backing to the cloth strips on the two side bars. Roll up the extra length and stretch it tight.
8. Spread the filling evenly over the backing. Lay the top over it, pin or baste its edges to the backing, and stretch the ends with muslin strips wrapped around the frame ends.
9. Mark the quilting lines. Use faint pencil on light fabric, or a chalked cord on dark fabric: stretch the cord tight just above the quilt and snap it. Diagonal lines are simplest. Plates and saucers make good guides for circles.
10. Quilt along the lines with running stitches that are as small and even as you can make them, going through all three layers. Keep one hand above the quilt and one below.
11. When you have quilted as far as you can reach, about 12 inches, roll the finished part onto the near bar. Unroll a fresh section from the far side, mark it and continue.
12. Take the quilt off the frame and bind the edges with a narrow bias strip, either white or a harmonizing color.

## From the source books

- Webster explains that diagonal quilting lines show off better, and the cloth is "less apt to tear or pull apart" than with lines run along its threads. Crossed lines give squares and diamonds. Shells, fans and "Ostrich Feather" designs are more advanced.
- For a very thick, warm cover that a needle cannot pass through evenly, Webster says to tie it instead of quilting. Draw threads through with a coarse needle and knot them tight at close, regular intervals. This tied quilt was called a "comfort."
- Klickmann describes a frameless method. She turns in the edges of each piece and "topsews" (whipstitches) them together on the wrong side. She then tacks the filling to the lining, turns in and stitches the outer edges, and quilts by machine or by hand in running stitch "with here and there a backstitch."
- Klickmann reuses old sheets or faded curtains for backing, and well-washed old wool underwear or flannel strips for filling.

## Safety and modern practice

- Wash and dry cotton fabrics before cutting so that shrinkage and loose dye come out first.
- Today most quilters cut with a rotary cutter, ruler and mat, and add a standard 1/4-inch seam allowance. Always close the cutter's blade guard and cut away from your body.
- Use a thimble when hand quilting through tightly stretched layers, and take breaks to rest your back, neck and hands. Webster herself notes the quilter's strained position.
