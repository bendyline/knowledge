# Sharpen a chisel or plane iron

Chisels and plane irons cut cleanly only when a flat back meets a straight, square bevel. Grinding shapes the bevel and is needed only occasionally; whetting on an oilstone makes the actual cutting edge and is repeated often. Griffith puts it plainly: "The grindstone does not sharpen tools; that is the work of the oilstone."

## What you need

- A grinding wheel or wet grindstone, kept true and never run dry
- An oilstone (a medium artificial stone, or a coarser Washita and finer Arkansas natural stone) with light oil; a slipstone for gouges
- A piece of leather fixed to a flat board, for stropping
- A try square, and sandpaper on a flat board to true a worn stone

## Steps

1. Grind only if the edge is nicked or the bevel has become rounded from repeated whetting; otherwise skip to step 5.
2. Grip the blade firmly with one hand close to the edge and use the other hand behind it to set the angle. With both thumbs on top, the tool twists and the corners dig in.
3. Grind the bevel flat at about 20 to 25 degrees, using a slightly lower angle for soft wood. Aim for a bevel about 3/16 to 1/4 inch long on a plane iron and 3/8 to 1/2 inch on a chisel. Keep the stone wet, move the tool from side to side across the face, and press lightly.
4. Check often that the edge is straight and square to the sides of the blade. Stop when the bright line along the edge has just disappeared; grinding further only raises a heavy wire edge.
5. Put enough oil on the stone to keep the working area wet. Lay the bevel flat on the stone, then raise the handle slightly until the oil is squeezed out from under the edge. This gives an angle just a little steeper than the grinding angle (Noyes shows 20 degrees ground and 25 degrees whetted).
6. Rub the tool back and forth, or in small circles, holding the angle steady. Any rocking rounds the bevel. Spread the strokes over the whole stone so it wears evenly.
7. Feel along the flat back for the wire edge (a thin, rough lip of steel). When it runs the full width, turn the tool over, hold the back perfectly flat on the stone, and rub it a few strokes. Never tip the back up to form a second bevel.
8. Alternate a stroke or two on the bevel and on the back until the wire edge breaks off.
9. Strop: draw the tool toward you over the leather several times on the bevel at the same angle, then once or twice with the back flat.
10. For a plane iron, press a little harder at the two corners during whetting so the edge is very slightly rounded at the ends; this keeps the corners from leaving ridges. A jack-plane iron used for rough work may be ground with a slightly curved edge.
11. Hold the edge toward a light. A dull edge shows as a white line; a sharp edge shows nothing.

## From the source books

- Zerbe warns that grinding narrow tools one after another grooves the stone; alternate chisels with plane irons and other wide tools.
- Zerbe and Noyes want the stone turning toward the edge, which holds the tool steadier and raises less wire edge. Noyes makes an exception for a stone that does not run true.
- Noyes explains that water on the grindstone carries away grit and keeps the steel from losing its temper, "which would show by its turning blue."
- Griffith sets the cap iron (chipbreaker) about 1/16 inch back from the edge on a smooth plane and 3/32 inch on a jack plane, with the blade projecting about the thickness of a sheet of drawing paper.
- For a gouge, Griffith rolls the tool as it moves along the stone, then removes the inside burr with a slipstone that matches the curve, held flat against the inside face.

## Safety and modern practice

- Wear safety glasses at any powered grinder. Keep the tool rest close to the wheel and the guards in place, and dip the steel in water often so it never turns blue.
- Do not test sharpness by drawing a thumb or finger along the edge, as the old books suggest. Shave end grain or slice paper instead.
- Waterstones, diamond plates and honing guides that hold a fixed angle do the same job as the oilstone. Use honing oil or water rather than kerosene.
- Oily rags can catch fire on their own. Lay them flat to dry outdoors or keep them in a closed metal can.
