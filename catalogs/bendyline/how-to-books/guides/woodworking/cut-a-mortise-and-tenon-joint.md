# Cut a mortise and tenon joint

The mortise and tenon joins two pieces meeting at right angles, such as table rails to legs or the rails and stiles of a door frame. A rectangular tongue (the tenon) on one piece fits a matching hole (the mortise) in the other. Fairham calls it, in its many forms, "the most important joint in the general woodworking trade."

## What you need

- Two pieces planed straight, square and to size
- Try square, marking knife and pencil
- Marking gauge or two-pin mortise gauge
- Tenon saw or backsaw (a rip saw for large tenons)
- Mortise chisel the width of the tenon, a paring chisel and a wooden mallet
- Brace and bit (optional, to bore out waste)
- Vise, and a block under the work while chopping
- Glue and clamps, or wedges or a 3/8-inch hardwood peg

## Steps

1. Plane the face side flat and mark it, then plane a face edge square to it and mark it. Gauge and plane to width and thickness, working every measurement from these marked faces.
2. Leave the mortised piece 1 to 3 inches long at each end (the "horn") so the end does not burst while you chop; saw it off after assembly. Leave a through tenon about 1/8 inch long for trimming flush.
3. Lay out the mortise length with a knife and try square, squaring the lines across the edge and around to the far edge. Mark matching pieces together in pairs so the lines agree.
4. Knife the tenon shoulder lines all the way around the rail.
5. Set the gauge to the chisel you will use and make the tenon one-third the thickness of the stock. That leaves the same amount of wood on each side of the mortise as in the tenon. Gauge both the mortise and the tenon from the face side.
6. Clamp the rail upright or angled in the vise and rip down the waste side of each cheek line, sawing diagonally from one side, then the other, then straight down to the shoulder. Split the line, so the tenon comes out as thick as the mortise is wide.
7. Pare a small V-shaped channel on the waste side of each knifed shoulder line. Set the saw in it and cut the shoulders square, stopping exactly at the cheek.
8. Clamp the mortised piece in the vise with a block underneath. Optionally bore out most of the waste with a bit just smaller than the mortise width.
9. Hold the mortise chisel upright, about 1/8 inch inside one end line, and drive it with the mallet. Step it along, flat face toward the end you are cutting toward, levering out chips. Take moderate blows; hard blows drive the chisel off true.
10. Cut about halfway through, turn the piece over, and finish from the other edge for a through mortise.
11. Clean out the chips, then pare the ends exactly to the knife lines. Keep the ends square and never undercut them, or the joint will show gaps.
12. Test the fit. The tenon should slide in snugly without being forced; a tight tenon bulges the mortise sides. Glue and clamp, or wedge or peg the joint. Saw off the horns and trim the tenon flush.

## From the source books

- Ross notes that a rounded, rather than square, mortise end lets the tenon squeeze the edges out of true, leaving gaps at both ends.
- Fairham's drawbore method needs no clamp. Bore a 3/8-inch hole through the mortise cheeks, insert the tenon and mark the hole on it, then bore the tenon about 1/8 inch nearer the shoulder. A hardwood pin driven through pulls the shoulders tight.
- For a stub (blind) tenon, Fairham sticks gummed paper on the chisel as a depth mark. For light cabinet work he cuts the mortise about seven-eighths of the way through, so the edge shows no joint.
- At a frame corner, Fairham narrows the tenon and leaves a short haunch, so the stile end stays strong and the faces stay flush.
- Fox wedging locks a stub tenon. Make two saw cuts about 3/16 inch from the edges, put small wedges in the kerfs, and clamp. The wedges spread the tenon in a slightly dovetailed mortise.

## Safety and modern practice

- Fairham seals outdoor joints with white lead mixed into varnish, or with thick paint. White lead is toxic and should not be used. Use a waterproof exterior wood glue (Type I or Type II) or epoxy instead, with pegs or wedges for strength.
- Keep both hands behind the chisel edge and clamp the work rather than holding it. Wear eye protection when chopping, because chips fly.
- A hollow-chisel mortiser does the same job by machine; clamp the work and keep fingers clear of the bit.
