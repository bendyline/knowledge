# Turn a spindle on a wood lathe

Spindle turning holds the wood between the headstock (drive) center and the tailstock center, for tool handles, chair rungs and table legs. Milton and Wohlers teach the cutting method: sharp gouges and skews shear the wood rather than scrape it, which is faster and easier on tools. Every shape starts from a true cylinder, so learn that first.

## What you need

- A wood lathe with a spur (drive) center and a cup or point tailstock center
- Square stock a little longer than the finished piece
- Large and small gouges (for example 1 1/4 inch and 1/2 inch), a large and a small skew chisel, and a 1/8-inch parting tool, all freshly sharpened
- Outside calipers, a 12-inch rule and a sharp pencil
- A wooden mallet; oil or wax

## Steps

1. Draw diagonals across both ends to find the centers. On very hard wood, saw shallow cuts along the diagonals so the spur can bite.
2. Remove the spur center and drive it into one end with the mallet; never hammer wood onto a center mounted in the spindle. Oil the other end, then push center and stock back into the spindle.
3. Bring the tailstock to within 1/2 to 1 inch of the work and clamp it. Wind the tailstock center in while turning the work by hand until it is seated firmly, then back it off just enough that the work turns freely. Lock it.
4. With the lathe stopped, set the tool rest about 1/8 inch from the farthest corner, at about center height. Never move the rest while square stock is spinning. Spin the work by hand to check clearance.
5. Rough it round with the large gouge, handle low and the tool rolled slightly to one side so it shears. Raise the handle until it cuts. Take the corners off in short sections working back from the tailstock end, then run the gouge along the length.
6. Set the calipers to the finished diameter. Scrape grooves about 1 inch apart with the small gouge, holding the calipers in the groove until they slip over.
7. Smooth with the large skew, held at about 60 degrees to the surface. Raise the handle until the edge cuts about 1/4 to 3/8 inch from the heel. Start 1 to 2 inches from an end and cut toward it, then cut the other way. Never start right at the end.
8. Take a final light pass until the sizing grooves just disappear, and check the cylinder with a straightedge.
9. Hold the rule on the rest and pencil the lengths, starting from the tailstock end, so the extra wood stays at the spur end.
10. Square each end with the toe of a small skew, or size down first with the parting tool to within 1/8 inch of the line. Finish with thin cuts of about 1/32 inch.
11. Cut the ends down until only a thin neck remains. Then, with your other hand cupped around the work to catch it, ease the skew's toe through at the spur end with light pressure. Trim off the tailstock waste.

## From the source books

- Milton and Wohlers had the right hand hold the very end of the handle, for leverage if the tool catches. The left hand guides from above, with the little finger and heel of the palm on the rest, and does not grip.
- They checked smoothness by laying a flat palm on the back of the work, opposite the rest, so the hand cannot be drawn in.
- Their speeds were 2400 to 3000 rpm for stock up to 3 inches, slower for larger work. They also said glued-up or off-center pieces should run slowly until true.
- On a taper they set the calipers 1/16 inch oversize so the finishing cut removes the caliper marks. A chisel handle is turned with its small (ferrule) end at the tailstock.
- They sharpened the skew on both sides, with each bevel about twice the blade's thickness.

## Safety and modern practice

- Wear a full face shield. Do not wear gloves, loose sleeves, ties or jewelry, and tie back long hair.
- The book's speeds are high for roughing square stock. Start at the lathe's lowest speed and raise it only once the work runs true and without vibration; follow the manufacturer's speed chart. Stand out of line with the work when you switch on.
- Swing the tool rest away before sanding, and wear a dust mask.
- A modern ball-bearing (live) tailstock center needs no oil. With a plain cup center, use wax and check the tension as the wood heats.
