# Cut a through dovetail joint

Through dovetails lock two boards together at a corner with interlocking, wedge-shaped pins and tails that show on both faces. They are the classic joint for boxes, chests and carcase corners. Once glued they cannot pull apart in the direction of the tails. The surest method is to cut one piece completely and mark the other directly from it, so any small error is copied and the two still fit.

## What you need

- Two boards planed to exact thickness, with the ends squared (a shooting board helps)
- Cutting gauge or marking gauge, try square and marking knife or awl
- Sliding bevel, or a template set to the dovetail slope
- Fine dovetail saw or backsaw
- Bevel-edged chisels and a mallet; brace and bit (optional)
- Glue and clamps; a scrap of pine or whitewood for a practice joint

## Steps

1. Square up both boards so their ends are true and the boards are exactly the same width where they meet.
2. Set the gauge to the thickness of the boards and scribe a line all around the end of each piece. A cutting gauge makes a line about 1/32 inch deep that severs the fibers. Tilt it slightly so it does not bite too hard.
3. Set the bevel to a slope of about 1 in 8. To find it, square a line from a straight edge, measure 8 inches along it, and step 1 inch sideways at the end; join the points.
4. On the end of the pin board, mark the pins with the bevel. Square the lines down both faces to the gauge line with a try square and knife.
5. Clamp the pin board upright and saw down to the gauge line on the waste side of each line.
6. Remove the waste between the pins. Start with a small notch about 1/8 inch from the gauge line, then set the chisel on the gauge line so it cannot drift back past it. Chop halfway through holding the chisel nearly upright, turn the board over, and finish from the other face. On hard wood, bore out some of the waste first.
7. Lay the tail board flat, stand the finished pin board on its end in exact position, and scribe around the pins with a knife or awl. Square the marks across the end.
8. Saw the tails, keeping the kerf on the waste side so you split the scribed line rather than remove it. Saw off the half-tail waste at the edges.
9. Chop out the waste between the tails the same way as in step 6, from both faces. Keep the chisel upright; an undercut joint is weak and holds glue poorly.
10. Test the fit. The joint should go together with light driving and be square on the inside. If it does, take it apart, glue it, and clamp it.
11. When the glue is dry, plane the projecting ends flush.

## From the source books

- Fairham holds that the slope should never be steeper than 1 in 6; too acute an angle weakens the joint. Ross's school exercise draws a much steeper 1 in 4 taper. Fairham suggests a metal template for anyone cutting dovetails often.
- Fairham describes cutting the tails first and marking the pins from them. You can saw several drawer sides at once this way. He also marks pins by drawing the saw through the kerfs, or by dusting chalk through them with a muslin "pounce-bag."
- He calls hammering over protruding pins "bishoping" and says it is unnecessary in properly fitted work.
- Foster says pine will do for practice joints, but whitewood is better. Because it is harder, a poorly fitted joint breaks instead of forcing together, so it shows your mistakes.
- For a box, Foster dovetails all four corners and glues them overnight in hand screws with even pressure so the box stays square. He then rabbets the top and bottom panels to fit, glues them on, and saws off the lid afterward.
- Ross explains that common (through) dovetails are stronger and quicker than lapped or mitered ones. Lap dovetails are used where end grain must not show, as on drawer fronts.

## Safety and modern practice

- Clamp the work for sawing and chopping, and keep both hands behind the chisel edge. Wear eye protection while chopping.
- Router dovetail jigs make the same joint quickly. Use hearing and eye protection, and a dust mask or collector for the fine dust.
- Modern PVA wood glue is a good choice for indoor boxes and furniture. Use a waterproof glue for anything that will get wet.
