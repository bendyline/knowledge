# Chip-carve a simple border

Chip carving decorates a flat wooden surface by cutting out small, clean, three-sided chips in repeated patterns. It needs only a sharp knife or a small chisel, so it makes a good first carving project. Foster's whole method rests on one stroke: every chip, straight-sided or curved, is a triangle removed with a few cuts. A border of triangles can be built into diamonds, frames, box lids and key racks.

## What you need

- A smooth, close-grained board: 1/4-inch basswood for small pieces (Foster), or pine, whitewood or cypress about 7/8 inch thick (Adams suggests 6 by 12 inches)
- A sharp chip-carving knife, or a 1/4-inch flat chisel used by its corner (Jack)
- A hard (H) pencil, ruler and paper; carbon or transfer paper
- Clamps, with card or scrap pads under the jaws
- A fine oilstone and slips, with light oil, for keeping the edge keen

## Steps

1. Draw the pattern on paper first. For a border, draw a row of equal triangles between two parallel lines. For a symmetrical design, draw only half or a quarter and repeat it.
2. Transfer it to the wood. Lay transfer paper face down under the drawing and go over the lines firmly with a hard pencil, then turn the drawing to complete the pattern. Keep the lines thin and clear.
3. Clamp the board to the corner of the bench, with card or thin wood between the clamp jaws and the face so they do not bruise it.
4. Hold the knife upright, edge facing away from you, with the point on the apex of the first triangle.
5. Press the point down and draw it along one side of the triangle, exactly on the line. The cut is deepest at the apex and shallower toward the base.
6. Return to the apex and make the same cut along the second side.
7. Cut along the third side (the base) to lift out the chip. You can cut toward or away from yourself; practice both, since the grain will force you to change direction.
8. Repeat the same three strokes on every triangle. Make all the downward cuts first in an area, then take out the chips, so each one comes away clean.
9. Once straight triangles go well, try two inverted rows to form a diamond border. Then try curved chips, which are still three-sided cuts with one or two sides gently curved.
10. If chips tear instead of cutting cleanly, stop and resharpen. Adams's test is a furrow across the grain of white pine that comes out smooth, "almost polished."

## From the source books

- Foster warns that triangles whose cuts run across the grain are a little harder than those parallel to it. Start with borders whose cuts run with the grain.
- Foster carves his pieces before assembling a box, leaving a 1/4-inch plain margin around carved panels for the brads. He plans the corner of a border first, because it is the hardest part of the design.
- Jack prefers a 1/4-inch carving chisel to the knife "usually sold for this kind of work," using its corner and sloping it to suit each hole. He favors very small, patiently repeated holes, from 1/16 to just under 1/4 inch long, over large showy geometric patterns.
- Adams outlines the design with a small V-tool, then cuts leaf shapes with two curved incisions and a central angled cut for the vein. He keeps carving tools in a rolled cloth case and the oilstone covered against grit.
- Adams starts beginners on soft, close-grained woods such as pine, poplar or cypress. He leaves oak, ash and chestnut until later because of their open grain.

## Safety and modern practice

- Most carving cuts happen with a hand in front of the blade. A cut-resistant glove on the hand that steadies the work, and a leather thumb guard on the knife hand, prevent the commonest injuries.
- Clamp the work rather than holding it in your lap, and keep the free hand behind the edge whenever you push a chisel.
- A dull knife slips and needs force; strop often. Modern ceramic or diamond hones and a leather strop with honing compound keep a chip-carving knife keen.
- Finish carved pieces with a wipe-on oil or wax in a ventilated room, and dry oily rags flat outdoors before you throw them away.
