# French polish a wood surface

French polishing builds a thin, clear, brilliant finish by rubbing shellac onto wood with a pad instead of brushing it. Noyes calls it "the finest of shellac finishes." It takes patience rather than special equipment, and because shellac redissolves in alcohol, worn polish can be repaired later. The whole art is in keeping the pad barely damp, moving it constantly, and building the coats up slowly.

## What you need

- Shellac dissolved in alcohol (Bitmead's home recipe: 12 ounces orange shellac with 1 ounce each benzoin and sandarach in 1/2 gallon spirit; plain shellac in alcohol also works). Use white (bleached) shellac for pale woods.
- Alcohol for thinning and spiriting off
- Raw linseed oil, a few drops at a time
- For the pad: a walnut-sized wad of cotton waste or old flannel, and squares of soft, lint-free washed cotton or linen
- Very fine sandpaper (No. 00) or fine steel wool; powdered pumice
- Grain filler for open-pored woods such as oak, ash or mahogany

## Steps

1. Sand the wood smooth along the grain, then stain it if you want a different color. On woods whose grain rises (ash, birch, oak), wipe with a damp sponge, let it dry, and sand again before polishing.
2. Rub filler into open pores, scrape and wipe off the surplus with rags, and let it dry hard.
3. Make the pad. Put polish on the wad, not enough to drip, and wrap it tightly in the cloth so the face is smooth and slightly rounded. Twist the cloth closed at the back. Press the pad on your palm to spread the moisture evenly, then touch a fingertip of oil to its face.
4. Pass the pad lightly a few times along the grain. Then work across the surface in small, overlapping circles, all in one direction, never stopping on the wood. Press lightly while the pad is wet and harder as it dries.
5. Recharge the pad with polish when it drags, adding only enough oil to keep it gliding. Move to a clean part of the cover cloth when the face turns shiny.
6. Continue until the surface has a full, even look and the pores are filled. Set the work aside for several hours, or overnight, to harden and sink in.
7. Sand lightly with the finest paper, dust off, and apply a second body coat the same way. Use a sprinkle of pumice with the pad on open-grained wood to help fill it. Let it harden for a day.
8. Apply further thin coats with less shellac and less oil each time, ending each session with straight strokes along the grain to remove the circular marks.
9. Spirit off. Make a fresh pad dampened only slightly with alcohol, add a trace of oil, and wipe quickly and lightly along the grain until the surface is clear, dry and bright. Too much alcohol will dissolve the polish and leave a dull spot.

## From the source books

- Bitmead warns that bearing down on a wet pad, or using too much oil, causes most failures. If the pad sticks even for an instant, it can pull the polish off to bare wood. Remove rubber marks with a half-dry pad rubbed against their direction.
- Bitmead lets at least a couple of hours pass before spiriting off, for a harder, more brilliant surface. He fixes polish that turns dull from excess oil by cleaning it with a slightly damp cloth and going over it again with equal parts polish and spirit.
- Noyes uses three separate pads, for shellac, oil and alcohol, and keeps them in stoppered jars between sessions.
- Milton and Wohlers polish turned work on the lathe at slow speed, using a pad about 1 1/2 inches across. A coat of boiled linseed oil, applied twelve hours earlier, brings out the grain of natural-finish work. All oil must be cleared off with the last coat, "or the piece will lose its polish."

## Safety and modern practice

- Bitmead's spirit was methylated spirit, and he notes that an earlier naphtha spirit damaged polishers' eyesight. Today's denatured alcohol often contains methanol, which is toxic to breathe and absorb. Work with good cross-ventilation, wear nitrile gloves and eye protection, and keep it away from flames, pilot lights and sparks.
- Rags and pads wet with linseed oil can heat up and catch fire on their own. Spread them flat outdoors to dry, or store them in a sealed metal can of water.
- Bought shellac flakes dissolved in fresh alcohol make a reliable polish; premixed shellac goes off with age. Dewaxed shellac is best when another finish will go on top.
- Wear a dust mask when sanding and when using pumice.
