# Air-dry lumber

Green wood is full of water. As it dries it shrinks, often unevenly, so boards used too soon warp, check and open up their joints. Seasoning makes wood lighter and stronger and keeps it stable once worked. Wagner calls slow outdoor drying in well-built piles "the most effective seasoning." Noyes calls it the most common and in some respects the best method. The aim is steady air circulation without sun or rain beating on the wood.

## What you need

- Green boards or planks, sawn close to the thickness you will finally use
- Straight, dry strips of uniform thickness to use as stickers (spacers)
- Solid supports (skids, blocks or timbers) to raise the pile a foot or two off the ground
- Boards or sheet material for a sloped roof, and weights to hold it down
- A shady, high, well-drained site where the wind moves freely

## Steps

1. Saw the wood green into pieces as thin as the work allows. Noyes says an inch board dries more than four times as fast as a four-inch plank and twenty times as fast as a ten-inch timber.
2. If you can choose when to cut, cut in autumn or early winter. Wagner notes that such wood checks less, because it dries more slowly through the cool months.
3. Strip off any bark, or the boards may be attacked by borers.
4. Choose high, dry ground. Clear weeds and plant growth from under and around the pile site.
5. Build a level foundation that holds the bottom layer a foot or two off the ground, so air passes beneath the pile.
6. Lay the first layer of boards flat, with gaps between them.
7. Place stickers across the boards close to each end and at regular intervals between, keeping each row of stickers directly above the row beneath. The weight of the pile then holds the boards flat and stops warping.
8. Continue layer by layer, stickering every layer. Keep separate piles far enough apart for air to circulate around them.
9. Put a roof of boards over the top, sloped so rain runs off, and weight it down. Shade the board ends from the sun.
10. Leave the pile alone to dry slowly in cool, moving air. Watch it most closely in spring: Wagner names the March winds and April showers as the worst time for checking.
11. Once dry, store the wood under cover with stickers between the boards, and never expose it to the weather again.

## From the source books

- Noyes reckoned good air-drying took "from two to six years, the longer the better." Air-dried wood still held 10 to 20 percent moisture, against about 5 percent straight out of a kiln. Hardwood lumber was air-dried for two to six months before going into a kiln.
- Wagner stresses that wind, not sunshine, does the drying. Direct sun on the pile only increases checking.
- Wagner found that checking is worse in hardwoods than softwoods, in sapwood than heartwood, and in thick stock than thin. Woods that check badly should be cut to the smallest practical size before drying.
- Freshly sawn hardwood piled closely in warm, damp weather invites wood-boring beetles. Wagner advises cutting heavy stock in winter and piling it so it is well dried by mid-March. Boards with bark left on should be piled bark side up, in loose, open piles.
- In the shop, Noyes stacks boards flat with sticks between them, never on edge. He clamps a warped pair hollow side to hollow side, and planes both faces of a board, never just one.

## Safety and modern practice

- Drying time depends on species, thickness and climate. Measure with a moisture meter rather than counting years. Wood dried outdoors levels off at about 12 to 15 percent moisture in much of the US. For indoor furniture, finish drying it indoors or in a kiln to roughly 6 to 9 percent.
- Today's practice is to coat the end grain of fresh boards with a wax-emulsion end sealer or paint. This slows end drying and greatly reduces end checks.
- Space stickers about 16 to 24 inches apart and line them up vertically.
- Build piles on firm, level ground and keep them low enough to be stable. Green lumber is very heavy, so lift with your legs, get help with long boards, and wear gloves against splinters.
