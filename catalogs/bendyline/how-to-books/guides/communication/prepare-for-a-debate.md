# Prepare for a debate

In a formal debate, two sides argue a stated proposition in timed speeches, usually before judges. Lyon's high-school manual says few debates are won on the platform: "They are won by thoughtful preparation." Foster's book for boys' clubs adds the practical arrangements and an insistence on fair play.

## What you need

- The exact wording of the proposition, such as "Resolved, That…"
- A pack of plain cards, about 2½ by 4 inches, or the digital equivalent
- Reliable sources on both sides of the question
- Teammates, an agreed schedule, someone to preside and (usually) three judges

## Steps

1. Meet the other side early to settle the arrangements: the wording and meaning of the question, the order and length of speeches, who presides and who judges. Foster says to be frank about what the question means and not to try to trap the other team.
2. Divide the work fairly within your team. Foster's rule is to "know a little something about all the case and all there is to know about your part of it."
3. Make sure you understand the question and its key terms before deciding anything else. Ask what the other side will admit, and what is not essential to the question.
4. Read on both sides, writing each reason for or against on its own card.
5. Sort the cards. Lyon says to stack under each main reason the smaller reasons that support it. You will usually end up with two or three piles. The cards on top are probably the issues, the few points that, once proved, settle the question. His motto: "Always think first, then read, then think again."
6. Test your evidence the way Foster says a lawyer tests a witness. Is the source honest or prejudiced? Is the statement consistent with known facts? Was it given willingly? Is the expert actually expert in this particular field?
7. Check how each argument hangs together. A generalization needs many cases behind it. For a claimed cause, make sure the link is "a causal relation and not a casual one." A comparison holds only if the conditions really are alike.
8. Prepare rebuttal cards. For each point the other side may make that your own speeches don't already answer, put your reply and its source on a card. During the debate, lay out the cards you need as they speak.
9. In each speech after the first, begin with a few sentences answering the opponent who has just spoken. State plainly what you are disproving, and quote your opponents accurately.
10. Keep pulling attention back to the issues instead of arguing minor details.
11. In the closing rebuttal, point out the opponents' main errors, then sum up your side's case issue by issue so the judges see it whole.

## From the source books

- Foster's sample schedule gives six seven-minute speeches, alternating affirmative and negative, then five-minute rebuttals by the first negative and first affirmative speakers. That is fifty-two minutes in all. He prefers short, snappy speeches to "ponderous proceedings."
- Foster's rule for spirit is to "be merciless to your opponent's argument" but to treat the opponent "frankly and generously," pursuing "the contention and not the contender."
- Lyon warns that sarcasm and insinuations of dishonesty are "two-edged" and hurt the speaker more than the opponent. He tells how Lincoln would first restate his opponent's argument at its strongest, then take it apart, so that its collapse looked complete.
- Lyon also says to know the other side as well as your own from the start, not as an afterthought. Many debates are lost by teams who built their own case first and only later wondered what their opponents would say.
- For judges, Foster wants three people with no prejudice about the speakers or the question, who decide "on the merits of the debate, not on the merits of the question."

## Safety and modern practice

- Today's school and league formats, such as policy, Lincoln-Douglas, public forum and parliamentary, set their own speech times and rules. Get the current rules from your league before you plan.
- Apply Foster's witness questions to online sources too. Check who wrote it, when, who funded it and whether other reliable sources agree, and record full citations so you can produce them if challenged.
- Keep the debate about ideas. Personal attacks are unfair and can lose the round.
