# Introduce people to each other

A good introduction does more than swap names. It gives two strangers a reason to start talking. The etiquette books of the early 1920s rest on one rule that still works: present the less senior person to the one you wish to honor, and make sure both names are heard.

## What you need

- Both people's names, pronounced correctly, with any titles they use (Doctor, Judge, Senator)
- One fact about each person that the other may find interesting
- A natural pause, so you are not breaking into a conversation

## Steps

1. Decide whom you are honoring. Post and Eichler both present the younger person to the older or more distinguished one. Eichler adds that guests are presented to a guest of honor, and that a host in his own home is named first.
2. Say the honored person's name first: "Mrs. Jones, may I present Mr. Smith?" Simpler forms are fine too, such as "Mrs. Jones, do you know Mr. Smith?" or just "Mr. White, Mr. Jones" between equals.
3. Pronounce both names slowly and clearly. Eichler calls mumbling over names "a very bad fault," because it leaves both people not knowing who the other is.
4. Add a short bridge to conversation. Eichler's example: "Mr. Rogers, let me present Mr. Brown, who is making a study of social science." A shared interest gives them somewhere to start.
5. Introduce relatives by relationship. Eichler suggests "Mother, this is Mr. Jones." Leave off a surname you share ("my sister, Mildred") but say it when it differs ("my sister, Mrs. Graham").
6. To bring someone into a group, wait for a lull and then name the newcomer followed by the others: "Mr. Jones—Miss Smith, Miss Roberts, Mr. Frank." Post warns against leading anyone around a room to be introduced "right and left." At a large gathering, introduce the newcomer to one or two people nearby.
7. Know when to hold back. Post says not to interrupt a lively conversation to introduce a third person, and not to introduce a guest who is arriving to one who is leaving. In public places, introduce people only when you are sure both will welcome it.
8. When you are the one introduced, rise if you are seated, smile and answer. Post says "How do you do?" is the only reply needed. Eichler recommends repeating the other person's name, which gives them a chance to correct you.
9. If you did not catch a name, ask right away: "I beg your pardon, I did not catch your name." Eichler says never to guess.
10. When you part from someone you have just met, Post's form is "Good-by, I am very glad to have met you."

## From the source books

- Post prefers "present" to "introduce" on formal occasions. She rules out "Mr. Smith, meet Mrs. Jones," "shake hands with" and "make you acquainted with." She also objects to calling one person "my friend," which implies the other is a stranger.
- In the short form "Mrs. Worldly, Mrs. Younger," Post marks who is honored by tone of voice. The first name gets a slight questioning rise; the second is said as a plain statement.
- Both writers describe drawing someone into a conversation without a formal introduction. Post's example: "Mr. Smith is suggesting that I dig up these cannas and put in delphiniums." Eichler calls this an "indirect introduction."
- At a dinner, Post says, people seated side by side may introduce themselves: "How do you do, Mrs. Jones. I am Arthur Robinson."
- Eichler introduces children simply: "Harry, this is John Brown. I am sure you will enjoy hearing all about his new pony."
- Much of the period detail has changed. Both books make sex the first rule: a man is always presented to a woman, and he waits for her to offer her hand. Today age, rank and the guest of honor decide the order, and either person may offer a hand. Post's view that "Pleased to meet you" is not good form is no longer followed. "Nice to meet you" is now standard.

## Safety and modern practice

- Not everyone shakes hands. Some people avoid it for health, religious or cultural reasons. Follow the other person's lead; a nod and a smile are always polite.
- Use the name, pronunciation and title people give for themselves. If you are unsure, ask privately beforehand.
- For email or online introductions, check that both people want to be connected before you share contact details. It is the modern version of Post's warning about unwelcome introductions.
