# Hold a good conversation

Good conversation is a partnership. Each person gets a turn, and each tries to interest the other. Writers on manners from the 1910s and 1920s give practical, often blunt advice that still holds up: think first, find the other person's interests, listen properly and leave out whatever would make the evening unpleasant.

## What you need

- A little general reading or news, so you have several topics to offer
- Genuine curiosity about the person in front of you
- The patience to let other people finish their sentences

## Steps

1. Stop and think before you speak. Post calls this "the first, last and only rule." Ask yourself whether what you are about to say will interest this particular listener.
2. Go "fishing" for a topic. Post compares the good talker to a fisherman who offers one subject, then another, until the other person "rises" to one. Kleiser quotes Matthew Arnold, who said his favorite topic was "That in which my companion is most interested."
3. Keep the turns even. A passage Kleiser quotes says conversation "is like lawn-tennis," needing quick returns as much as strong serves. Say your piece briefly, then hand the ball back with a question or a comment that invites a reply.
4. Listen with your whole attention. Conklin calls interruption the surest killer of conversation. If you catch yourself cutting in, stop, say "I beg your pardon," and let the other person finish.
5. Don't sit waiting for a pause so you can launch your own story, and don't finish other people's sentences for them. Conklin reminds you that your anecdote will keep.
6. Let small errors pass. Conklin mocks the "Gradgrinds" who halt a story to correct which street or which sister. Don't be your own Gradgrind by correcting trivial slips in your own stories either.
7. Disagree gently. Post suggests "No. I don't think I agree with you" or "It seems to me thus and so," and never "You are wrong!" If your views are completely opposed, change the subject.
8. Leave out unpleasant subjects. Post's list includes illnesses, operations, misfortunes, cruel remarks about people's looks, and endless praise of your own children. Before speaking of anyone, imagine them overhearing you.
9. Don't repeat yourself or stretch a story out. Post says nothing interests "when too long dwelt upon."
10. Admit what you don't know. Post: "Only the very small mind hesitates to say 'I don't know.'"
11. Be the listener people remember. Post gives the prize for the art of conversation to the one who looks glad to see you and gives "spontaneous and undivided attention."

## From the source books

- Post describes types to avoid. "Door slammers" answer every opening with a flat contradiction ("No, hate the theater"). The self-appointed lecturer is at home only on a platform. The tactless blunderer talks about the joys of dancing to someone who is lame.
- Kleiser says the best talker "is slow to express positive opinions, is sparing in criticism." He tells how Emerson found Thoreau's habit of contradicting every proposition "chilling to the social affections."
- Post warns that saying "What?" out of habit is careless. People who are hard of hearing, she notes, have good reason to ask for a repeat.
- Conklin treats the family dinner table as the training ground. Children should be welcomed into the talk on interesting subjects, rather than silenced or allowed to interrupt.
- Kleiser sees everyday conversation as the best practice for public speaking, since a good platform talk is "conversation enlarged and elevated."
- Some of Post's advice reflects 1920s high society, such as how a wife should refer to her husband in company, and her claim that American women make more effort in conversation than men. Those rules no longer apply. Her points about listening and tact still do.

## Safety and modern practice

- Put your phone away or face down. Checking it mid-conversation sends the same message as the "wandering eye" Conklin describes.
- When talking with someone who is hard of hearing, face them, speak clearly at a normal pace, and rephrase rather than simply repeat louder.
- Topics that are safe in one setting may not be in another. At work and with new acquaintances, approach politics, religion, money and health with care, and follow the other person's lead.
