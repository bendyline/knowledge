# Prepare and give a speech

A toast, a club talk or a work presentation succeeds or fails mostly in the preparation. Esenwein and Carnegie's 1915 course says: know your subject, organize it around a few main ideas, practice until delivery is habit, then think only of your message.

## What you need

- The facts of the occasion: audience, setting, other speakers and your exact time limit
- Index cards or slips of paper for gathering ideas and outlining
- A notebook for facts, examples and anecdotes as you come across them

## Steps

1. Find out what you are walking into. Their checklist asks: What is the occasion? How large an audience, and who are they? What do they already think of the subject? Who else speaks, and in what order? What is the room like, and is there a desk? "Precisely how much time am I to fill?"
2. Settle the subject by asking "What do I think?" rather than "What shall I say?"
3. Gather material, first-hand where you can; a fact you checked yourself outweighs any quotation. Note each useful idea on a card.
4. Write each big central idea on its own card. Arrange the cards so the talk rises in interest toward a climax, then file your facts, examples and anecdotes under the heads where they belong.
5. Budget your time. Give the most time to your central point, and don't spend "eight minutes of a ten-minute speech in merely getting up steam." In their words: "Let ten minutes mean neither nine nor eleven—though better nine than eleven."
6. If you write the speech out, write it quickly, then revise it as if already facing the audience.
7. Choose a delivery method. Esenwein and Carnegie recommend speaking from notes for beginners. Condense your outline to a few key words for the lectern, and mark the important points with underlines or circles so your eye finds them quickly. Avoid reading a manuscript aloud.
8. Work out your first few sentences completely, so you are never hunting for words at the start.
9. Practice aloud: "You must learn to speak by speaking."
10. When you are called up, don't rush. Pause, look the audience over, take a deep breath and begin in a quiet, conversational tone, "as though you were speaking to one large friend."
11. Don't apologize. Keep your attention on your message rather than on yourself; the authors say concentrating on the subject crowds out fear.
12. Skip the worn openings and closings Kleiser lists, such as "Unaccustomed as I am to public speaking," "I shall not detain you" and "I have already taken up too much time."
13. Stop when you have finished, and on time. "Blessed is the man that maketh short speeches, for he shall be invited to speak again."

## From the source books

- Esenwein and Carnegie describe four methods of delivery: reading from a manuscript, memorizing, speaking from notes, and extemporaneous speech, by which they mean fully prepared but not memorized word for word. They call extemporaneous speech the ideal, and say notes are "like crutches." They also describe a "joint" method: memorize the introduction, the conclusion and a key argument, and speak the rest freely.
- To cure stage fright, the authors use the image of a horse that stops shying at trains once it sees them often: "face an audience as frequently as you can." They note that Daniel Webster sat down in his first speech without finishing, and that Gladstone and Beecher were nervous speakers too.
- The title often comes last. Make it "fresh, short, suited to the subject, and likely to excite interest."
- The authors' card-and-envelope filing system for clippings belongs to its period; note-taking apps do that job now, and many speakers work from slides or a teleprompter. Their main point still holds: talk to the audience, don't read at them.

## Safety and modern practice

- Some nervousness is normal and usually fades once you begin. Arrive early to test the room, microphone and any slides.
- If you use slides, keep text short and large. Never read them aloud word for word, which is the modern form of the "read speech" the authors warn against.
- Face the audience and use the microphone even in a small room, so people with hearing loss can follow.
