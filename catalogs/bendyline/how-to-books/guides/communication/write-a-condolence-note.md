# Write a condolence note

A condolence note tells a grieving person that you know of their loss and that you care. Early twentieth-century etiquette writers disagree on some details. On the essentials they agree: write soon, keep it short, mean every word, and let sincerity count for more than polish.

## What you need

- Plain white note paper or a plain card, and an envelope
- A pen with dark ink (handwritten is warmer than typed)
- The correct name and address of the person you are writing to
- A few quiet minutes to think about the person who died and the person you are writing to

## Steps

1. Write as soon as you hear the news. Crowther says the letter "should be sent immediately upon receiving news of death."
2. Decide on the form. Crowther keeps a full letter for relatives, close friends and people you know well. For a mere acquaintance she suggests a short card saying "sincere sympathy."
3. Before you start, think about the person you are writing to and the one who has died. Post's single rule is: "Say what you truly feel. Say that and nothing else."
4. Open with a direct expression of sympathy. Crowther's model begins "I am sorely grieved to learn of the death of your husband." Today you might say simply "I was so sorry to hear about your husband."
5. Add one sincere personal line: what the person meant to you, a quality you admired or a small memory. Crowther's model note says of a friend's mother, "She was so wonderful to all of us." Post says even a trifling incident is worth recording, since many such letters together can "plant a whole memory-garden."
6. Offer help, and mean it. Eichler's model reads: "If there is anything I can do, I hope you will not hesitate to call upon me."
7. Stop there. A few sentences are enough. Close warmly, as the models do: "Affectionately yours" for a close friend, "Very sincerely yours" or "Cordially yours" otherwise. Eichler advises signing your full name unless you are an intimate friend.
8. Reread once to check that you have not dwelt on the illness or the manner of death, and then send it.
9. If you are the one bereaved, answer later with a short note of thanks. A relative or friend may write the replies for you, and a brief printed card will do for acquaintances. Crowther notes that if the writer calls on you in person, thanking them aloud is enough.

## From the source books

- Crowther and Eichler both warn against filling the letter with "sad quotations," "poetic quotations and sentimental expressions," or "mushy sentiment."
- Eichler says a condolence letter should be written only when the writer is "genuinely moved to sympathy." Letters that "refer constantly to the deceased are unkind."
- Post is the most forgiving: a condolence letter "may be abrupt, badly constructed, ungrammatical—never mind," because sincerity is all that counts. She advises against details of the illness and long quotations "from the poets and Scriptures."
- Post's hardest case is a death that came as a release after long illness. She suggests directing your sympathy to the years of illness rather than to the death itself: "Your sorrow during all these years—and now—is in my heart."
- Crowther reprints Lincoln's letter to Mrs. Bixby as a classic of the form.
- Some customs have faded. These books describe black-bordered acknowledgment cards, mourning stationery and the "customary call" on the family afterward. Post also cautions that an engraved thank-you card sent by a private person to a close friend is rude. A plain handwritten note of thanks is still the standard.

## Safety and modern practice

- A text, email or online comment is fine for a quick first word. A handwritten note or card is still the most valued way to send sympathy, and it can be kept.
- Avoid phrases that explain the loss away or compare it with your own, such as "everything happens for a reason" or "I know exactly how you feel." Keep the focus on the person grieving.
- Make offers of help concrete: a meal on a named day, a school pickup, help with paperwork. Then follow through.
- Today it is considered kind to write weeks or months later, or on an anniversary, when most messages have stopped.
