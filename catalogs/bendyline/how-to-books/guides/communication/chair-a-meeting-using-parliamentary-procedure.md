# Chair a meeting using parliamentary procedure

Parliamentary procedure lets a group reach decisions fairly. Foster's chapter for a boys' debating club sums up its "cardinal principles": equal rights for every member, full and free debate, rule by the majority, and only one thing considered at a time. The chair's job, in Cushing's classic manual, is to apply those rules and "represent and stand for the assembly."

## What you need

- The group's bylaws or rules of order, and its chosen parliamentary manual
- A written order of business (agenda) for the meeting
- A secretary to take minutes and read papers aloud
- The minutes of the last meeting and any reports that are due

## Steps

1. Prepare the order of business. Foster's model: call to order; minutes of the last meeting; communications; reports of officers; reports of committees; unfinished business; new business; program; adjournment.
2. Check for a quorum before taking the chair. Cushing says that unless a rule sets the number, a quorum is a majority of the members. If the count falls below a quorum during the meeting, the meeting must adjourn.
3. At the set time, take the chair and call the meeting to order.
4. Have the secretary read the minutes, then ask for corrections and approval. Cushing says minutes chiefly record what was done, not each member's speeches.
5. Announce each item of business in turn.
6. Recognize members before they speak. A member rises and addresses the chair, and speaks only after you say their name.
7. When a member says "I move that…," wait for "I second the motion." Then repeat the motion word for word, or ask for a long one in writing.
8. Open debate with "Are you ready for the question?" Give the maker of the motion the first chance to speak. Recognize members in turn, and let no one speak twice while others still wish to speak.
9. Deal with amendments before the main motion. Rule out of order an amendment on a different subject. Before each vote, read out the whole motion as it would stand if amended.
10. Handle the other motions as they come. Referral to a committee, postponement and laying on the table set the question aside. The "previous question" ends debate; it is not debatable and needs a two-thirds vote.
11. Put the question: "All in favor of the motion will say 'Aye'; all opposed, 'No.'" Announce the result: "The ayes have it, and the motion is carried." If any member doubts a voice vote, take a rising vote and count.
12. Keep order. Rule on points of order as they are raised. A member whose language is discourteous can be called to order. A ruling of the chair can be appealed, and a tie vote on the appeal sustains the chair.
13. When business is done, take a motion to adjourn. It is not debatable, and you then declare the meeting adjourned.

## From the source books

- Cushing lists the presiding officer's duties: open the sitting, announce business in order, receive and put all motions, keep members within the rules and keep order. Through all of it, the chair obeys "implicitly" the assembly's commands. He adds that the chair may read while seated but should stand to state a motion or put a question.
- Cushing sorts motions by the assembly's state of mind. A proposal it wants to kill gets the previous question or indefinite postponement. One it wants to delay is postponed or laid on the table. Good ideas in poor form go to a committee, and acceptable ones are amended.
- Foster suggests rewording a negatively phrased amendment as a positive one, so that "Aye" never means "don't do it."

## Safety and modern practice

- These manuals use "Mr. Chairman." Today "Madam Chair," "Mr. Chair" or simply "Chair" are usual.
- Rules differ in detail between manuals and over time. Most American groups now name a current edition of Robert's Rules of Order in their bylaws. Where your bylaws and a historical manual disagree, the bylaws govern.
- For online or hybrid meetings, check that your bylaws permit them. Agree in advance how members will seek recognition and how votes will be taken and recorded. Public bodies must also follow their state's open-meeting laws.
