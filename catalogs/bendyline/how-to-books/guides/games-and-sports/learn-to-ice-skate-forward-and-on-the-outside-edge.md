# Learn to ice skate forward and on the outside edge

Learning to skate comes down to balance over a curved blade. First you learn to glide forward. Then you learn the outside-edge curve, which Charlotte Oelschlager calls "the basis of all further progress in the art." As an old skating teacher quoted in Every Boy's Book put it, "Throw fear to the dogs."

## What you need

- Figure skates with a curved blade. Oelschlager says a flat-bladed hockey skate "is the right skate for hockey, but the wrong skate for anything else."
- Skating boots that fit snugly at the heel and over the instep and lace from near the toes. She prefers boots 7 to 8 inches high and suggests stiff leather for beginners.
- Thin wool socks, and close-fitting clothes that let you move freely.
- Smooth, safe ice. Every Boy's Book warns against both rough ice and ice that is too glassy.

## Steps

1. Put on your skates sitting down or kneeling on one knee. Lace firmly around the instep, but not so tight that it cuts off circulation or stops your toes from moving.
2. Stand up and shift your feet to check that the skates feel firm. Walk a few steps, holding a rail if you need support.
3. Set your posture. Keep your head up, your body upright and your skating knee bent. Hold your arms out from your body, not clamped to your sides or flung about, with the palms down. Don't bend sideways at the hips out of fear of falling.
4. Glide on the inside edge. Push off slowly with your right foot, leaning onto the inside edge with your weight under the ball of the big toe and your body tipped slightly forward. After about a yard, set the left foot down the same way. Every Boy's Book says to practice this "for some days."
5. Stop by bringing your feet together and slowing gradually. Every Boy's Book also describes stopping short by pressing down on the heels, as long as you don't throw your toes up too far.
6. Start the outside edge. Stand with your feet together and push off from the edge of your left blade, not its point. Bend your right knee deeply and lunge forward, leaning toward the center of the circle you plan to make.
7. Hold the first position. Carry the left leg behind, knee bent and toe turned out, with your right shoulder over your right foot. Curve the right arm in front of your chest, about six inches away, and stretch the left arm out behind.
8. About halfway around the circle, slowly bring the free foot forward close past the skating foot. Let your shoulders, then your hips, turn gradually, so that by the end you are facing the center of the circle.
9. Aim for a circle about eight feet across at first. Skate it on both feet, and practice more on whichever foot is weaker.
10. Rehearse the positions standing on your floor at home, as Oelschlager recommends, so your body knows them before you step onto the ice.

## From the source books

- Oelschlager's skates weighed four ounces, with blades curved to about a nine-foot radius. She notes that right-handed people are often better on the left foot.
- Every Boy's Book was written for strap-on skates. It tells beginners to use blades no deeper than ¾ inch and no wider than ¼ inch, and to steady their first steps with a light six-foot pole. Once the edges are learned, it goes on to the Dutch roll, the figure eight and the figure three.
- If you hit rotten ice, Every Boy's Book says not to stop but to cross it quickly, or to roll lengthwise toward firm ice if you fall. Its advice to drink brandy after a fall through the ice is outdated.

## Safety and modern practice

- On natural ice, many safety agencies advise at least 4 inches of clear, solid ice for skating, and more for groups. White or slushy ice and ice over moving water or springs are weaker. Supervised rinks are safest.
- Many learn-to-skate programs recommend helmets for beginners and children. Wear gloves to protect your hands from blades.
- If someone falls through, call emergency services and reach or throw them something rather than going out yourself. Treat cold-water victims for hypothermia and get medical care. Don't give them alcohol.
- Today beginners learn to stop with a snowplow: push one or both heels outward to scrape the ice.
