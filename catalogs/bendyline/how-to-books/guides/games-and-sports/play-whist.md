# Play whist

Whist is the classic partnership card game that bridge grew out of. It is still a good game for any four people who want something with more strategy than luck. All 52 cards are dealt, one suit is trumps, and two pairs of partners compete to win the most of the 13 tricks.

## What you need

- A standard 52-card deck. Cards rank from ace (high) down to two, but the ace counts as low when you cut. Foster notes that serious players use two packs, shuffling one while the other is dealt.
- Four players in two partnerships, with partners sitting opposite each other.
- Counters or a pencil and paper for keeping score.

## Steps

1. Draw cards to form partnerships. Under Hoyle's laws, the two players who draw the lowest cards play against the two highest, and the lowest card deals.
2. Shuffle, then have the player on the dealer's right cut the pack. Each part of the cut must have at least four cards.
3. Deal one card at a time, face down and clockwise, beginning on the dealer's left, until each player has 13. Turn the dealer's last card face up. Its suit is trumps for this hand. The dealer picks it up when it is his turn to play to the first trick.
4. The player on the dealer's left leads any card. Going clockwise, each other player must play a card of the same suit if he has one.
5. If you have no card of the suit led, you may either play a trump or throw away a card from another suit (a "discard").
6. The highest card of the suit led wins the trick, unless someone has played a trump. Then the highest trump wins. The winner of a trick leads to the next one.
7. Stack the tricks neatly so everyone can count them at a glance. A side's first six tricks are its "book" and score nothing. Each trick after that counts one point.
8. Play to an agreed total: 5, 7 or 10 points, Foster says. The English game is 5 points, played in rubbers (best of three games). It also counts "honours." If you and your partner were dealt three of the four top trumps (ace, king, queen, jack), you score 2 points; all four score 4.
9. When you lead, start from your longest suit. Hoyle says "the strongest suit is that consisting of the greatest number of cards." If a long suit has only one court card and no ace, lead its fourth-best card. From a three-card suit, lead the top card, unless that card is an ace, king or queen; then lead the lowest.
10. As second player, if the leader plays a card higher than a ten, usually play your lowest card (Foster). As the leader's partner, return your partner's suit before opening a new one, unless you are leading trumps.

## From the source books

- Foster states the object plainly: it is "to win tricks," not to show off how many cards you can remember. He advises beginners to lead trumps whenever they hold five or more, and never with three or fewer unless they are strong in every other suit.
- Failing to follow suit when you could have is called "revoking." Foster gives the penalty as two tricks under American laws and three under English laws.
- Foster notes that the American Whist League stopped each game at seven points and played without honours.
- Hoyle's rules of etiquette forbid giving your partner any hint "by word or gesture." He also warns against drawing your next card out of your hand before your partner has played, because that signals you hold the winner.
- Hoyle's maxims advise always giving your partner the chance to trump a suit he is out of. Never "dash out a card" after winning a trick without first looking at what was just played. When the game is safely in your hand, play the rest calmly instead of throwing your cards down.
- Hoyle's Games Modernized also describes Dummy, a version for three players in which one hand is laid face up on the table.

## Safety and modern practice

- These rules come from 19th- and early 20th-century club codes. Social whist games today are usually played to 5 or 7 points, often without honours. Agree on game length, honours and the revoke penalty before you deal.
- Whist is often played for small stakes. If money is involved, follow your local gambling laws and keep it friendly.
