# Play checkers

Checkers, called draughts in Britain, is a game for two people on an ordinary chessboard. You can learn the rules in a few minutes, but the game still takes skill to play well. Each side tries to capture all of the other side's pieces, or to hem them in so that they cannot move.

## What you need

- A board of 64 squares in two colors (a chessboard works). Only the 32 dark squares are used.
- 24 flat pieces called "men": 12 dark (black or red) and 12 light (white).
- Optional: a pencil and paper for recording moves. Checker books number the playing squares 1 to 32, starting from Black's side.

## Steps

1. Sit facing each other and turn the board so that each of you has a playing square in the near left-hand corner. Hoyle's laws say the bottom left-hand corner square "shall be black." The corner on your right, where two playing squares sit side by side, is the "double corner."
2. Put each side's 12 men on the dark squares of the three rows nearest that player. In Lasker's numbering, Black takes squares 1 to 12 and White takes 21 to 32.
3. Decide colors by lot. Black always moves first, and after that the players take turns.
4. On your turn, move one man a single square diagonally forward onto an empty dark square. Men never move backward.
5. Capture by jumping. If an enemy man is diagonally next to yours and the square beyond it is empty, jump over it and take it off the board. If the square you land on sets up another jump, keep jumping in the same turn.
6. When one of your men reaches the far row, your opponent crowns it by stacking a captured man on top. That piece is now a king, and it can move and capture both forward and backward. Lasker notes that a man that reaches the king row during a capture stops there and cannot keep capturing as a king on that move.
7. Play continues until one player has no men left or cannot make a legal move, and that player loses. If neither side can force a win, the game is a draw.
8. Play with a plan. Keep your men toward the middle of the board in groups that protect each other. Don't string them out along the edges, and don't split them into two separate wings. Leave your back-row men at home as long as you can, because they stop your opponent from getting kings.

## From the source books

- Older laws dealt with a missed capture by "huffing." Lasker, Hoyle's Games Modernized and Every Boy's Book all describe it: the opponent could remove the man that should have jumped, or could make the player take the move back and jump instead. Huffing did not count as a move.
- Hoyle's laws let "Time" be called after five minutes, and a player who still hadn't moved a minute later lost. Every Boy's Book adds that "Bystanders must make no remarks during the game."
- If one side seems stronger in a drawn-out game, Lasker and Hoyle both let the weaker player demand that the win be shown within 40 moves. Otherwise the game is a draw.
- Every Boy's Book also describes a "Polish game" on a 10-by-10 board, an ancestor of today's international draughts.
- Hoyle advises that men on side squares lose "one-half of their power." Once you are ahead in men, trade pieces to increase your lead. Against a weaker player, keep the game complicated; against a stronger one, simplify. He also says that "One man ahead with a clear game should be a certain win."
- Lasker thinks 11 to 15 is probably the best opening move. Hoyle names the traditional openings: the "Single Corner," for example, is 11 to 15 answered by 22 to 18. He adds that beating one king with two in the double corner baffles learners, yet "once known, nothing is more simple."

## Safety and modern practice

- Under modern tournament rules for English draughts and American checkers, jumping is compulsory and huffing has been dropped. A player who can capture must do so. In casual games, agree on house rules before you start.
- Most modern rules still use touch-move: if you touch a playable piece, you must move it.
- Checkers are small enough to choke on. Keep the pieces away from children under three.
