# Make a coin vanish with the French drop

The French drop (Hoffmann calls it *le tourniquet*) is the classic first coin vanish. You seem to take a coin from one hand with the other, but it never really leaves the first hand. Hoffmann calls it "an easy and yet most effective pass." It works with almost any coin and needs no special props.

## What you need

- A coin. Hoffmann practiced palming with a large British half-crown, florin or penny. A US quarter or half dollar works the same way. He notes the French drop also works with coins too small to palm.
- A wand or a pencil, to give the hand that is hiding the coin something natural to do.
- A few lines of patter worked out ahead of time.
- A place to rehearse in private.

## Steps

1. Learn the basic palm. Lay the coin on your open palm and close the hand very slightly so the palm muscles grip its edges. Practice moving the hand and fingers naturally while it is there. Keep the palm facing down or toward your body.
2. Check that the hiding hand looks normal. Sachs advises comparing it with your hand when it is empty. That relaxed, empty shape is the "model the conjuror must copy."
3. Show the coin held in your left hand, palm up, between the thumb and the tips of the fingers.
4. Bring your right hand over to "take" it. Your right thumb goes under the coin and your fingers go over it, closing as they pass, as if gripping it.
5. At the moment your right fingers hide the coin, let it fall quietly into the left palm.
6. Carry the closed right hand up and forward, and follow it with your eyes. Hoffmann says this draws the audience's attention away from the left hand.
7. Don't hurry the left hand. Turn its palm slightly toward you with the fingers a little bent, pause, then let it drop gently to your side. The hollow of the bent fingers holds the coin.
8. Pick up the wand with the hand holding the coin. Both authors say gripping it naturally hides the coin. Wave the wand over the right hand, then open that hand slowly to show it empty.
9. Get rid of the coin in a pocket, or "find" it again somewhere. Sachs likes to produce it from a spectator's hair, done "in an elegant and inoffensive manner."
10. Rehearse the moves and the words together many times before you perform. Hoffmann's first rule is "Never tell your audience beforehand what you are going to do."

## From the source books

- Hoffmann describes eight different "passes," or fake transfers from hand to hand, so you can vary your method. Pass 2, which nips the coin in the crook of the thumb, was said to be a favorite of the conjuror Frikell. He also warns never to perform the same trick twice in one evening, because the audience will know exactly where to look.
- Hoffmann notes that the hand-position drawings in his book show the performer's own view of his hands, not the audience's.
- Sachs describes four ways to hide a coin: the palm proper, the finger palm, the thumb palm and the "reverse palm." He found the florin the easiest coin for the regular palm because of its edge.
- For practice, Sachs suggests tossing a coin from hand to hand a few times and then only pretending to throw it. Snap the two middle fingers against the fleshy base of the thumb so it sounds as if the coin landed. He reminds learners that "the mind is what the conjuror has to deceive."
- Hoffmann says the conjuror must learn to use "his eyes and his tongue independently of the movement of his hands." Talking and looking are part of the trick.

## Safety and modern practice

- Coins are a choking hazard. Keep them away from small children, and never hold a coin in your mouth as part of a trick.
- Many magicians now rehearse in front of a mirror or film themselves to check the angles. Remember that a phone camera sees from only one point, just as one spectator does.
- If you borrow a coin from someone in the audience, give it back afterward.
