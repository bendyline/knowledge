# Do a forward roll, back roll and cartwheel

Ground tumbling needs no equipment except a mat, and it builds strength, coordination and confidence. Gwathmey points out that because the body stays close to the ground, the risk of injury is low. The forward roll is the foundation skill. The back roll and cartwheel come next, and then the two rolls can be combined.

## What you need

- Padded mats on a level floor. Gwathmey says that for adults the small mats "cannot be piled too high" to prevent jars. He also suggests a large mat about 15 feet long, 6 feet wide and 2 feet high, stuffed with straw or hay.
- Clear space, about the length of the mat, in front of and behind you.
- A teacher or partner to watch. Gwathmey requires a safety belt, or "lunger," for the first try at any partner stunt.

## Steps

1. Forward roll setup (Worth): stand with your heels together and toes turned out. Bend over and put your hands on the mat about a foot apart and about 1½ feet in front of your feet.
2. Tuck your head in toward your body so the back of your head, not the top, touches the mat between your hands.
3. Push off with your feet and roll over your rounded back. "Curl up," says Worth, and bring your feet well under your body so you come straight up onto them.
4. Finish the way Gwathmey describes: "Double up close, and finish standing erect on toes." Repeat until the roll is smooth.
5. Learn the sit-down that leads into the back roll. Stand with your feet about a foot apart, bend forward with straight legs until your fingertips nearly touch your toes, and sit back onto the mat without bending your knees.
6. Back roll: do the sit-down, but keep rolling back onto your shoulders. Use your hands to push yourself over and come up onto your feet.
7. Combine the rolls. Gwathmey has you cross your legs as you finish a forward roll so that you turn and flow straight into a back roll.
8. Cartwheel (Worth): stand up straight and put your left hand down hard about two feet from your left foot. Put your right hand down two feet beyond that, then your right foot two feet beyond the right hand, and then your left foot, all on one straight line, so your limbs move "like the spokes in a wheel."
9. Gwathmey adds to keep your head back and your chest out, and to spread your hands and feet as wide as you can. Practice leading with each side.
10. For each session, pick ten or twelve movements as a drill, as Gwathmey suggests. Change the order next time, and use a new drill for the third session.

## From the source books

- Gwathmey lists eleven versions of the forward roll: holding your toes, crossing your legs, keeping your arms out to the sides without touching the mat, rolling on one foot, and more. He says to repeat them all backward too.
- For the dive roll, a forward roll from a jump, he warns: "Never strike middle of back first." Start low and add height or distance gradually. Worth's longer "lion's leap" dive should be practiced only onto mats or "some soft place."
- Gwathmey's head stand forms a triangle with the hands and the head, which sits about 10 inches in front of them. He cautions: "Do not compete in holding head stands for time." For a handstand, he has beginners kick up against a wall with their hands about 1½ feet from it.
- Once you know a few moves, Gwathmey says you can tumble "on any lawn, sand-bar, or sawdust pile." He excuses pupils who suffer from dizziness.

## Safety and modern practice

- Never put your weight on the top of your head or your neck. Tuck your chin, roll across the back of your shoulders, and stop if your neck hurts. People with neck or back problems, osteoporosis or a recent injury, and anyone who is pregnant, should get medical advice before doing rolls.
- Use proper gymnastics mats on a firm, level floor, not loose piles of hay or furniture cushions. Warm up first, and clear away anything you could roll into.
- Learn head stands, handsprings and dives only from a qualified coach who can spot you.
- Dizziness after several rolls is common. Pause and stand still until it passes before trying anything else.
