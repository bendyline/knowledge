# Make and fly a two-stick kite

A two-stick kite takes only two light sticks, some cord and paper or cloth. Harris W. Moore's version flies with a tail, and Edwin W. Foster's bowed "Eddy" kite needs none. Moore notes that a kite's adjustments are "so subtle" that extra care in the workshop saves time outdoors.

## What you need

- Two straight-grained spruce sticks, each 3 feet × ⅜ inch × ¼ inch. Moore rips them from a ⅞-inch board. Foster says white pine also works.
- Glue, and strong thread, linen kite cord or fine soft wire for binding the joint. Foster says not to notch the sticks into each other or nail them, because that weakens or splits them.
- Cord that will not stretch, for the outline.
- Strong, light paper for the cover. Foster uses light wrapping paper for Eddy kites up to 3 feet, and thin cloth such as lawn or China silk for bigger ones.
- More cord for the bridle.
- For a tailed kite, strips of soft, light cloth such as cheesecloth or bunting, 3 inches wide.
- Flying line. Foster recommends linen kite twine; a ball of 600 yards will hold any 3-foot kite.
- A cloth or glove to protect your hand.

## Steps

1. Taper both ends of each stick to 3/16 inch thick. Saw a slot 3/16 inch deep into each end.
2. Mark a point 8 inches from the top of the spine. Glue the middle of the crosspiece there and bind it tightly, taking the last few turns around the others.
3. Square the frame. Measure from each end of the spine to each end of the crosspiece, and adjust until both halves match.
4. Run the non-stretch cord through the slots around all four ends and tie it tight. Wrap each end so the cord can't slip and the stick can't split. Measure again to make sure both sides are equal.
5. Cover the frame. Glue the paper to the sticks and fold a ½-inch margin over the cord. Reinforce the corners with 2-inch strips of paper. Foster stresses folding the same amount all the way around, because uneven weight makes a kite fly erratically.
6. Attach a bridle. Moore ties short loops at the top of the spine, on the crosspiece 10 inches out on each side, and on the spine 12 inches below the crosspiece. He gathers cords from all four loops into a single "flying-knot" about 2 inches above the crosspiece, standing out from the face of the kite, with every leg taut. Tie the flying line to this knot.
7. Add a tail. Moore says "A flat kite like this always needs a tail." Tie on cloth strips, and use a longer tail in high wind.
8. Or make it tailless. Bow the crosspiece back with a string from end to end until its bend is about 10 percent of its length. For an Eddy kite, Foster sets the crosspiece 14 to 18 percent of the way down the spine and makes the two sticks the same length. Moore covers his bowed kite with cloth.
9. Launch it. Let out about 100 yards of line, have a helper hold the kite up and let go on your signal, and take only a few steps backward. Foster says you only need to run when there isn't enough line out.
10. Fly it. If the kite is unsteady, bring it down and adjust the bridle or tail. If it dives, let the line go slack just before it hits so it lands softly. Protect your hand when line runs out fast.

## From the source books

- Moore says a tailless kite is handy where trees and houses crowd the field, but it has to be built more accurately.
- Foster flew three or four Eddy kites in tandem from one main line. He also hung a lantern on the line after dark. He attributed the way the lines spread apart to "electrical repulsion," which is period guesswork.
- Foster pivots his reel on a stake driven into the ground, after once watching his reel go "tearing across the fields."
- Moore has two people test the line about 100 feet at a time as it goes out, so it won't break when the kite is high.

## Safety and modern practice

- Never fly near power lines, roads or airports. Never use wire or metallic line. If a kite catches on a power line, let go and call the utility company. Don't fly in thunderstorms. Local rules may limit kite height.
- Don't hang lanterns or anything burning on a kite line, as Foster did.
- Avoid glass-coated or other cutting lines. Wear gloves when handling a strong pull.
- When tapering the sticks with a knife or plane, cut away from yourself and wear eye protection.
