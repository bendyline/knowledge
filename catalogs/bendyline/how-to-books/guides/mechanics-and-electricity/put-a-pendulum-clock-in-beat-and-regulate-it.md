# Put a pendulum clock in beat and regulate it

A pendulum clock that stops after being moved is very often simply "out of beat": its tick and tock are unequal, so the escapement can't keep the pendulum swinging. Once it runs steadily, you regulate it by changing the pendulum's effective length. Williams's *How It Works* states the principle: "the shorter a pendulum is the more quickly will it swing." These are adjustments you can make without opening the movement.

## What you need

- A firm, level shelf or a secure wall fixing
- A spirit level, and small wedges or shims
- A reliable time reference, such as a phone or radio-synchronized clock
- A notebook to record the error each day

## Steps

1. Set the clock up firmly. Goodrich says instability spoils timekeeping. A pendulum that swings its case never settles to a steady rate. He knew tall clocks that kept stopping until they were fixed to the wall.
2. Level the case and start the pendulum with a small push.
3. Listen. In beat, the ticks are evenly spaced. Out of beat they come unevenly, and in Goodrich's words the escapement "bangs up heavily on one side."
4. To correct a mantel or wall clock, Goodrich bends the crutch or verge wire, the wire that drives the pendulum rod, "the same way" as the heavy side. Bend it a little at a time and listen again after each bend.
5. If the clock has a degree scale under the pendulum, use it. Move the pendulum slowly by hand and note where a tooth escapes on each side. The clock is in beat when the escapes happen the same number of degrees left and right of zero.
6. Check that the pendulum rod moves freely in the crutch slot. Goodrich says the slot should let the clock tip a little forward or back without the rod binding. It should be open enough to be free, but not so loose that the crutch jumps. He blames binding here for more stops in freshly repaired clocks "than all other causes."
7. Once the clock runs steadily, set it to the correct time and note the time each day for three or four days.
8. If it gains, lower the bob by turning the rating nut down. If it loses, raise the bob. Lowering lengthens the pendulum and slows the clock; raising shortens it and speeds it up.
9. Make small changes. Goodrich's figures for a seconds pendulum (about 39 inches long): moving the bob 1/18 inch changes the rate by about one minute a day. On a rod with 18 threads to the inch, one full turn of the nut does the same. A short mantel-clock pendulum is far more sensitive, so turn its nut only a little at a time.
10. Keep the bob from twisting on the rod as you turn the nut, then restart the pendulum gently.
11. Repeat the daily checks and small corrections until the error is as small as you need.

## From the source books

- Goodrich puts a graduated scale on the bob or rating nut, so that each division equals a known number of seconds a day. A clock gaining twelve seconds a day can then be corrected "in one operation."
- His tables show that a seconds pendulum must be lengthened about 1.4 mm (just over 1/18 inch) to lose one minute a day.
- Williams explains why temperature matters. A steel rod lengthens in warm weather and the clock slows; in cold it shortens and the clock gains. Common clocks often used wooden rods, which hardly change length. Precision clocks used compensating pendulums.
- Goodrich also describes fine regulation by adding small weights to a cup on the pendulum. His version uses lead shot, which you should not handle.

## Safety and modern practice

- Don't let down or remove a mainspring, or take the movement apart, without proper tools and training. A wound spring stores enough energy to cut hands badly. Take valuable or antique clocks to a professional clock repairer.
- Some precision pendulums used jars of mercury as the bob. Never open or tip one; mercury vapor is toxic. Leave mercury pendulums to a specialist.
- Old luminous dials and hands may contain radium paint. Don't scrape or sand them.
- Never oil a clock with household or spray lubricants, which gum up the movement. Use proper clock oil, sparingly, or leave oiling to a repairer.
