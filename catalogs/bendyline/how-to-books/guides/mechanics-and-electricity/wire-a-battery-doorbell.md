# Wire a battery doorbell

A battery bell circuit suits a shed, workshop or gate and teaches the basics of circuits. Schneider's 1913 handbook shows how simple it is: a push button, a bell and a battery joined in a loop. Most failures, he says, come from loose or dirty connections.

## What you need

- A vibrating (trembling) electric bell or buzzer made for low voltage
- A push button
- Two 1.5-volt dry cells in a holder, connected in series (the + of one to the − of the next)
- Bell wire, No. 18 or No. 16 gauge; two colors make tracing easier
- Insulated staples, a screwdriver, wire strippers and insulating tape
- A soldering iron and lead-free rosin-core solder for any joints
- A multimeter for testing

## Steps

1. Plan the layout: where the push, the bell and the battery will go. In an occupied house Schneider fixes the bell and battery first, then runs the wire.
2. Remember the circuit: one wire from the push to the bell, one from the push to the battery, and one from the bell to the battery. For an ordinary vibrating bell, battery polarity doesn't matter.
3. Run the wires, leaving about a foot of slack at each end. Staple each wire separately, never two under one staple and never through the insulation, about every foot (up to three feet on straight runs).
4. Tuck wire along moldings, go around doorways rather than across sills, and don't pull it tight; woodwork shrinks and swells and can snap it.
5. If you must join two lengths, strip about three inches of each and scrape them bright. Hook them at right angles and twist each firmly around the other, then trim the ends. Solder the joint and tape it, carrying the tape about half an inch onto the insulation on each side.
6. Connect the push: bare about an inch of each wire, clamp it under the terminal screw and washer, and cut off any loose ends.
7. Connect the battery, then the bell, last of all. Scrape every wire end bright and tighten every screw so the wire is firmly clamped.
8. Press the push. The bell should ring.
9. If it doesn't, check the battery with the meter and every connection for dirt or looseness.
10. Next lay a screwdriver blade across the two push springs. If the bell now rings, the push is at fault.
11. Look at the bell itself. An armature stuck to the magnet cores may have a weak spring or a missing brass stop; Schneider sticks a scrap of postage stamp over the core end. A high screech means too much battery or the contact screw set too far forward. Clean dirty contacts.
12. To ring one bell from two pushes, connect the pushes "in multiple," side by side, so either one completes the circuit.

## From the source books

- St. John explains the trembling bell. Current through the electromagnet pulls the armature, which strikes the gong and at the same moment breaks the circuit at the contact screw. The armature springs back, remakes the circuit and is pulled again, many times a second. Remove the gong and you have a buzzer.
- A single-stroke bell, used for signaling in code, strikes once per press. To adjust one, Schneider presses the armature against the cores and bends the hammer until it just clears the gong, so it strikes cleanly without damping the tone.
- Schneider's batteries were Leclanché cells, zinc and carbon in a jar of sal ammoniac (ammonium chloride) solution. A cell worked too hard would recover after a night's rest. Sealed alkaline cells replace them today.

## Safety and modern practice

- Keep this project on batteries. Never connect a bell circuit to household wiring or the mains side of a doorbell transformer. Installing a transformer is mains work under your local electrical code; hire a licensed electrician.
- Schneider's old "tongue test" for current and his nitric-acid test for platinum contacts are unsafe. Use a multimeter instead.
- His tip of grounding one side of a bell circuit to a gas or water pipe is not acceptable today. Never connect anything to a gas line.
- Wear safety glasses when stripping wire or driving staples, and use a stable ladder for high runs.
