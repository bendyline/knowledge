# Make an electromagnet

An electromagnet is a coil of insulated wire around an iron core. It is magnetic only while current flows. Morgan says electromagnets "form the essential parts of dynamos, motors, telephone receivers, telegraph relays and sounders," and building one is the classic first experiment. Everything here runs on one or two ordinary flashlight batteries.

## What you need

- An ordinary large iron nail
- Several feet of insulated copper wire, enough for fifty turns around the nail. Morgan uses No. 18; thinner enameled "magnet wire" also works if you sand the coating off the ends.
- One or two 1.5-volt D cells, ideally in a battery holder
- A pocket compass
- Some small iron tacks, paper clips or iron filings
- For the coil experiment: a paper tube about ½ inch across and 4 inches long

## Steps

1. Check the effect first. Lay a wire across the top of a compass, parallel to the needle. Touch its ends to the battery for a moment and watch the needle swing. Loop the wire several times around the compass and the swing gets bigger. Morgan credits this experiment to Oersted in 1819.
2. Wind two or three turns of wire around the nail. Leave a few inches of free wire at each end.
3. Scrape or strip the insulation off the two ends so bright copper shows.
4. Touch the bare ends to the battery terminals and bring the nail to some tacks. Morgan expects two or three turns to lift only "one or two ordinary carpet tacks."
5. Disconnect, then wind on more turns, laying them neatly side by side. Go up to forty or fifty turns, in several layers if needed, always winding in the same direction. Reconnect and see how many more tacks it lifts.
6. Break the circuit and watch the tacks fall. The magnetism lasts only while current flows.
7. Find the poles. Bring one end of the nail near the compass. Morgan's rule: the end of the electromagnet that attracts the compass's north-pointing end is a south pole.
8. Swap the battery connections and test again. Reversing the current reverses the poles. St. John's rule: if you face the end of the coil and the current runs anticlockwise, that end is a north pole.
9. Try a solenoid. Wind three neat layers of wire on the paper tube and connect it to the battery. Hold a nail at the mouth of the tube, and the coil will draw it in. Slide an iron bar inside instead and the coil becomes much stronger.
10. Disconnect the battery whenever you are not testing.

## From the source books

- St. John explains why the iron core helps: "lines of force will go out of their way to go through iron," so a core strengthens the coil's field greatly. A soft iron core loses its magnetism when the current stops, while a steel core can be left permanently magnetized.
- Morgan warns that turns alone are not the whole story. Strength depends on "ampere turns" (current times number of turns). Add too many turns of thin wire and the extra resistance cuts the current, so the magnet gets weaker.
- For a horseshoe electromagnet, St. John winds two coils on separate iron cores joined by an iron yoke. The current must circle the two cores in opposite directions, so the free ends become unlike poles.
- Morgan describes lifting magnets at the Gary, Indiana, steel mill that picked up ten hot rails at once and dropped them when the current was cut.
- The period books often power these experiments with wet cells of bichromate of potash or dilute sulfuric acid. Do not make or use those cells. Sealed alkaline batteries give the same results safely.

## Safety and modern practice

- Use only 1.5-volt cells, or a small pack of up to four, never a wall outlet, car battery or lithium power-tool pack. A coil of wire is close to a short circuit, so a strong source can make the wire and battery dangerously hot.
- Even with D cells, connect only for a few seconds at a time. If the wire or battery feels warm, disconnect and let it cool. A push button or switch in the circuit makes this easy.
- Wear safety glasses when stripping or cutting wire. Keep iron filings away from eyes, and wash your hands afterward.
