# Build a crystal radio

A crystal set is a radio receiver powered entirely by the signal it picks up; it needs no battery and never connects to the mains. Collins's 1922 handbook describes one costing "$5.00 or less" that hears broadcasting stations "if you do not live too far away from them." Its success depends mostly on a good aerial and ground.

## What you need

- A cardboard mailing tube 2 inches across and 3 inches long
- No. 40 enameled magnet wire for 250 turns (Collins's size; heavier wire is easier to handle)
- A rotary switch with 7 contact points
- A crystal detector: a piece of galena in a cup with a fine phosphor-bronze "cat-whisker" wire resting on it
- A high-resistance earphone or headphones (Collins specifies 1,000 to 2,000 ohms or more)
- A wood base about 3½ × 5½ inches, binding posts, hookup wire, solder
- For the aerial: bare copper or aluminum wire no smaller than No. 16, two insulators, a lead-in insulator, a lightning arrester and a ground clamp or rod
- A buzzer, switch and dry cell for testing

## Steps

1. Wind 250 turns of wire on the tube. Every 15th turn, scrape the wire bare and solder on a 7-inch lead, until you have six taps. Bring the leads inside the tube and out through one end.
2. Mount the coil, switch, detector and two earphone binding posts on the base. Connect one end of the coil to an end contact of the switch and each tap in order to the next contacts.
3. Wire the circuit. The aerial goes to the switch lever, and the free end of the coil goes to ground. The detector and earphone go in series between the aerial side and the ground side, as in Collins's first receiving set. A small fixed capacitor ("condenser") across the earphone posts improves reception.
4. Use No. 16 or 18 wire for connections and scrape every end clean. Where wires cross, keep them an inch apart and at right angles.
5. Put up the aerial: a single wire 25 to 100 feet long, as high as you can safely get it. Keep it level, or sloping no more than about 30 degrees. Fix an insulator at each end, and keep the wire clear of trees and other wires.
6. Solder the lead-in wire to the aerial and bring it to a lightning arrester mounted outside, then through an insulating tube in the window frame to the set. Run heavy copper wire from the arrester to a metal rod driven into the earth, and connect the set's ground terminal to a good ground.
7. Adjust the detector. Wire the buzzer, switch and dry cell in a loop, and connect the buzzer's vibrator side to the set's ground wire. With the buzzer running and phones on, move the cat-whisker over the crystal until the buzz is loudest. Disconnect the buzzer and don't jar the detector.
8. Tune in by stepping the switch across its contacts until a station comes in loudest.

## From the source books

- Morgan explains the detector: aerial currents alternate thousands of times a second and cannot work an earphone, but galena, silicon and iron pyrites pass current one way only, turning the signal into pulses the earphone can sound.
- Collins finds galena less sensitive than silicon or pyrites, but its sensitive spots easier to find.
- Morgan forbids iron aerial wire, "even if galvanized or tinned," and wants the aerial clear of trees and telephone wires, hanging a little slack.
- Lacking an aerial, Collins suggests an ungrounded rain pipe or "the steel frame of an umbrella."

## Safety and modern practice

- Keep the aerial so far from power lines that it could never touch one if it fell. Never climb in wet or windy weather, and disconnect the aerial during thunderstorms.
- Ground the aerial to a ground rod as your electrical code requires (in the US, NEC Article 810). Never use a gas pipe, as both books suggest; water pipes are often plastic now. If in doubt, ask an electrician.
- A germanium diode reliably replaces the galena detector, and a high-impedance crystal earpiece works far better than modern earbuds.
- Use lead-free rosin-core solder, and solder in a ventilated area.
