# Solder with lead-free solder

Soft soldering joins metal with a low-melting alloy. It is used for electrical connections, small repairs and tinplate work. Jones's workshop manual, written for copper "bits" heated in a fire or gas stove, sums up the secret. The surfaces must be chemically clean, and the right flux must keep oxide from forming while the solder flows. The method is the same with a modern electric iron and lead-free solder.

## What you need

- An electric soldering iron with a stand, ideally temperature-controlled
- Lead-free solder; rosin-core wire for electrical work
- Flux: rosin or a rosin-based paste for electrical work; a paste flux for sheet metal
- A file, scraper or abrasive paper for cleaning
- A damp sponge or brass wool for wiping the tip
- Pliers or a clamp to hold the work, a heat-proof surface, and safety glasses

## Steps

1. Clean the metal mechanically. File or scrape the joint area until it is bright. Jones says this alone isn't enough, because a film of oxide is always forming. That is the flux's job.
2. Apply flux only where the solder is to go.
3. Tin the iron. Let it heat, wipe the tip clean, and melt solder onto the working faces until they carry a smooth, complete coat. Re-tin whenever the coating turns patchy or pitted. Jones: "This must always remain so, or the bit will not act."
4. For a wire joint, follow Schneider. Bare the ends and scrape them bright, bend them at right angles, hook them together, and twist each firmly around the other. Trim the ends, then solder the joint so it cannot corrode.
5. Heat the work, not just the solder. Hold the tinned tip against the joint and feed solder where tip and work meet, so it melts and runs into the joint. Jones describes drawing a stick of solder along a seam while the bit touches it.
6. Judge the heat by the result. Lumpy solder "put on with a trowel" means the iron or work is too cool. A sandy, dull surface and discolored work mean too much heat.
7. On seams, tilt the work so you solder downhill and the solder travels with the tip, not away from it.
8. Hold the iron with your thumb under the handle as a pivot, so you can raise or lower it at will. On thin or soft metals, never let its weight rest on the work, or it may melt a hole.
9. Lap sheet-metal seams. A butt joint with edges only touching has little strength; Jones calls for at least a ¼-inch lap on a small cylinder.
10. To "sweat" two parts together, flux and tin both faces first. Press them together with the hot iron until the top part "floats" and then settles down.
11. Clean the finished work. Jones soaks off acid-type flux in water, then washes with soda, soap and water to prevent corrosion.

## From the source books

- Jones's standard flux was "killed spirit" (zinc chloride), made by dissolving zinc in hydrochloric acid. He warned that it is poisonous, rusts work unless washed off, and should never be used on food containers. For those he used a rosin-in-oil flux.
- Collins, writing for radio builders, puts it bluntly: "Don't use soldering fluid, use rosin," and "Don't fail to solder each connection."
- Jones tinned his bits on a block of sal ammoniac (ammonium chloride), which gives off "profuse white fumes" and rusts nearby tools.

## Safety and modern practice

- Older soft solders were tin-lead alloys. Use lead-free solder (tin-copper or tin-silver-copper) for all new work; lead solder is banned for drinking-water plumbing and best avoided entirely. Lead-free alloys melt hotter, so a temperature-controlled iron at about 350°C (660°F) is typical. Rosin residue cleans off with isopropyl alcohol.
- Don't make killed spirit or work with hydrochloric acid. Buy a ready-made flux suited to the metal, and never use acid flux on electrical or electronic work.
- Solder with ventilation or a fume extractor; rosin fumes irritate the lungs. Wear safety glasses, since solder can spit. Wash your hands afterward.
- Always return the iron to its stand, keep the cord clear, and unplug it when you finish. Never solder anything that is connected to power.
