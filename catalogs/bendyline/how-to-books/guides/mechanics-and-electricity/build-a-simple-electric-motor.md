# Build a simple electric motor

An electric motor turns electricity into motion. Morgan's 1919 "Simplex" toy motor takes a couple of hours to build from scrap tinplate, a knitting needle and fine wire. It runs on one or two dry cells and works on the same principles as full-size motors.

## What you need

- Flat tinplate or thin sheet iron, from a flattened cocoa or baking-powder can, plus tin snips
- A steel knitting needle, for a shaft 1⅞ inches long
- No. 25 or No. 26 insulated copper wire (Morgan uses double cotton-covered; enameled magnet wire works)
- Paper, shellac and silk thread
- Two small washers, a soldering iron and lead-free solder
- A wood base about 2½ × 1⅞ × ½ inches, a scrap block for a bending form, small wood screws and copper wire for brushes
- One or two 1.5-volt dry cells

## Steps

1. Cut two strips of tin ½ inch by 1½ inches for the armature and bend them into identical shapes, curving the middle of each over the knitting needle to fit the shaft.
2. Bind the two halves to the shaft with iron wire, solder them together, then remove the binding wire.
3. Cut a strip ⅝ inch by 5 inches for the field magnet and bend it over a wooden form into a frame with two upright arms either side of the armature and two feet. Drill the feet for screws.
4. Cut two bearings from tin and solder a small washer to the shaft near each one as a collar.
5. Make the commutator core. Coat a paper strip ⅜ inch by about 5 inches with shellac. When it turns sticky, wrap it round the shaft until it is 3/16 inch thick.
6. Wrap shellacked paper wherever wire will lie, so sharp tin edges can't cut the insulation.
7. Wind the field first: anchor the start under a loop of tape, wind three layers on one arm, cross over, and wind three layers on the other. Tie off and shellac it.
8. Wind the armature. Lay the middle of about seven feet of wire diagonally across the armature's center, with paper under the crossing. Wind four layers on each half, one with each end, tying each off.
9. Scrape the two wire ends bare, lay them on opposite sides of the paper core and bind them tightly with silk thread, not touching. These bare wires are the commutator.
10. Assemble the motor on the base. The armature must sit centered in the field, clear it by about 1/16 inch all round, and turn freely.
11. Make two brushes by flattening short pieces of copper wire with light hammer blows. Clamp them under tin strips so they bear on opposite sides of the commutator, firmly but not tightly.
12. Connect one end of the field winding to one brush. The other brush and the other field end are the battery terminals.
13. Oil the bearings, connect the battery and twist the shaft to start it; a two-pole armature can't self-start. Adjust the brushes while it runs.

## From the source books

- Morgan explains the principle. Field and armature are both electromagnets, and the field pulls the armature's opposite pole round. Just as they line up, the commutator reverses the current in the armature, its poles flip, and it keeps turning.
- A three-pole armature makes the motor self-starting. Morgan winds three sections the same way, joining the outside end of each to the inside of the next; the joins form the commutator.
- Morgan notes that a dynamo is built almost the same way but turns motion into current. St. John adds that a direct-current dynamo will run as a motor if current is fed through its brushes.
- St. John explains why big motors needed starting boxes: a spinning armature generates a back current that limits what it draws, but at rest it has none, so full current could overheat it.

## Safety and modern practice

- Use only one or two 1.5-volt cells, and disconnect a stalled motor: as St. John explains, a motor that isn't turning draws its heaviest current, and wire and battery can get hot.
- Cut tinplate and steel wearing gloves and safety glasses, and file sharp edges.
- Use lead-free solder in a ventilated space. Alcohol-based shellac is flammable; keep it away from the hot iron.
