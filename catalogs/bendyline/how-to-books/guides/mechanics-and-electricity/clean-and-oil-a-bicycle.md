# Clean and oil a bicycle

A bicycle kept clean and lightly oiled runs easier and lasts far longer. Maria Ward's 1896 *Bicycling for Ladies* sets out a routine that still fits: inspect before and after riding, keep grit out of the moving parts, oil sparingly, and keep oil off anything rubber or anything that works by friction.

## What you need

- Old newspapers, soft rags (Ward uses cheesecloth and canton flannel) and old gloves
- A bicycle stand, or floor space to turn the bike upside down
- A shallow tray for small parts
- A chain degreaser (Ward used kerosene) and a container to soak the chain
- Bicycle chain lubricant or light oil in a can with a fine spout
- A tire pump, and a bucket of soapy water for finding leaks
- The tools from your bicycle kit, including a chain tool if your chain needs one

## Steps

1. Look the bicycle over before every ride. After riding, check the gears and pedals, look at the spokes and tires, and note any repair needed. Ward adds: always examine the bike thoroughly after a collision.
2. After each ride, dust the frame and wash off mud with a wet sponge.
3. For a full cleaning, set the bike on a stand or turn it upside down on newspapers, and remove all mud and grit, especially around the joints where the wheels turn.
4. Every two or three hundred miles of dusty road, Ward takes the chain off and soaks it overnight. Hold the rear wheel still while you open the chain, or your fingers may be caught in the sprocket.
5. While the chain soaks, wipe the axles, bearings and sprockets free of dust and grit.
6. Check that the wheels are true. Hold a pencil steady against the frame with its tip just touching the rim, and spin the wheel. If it touches unevenly, take the wheel to a repair shop.
7. Hang the chain to drip dry, wipe it, and refit it. Oil each joint, then wipe off the excess.
8. Give each bearing two or three drops of oil. Ward warns that too much is "worse than useless," because it spreads over the whole machine and collects dirt.
9. Keep oil off the tires, since it "will injure the rubber." Also keep it off the handlebar stem and its socket, the seat post and its clamp, and the brakes, which all work by friction and will slip.
10. Check the adjustment. The chain should be neither too tight nor too loose, the sprockets in line, and the rear wheel tracking in the same plane as the front. Bearings should spin freely without play.
11. Inflate the tires and check the valves. If a tire keeps going soft, Ward says to rule out the valve first. Then wet the inflated tire with soapy water and watch for bubbles, which mark the puncture.
12. Set the saddle so your knee never fully straightens at the bottom of the stroke. Ward warns that a fully straightened knee strains the knee and the back of the leg on hills.
13. Store the bike somewhere dry, with an even temperature, out of direct sun (which spoils rubber) and frost.

## From the source books

- Ward advises anyone not mechanically minded "never to touch the bicycle except to ride it." She also suggests practicing on an old machine before taking a good one apart.
- She notes that pedal spindles have right- and left-hand threads, so that pedaling forward drives them tighter.
- Ward used graphite on the chain where it meets the sprocket teeth, and oil in the joints. Modern chain lubricants do both jobs.
- Her rule for any repair: "First, finding out what is to be done, then doing it, then seeing that it has been done right."

## Safety and modern practice

- Kerosene is flammable and harsh on skin. A water-based bicycle degreaser, gloves and good ventilation are safer. Dispose of used solvent according to local rules.
- Never get lubricant on rims or disc-brake rotors and pads. Contaminated brakes may fail to stop you, and oily pads usually need replacing.
- Close quick-release or thru-axle wheel fasteners fully, and leave brakes, headsets and bearing adjustment to a bike shop unless you know the system.
- Inflate tires to the pressure printed on the sidewall, and wear a helmet when riding.
