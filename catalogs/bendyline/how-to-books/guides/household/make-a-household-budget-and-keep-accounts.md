# Make a household budget and keep accounts

A budget is a plan for dividing income among the family's needs. Accounts are the record that shows whether you are keeping to it. Elizabeth Hale Gilman and the home-economics teachers Helen Kinne and Anna Cooley treat these as two halves of one job. Plan the year's spending by category, then check it every week in writing.

## What you need

- An account book ruled for accounts, or a spreadsheet. Gilman tabs the pages so each category is easy to find.
- Small scratch pads kept wherever money gets spent, such as the kitchen and a desk.
- A record of all your income: wages, interest, gifts and anything else.
- A fixed time each day for writing up the accounts.

## Steps

1. Pin down the income you actually have. If it is irregular, Gilman plans on a figure "somewhat under one's brightest hopes," and Kinne and Cooley budget on the minimum.
2. Make the plan together. Kinne and Cooley want husband and wife, or the whole household, to agree on the division and the methods.
3. Divide the income into departments: food, shelter, operating (fuel, light, water, help), clothing, and a "higher life" share for education, recreation, charity, insurance and savings.
4. Begin with a model split and adjust it. The "ideal division" in Kinne and Cooley is about 25 percent for food, 20 for rent, 15 for operating, 15 for clothing and 25 for the higher life.
5. Keep housing within bounds. Kinne and Cooley set 25 percent of income as the limit for rent, or 28 percent if heat is included. Above that, they say, it becomes almost impossible to avoid debt.
6. When one department must be larger, decide on purpose which other department gives way. Gilman works through a family that wants a dearer flat and finds the difference in running expenses and miscellaneous spending.
7. Set up the book. Give each department a pair of facing pages. Write the money allotted to it on the right-hand (credit) page, and each purchase with its date on the left-hand (debit) page.
8. Note every purchase on a scratch pad when you make it. Copy the day's items into the book at your fixed time.
9. Balance each department weekly. Carry any money left over to the top of next week's credit page. If you overspent, carry the excess forward as a warning to spend less.
10. Keep one page, or a monthly "general account," for the income actually received. This checks the plan against real money, not just the planned allotments.
11. For anything bought on credit, keep a separate account for each store, listing purchases and payments, and pay it off regularly.
12. Save something every period, even a little. Give each family member a personal allowance, however small.

## From the source books

- Gilman's worked example is a $2,000 yearly income. Following Ellen H. Richards's "The Cost of Living," it gives $500 to food, $400 to rent, $300 each to running expenses and clothes, and $500 to everything else.
- Gilman calls keeping cash in labeled envelopes or boxes "primitive." She prefers written figures, which show "just what has been bought."
- Gilman warns that checkbook stubs marked "Incidentals" swallow "the trip the family meant to take," and that charging makes it easy to buy more than you can pay for.
- Kinne and Cooley note that the poorer the family, the larger the share spent on food. Their suggested budget for incomes under $500 puts 60 percent toward it.
- Gilman also offers a column method: one column per department and one line per day. Any overspent total is written in red ink at the top of the next week's column.

## Safety and modern practice

- Budget from take-home pay, after taxes and deductions. Common modern guidance treats housing costs above about 30 percent of income as a strain.
- Build an emergency fund, often suggested as three to six months of essential expenses, before saving for other goals.
- Credit cards are today's charge accounts. Pay the full statement balance each month to avoid interest, and keep a list of every recurring subscription and automatic payment.
- Banking apps and spreadsheets can sort spending by category, but still review the totals every week, as Gilman advised.
- Protect your financial records. Use strong, unique passwords, and shred paper statements before throwing them away.
