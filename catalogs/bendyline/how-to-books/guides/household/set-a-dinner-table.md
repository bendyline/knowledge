# Set a dinner table

A well-set table is straight, uncluttered and laid in the order the meal will be eaten. Lillian Lansdown and Anne Frances Springsteed, writing for waitresses and hostesses, give nearly the same rules. Cover the table neatly, line up each place with the one across from it, and put the silver for the first course on the outside.

## What you need

- A table pad or "silence cloth." Lansdown suggests table padding or doubled cotton flannel.
- A tablecloth, ironed with one straight fold down the middle.
- A low centerpiece, and candles if you like.
- For each person: a dinner or service plate, knives, forks, spoons, a water glass, other glasses as needed, a bread-and-butter plate and butter spreader if bread is served, and a large napkin.
- Salt and pepper for each two people, and water carafes or pitchers.

## Steps

1. Air the dining room and dust any polished surfaces. Square the table, under the light if you can.
2. Lay the pad smooth, then the cloth flat and without a wrinkle. Sherwood uses the center fold of the cloth as the guide line down the middle. Lansdown lets the cloth hang at least 9 inches below the table edge.
3. Put the centerpiece in the middle, low enough not to block anyone's view or conversation. Springsteed sets candlesticks on each side of the flowers, in line with them.
4. Set a plate at each place. Line each place up directly opposite the one across the table, with the pieces close but not touching.
5. Lay the knives to the right of the plate, cutting edge toward the plate, about half an inch from the table edge. Put spoons to the right of the knives, bowls up.
6. Lay the forks to the left of the plate in the order they will be used, the first course's fork on the outside. Lansdown limits the table to three forks and brings any others with their courses.
7. Put the water glass just above the tip of the knife. Group any other glasses to its right, in order of use.
8. Set the bread-and-butter plate above the forks, a little to the left. Lay the butter spreader across its upper right side.
9. Place salt and pepper between each two places, or one set for every two people.
10. Fold the napkin flat and square and lay it to the left of the forks, with its hem parallel to the forks and the table edge.
11. Ready the sideboard with dessert plates, extra silver and glasses.
12. A few minutes before you call everyone to the table, light the candles and fill the water glasses about halfway.

## From the source books

- Sherwood describes a butler measuring from the table edge to the tip of his middle finger to set each glass, so the glasses form an even line all around the table.
- Springsteed tucks a piece of bread into each napkin, "folded simply." Sherwood rejects fancy "hotel" folds in favor of a "three-cornered pyramid" that stands up and holds the roll.
- How to Fold Napkins says elaborate shapes such as the fan or mitre need fine, stiff damask. The napkin must be "exactly square," dampened with raw starch, ironed, and folded while still crisp and steaming.
- Among Springsteed's rules for the waitress: "Carvers must be treated with as much respect as if they were razors."
- The two waitress manuals disagree on service. Springsteed removes plates from the right. Lansdown serves and removes from the left, except for drinks, which are served and removed from the right.

## Safety and modern practice

- Keep candles well away from napkins, flowers and greenery, set them in stable holders, and never leave them burning in an empty room.
- Lansdown suggests asbestos as one choice of table pad. Use a felt or vinyl pad instead, and do not use any old pad that might contain asbestos.
- Keep hot foods at 140 °F or above and cold foods at 40 °F or below. Do not leave perishable food at room temperature for more than two hours.
- Use trivets or mats under hot serving dishes to protect the cloth and the table finish.
