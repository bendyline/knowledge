# Remove common stains from washable fabrics

Most stains come out if you treat them early and choose the treatment to fit both the stain and the cloth. Margaret Smith Furry's bulletin on stain removal gives the core rules. Start while the stain is fresh, test first, and try the mildest method before any bleach.

## What you need

- Clean white cloths or paper towels to use as a pad and for sponging.
- A bowl, a teakettle and cold water.
- Mild soap or a mild detergent.
- Absorbent powder: cornstarch, talcum powder or chalk.
- Glycerine.
- Lemon juice and salt.
- 3 percent hydrogen peroxide.

## Steps

1. Start before the stain dries if you can. Keep hot water, soap and a hot iron off any stain you have not identified, because they set many stains.
2. Check the fiber and test any remover on a seam or hem first. Use only lukewarm water on wool and silk.
3. Lay the stained area face down on a clean pad and work from the back. Sponge with light strokes from the outside of the stain toward the center, and spread the moisture out at the edges so no ring forms. Change the pad as it soils.
4. For blood, egg or milk, soak washable items in cold water. Furry soaks blood until it turns light brown, then washes it in warm suds. For old blood she adds about 2 cups of salt to a gallon of soaking water.
5. For coffee, tea and most fruit stains on cotton or linen, stretch the cloth over a bowl. Pour boiling water through it from a kettle held 2 to 3 feet up, then launder. This method is not for wool or silk, or for peach, pear, cherry or plum stains.
6. On wool or silk, or for those fruits, sponge with cool water instead. Work in glycerine, let it stand, then rinse. Do not use soap first, because Furry says soap sets fruit stains.
7. For grease, dust on cornstarch, talc or chalk. Let it soak up the grease, brush it off and repeat. Then wash in warm suds, rubbing the spot well with plenty of soap.
8. For grass, wash in hot soapy water and rub well.
9. For ink, blot from the edges inward so it does not spread. Work in an absorbent powder, then glycerine or a mild shampoo, rinse, and wash.
10. For rust on white cotton or linen, sprinkle salt, squeeze lemon juice over it and lay it in the sun. Or stretch it over a pan of boiling water and squeeze on lemon juice. Repeat, then rinse well.
11. For mildew and light scorch marks, wash in soap and water, then dry in the sun.
12. If a trace remains on a white or colorfast fabric, sponge it with hydrogen peroxide and rinse well.
13. Check that the stain is gone before you dry or iron the item. If it is not, treat it again.

## From the source books

- To remove a water ring, Furry steams the spot over a boiling kettle or rubs the cloth between her hands.
- Furry recommends powdered pepsin, an enzyme, for stains such as blood, egg and milk, even after heat has set them. Enzyme laundry products now do the same job.
- Harper's Household Handbook says to let mud dry untouched and then brush it, because wiping wet mud smears it in. It adds that soap will set tar and asphalt "hopelessly" if used first.
- For wine on table linen, Harper's pours boiling water through the stretched cloth. For ink on carpet, it soaks up what it can with damp cloths and then dabs with milk.
- Both books treat iron rust with salt, lemon juice and sunshine, and warn that chlorine bleach takes out color as well as stains.

## Safety and modern practice

- Furry recommends carbon tetrachloride, benzene, gasoline and ether for grease, and Harper's recommends gasoline and naphtha. Do not use any of them. They are toxic, flammable or both, and carbon tetrachloride is banned from consumer products. Use dish detergent, a commercial laundry pre-treater or a dry-cleaning spot remover, following the label in a ventilated room.
- Never mix cleaning products. Chlorine bleach mixed with ammonia, vinegar or other acids releases toxic gas. Furry's old routine of following bleach with vinegar is unsafe. Rinse thoroughly with plain water between any two products.
- Oxalic acid, which these books use for rust and ink, is poisonous. Use a commercial rust remover for fabrics, exactly as labeled.
- Wear gloves when using bleaches or removers, keep them labeled and out of children's reach, and check the garment's care label first.
