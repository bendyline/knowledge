# Iron a shirt

A shirt irons best evenly damp, on a firm padded board, one section at a time in a set order. The old household manuals agree on the essentials. Iron with the grain, iron each part until it is completely dry, and let the shirt air before it is folded or put away.

## What you need

- An ironing board padded with several layers of blanket or felt and covered with a clean cotton cloth stretched tight, so it has no wrinkles to press into the shirt. Gilman also likes a narrow sleeve board.
- An iron and a stand to rest it on, set at the right-hand end of the board.
- A dish of water and a sponge or soft cloth for re-dampening.
- A scrap of paper or cloth for testing the iron.
- A clean cloth on the floor under the board, so long pieces do not touch the floor.
- A hanger or clothes horse for airing.

## Steps

1. Sprinkle the shirt with clean water, using your hand or a clean whisk, and warm water in winter. Fold it, roll it tightly, and cover the roll so it stays evenly damp. Gilman sprinkles the night before or early in the morning, and only in the morning in hot weather, because damp clothes left overnight can sour.
2. Set up the board in good light and out of drafts. Adjust the height so you neither stoop nor hunch your shoulders. Keep the clothes on your left and the iron on your right.
3. Let the iron heat fully, then test it on the paper or cloth. Use a cooler iron for colored cotton. The Complete Home irons colored fabric on the wrong side so the color is not harmed.
4. Follow Catharine Beecher's order: "first do the back, then the sleeves, then the collar and bosom, and then the front."
5. Iron the double parts on both sides: the collar, the cuffs, the button bands and the hems.
6. Lay each section straight on the board. Iron along the long thread of the cloth and move the shirt toward you as you go.
7. Press steadily and lift the iron from the board as little as possible.
8. Keep ironing each part until it is dry. Re-dampen any area that has dried before you reach it with the damp cloth or sponge.
9. If a fold or tuck sets crooked, wet it well and iron it again.
10. Hang the finished shirt on the clothes horse or a hanger until it is completely dry and aired, then fold it or put it away.

## From the source books

- Helen Kinne and Anna Cooley would begin with the parts that wrinkle least, such as trimmings and sleeves. They ask students to decide for themselves whether to do a sleeve or the body first.
- The Complete Home, edited by Clara Laughlin, irons the body of a stiff-fronted shirt first, then stretches the starched front over a board and finishes it with a polishing iron. It says shirts "would far better go to the laundry."
- Beecher irons calicoes generally on the right side, while The Complete Home irons colored cottons on the wrong side.
- Beecher wanted at least three irons for each person ironing, heated in turn, and a separate "bosom-board" covered in white flannel for shirt fronts.
- Gilman tested a hot iron by touching it with a wet fingertip to see if it spat. She rubbed sticky irons with beeswax tied in a cloth.
- Gilman treats light scorches by sponging with a wet cloth and hanging the item in strong sunshine.

## Safety and modern practice

- Set a modern iron by the fabric dial and the garment's care label rather than Gilman's wet-finger test. Steam can burn, so keep your fingers away from the soleplate and the steam vents.
- Never leave a hot iron face down or unattended. Unplug it as soon as you finish, and let it cool standing upright, out of reach of children, with the cord away from the edge.
- Use an iron with automatic shut-off if you can. A spray bottle does the job of the old sprinkling and rolling.
- Old iron holders and stand pads were sometimes made of asbestos. Do not use or cut up any vintage pad that might contain it.
