# Hand-wash wool sweaters and blankets

Wool shrinks and felts when it is rubbed, wrung, or moved between hot and cold water. Old laundry manuals guard against all three. They keep the water at one mild temperature from start to finish, use gentle soap, and handle the wool as little as possible.

## What you need

- A clean tub or basin used only for the woolens, never water already used for other washing.
- A mild white soap or a mild synthetic detergent. Furry warns that soaps with added alkali make wool harsh and colors run.
- Lukewarm water for washing and for every rinse, all at the same temperature.
- Large clean towels.
- A flat drying surface, or a line in the shade for blankets.

## Steps

1. Mend any holes and treat any stains before washing. Gilman warns that stains are often impossible to remove once they have been soaped.
2. Sort the woolens, and do the white ones first.
3. Dissolve the soap fully to make suds. Elizabeth Hale Gilman wants the water to feel "neither warm nor cold to the hand." Martha McCulloch-Williams calls for "blood-warm" suds.
4. Fill the rinse water now, at the same temperature as the wash.
5. Put the article in and squeeze and press it gently through the suds. Do not rub soap directly on the wool or scrub it on a board.
6. Work quickly. McCulloch-Williams says knitted things "must be washed quickly." Do not leave wool soaking, and never boil it.
7. Lift it into the rinse water without twisting. Rinse more than once, at the same temperature, until no soap remains.
8. Press out the water with your hands. Gilman forbids wringing by hand or through a wringer.
9. Dry knitted sweaters and scarves without hanging them. McCulloch-Williams warns that "Hanging ruins them."
10. Shake out flannels and blankets well before hanging them, and shake them again now and then while they dry. Keep them out of hot sunshine and away from a fire.
11. Do not iron knitted articles. Once dry, flannels may be pressed with a moderately hot iron.

## From the source books

- Gilman, in The Library of Work and Play, says woolens are "neither soaked, boiled, blued nor starched." Blankets may be washed in warm rather than lukewarm water, as long as the rinse is just as warm.
- McCulloch-Williams says that sudden changes of water temperature "'full' every sort of fabric." Fulling is the old word for shrinking and thickening wool. Even so, she rinses flannels in water "the least bit hotter" than the suds.
- She dried knitted things "in a crumpled heap in the sun," while Gilman kept woolens out of hot sunshine. Today's practice is to dry flat in the shade.
- Margaret Smith Furry warns that hot water yellows and shrinks wool, and that chlorine bleaches destroy it. For white woolens she recommends sodium perborate (an oxygen bleach), which "leaves them soft and fluffy."
- Harper's suggests naphtha suds for very fine flannels and gasoline for fluffy knits. Gilman mentions naphtha cleaning but calls it "extremely dangerous work."

## Safety and modern practice

- Never clean clothes with gasoline, naphtha or other flammable solvents. Their vapors can ignite from a pilot light or a spark. Send anything you cannot wash to a professional dry cleaner.
- Never use chlorine bleach on wool. If white wool needs brightening, choose an oxygen bleach labeled safe for wool and test it on a hidden spot.
- Follow the care label. Some woolens are made to be machine washed, and some must be dry-cleaned only.
- To dry a sweater, roll it in a towel to press out water, then lay it flat on a dry towel and pat it into shape. Turn it once while it dries.
- Dry woolens away from radiators, heaters and stoves, both to protect the wool and to avoid a fire risk.
