# Fix a fireplace that smokes

A fireplace smokes when something prevents a steady upward draft: a flue that is blocked, too small or too short, a badly formed throat and smoke shelf, wind eddies at the chimney top, or a room so tight that no air can enter. Work through the checks below from the simplest to the most involved.

## What you need

- A flashlight or lantern on a strong cord, or a hand mirror
- A tape measure, and a ladder with safe roof access
- A sheet of thin metal (or two thin boards) wider than the fireplace opening
- Chalk or a pencil for marking the jambs
- For a permanent fix: a sheet-metal or wrought-iron hood or curtain, fitted by a mason or metalworker

## Steps

1. Look up the flue, or lower a light down it from the top. Check for fallen bricks, debris or heavy soot, and probe mortar joints at the top of the chimney, where they fail first. Have any blockage cleared before going further.
2. Open a window near the fire slightly. If the smoking stops, the room is too tight to supply the fire's air, and the fireplace needs an outside air source rather than rebuilding.
3. Check the chimney height. Senner and Miller call for at least 2 feet above the ridge of a peaked roof and 3 feet above a flat roof. Note any nearby trees or taller buildings that could send wind eddies down the flue.
4. Measure the fireplace opening, width times height. Then find the flue's inside size, from the top of the chimney or by counting bricks. Saylor's rule is that the flue area should be about one-tenth of the opening area.
5. Test the proportion directly. With a fire burning, hold the metal sheet across the top of the opening and lower it slowly until smoke no longer comes into the room. Saylor uses two six-inch boards wedged in the opening, sliding one down past the other.
6. Mark the jambs at the lower edge of the sheet at that point.
7. Have a permanent metal hood or curtain fitted across the top of the opening, with its lower edge at your marks. Senner and Miller note that extending the flue upward often cures the problem as well.
8. If the opening and flue are in proportion but the fire still smokes, look at the throat and smoke shelf. The fireplace sides should rise vertically to a throat at least 6 to 8 inches above the lintel. The throat should run the full width of the opening, with an area no less than the flue's. Behind it, a shelf 6 to 12 inches deep stops cold air falling down the flue from pushing smoke out.
9. Fixing a missing throat or shelf means rebuilding the back of the firebox, or taking down part of the chimney breast to set a cast-iron throat damper. That is a job for a qualified mason.

## From the source books

- Saylor considered 3 feet wide by 2 1/2 feet high, and half the width deep, a good size for an ordinary room. He thought big fireplaces in tight modern rooms caused trouble because the room cannot supply enough air.
- He wanted the chimney top kept clear of cowls and whirligigs. To keep rain out, he capped it with a stone and left openings on all four sides beneath.
- Senner and Miller give a table pairing opening sizes with flue linings. For example, a 36-by-28-inch opening calls for an 8 1/2-by-13-inch lining or a 12-inch round one.
- They recommend a damper in cold climates. A slow hardwood fire may need it open only 1 or 2 inches, while a roaring pine fire needs it fully open.

## Safety and modern practice

- Have the chimney inspected and swept by a certified chimney sweep at least once a year, and before using a fireplace that has stood idle. Hidden cracks and creosote cause chimney fires.
- Do not copy the old bulletin's methods of testing with a smoky fire under a blocked chimney, or burning soot out. Leave leak testing and cleaning to professionals.
- Modern codes generally require the chimney to extend at least 3 feet above where it passes through the roof, and 2 feet above anything within 10 feet. Rebuilding a firebox, throat, damper or chimney usually needs a permit and inspection.
- Keep combustible mantels and trim well clear of the opening; check local code clearances. Install smoke and carbon monoxide alarms.
- On the roof, use a properly secured ladder that extends above the eave, wear soft-soled shoes, and use fall protection on steep pitches.
