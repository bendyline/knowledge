# Hang a wooden gate that will not sag

A gate sags in two ways: its hinge post leans toward it, or the gate frame racks out of square under its own weight. The cure is a firmly planted post, a gate as light as its strength allows, and a diagonal brace set so that it is squeezed, not stretched, as the gate tries to drop. "There is but one right way to brace a gate," says Martin's manual, "and many wrong ones."

## What you need

- A large, durable hinge post (Martin's examples are oak or cedar, up to 8 inches square) and a latch post
- Seasoned, preferably planed lumber. Martin's standard gate uses five 6-inch pine boards mortised into heavier end pieces, with one diagonal brace.
- Bolts, or wooden pins wedged at both ends, for the joints
- Strap hinges or through-bolt hinges, and a latch
- Medium stones, and optionally cement, for setting the post in soft ground
- A digging bar or post-hole digger, a tamper and a level

## Steps

1. Dig a big hole for the hinge post, below the frost line; Martin says 3 feet "is none too deep." His heavier gates set cedar posts 4 feet down.
2. Before setting the post, stand a flat stone on edge at the bottom of the hole. Put it against the side the post's foot will push toward, which is away from the gate.
3. Set the post plumb. Backfill in thin layers and tamp each one as hard as you can, walking around the post so it is firmed on all sides.
4. When the hole is half full, set a second stone against the post on the side the gate's weight will pull it toward. Then finish filling and tamping.
5. In soft ground, pack stones around the post instead, bedding each layer in cement. Keep the post plumb throughout, since it cannot be straightened once the cement sets. Leave it undisturbed for a few days.
6. Build the gate. Mortise the boards into the end pieces and coat each tenon before assembly (see the safety notes). Fasten the joints with bolts or wedged pins. Keep the gate as light as its strength allows.
7. Fit the diagonal brace so it runs from the bottom corner on the hinge side up to the top corner on the latch side. As the latch end tries to drop, those two corners would have to move closer together, and the brace stops them. A brace running the other way does nothing but hold nails.
8. Bolt or clinch-nail the brace to every board it crosses.
9. Hang the gate on strong hinges. Martin prefers a hinge whose iron passes through the gate's end piece and is fastened with a nut; next best are strap hinges fastened with bolts or screws.
10. Take the strain off the hinge post when the gate is shut. Let the free end rest on a block, or on a pin that sits in a slot or shoulder on the latch post.

## From the source books

- Martin's manual recommends gateways at least 14 feet wide for farm use. It also notes that a gate is much quicker to use than the old removable bars.
- One light pine gate in the manual went 23 years without sagging. It hung on strap hinges a blacksmith made from old wagon-tire iron, which acted as braces themselves.
- For adjustment, the manual shows a gate hung on eye-bolts running through the post: when it sags, tightening the nut on the upper bolt pulls the top back. Another design uses a tall hinge post with a cap piece and wedges that can be reset.
- An extra-tall hinge post with an iron rod running down to the gate's far end also carries much of the load.

## Safety and modern practice

- The manual coats tenons with thick paint. Old paints often contained lead, so use a modern exterior primer, waterproof glue or epoxy instead. Do not reuse old painted boards without testing them for lead.
- Use ground-contact-rated pressure-treated lumber or a naturally durable wood such as cedar or black locust for posts. Do not use creosote or other banned preservatives.
- Call 811 to have underground utilities marked before digging post holes. Check local rules on fence and gate height, setbacks and pool-barrier gates, which must self-close and self-latch.
- Modern hardware makes the same fixes: heavy galvanized strap or lag-screw hinges, and turnbuckle anti-sag kits. A turnbuckle cable runs the opposite way to a wooden brace, from the top of the hinge side down to the bottom of the latch side, because it works in tension.
