# Build a log wall with saddle-notched corners

The round-notch, or saddle, corner locks cabin logs together at the corners; the Forest Service authors Fickes and Groben call it "the most satisfactory from every standpoint." Each log is scribed to the one beneath and notched on its underside only, so the joint sheds water and the logs settle as they dry.

## What you need

- Straight, even, peeled logs, ideally cedar, pine, fir or larch, with top diameters of 10 to 12 inches. They should be at least 6 feet longer than the inside dimension if the corners are to project.
- A continuous stone or concrete foundation with small vents. Make the end walls half a log's thickness higher than the side walls.
- Log dogs, and 10- or 12-inch wing dividers with a pencil holder and level bubble
- A sharp axe, a 2-inch outside-bevel gouge, a crosscut saw, a spirit level and a 6- to 8-foot plumb board
- A drill or auger for 3/4-inch and 7/16-inch holes; 10- or 12-inch spikes or hardwood pins
- Caulking compound, or split or pole chinking for wide gaps

## Steps

1. Cut the logs in late fall or winter. Peel them, trim off knots, and store them in a single layer on skids with 2 or 3 inches between them for at least six months. Pile curved logs hump up so their weight helps straighten them.
2. Sort the logs. Save the best for conspicuous walls and use the largest at the bottom.
3. Hew a flat face 2 to 3 inches wide on the underside of each bottom log so it sits steady. Set the two side logs on the foundation, then the two end logs, centered so all four inside faces match the planned inside dimensions.
4. Dog the logs so they cannot move while you mark them.
5. Set the dividers to half the diameter of the side log. Keep the bubble level and the points vertical, one above the other. Run the lower leg over the curve of the log beneath while the pencil traces the same curve onto the upper log. Mark all four sides of each corner.
6. Roll the log over, go over the scribed line with an indelible pencil, and chop out the notch roughly with the axe.
7. Chip close to the line and finish with the gouge. Cup the notch slightly so the log's weight bears on its outer edges for a tight joint.
8. Roll the log back into place. For each later log, set the dividers to the gap between it and the log below. Set them slightly wider if you want the upper log to "ride" down for an especially tight fit.
9. Every few rounds, measure up from the floor joists to keep the corners level; an inch of variation is acceptable. Adjust by cutting the notches deeper or shallower, or by swapping butt and top ends.
10. Keep the inside faces plumb with the plumb board.
11. Pin each course about 2 feet from each corner and beside each door and window opening. Bore a 3/4-inch hole halfway through the upper log, continue it at 7/16 inch, and drive a 10- or 12-inch spike. Stagger the spikes from course to course.
12. Leave 3 to 4 inches of settling space above door and window frames, even in dry logs. Then caulk the joints inside and out, or chink wide gaps with split pieces or small poles first.

## From the source books

- Fickes and Groben prefer a continuous foundation to piers. It supports the logs fully and keeps rodents out, and logs should never touch the ground.
- They rate the dovetail (box) corner strong but prone to wide cracks and rot, because it is hewed down where moisture collects. The plain tenon corner is simple but must be pinned, and suits temporary buildings.
- A badly curved log can be straightened by sawing cuts one-third to one-half of its depth into the upper, humped side.
- Beard shows simpler notches for camps and club houses: flat notches, the flattened "General Putnam" joint and the wedge-shaped Pike notch. He fills cracks with quartered sticks spiked in place and daubs the inside with clay or lime mortar.
- Beard advises planning the cabin first with a stick model about 15 by 10 inches.

## Safety and modern practice

- A log cabin meant for living or sleeping needs a building permit almost everywhere. Codes cover foundations, roof loads, energy and fire safety; the ICC 400 standard governs log structures. Have an engineer review the foundation and roof framing.
- Wear chainsaw chaps, a helmet with face screen, eye and hearing protection, and steel-toed boots when cutting or notching. Keep axe swings clear of your legs.
- Green logs are very heavy. Use ramps, cant hooks, ropes or a machine, and never stand downhill of a log being rolled.
- Borate treatments and flexible log-home chinking compounds are common modern choices.
