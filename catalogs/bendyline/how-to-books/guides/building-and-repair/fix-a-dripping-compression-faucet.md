# Fix a dripping compression faucet

A compression faucet, the kind with separate hot and cold handles that you screw down to close, shuts off the water by pressing a rubber or fiber washer onto a brass seat. When the washer wears out, the faucet drips, and turning the handle harder only makes it worse. Warren's USDA leaflet notes that "moderate force on the handle of a faucet in good repair should stop all flow and drip." A new washer is usually all it takes.

## What you need

- A new seat washer of the right size and shape (flat for a seat with a raised ridge, beveled for a tapered seat) and a new brass washer screw
- An adjustable wrench, with a cloth to protect the nut's finish
- A screwdriver that fits the slot squarely; a small hacksaw for a stuck screw
- Kerosene or penetrating oil
- Replacement stem packing or a top washer, if the faucet leaks around the handle
- Optional: a seat-dressing tool, or a seat wrench for removable seats

## Steps

1. Shut off the water at the valve nearest the faucet, either under the sink or on the branch pipe. Open the faucet to drain it. On a mixing faucet, repair each side separately.
2. Pry off the handle cap if there is one, then remove the handle screw and the handle.
3. Wrap the cap (packing) nut with cloth and unscrew it counterclockwise with the wrench.
4. Unscrew the stem out of the faucet body by turning it the opening direction. You may need to slip the handle back on to get a grip.
5. Remove the screw holding the washer at the bottom of the stem. If it is stuck, put a drop of kerosene or penetrating oil on it and tap the head lightly. If the head splits, deepen the slot with a hacksaw and try again.
6. Clean the washer cup and fit the new washer and screw.
7. Look into the faucet body at the seat. If it is nicked or grooved, washers will keep failing. Unscrew it with a seat wrench and replace it if it is removable; otherwise dress it with a seat-dressing tool. Screw the tool's cone into the body to center it, turn the handle a few times to square the seat, then shake out the brass cuttings.
8. Screw the stem back in, replace the cap nut, and refit the handle. Line the handles up with each other on a mixing faucet.
9. Turn the water back on slowly and open the faucet to flush out grit. Check that it closes with moderate pressure.
10. If water seeps up around the stem when the faucet is open, the packing is worn or loose. Hold the faucet closed, unscrew the cap nut, and fit a new top washer or wind packing around the stem. Tighten the nut just enough to stop the leak without making the handle stiff.

## From the source books

- Warren suggests keeping a few washers of each needed size in the house. In an emergency a temporary washer can be cut from leather (better for cold water) or rubber (better for hot).
- He notes that seat damage is more common on hot-water faucets. A worn washer lets grit and steady leakage cut the seat.
- The farmstead bulletin adds that a washer needing frequent replacement may simply be the wrong type for the seat. A faucet that chatters while running may have a loose washer or worn stem threads; replacement stems and seats can restore all the wearing parts.
- Warren's clock rule for turning nuts: clockwise tightens, counterclockwise loosens. He also warns that small brass screws and stems twist off easily under a big tool.
- Both bulletins stress that sewage and impure water must never be able to reach the drinking supply. The farmstead bulletin notes that codes forbid secondhand pipe for this reason.

## Safety and modern practice

- This guide covers water faucets only. Never attempt repairs on gas piping or appliance gas valves; call the gas utility or a licensed professional.
- Many modern faucets are single-handle cartridge, ball or ceramic-disc types. These are repaired by replacing the cartridge or seals listed by the manufacturer, not by fitting a washer.
- The old books lubricate packing with tallow and use lead packing rings. Use silicone faucet grease rated for drinking water and PTFE packing instead. Any replacement faucet should be certified lead-free for potable water.
- Local plumbing codes may require a licensed plumber or a permit for anything beyond like-for-like repairs, such as moving pipes or replacing a water heater valve.
- Hot water can scald. Shut off and drain the hot side fully before opening a hot-water faucet.
