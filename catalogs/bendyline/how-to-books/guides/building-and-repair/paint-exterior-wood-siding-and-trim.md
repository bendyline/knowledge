# Paint exterior wood siding and trim

Exterior paint is a very thin film, Walsh estimates "one thousandth part of an inch thick or less," that must survive sun, rain and the swelling and shrinking of the wood beneath. Most failures come from painting damp wood, skipping the knots, laying the primer on too thick, or hurrying the coats. The old manuals agree that careful preparation and patience matter more than anything else.

## What you need

- Exterior primer and exterior finish paint (see the safety notes on choosing modern products)
- Shellac, or a modern shellac-based stain-blocking primer, for knots and sappy spots
- Sandpaper on a block, scrapers, a nail set and a hammer
- Exterior wood putty or filler
- Good brushes, including a small one for knots
- Drop cloths, and a sturdy ladder or scaffold

## Steps

1. Pick settled, dry weather. Walsh warns that paint will "only properly adhere to a wood surface when it is free of any moisture," so wait for rain or dew to dry off completely.
2. Prepare the surface. Rub rough places smooth with sandpaper on a block, and clean out moldings and beads so they are free of dust.
3. Set all nail heads slightly below the surface with a nail set.
4. Seal every knot, sap streak, pitch pocket or discolored spot with shellac. Use white shellac under light colors and ordinary orange shellac under dark ones. The *Hints for Painters* author gives knots two coats, sanding when dry. Pitch bleeds through oil paint, and shellac stops it.
5. Prime the bare wood. Press the brush firmly so the primer is forced into the pores, and rub it out thin. A primer flowed on loose or thick will blister and run.
6. Tint the primer toward the finish color instead of using pure white. Make each later coat a little lighter in tint, which gives a more solid, uniform result.
7. When the primer is dry, fill nail holes, cracks and defects with putty. Doing it before priming lets the dry wood suck the oil out of the putty.
8. Apply the finish coats, allowing plenty of drying time between them. New work needs at least two coats over the primer for good results; previously painted work in sound condition usually needs two.
9. Finish each surface in one application where you can, stopping at natural breaks. This avoids visible "laps" where fresh paint meets paint that has begun to dry.
10. Inspect the paint each year, especially on the sunny south side, and repaint before the film fails down to bare wood.

## From the source books

- Cushman and Gardner describe the signs of failing paint: chalking (a powdery surface), checking (fine cracks), cracking, scaling, peeling and blistering. They trace most of them to poor paint or poor application.
- They also found that tinted paints outlast pure white, a point Walsh repeats. He notes that light colors fail first on the south side, because sunlight breaks down the oil binder.
- Walsh and the *Hints* author both stress that each coat must be thoroughly dry before the next goes on. The builders' pamphlet on painting expected good interior work with three coats to take several weeks.
- Several authors used benzol (benzene) in primers for resinous pine, and added extra turpentine to coats in strong sun to prevent blistering.

## Safety and modern practice

- The historical recipes in these books are based on white lead, red lead and other toxic pigments. Never make or use them. Any home built before 1978 may have lead paint: test before scraping or sanding. Use wet scraping, plastic sheeting and HEPA vacuums, and never dry-sand or burn off old paint. In the US, paid contractors must be lead-safe certified under the EPA's renovation rule.
- Do not use benzol (benzene), a carcinogen. Use modern exterior acrylic latex or alkyd primers and paints, following the label for temperature, humidity and recoat times.
- Shellac and solvent-based primers are flammable. Ventilate, keep them away from flames, and dry or dispose of oily rags safely.
- Set extension ladders at about one foot out for every four feet up, with the top extending 3 feet above the roofline if you are stepping onto it. Keep your belt buckle between the rails, and use scaffolding for high or long runs.
