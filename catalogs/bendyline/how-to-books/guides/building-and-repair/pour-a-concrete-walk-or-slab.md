# Pour a concrete walk or slab

A concrete walk or ground-level floor is a slab cast on a firm, drained base; the Atlas Portland Cement Company's guide calls a floor "nothing more than" a large sidewalk. Durability comes from a solid base, a mix with no extra water, joints that let the concrete move, and slow, damp curing.

## What you need

- Portland cement (one 94-pound sack is counted as 1 cubic foot), clean coarse sand free of loam, and gravel or crushed stone between 1/4 and 3/4 inch
- Broken stone, coarse gravel, brickbats or broken tile for the base (never ashes)
- 2x4s and stakes for side and cross forms
- A tight, level mixing platform (about 9 by 10 feet), square-end shovels, a rake and water
- Bottomless measuring boxes 12 inches deep (for a 1:2:4 mix with one sack: 1 by 2 feet for sand, 2 by 2 feet for gravel)
- A tamper, a template or straightedge, a wood float, a trowel, an edger and jointer, and a stiff brush
- Burlap, straw or old carpet for curing

## Steps

1. Stake out the walk and dig out about 16 inches. Ram the bottom firm and even, and cut off any tree roots running less than 18 inches below the surface.
2. Fill about 12 inches with the hard, porous base material for drainage. In clay soil, add side drains of tile leading to pockets of stone so water does not collect and heave the slab in frost.
3. Set 2x4s on edge as side forms and hold them with stakes. Make the tops follow the finished grade, crowned in the center or sloped to one side about 1/4 inch per foot. Set cross forms to divide the walk into slabs no more than 6 feet square.
4. Spread the measured sand in a 3- to 4-inch layer on the mixing board. Spread one sack of cement over it, then turn the pile two or three times until it is one even color with no streaks.
5. Spread the gravel on top and soak the pile with about half the water. Turn it, dragging each shovelful back so the wet gravel picks up the sand and cement. Add water to dry spots until the mix is uniform. Use roughly 4 to 5 gallons per sack in all.
6. Aim for a mix that does not quake when tamped but brings a little moisture to the surface. Use no more water than this; extra water weakens concrete.
7. Fill every other slab to full depth: 4 inches for walks and ordinary floors, 6 inches for driveways. Place each batch within half an hour of adding water, and throw out any that has begun to set.
8. Tamp thoroughly, then strike the surface with the curved template or straightedge resting on the side forms.
9. When those slabs have stiffened, pull the cross forms and fill the gaps. Run a trowel down through the full depth at each joint so the slabs can move independently.
10. Smooth with the wood float. Run the jointer along the joints and the edger along the sides.
11. When the surface is nearly hard, brush it lightly to remove float marks and leave a non-slip texture.
12. Once it is hard enough, cover it with hay, straw or old carpet and keep it soaked for at least three or four days. Keep people and animals off it.

## From the source books

- Miller's farm bulletin gives three standard mixes: 1:1 1/2:3 (rich) for wear and watertightness, 1:2:4 (standard), and 1:3:5 (medium) for plain concrete of moderate strength. Atlas allows 1:2 1/2:5 when the sand is coarse and the gravel well graded.
- Miller reckons one man can hand-mix and place about 1 1/2 to 1 3/4 cubic yards a day.
- Atlas says interior floors such as cellars need no joints, but outdoor slabs should not exceed 6 feet square. It warns against pouring in freezing weather.

## Safety and modern practice

- Wet cement is strongly alkaline and can cause chemical burns. Wear waterproof gloves, long sleeves, boots and eye protection, and wash splashes off skin promptly. Wear a dust mask when handling dry cement.
- Call 811 (or your local utility-locating service) before digging. Check with your building department: driveways, slabs for structures and any walk in the public right-of-way often need a permit and must meet local specifications.
- Modern practice usually uses 4 to 6 inches of compacted gravel base, welded wire or rebar in many slabs, and air-entrained concrete in freeze-thaw climates. Control joints are typically spaced about 2 to 3 times the slab thickness in feet, with fiber expansion strips where a slab meets a building.
- Keep the slab moist for about a week for best strength.
