# Start a culinary herb garden

Culinary herbs such as thyme, sage, savory, marjoram, basil and parsley are easy to grow if you remember what gives them flavor: aromatic oils that develop best in full sun and moderately fertile soil. Kains, writing in 1912, starts most herbs from seed and propagates a few perennials by division, layers or cuttings.

## What you need

- The sunniest patch in the garden, out of the shade of trees, buildings and fences
- Well-decayed manure, 1 to 2 bushels per square yard of bed
- A spading fork, a rake and a hoe
- Shallow boxes or seed pans, plus small pots or deeper flats, for starting seed
- Herb seed, and a few seeds of a fast, small-leaved, turnip-rooted radish

## Steps

1. Choose the site. Kains prefers a smooth, gentle slope facing south, though he found north slopes often do remarkably well too. On uneven ground, put moisture-lovers in the hollows and dry-lovers on the ridges.
2. Judge the soil. A light, sandy loam over a porous, well-drained subsoil is ideal because it warms early and is easy to work. Don't make it very rich; rank growth gives more leaf but proportionally less oil.
3. Prepare the bed when the grass nearby starts to sprout. Spread the manure, then dig as deep as you can without bringing up subsoil, breaking the earth finely with the fork.
4. On heavy clay, dig or plow late in the fall instead and leave the clods rough over winter. Frost breaks them down, and a raking in spring finishes the job.
5. Sow small or slow seeds indoors in shallow boxes or seed pans. When the seedlings can be handled, move them to small pots or deeper flats, about 2 inches apart.
6. Set the young plants in the garden once the soil is moist and warm and the season has settled.
7. If you sow outdoors, drop a few radish seeds in the row with slow, fine-seeded herbs such as marjoram, savory and thyme. The radishes come up fast and mark the row so you can start hoeing early. Sow them thinly, no more than six to the foot, and pull them as soon as the herbs appear.
8. For tarragon, which sets no seed, and for some other perennials, start with divisions, layers or cuttings instead of seed.
9. Cultivate the surface every week or 10 days, and after any rain that leaves a crust, until the plants cover the ground. This keeps down weeds and holds moisture in the soil below.

## From the source books

- Kains illustrates the radish trick with a class of boys who were told to sow "lonesome" seeds but planted more than 100 to the foot. The radishes were harvested before they harmed the herbs.
- He jokes that, for soil, "Hobson's choice ranks first," meaning you work with the soil you have. He tells of fall-plowed river mud that turned up in "chunks as large as a man's body" yet crumbled under a rake by spring.
- His weekly hoeing aimed to keep the top inch "powdery" as a dust mulch. Most gardeners today use an organic mulch instead, because repeated stirring dries and erodes the soil and harms its structure.

## Safety and modern practice

- Use composted or well-aged manure on beds of edible plants, never fresh manure, and wash your hands after handling it. Many gardeners use finished compost instead.
- A soil test from your local extension office shows whether lime or fertilizer is needed. Thyme, sage and other Mediterranean herbs generally do well in neutral to slightly alkaline soil.
- Mint spreads aggressively by underground stems. Grow it in a sunken pot or a separate bed.
- Wear gloves and sturdy shoes when digging. Keep fork tines pointed down when you set the tool aside.
