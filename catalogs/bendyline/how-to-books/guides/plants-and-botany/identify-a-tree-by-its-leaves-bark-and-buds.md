# Identify a tree by its leaves, bark and buds

Leaves are the obvious clue, but they are gone half the year. Levison's method in *Studies of Trees* relies on features you can see in every season: overall form, branching, bark, buds, fruit and, for evergreens, the needles. Most trees have "some one trait" that marks them at a glance. The Texas forestry bulletin by Mattoon and Webster adds a checklist for studying any tree thoroughly.

## What you need

- A notebook and pencil
- A hand lens
- A pocketknife, for cutting across fallen twigs to see the pith
- A tree guide or key for your region

## Steps

1. Look at the whole tree. Note its size, shape and branching, and whether neighboring trees have crowded it. Some trees are known by form alone: the American elm is vase-shaped, the Lombardy poplar is narrow and spire-like, and the weeping willow is pendulous.
2. Decide whether it is a conifer. On pines, the needles grow in clusters, so count them: five in white pine, three in pitch pine, two in Scotch, Austrian and red pine.
3. Tell spruce from hemlock. Spruce needles are four-sided and set spirally, which makes the twigs coarse to the touch. Hemlock needles are flat, in flat sprays, with two white lines underneath. Red cedar has small needle- or scale-like leaves; arborvitae has flat, fan-like sprays that smell aromatic when bruised.
4. On a broadleaf tree, check whether branches and buds are opposite or alternate. Levison groups the opposite-branched trees as horsechestnut, ash and maple. A big, sticky, dark-brown bud means horsechestnut. Small rusty-brown to black buds on twigs flattened below the buds mean ash. Many small bud scales point to a maple.
5. Study a leaf. Note whether it is simple or compound, how leaves are arranged on the twig, and the shape of the blade, margin, base and tip, plus the veins, texture and color.
6. Examine the bark. Sycamore bark is mottled and peels in plates. Beech bark is smooth, gray and close-fitting. Gray birch bark is dull white. Hackberry bark has corky warts, and blue beech has a fluted trunk.
7. In winter, read the twigs and buds. Note bud size, scales and shape; the leaf scars and the bundle scars inside them; and the pith in a cut twig. Beech buds, for example, are long, slender, sharp and chestnut brown.
8. Use fruit and flowers in season: acorns, winged samaras of maples, ashes and elms, burs, cones, and the hanging seed balls of sycamore.
9. For oaks, sort out the group. White oaks have rounded lobes and light, flaky bark, and their acorns ripen in one year. Black or red oaks have sharp-pointed, often bristle-tipped lobes and darker, ridged or tight bark, and their acorns take two years, so two sizes of acorn hang together.
10. Note the habitat: wet or dry ground, open or sheltered, and the trees growing nearby. Then confirm your guess in a regional key, and visit the tree again in another season.

## From the source books

- After each tree, Levison adds a "Comparisons" note on look-alikes. Scotch and Austrian pines both carry two needles, but the Austrian's needles are much longer, coarser and darker.
- The Texas bulletin's lesson plan asks students to study one tree at a time, collect samples of leaves, twigs, bark, flowers and fruit, and cover eleven points from form to habitat. It warns that it is "better not to have samples than to deface or injure the tree."
- Levison wrote as chestnut blight was spreading. He warned it threatened the tree with extinction, and mature American chestnuts are indeed now rare. He called the American elm unequaled for avenues, but Dutch elm disease, which arrived later, killed most large elms.
- Some of Levison's scientific names have since changed. Norway spruce, for instance, is now *Picea abies*, not *Picea excelsa*.

## Safety and modern practice

- Use fallen twigs and leaves where you can, and never strip bark from a living tree.
- Learn poison ivy before you go: three leaflets, often climbing tree trunks as a hairy vine.
- Ask permission on private land. Watch for ticks, and check yourself after walking through brush.
- Free regional apps and extension-service keys can help confirm an identification, but check their suggestions against the features above.
