# Propagate plants from cuttings and layers

Cuttings and layers give exact copies of the parent, which seed often can't do for named varieties. Cuttings are quickest for house plants and soft-stemmed plants. Layering, where the stem roots while still attached to the parent, suits hard-wooded plants that root poorly as cuttings.

## What you need

- A healthy, vigorous parent plant
- A sharp knife
- A flat 4 to 5 inches deep, with an inch or two of gravel in the bottom covered by moss or a layer of old sacking
- Clean, medium-coarse sand, like builders' sand: not so fine that it packs, not so coarse that it dries out
- A pointed stick (dibber), a watering can, and newspaper for shade
- For layering: crotched sticks or pegs, or a stone, and loose soil

## Steps

1. Choose shoots of new growth from a plant in full vigor. Bend one between your fingers: if it snaps cleanly like a green bean, it is right. If it just bends or crushes, it is too old or too soft.
2. Cut pieces 2 to 4 inches long with a slanting cut. Strip off the lower leaves and shorten any large leaves that remain, so the cutting loses less water.
3. If you can't plant them at once, keep the cuttings shaded and sprinkled. Rockwell revived badly wilted cuttings by soaking them in water for several hours.
4. Fill the flat nearly to the top with sand, level it, soak it thoroughly, and let it drain for about an hour.
5. Mark rows 2 or 3 inches apart. Set the cuttings close together without touching, one-third to one-half their length deep, firming them in with the dibber. Water to settle the sand.
6. Keep the flat at about 50 to 55°F at night. Bottom heat 10 to 15 degrees warmer speeds rooting. Shade the flat with newspaper in hot sun, and sprinkle lightly if the room goes above 70°F.
7. Never let the sand dry out. It usually needs a thorough soaking every morning.
8. Look for roots in 8 to 20 days. Pot the cuttings while the roots are still under half an inch long.
9. To layer a woody plant, choose a low, flexible branch in spring as the leaves form. On a stiff branch, cut halfway through the underside at a slant (Bailey calls this "tongueing").
10. Bend the branch down and cover the wounded part, or a section near a good bud, with 1 to 3 inches of soil. Hold it down with a crotched stick or a stone.
11. Leave the layer undisturbed until it has rooted well, then cut it from the parent and plant it. Kains found sage, savory and thyme layers rooted in three or four weeks.

## From the source books

- Rockwell's "saucer" method needs no bottom heat. Pack cuttings into sand in a deep, watertight dish and keep the sand saturated like mud, in a warm, light place. Bailey describes the same trick with a plate of sand filled to the brim with water.
- For new plants in midsummer, Rockwell cuts a shoot nearly through, leaves it attached for about eight days until it forms a callus, then pots it.
- Bailey's hardwood cuttings of shrubs such as currants and grapes are 6 to 10 inches long. They are taken from late summer to spring, stored over winter in moss, sand or sawdust, and set upright with only one or two buds above the soil.
- Bailey warns that some shrubs, such as weigela, die if the tops of their cuttings are clipped.
- Bailey also describes serpentine layering of grapevines, mound layering of gooseberries, and pot or "air" layering, where a girdled stem is wrapped in a pot of soil or moss.
- Kains rooted nearly 100 plants by layering from a single large sage, and took more than 100 cuttings from the same plant.

## Safety and modern practice

- Most growers now use a sterile rooting mix of perlite, vermiculite or coarse sand with peat or coir instead of builders' sand, which can carry salts or contaminants. Rockwell's coal ashes for drainage should be replaced with clean gravel.
- Rooting hormone powder is an optional modern aid. Wear gloves and follow the label.
- Clean your knife between plants to avoid spreading disease, and always cut away from your hand.
- Many recent named varieties are protected by plant patents or trademarks. Propagating them for sale may be restricted, so check the plant tag.
