# Harvest and dry culinary herbs

Dried herbs keep their flavor only if they are cut at the right moment and dried gently. The flavor comes from volatile oils that are richest just before flowering and that heat drives off. Kains and the compilers of Vaughan's cook book both explain how to catch the oils at their peak and keep them.

## What you need

- Sharp scissors or a knife, and a shallow basket
- Trays covered with clean, stout paper, or wire-bottomed trays
- A warm, airy room, attic or kitchen; or a fruit dryer (dehydrator) for large batches
- Airtight glass jars, earthenware jars or metal tins, and labels
- For seed herbs: large sheets of close-woven cloth such as ticking or cotton duck, a light rod, and sieves

## Steps

1. Harvest leafy herbs when the plants look ready to flower. Cut in the morning as soon as the dew has dried and before the day turns hot, because the oils fade as the sun climbs.
2. Pick parsley differently. Take the mature, deep-green leaves every week or so; this also keeps new leaves coming and delays flowering.
3. Wash the cut stems to remove all dirt, then let every bit of water evaporate. Spread them in a shallow basket or on a wire tray in a breeze. Give the air less force as they dry so they don't blow away.
4. For a small amount, hang loose bunches in a warm, dry attic or kitchen. Kains prefers spreading thin layers of leaves on paper-covered trays in the sun or in a warm kitchen with good air flow.
5. Turn the leaves once a day until the soft parts are crisp.
6. For large amounts, spread the herbs thinly on wire-bottomed trays in a fruit dryer. Keep the temperature below 120°F. Put the freshest trays at the top and move them down toward the heat as they dry.
7. Never try to hurry drying with higher heat. Kains once lost an entire crop of parsley in a gas oven and says the flavor "was literally cooked out of the leaves."
8. Rub the dry leaves between your hands, throw out the stalks and hard parts, and store the crumbled leaves in airtight glass, earthenware or tin in a cool place. If there is the slightest trace of moisture, dry them further first, or they will mold.
9. Label and date each jar. Kains found that even sealed sage was generally useless within two years, so use up old stock.
10. For seed herbs such as dill, anise and caraway, cut the heads as they ripen but before the seeds drop. Spread them thinly on cloth in a warm, open shed. In about a week, beat out the seeds gently on a sheet laid over short grass. Sieve them, then dry them in a very thin layer, turning daily, for one to two weeks before storing.

## From the source books

- Vaughan's cook book gives a calendar for drying: parsley, mint and tarragon in June or July; thyme, marjoram and savory in July and August; basil and sage in August and September.
- The two books disagree on heat. Vaughan's says quick drying with artificial heat loses less flavor. Kains warns that too much heat cooks it out.
- Kains says paper or pasteboard packets ruin dried herbs, because the oils escape through the paper until the herb is "as valueless... as ordinary hay." Sage, he notes, is the easiest to spoil.
- Both books also preserve herbs as flavored vinegar. Kains packs fresh leaves in stoppered jars of good vinegar for a week or two. Vaughan's covers basil leaves with cider vinegar for 14 days.

## Safety and modern practice

- A modern food dehydrator set around 95 to 115°F works well for leafy herbs and matches Kains's warning to keep the heat low.
- Herb vinegars made with clean, dry herbs keep best refrigerated. Never store fresh herbs or garlic in oil at room temperature, because of the risk of botulism. Refrigerate herb oils and use them within a few days, or freeze them.
- Harvest only herbs you planted or can identify with certainty, and never from roadsides or ground treated with chemicals.
- Glass jars kept in a dark cupboard away from the stove keep color and flavor longest.
