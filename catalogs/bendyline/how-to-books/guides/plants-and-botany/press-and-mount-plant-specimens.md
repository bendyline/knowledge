# Press and mount plant specimens

A pressed and labeled specimen, or herbarium sheet, lets you study a plant's leaves, flowers and seed pods long after the season has passed. Mary Rogers Miller calls making a collection "an extremely simple job" that needs only cheap materials, patience with the daily drying, and a careful label.

## What you need

- A dozen or so newspapers, cut into half sheets
- Two or three dozen driers, 12 by 18 inches, of cheap blotting paper or carpet felt
- Two boards, 12 by 18 inches
- A stone of 20 to 30 pounds, or a strap press (see the source notes)
- Heavy white mounting paper, 11½ by 16¼ inches
- Glue, a small brush, one dry cloth and one damp cloth
- Labels and a field notebook
- Folders of manila paper (Miller's "genus covers"), 11¾ by 16½ inches folded

## Steps

1. Collect sparingly. Take only what you need, leave rare plants alone, and don't uproot neighboring plants. Miller says "a live plant is better than a dead one."
2. Give each specimen a number and record where and when you found it, and anything you noticed, in the notebook.
3. Lay the plant in a folded half sheet of newspaper as it grew. Spread the leaves so they don't pile on one another, and don't force a naturally drooping flower upright.
4. Build the stack on one board: two or more driers, a folded specimen, then one or two driers. Use extra driers next to juicy plants. Repeat until the day's collecting is done or the pile gets unsteady.
5. Finish with a drier, put the second board on top, and weight it with the stone.
6. Change the driers every day. Lift each folded newspaper, specimen and all, onto fresh driers without opening it.
7. Expect most plants to be fully dry in 7 to 10 days. Any moisture left will make them mold after mounting. Dried specimens are brittle, so handle them gently.
8. Mount the specimen. Lay it on a sheet to plan its position, then turn it face down on the newspaper. Brush glue lightly over the back of the stems, leaves and flowers, flip it onto the sheet, and press lightly. Place a small specimen a little below the center.
9. Glue a label in the lower right corner. Give the plant's name (Gibson suggests both common and botanical names), the collector, the date, the locality, and any other useful facts.
10. File the sheets in folders, grouping closely related plants together as you learn to recognize their families.

## From the source books

- Gibson's camping manual reproduces Dan Beard's homemade "Vreeland press." The stack sits on a bottom board with a hinged lever; a strap pulled tight around the lever and the board's handle keeps the lever bent. This maintains steady pressure as the plants shrink.
- Miller quotes Liberty Hyde Bailey: "Learn first to classify plants; names will follow." She suggests grouping plants as sunflower-like, mint-like, pea-like and so on before you reach for a key.
- Miller encourages collecting the same plant at other stages, such as a violet in seed or a trillium in fruit, to answer questions a flowering specimen can't.
- For young children she suggests blueprint (sun-print) paper and printing-ink leaf prints, made with a roller, as quick ways to record leaf shapes.
- Gibson's text gives the standard sheet size as "1-1/2 X 16-1/2" inches. That is evidently a misprint for about 11½ by 16½, close to Miller's 11½ by 16¼.

## Safety and modern practice

- Get permission before collecting on private land, and check the rules for parks and public lands, where collecting is often banned or needs a permit. Never collect protected or endangered species.
- Learn to recognize poison ivy, poison oak and poison sumac before you collect, and wear gloves if unsure.
- For a collection meant to last, use acid-free mounting paper and archival glue or gummed linen tape instead of ordinary glue and newspaper.
- Herbaria commonly freeze dried specimens for several days before storage to kill insect pests.
- Bag invasive weeds securely so their seeds don't spread.
