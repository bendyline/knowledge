# Grow hardy ferns in a shady garden

Hardy ferns can turn a dark corner, such as a north or east wall or a narrow side yard, into the greenest part of a garden. Frances Bardswell made a fern bank against a bare east-facing wall in a new suburban garden, and it became "the best part of the garden." Frances Theodora Parsons's notes on where wild ferns grow help you match each kind to the right spot.

## What you need

- A shady, sheltered site out of drafts
- Drainage material for the bottom of the bed: broken pots (crocks) or broken tiles
- Leaf mold, good loam and a little peat
- Stones, tree stumps and gnarled roots (Bardswell prefers oak, which resists decay)
- Hardy ferns from a nursery
- A watering can or sprinkler with a fine spray
- Fallen leaves for a winter covering

## Steps

1. Choose shade, but not under large trees. Bardswell notes that trees take most of the moisture and keep off the rain. She quotes a figure of about 123 tons of water drawn up by one fair-sized oak in a season.
2. Protect the spot from wind. Hardy ferns stand cold well but "hate draught." Bardswell curved the ends of her border and planted shrubs to make a sheltered corner.
3. Dig the bed out and put a deep layer of drainage material in the bottom. Ferns need plenty of water but must never be waterlogged.
4. Build the bed up with leaf mold, loam and a little peat. Shape it into small hills, hollows and crannies, and set stones, stumps and roots among them.
5. Let the bed settle for a month or two before planting.
6. Choose ferns to suit each spot, following where they grow wild. Parsons finds the Royal, Sensitive and Virginia Chain ferns in wet meadows. The Christmas fern grows on rocky wooded hillsides, and the Hay-scented fern along roadsides and in partly shaded or open pastures. Spleenworts and the Walking Leaf grow on moist, shaded rocks, and the Purple Cliff Brake on dry limestone.
7. Plant in spring or autumn. In her English garden, Bardswell found February the best month to divide and replant established clumps.
8. Keep the bed "always dewy, fresh, and sprinkled." Use a fine spray rather than a hose. Bardswell found a few roadside ferns that would grow only with soft water.
9. In autumn, leave the fronds to wither naturally and scatter dead leaves over the bed.
10. In spring, top-dress with fresh leaf mold. Bardswell's advice for fern boxes is to remove last year's dead fronds only once the new fronds appear, since the old ones protect them.
11. Let shade-loving wildflowers settle among the ferns. Bardswell welcomed wood violets, primroses, wood sorrel, wild hyacinths, wood anemones and alpine strawberries.

## From the source books

- Bardswell urges buying ferns from growers rather than digging them wild, because "the careless Fern-hunter" had stripped many country places. Of common kinds, she took only part of a plant, never the whole root.
- To bring on a flush of fresh, pale-green fronds, she let the bank go dry for a day or two, then gave it a "deluge" overnight.
- For a north-east or north-west window, she suggests a window box of ferns alone. Hart's-tongue, male fern and lady fern grow well, and ferns want "more drainage and more water than flowers."
- Parsons traces the season: Osmunda fiddleheads in late April, most ferns in full leaf by mid-July, and the evergreen Christmas fern, evergreen wood fern and polypody still green in November.
- Bardswell's town ferns tolerated London smoke and fog, which made them one of the best plants for city gardens of her day.

## Safety and modern practice

- Many native ferns are protected by law. American hart's-tongue fern, for example, is federally listed as threatened. Never dig ferns from the wild; buy nursery-propagated plants.
- Use compost or leaf mold in place of peat. Peat extraction damages bog habitats.
- Choose ferns native to your region where possible. Avoid invasive kinds; Japanese climbing fern, for instance, is invasive in the southeastern United States.
- Bracken spreads aggressively and is toxic to livestock. Keep it out of small gardens.
- Wear gloves when handling leaf mold, compost and old wood.
