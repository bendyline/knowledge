# Care for houseplants in a sunny window

Most houseplant troubles come from four conditions: too little light, the wrong temperature, dry air and careless watering. Rockwell's *Gardening Indoors and Under Glass* and Bardswell's *Book of Town & Window Gardening* give simple rules for each.

## What you need

- The sunniest, broadest window available
- Shelves 6 to 12 inches wide on brackets, each with a narrow raised strip along the edges, painted and covered an inch or two deep with coarse gravel or fine pebbles
- A watering can with a fine rose, or a rubber plant sprinkler for spraying leaves
- A pail or tub deep enough to sink a pot to its rim
- A soft, dry cloth
- Pans of water for radiators or other warm spots
- Soft water at room temperature

## Steps

1. Put flowering plants such as geraniums and heliotrope in the sunniest spots. Foliage plants such as palms, ferns and decorative begonias need less sun. In winter you can hardly give too much light, but hang a very thin curtain if early-fall or late-spring sun is too strong.
2. Stand the pots on the gravel-covered shelves instead of in saucers. Rockwell found gravel better than saucers, especially for small pots.
3. Turn each plant half around every few days, conveniently each time you water, so it doesn't grow lopsided toward the glass.
4. Keep the room at 65 to 75°F by day and 50 to 55°F at night. An occasional 45°F night does no harm, but frequent chills check growth.
5. On freezing nights, move plants back from the windows so no leaf or flower touches the glass. Rockwell suggests hanging newspaper inside the panes, leaving an air space between paper and glass.
6. Fight dry winter air by keeping water evaporating near the heat, and air the room every day. Avoid direct drafts by opening a window in an adjoining room and ventilating gradually.
7. Water only when the soil is beginning to get on the "dry side," then water thoroughly until it runs into the saucer. Don't leave it standing there. In winter water on bright mornings; in early fall and late spring, in the evening.
8. Give a real soaking, never driblets. Bardswell's best method is to stand the pot in a pail with the water over the rim. Don't let any pot get dust-dry.
9. Keep leaves clean. Spray them, and wipe large leaves such as palms and rubber plants with a soft dry cloth. Never use oil.
10. Pick off dead or diseased leaves and faded flowers at once, and don't let flowering plants set seed. Prune untidy branches without fear.
11. Let plants rest in winter: very little water, and no repotting or feeding. When a growing plant has filled its pot with roots, feed it with weak liquid fertilizer.
12. After the danger of frost, harden plants off on a sheltered porch, then sink the pots to their rims in an outdoor bed for summer. Lift and turn them every week or so so they don't root into the ground.

## From the source books

- Rockwell describes pale, lanky growth and a "washed-out look" as the sign of too little light. He adds that a room too dry for plants is unfit for people too.
- Rockwell fed plants with "tea" made by steeping half-rotted cow manure in water, and gave a mixture of nitrate salts as a chemical alternative.
- Bardswell recommends letting hard tap water stand in shallow pans to warm and soften before use. She credits soap and water with keeping insects away, and calls a daily wash "the best thing in the world for town plants."
- For a frosty night Rockwell suggests leaving a kerosene lamp or stove burning in the room.

## Safety and modern practice

- Never leave an unvented kerosene or gas heater, or any open flame, burning indoors overnight. They can produce carbon monoxide and start fires. Move plants to a warmer room instead.
- Use a commercial houseplant fertilizer at the label rate, usually only in spring and summer, rather than manure tea or homemade chemical mixes.
- Many common houseplants, including dieffenbachia, philodendron and lilies, are toxic to children or pets. Check before you buy.
- Overwatering kills more houseplants than drought. Use pots with drainage holes and check the soil with a finger before watering.
- If no window is bright enough, an LED grow light on a timer works well.
