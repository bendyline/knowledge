# Cook eggs in the shell, soft or hard

Egg white begins to set at about 134°F and is fully set, soft and tender, by about 160°F. Water at a full boil is much hotter, so it toughens the white before the yolk is done. The old cooking-school method is to cook eggs in water that is very hot but not boiling. Soft eggs come out evenly creamy, and hard eggs come out firm without turning rubbery.

## What you need

- Fresh eggs
- A saucepan with a lid, holding about 1 pint (2 cups) of water per egg
- A spoon for lowering eggs in, and a slotted spoon for lifting them out
- A bowl of cold water for hard-cooked eggs
- A thermometer (optional). Fannie Farmer holds eggs at about 175°F, and Mrs. Rorer at 170°F

## Steps

1. Wipe or rinse the eggs just before cooking. Do not wash eggs you plan to store, since washing removes their natural protective coating.
2. Bring the water to a boil, using about a pint for each egg so the pan holds its heat.
3. Lower the eggs in gently with a spoon. They should be well covered.
4. At once take the pan off the burner, or turn the heat to its lowest setting, and cover it. From here on the water should stay hot but never bubble.
5. For soft-cooked, "jellied" eggs, leave them in the water 5 minutes. The Woman's Institute says they come out "the consistency of jelly all the way through." Fannie Farmer allows 6 to 8 minutes for a softer-centered egg held just below boiling.
6. Lift soft eggs out and serve them at once.
7. For hard-cooked eggs, keep them in the hot, non-boiling water for 40 to 45 minutes.
8. Serve hard eggs hot, or move them straight into cold water and let them cool before peeling. Quick chilling helps keep a dark ring from forming around the yolk.

## From the source books

- The Woman's Institute course prefers this method to boiling for 3 to 5 minutes. Boiling, it says, cooks the white next to the shell "very hard" while the yolk stays liquid.
- Mrs. S. T. Rorer, in *Many Ways for Cooking Eggs*, "steams" eggs in the shell. She covers four eggs with 2 quarts of water, lets them stand covered 10 minutes, then serves them in a bowl of hot water. One egg in a quart of water needs 5 minutes, and more eggs need a longer stand.
- Rorer and Farmer both test freshness by putting an egg in water: a fresh one sinks. Rorer adds that one standing on end is only fairly fresh, and one that floats should be avoided.
- Farmer offers a gentler start for soft eggs. Put them in cold water and heat it slowly; when it reaches boiling, they are soft-cooked.
- Farmer calls eggs cooked at a hard boil "tough and horny" and says they "should never be served."
- For poached eggs, the Woman's Institute uses the same principle. It adds a teaspoon of salt or vinegar per pint of water, brings it to a boil, and slides the eggs in once the water has stopped bubbling.

## Safety and modern practice

- Eggs can carry Salmonella. Soft-cooked eggs with runny yolks are not recommended for young children, older adults, pregnant women or anyone with a weakened immune system unless the eggs are pasteurized. For full safety, cook until both white and yolk are firm.
- Refrigerate hard-cooked eggs within 2 hours of cooking and use them within about a week.
- Many cooks today use a quicker variation for hard-cooked eggs. Start them in cold water, bring it just to a boil, then cover the pan off the heat for about 12 minutes for large eggs. Chill them in ice water afterward.
- Use a slotted spoon, and keep pot handles turned inward so a pan of hot water cannot be knocked over.
