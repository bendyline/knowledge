# Bake a loaf of yeast bread

A plain white loaf needs only flour, liquid, yeast, salt, a little sugar and a little fat. Yeast makes gas, kneading builds the gluten that traps it, and baking sets the risen loaf. Most of the skill is in temperature: lukewarm liquid, a warm rise, and a properly hot oven.

## What you need

For two large loaves or three small ones, from the Woman's Institute quick-process recipe:

- 2 tablespoons fat (butter, lard or oil)
- 2 tablespoons sugar
- 1 tablespoon salt
- 2 cakes compressed (fresh) yeast, or 1 cake for a slower rise. One cake is about the same as one ¼-ounce packet of dry yeast
- 1 quart lukewarm liquid: water, scalded-and-cooled milk, or a mix
- 3 quarts (12 cups) flour, plus about 1 cup more for kneading
- A large bowl, a floured board, greased loaf pans, a clean cloth, and a wire rack

## Steps

1. Put the fat, sugar and salt in a large bowl.
2. Dissolve the yeast in a few tablespoons of the lukewarm liquid. Hot liquid kills yeast and cold liquid slows it, so aim for "warm to the fingers."
3. Add the yeast and the rest of the liquid to the bowl. Sift in the flour gradually and stir until you have a dough stiff enough to turn out. Add the last cup or two with care so the dough does not get too stiff.
4. Turn the dough onto a lightly floured board and knead it. Fold the far edge toward you, push down and away with the heels of your hands, turn the dough, and repeat.
5. Keep kneading, dusting with flour only as needed, until the dough is smooth and elastic and no longer sticky. It should spring back when pressed. Too much flour makes hard, close bread.
6. Return the dough to the bowl, brush the top with melted fat, and cover it tightly.
7. Let it rise in a warm spot, about 80 to 86°F, until doubled in bulk. To judge this, press a small piece of dough into a glass, mark where double would be, and set the glass beside the bowl.
8. Turn the dough out, knead briefly, and divide it into pieces sized for your pans.
9. Knead each piece until the large bubbles are gone. Then form it into a smooth roll as long as the pan, even in thickness from end to end, with the smooth side on top.
10. Set each loaf in a greased pan, cover, and let it rise until doubled and level with the top of the pan.
11. Heat the oven to about 400°F. If you have no thermometer, white paper or a layer of flour should turn light brown in 5 minutes.
12. Bake the loaves with space around each pan. The crust should start browning in about 15 minutes. A small loaf takes 50 to 60 minutes, and a large one longer.
13. The bread is done when it is evenly brown all over and has shrunk from the sides of the pan. Turn the loaves out at once onto a rack and cool them uncovered before storing.

## From the source books

- The Woman's Institute course describes a slower "long process." You set a sponge of liquid, yeast and half the flour overnight at 65-70°F, using only ½ cake of yeast, then add the rest of the flour in the morning. Some cooks believed bread made this way "tastes better and keeps longer."
- For a steady warm rise, the course sets the bowl in a covered pan of water at 110-115°F and adds hot water now and then.
- The course warns that too short a final rise gives heavy, fine-grained bread, and too long a rise gives coarse bread with large holes. Fannie Farmer adds that pans set in too hot a place give "a heavy streak" near the bottom of the loaf.
- Farmer brushes loaves with milk before baking for a darker crust, or with melted butter three minutes before they come out for a tender one. She cools loaves uncovered for a crisp crust, or under a towel for a soft one.

## Safety and modern practice

- Do not taste raw dough. Raw flour can carry harmful bacteria, which baking kills.
- An instant-read thermometer takes the guesswork out of doneness. Most lean white loaves are done at about 190-200°F in the center.
- Scalded milk must cool to lukewarm, about 105-110°F, before the yeast goes in.
