# Make old-fashioned chocolate fudge

Classic fudge is a cooked sugar syrup that you cool, then beat, so it sets into a mass of tiny sugar crystals. Creamy fudge depends on three things. Cook the syrup to the right temperature, avoid starting crystals too early by stirring or splashing, and cool it nearly cold before you beat. Fudge that goes grainy or never sets almost always missed one of those.

## What you need

From the Woman's Institute's "Fudge No. 2," for about an 8-inch pan:

- 2 cups sugar
- ¾ cup milk
- ¼ cup corn syrup, which helps keep the fudge smooth
- 2 tablespoons butter, plus extra for the pan
- A pinch of salt
- 2 squares (2 ounces) unsweetened chocolate, chopped or shaved
- 1 teaspoon vanilla
- A heavy saucepan of 2 to 3 quarts, a candy thermometer, a cup of cold tap water for testing, a damp cloth or pastry brush, a wooden spoon, and a buttered pan

## Steps

1. Choose a cool, dry day if you can. The Woman's Institute warns that warm or damp weather slows cooling and spoils results.
2. Combine the milk, sugar, corn syrup, butter and salt in the saucepan. Stir over moderate heat only until the sugar dissolves and the mixture boils.
3. Once it boils, stop stirring. Wipe any sugar crystals off the sides of the pan with a cloth or brush dipped in water.
4. Boil until a few drops dropped into cold water form a very soft ball. Then add the chocolate.
5. Keep boiling to 236-238°F. In the water test, the drops should gather into a soft ball that holds together and can be handled. Test often near the end, take the pan off the heat while you test, and use fresh water each time.
6. Carry the pan gently to a cool spot and set it down without stirring or jarring it. Any agitation now makes the fudge grainy.
7. Let it cool, untouched, until no heat is felt when you lay the backs of your fingers lightly on the surface. Do not put it in the refrigerator.
8. Add the vanilla and beat steadily with a wooden spoon until the glossy syrup thickens and turns creamy.
9. If you are adding nuts, stir them in as it begins to turn creamy. When it is thick but can still be poured, spread it into the buttered pan.
10. When it has set, cut it into squares.

## From the source books

- The Woman's Institute table puts fudge at the "firm ball" stage, 238-240°F. It sets soft ball for cream centers at 234-236°F and hard ball for caramels at 246-248°F. Its own fudge recipes stop between 236 and 238°F, so a softer finish is fine.
- The course says to time the boiling only by test, never by the clock, because pan size and how hard the syrup boils change the timing.
- Mary M. Wright's *Candy-Making at Home* gives a simpler fudge: 2 cups sugar, 1 cup milk, 1 to 2 tablespoons butter and 2 ounces chocolate, cooked to about 235°F. "The secret of good fudge lies in the beating," she writes. Some cooks beat from the moment it leaves the fire, while others wait until it is nearly cool.
- Wright makes maple fudge with maple sugar and walnuts at about 240°F. She also makes layer fudge by pouring divinity between two layers of chocolate fudge.
- The Woman's Institute's two-layer fudge divides the syrup before adding chocolate, so one layer is white and one dark. Its brown-sugar fudge stirs in chopped peanuts as it turns creamy.
- If crystals form in the clear syrup before it cools, the course advises cooling it longer rather than stirring at once.

## Safety and modern practice

- Boiling sugar syrup is far hotter than boiling water and sticks to skin. Use a deep pan, keep children back, and never taste the syrup from the spoon.
- For the water test, drop the syrup into the water first, then gather it once it has cooled there.
- Check your thermometer in boiling water. If it reads, for example, 210°F instead of 212°F, aim 2°F lower than the recipe. Cooks at high altitude should make the same adjustment.
- Store fudge airtight at cool room temperature, or refrigerate it for longer keeping.
