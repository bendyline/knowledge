# Make cream of vegetable soup

A cream soup is a thin white sauce flavored and thickened with a vegetable, either puréed or in small pieces. Learn the one base, and you can make soup from potatoes, celery, peas, corn, asparagus, spinach, onions or tomatoes, including leftovers or canned vegetables. The same white sauce, made thicker, also dresses cooked vegetables.

## What you need

For about four servings:

- 1 pint (2 cups) milk, or part milk and part stock or vegetable cooking water
- 2 tablespoons butter
- 2 tablespoons flour (use 1 tablespoon if your vegetable purée is thick, such as potato)
- 1 teaspoon salt and 1/8 teaspoon pepper
- 1/2 to 1 cup cooked vegetable, pressed through a sieve or ricer, or finely chopped
- A slice of onion (optional) to flavor the milk
- A heavy saucepan or double boiler, a whisk or wooden spoon, and a sieve

## Steps

1. Cook the vegetable in a little boiling salted water until soft. Save the cooking water.
2. Press the vegetable through a sieve or ricer to make a smooth purée. Tender vegetables such as peas or corn can also be left in small pieces.
3. Heat the milk with a slice of onion until steaming, then remove the onion. If you like, replace part of the milk with stock or the vegetable water.
4. In the saucepan, melt the butter over low heat and stir in the flour, salt and pepper until smooth.
5. Pour in the hot milk gradually, stirring constantly so no lumps form.
6. Cook, stirring, until the sauce thickens. Thin white sauce for soup should be about as thick as heavy cream.
7. Stir in the vegetable purée and heat the soup through without letting it boil hard.
8. Taste, adjust the seasoning, and serve hot, with croutons or crisp crackers if you like.

## From the source books

- The Woman's Institute's potato soup cooks 2 medium potatoes with 2 slices of onion and a sprig of parsley, then mashes them. Its sauce uses 1 cup milk, 1 cup potato water, 2 tablespoons butter and only 1 tablespoon flour.
- Fannie Farmer simmers 3 cups of celery in 1 pint of water until soft, then rubs it through a sieve. She adds 2½ cups of milk scalded with onion, and binds the soup with 3 tablespoons butter and ¼ cup flour. Outer stalks are fine for this.
- For the Woman's Institute's tomato soup, sieve and heat 1 cup of canned tomatoes and stir in 1/8 teaspoon baking soda. Pour them slowly into a sauce made with 3 tablespoons each of butter and flour, stirring fast. If it starts to curdle, beat it hard with an egg beater.
- For the Woman's Institute's onion soup, brown 4 sliced onions in 2 tablespoons of butter. Add them to a sauce of 2 tablespoons butter, 2 tablespoons flour and 2½ cups milk.
- The Woman's Institute course makes the sauce another way. It heats the milk, salt and butter in a double boiler, then stirs in the flour mixed to a smooth paste with a little reserved cold milk.
- The course suggests cooking extra vegetables one day, so some are "left over and ready for soup the next day."
- It adds a pinch of soda to asparagus soup, too, against curdling, and combines the asparagus with the sauce just before serving.
- The course explains that a cream soup supplies nourishment rather than whetting the appetite. It makes a good main dish for a light meal, but is too heavy to start a big dinner.
- For vegetables served as a side dish, the course uses a "medium" white sauce, with half the liquid: 2 tablespoons each of butter and flour to 1 cup of milk.

## Safety and modern practice

- A blender or immersion blender can replace the sieve. When blending hot soup, fill the jar no more than half full and vent the lid under a folded towel, so steam cannot blow it off.
- Milk scorches easily. Keep the heat moderate, stir along the bottom of the pan, or use a double boiler as the old course did.
- Cool leftover soup quickly in shallow containers, and refrigerate it within 2 hours.
