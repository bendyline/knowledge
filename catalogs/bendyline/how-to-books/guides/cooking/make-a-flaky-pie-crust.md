# Make a flaky pie crust

Plain pastry is just flour, salt, solid fat and cold water. Flakiness comes from keeping the fat in small, cold pieces that melt and puff in the oven. Tenderness comes from using as little water and as little handling as possible. One batch makes a double-crust pie or two single shells.

## What you need

From the Woman's Institute "plain pastry" recipe, for one double-crust pie:

- 1½ cups flour, pastry flour if you have it
- 1 teaspoon salt
- 1/3 cup cold solid shortening: lard, butter, vegetable shortening, or a mix
- 1/4 to 3/8 cup ice water
- A bowl, two table knives or a pastry blender, a flour sifter, a rolling pin, a fork, and a pie pan

## Steps

1. Chill the fat and the water before you start. Everything should be as cold as possible.
2. Sift the flour and salt into the bowl.
3. Add the fat and cut it in with two knives, drawn past each other, until the pieces are about the size of small peas. Knives keep your warm hands off the fat.
4. Push the mixture into a heap, make a hollow in the center, and pour in the water in a thin stream. Stir with a knife as you pour.
5. Stop adding water the moment the mixture barely clings together. It should not stick to the bowl or the knife. The less water you use, the flakier the crust.
6. Divide the dough in half and press each half into a round with your fingers.
7. Flour the board lightly. Roll one round out from the center with light strokes, to about 1/8 inch thick, keeping it as round as you can.
8. Roll the pastry loosely onto the rolling pin and unroll it over the pan. Press it gently against the bottom and sides without stretching it, then trim the edge.
9. For a shell baked empty, prick the bottom and sides all over with a fork so air pockets cannot push it up. Bake until golden brown.
10. For a double-crust pie, add the filling but do not prick the bottom. Roll out the top crust and cut a few slits for steam. Moisten the bottom rim with water.
11. Lay the top crust on loosely, without stretching it, and press the edges together. Trim, then flute with your fingers or press with fork tines.
12. Bake in a hot oven until the crust is golden brown, neither pale nor scorched. For custard or slow-cooking fillings, start very hot and lower the heat once the crust has set.

## From the source books

- The Woman's Institute course allows anywhere from one-sixth as much fat as flour by measure, for "economy paste," up to half, for puff paste. Ordinary pie crust falls in between.
- The course calls hands the worst way to mix in fat, because they warm it. Two knives are the most satisfactory and the most sanitary.
- For juicy berry or cherry pies, the course rolls a small paper funnel and sets it in one of the top-crust slits as a steam vent, so the juice does not boil over.
- Fannie Farmer's *Boston Cooking-School Cook Book* says lard makes a more tender crust and butter a better-flavored one. Her "plain paste" uses ¼ cup of each with 1½ cups of flour. She rubs in the lard first, then rolls and folds the butter into the dough in layers, as for puff paste.
- Farmer's "paste with lard" spreads a little extra lard on the rolled dough and rolls it up like a jelly roll before cutting off a piece for the pan. She dots the top crust with lard for a flaky look, and brushes it with milk for a glaze.
- Farmer warns that "worked-over pastry is never as satisfactory" as a fresh rolling. Cut the round close to size and avoid rerolling scraps.
- Period cooks baked pastry in an oven "about as hot as it can be made," 500-600°F by the Woman's Institute's figure.

## Safety and modern practice

- Modern recipes usually bake pie pastry at about 400-450°F. An empty shell takes roughly 12 to 15 minutes, and a filled pie usually needs a lower temperature later in the baking.
- Custard and pumpkin pies contain eggs and milk. Cool them, then refrigerate them within two hours.
- Do not eat raw pie dough, since raw flour can carry harmful bacteria.
- Set pies on a rimmed baking sheet to catch drips, which can smoke or catch fire on the oven floor.
