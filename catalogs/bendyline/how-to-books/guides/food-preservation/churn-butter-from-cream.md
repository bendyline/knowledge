# Churn butter from cream

Butter is the fat of cream, beaten until its tiny globules stick together and separate from the watery buttermilk. Any churn works on the same principle, whether a barrel, dasher, paddle or simple jar. Agitate the cream at the right temperature, and keep doing it until the fat gathers. Good homemade butter depends on getting the temperature right, stopping at the right moment, and washing out every trace of buttermilk so the butter keeps sweet.

## What you need

- Fresh cream, sweet or lightly soured ("ripened")
- A churn or other closed vessel big enough that the cream fills only about a quarter to a third of it
- A dairy thermometer
- Very cold water, plus some ice in hot weather
- A wooden bowl and a wooden paddle or butter worker, scalded and then chilled in cold water
- A fine strainer or sieve
- Fine, pure salt, about 1 ounce per pound of butter, or to taste

## Steps

1. Scald every utensil, then chill the wooden bowl and paddle in cold water so the butter will not stick.
2. Bring the cream to churning temperature: about 58°F in warm weather, 60°F in spring and fall, and 62-68°F in cold weather. Cool it in cold water, or warm it gently by setting the churn in a warm room.
3. Put the cream in the churn, filling it no more than about a quarter to a third full. The cream needs room to move.
4. Start churning slowly and work up to a steady pace of about 40 to 50 strokes a minute. Keep going without stopping, since pauses set the process back. Expect 20 to 40 minutes.
5. Watch for the "break." The cream thickens, then suddenly separates into yellow flecks and thin buttermilk.
6. Stop as soon as the butter forms grains about the size of wheat kernels. Do not churn it into one big lump.
7. Add a little cold water to firm the grains, then drain off the buttermilk through a strainer. Save the buttermilk for baking.
8. Pour cold water over the grains, agitate them gently, and drain. Repeat until the water runs off as clear as it went in.
9. Turn the butter into the chilled wooden bowl. If it feels soft, cover it with a cloth wrung out in cold water and let it firm for about 30 minutes. Burch works it at about 56°F.
10. Press the butter flat with the paddle, sprinkle on some of the salt, and fold the edges back to the center. Press out again, salt again, and repeat two or three times until the salt is even and no beads of buttermilk show.
11. Stop working once the butter is compact and smooth. Too much working makes it greasy or crumbly. Pack it into a crock or mold, or wrap it, and keep it cold.

## From the source books

- Frederick Burch, in *ABC Butter Making*, warns against "lightning" churns that bring butter in five minutes. He says cream churned that fast tastes cheesy and soon turns rancid.
- Burch adds that half-worked butter, with "great drops of buttermilk standing all over it," may be 30 to 40 percent water and keeps only a short time.
- A Vermont dairyman quoted by Burch thins the cream with 3 to 6 quarts of water. He uses cold spring water or ice to cool it, or water up to 120°F to warm it, and washes the butter "until the water runs clear."
- Catharine Beecher stresses frequent churning, so the cream never turns bitter, and strict cleanliness. She works butter with a wooden paddle, never the hands. In hot weather she sets the churn in a tub of cold water and churns steadily but not fast, since fast churning makes soft butter.
- Beecher says butter sometimes will not come because air is shut out of the churn. She works out the buttermilk in one session: "Never work it three times."

## Safety and modern practice

- Use pasteurized cream. Raw cream can carry harmful bacteria. If you ripen cream, keep it clean, covered and cool, and throw it out if it smells off.
- Refrigerate homemade butter and use it within a week or two, or freeze it. Leftover buttermilk makes it spoil sooner.
- Skip Beecher's saltpeter (potassium nitrate) brine for long keeping. Salt and refrigeration are enough.
- Beecher warns that buttermilk dissolves some pottery glazes. Old glazes can contain lead, so store dairy only in glass, stainless steel or modern lead-free stoneware.
