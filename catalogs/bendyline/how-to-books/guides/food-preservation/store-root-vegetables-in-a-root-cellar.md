# Store root vegetables in a root cellar

A cold, damp, dark space keeps root crops alive and crisp for months after harvest, with no canning or freezing. The rules are simple: store only sound produce, cool it quickly, hold it just above freezing, and keep the air moist for roots but dry for onions and squash. A walk-in cellar is ideal, but an insulated basement corner, a buried barrel or a garden pit will also work.

## What you need

- A storage place that stays between 32 and 40°F: an outdoor cellar mostly below ground, or an insulated, vented basement room
- A reliable thermometer
- Boxes, crates, crocks or barrels, plus clean sand, sphagnum moss or peat, or perforated plastic bags
- Slatted shelves or a slatted floor for air and drainage
- Late-season beets, carrots, turnips, rutabagas, parsnips, cabbage and potatoes

## Steps

1. Leave root crops in the ground until late fall, when the weather is cool and steady. For potatoes, wait until the vines have died down and the skins have hardened.
2. Dig on a dry day. Handle the crop gently, because every bruise or cut becomes a starting point for rot.
3. Trim carrot, turnip and rutabaga tops to about 1/2 inch above the crown. Leave 2 to 3 inches of top on beets, and keep their long taproots, so they do not bleed.
4. Sort everything. Set aside damaged, diseased, very large or immature roots for eating right away.
5. If you wash the roots, let them dry again. Cool them before they go into storage, and keep them out of drying winds.
6. Spread potatoes in single layers in crates or baskets for a few hours or more, so the skins dry and cure.
7. Pack carrots, beets and turnips in layers of moist sand, moss or peat in boxes, crocks or barrels. Perforated polyethylene bags or box liners also work. Either way, the roots stay crisp instead of shriveling.
8. Store roots at 32-40°F with high humidity. Keep potatoes dark, at 45-48°F for the best eating quality, or 38-40°F to hold them longest without sprouting.
9. Manage the cellar. Open the vents on cool nights and close them on warm days. If produce starts to wilt, sprinkle water on the floor.
10. Store apples and pears apart from the vegetables. Ripening fruit makes carrots bitter, and fruit picks up the odors of potatoes and onions.
11. Check the stores often. Remove anything that is decaying before it spreads, and keep rodents out.

## From the source books

- The 1977 USDA yearbook suggests a cellar dug into a bank under 2½ feet or more of soil. Another option is a barrel, drain tile or garbage can buried upright with 4 inches showing, covered with straw and a waterproof sheet.
- F. F. Rockwell's *Home Vegetable Gardening* builds a garden pit. Pile dry vegetables in a ridge 3 to 4 feet wide and cover it with straw and 4 inches of soil just as hard freezing begins. Once that layer is partly frozen, add more straw and 12 inches of earth. The pit keeps vegetables well, but it cannot be opened until spring.
- Rockwell keeps a few cabbages in a trench, heads down and roots up, under straw and soil. He stands celery upright in boxes of wet sand in a cold cellar.
- Both books say parsnips can stay in the garden all winter. The USDA authors add that roots survive steady freezing but are damaged by repeated freezing and thawing.
- The USDA authors warn that turnips and rutabagas give off odors in a basement, and that roots kept at 45°F sprout and turn woody. Potatoes held below 45°F turn sweet, and light makes them green and bitter.
- For other crops, the USDA chart lists onions as near 32°F as possible but dry. Pumpkins and winter squash need curing at 80-85°F, then dry storage at 55-60°F. Neither belongs in a damp cellar or pit.

## Safety and modern practice

- Throw away potatoes that are green, sprouting heavily or soft, or cut away all green parts. The green layer contains solanine, a natural toxin.
- A bitter taste or slimy texture means spoilage. Discard such produce instead of cooking it.
- Keep dried beans, peas and nuts dry and check them for mold. The USDA authors note that molds on stored nuts and legumes can produce harmful toxins.
- A spare refrigerator works well for small amounts. If you store an empty one, take the door off so a child cannot get trapped inside.
- Keep cellar steps and floors dry and well lit, and make sure an outdoor cellar door can be opened from inside.
