# Ferment dill pickles in a crock

Brined, or fermented, dill pickles cure by fermentation, not by heating them in vinegar. Salt draws sugar and juice out of the cucumbers, and lactic-acid bacteria turn that sugar into acid. The acid gives the pickles their sour flavor, firm bite and olive color. The process takes a few weeks of attention, and success depends on fresh cucumbers, correct brine strength, and keeping everything below the surface.

## What you need

- A stoneware crock or jar, or a glass, stainless or unchipped enamel container. A 4-gallon crock holds about 12 pounds (a quarter bushel) of cucumbers
- Pickling cucumbers picked within the last 24 hours, unwaxed, of even size
- Dill, preferably mature heads with seed, plus about 1 ounce of whole mixed pickling spice for a 4-gallon crock
- Grape leaves (optional) for the top and bottom layers
- Brine: 1 pound of pure salt and 1 pint of vinegar (4-6 percent acidity) to 2 gallons of water. Use pickling or canning salt with no iodine or anti-caking agent
- A plate or board that fits inside the crock, a weight such as a water-filled jar with its lid on, and a cloth to cover the crock

## Steps

1. Wash the cucumbers gently in cold water, lifting them out so grit does not settle back on them. Leave about 1/4 inch of stem, cut off the blossom end, and discard any that are bruised or soft.
2. Make the brine by dissolving the salt in the water and adding the vinegar. Use it cool. A hot brine would kill the bacteria the pickles need.
3. Line the bottom of the crock with grape leaves, a layer of dill and half the spice.
4. Fill the crock with cucumbers to within 2 or 3 inches of the top. Add the rest of the spice, another layer of dill, and more grape leaves.
5. Pour in enough brine to cover everything well. You will need roughly half as much brine as the volume of cucumbers.
6. Set the plate on top and weight it so every cucumber stays well under the brine. Cover the crock with a cloth.
7. Keep the crock in a warm spot. Bubbles and froth will appear as fermentation begins.
8. Check the crock every day or two and skim off the scum that forms on the surface. Left alone, it weakens the acid and softens the top layer.
9. Wait for the bubbling to stop. The pickles are cured when they are olive green and translucent all the way through, with no white, opaque centers, and taste pleasantly sour.
10. Move the cured pickles to clean glass jars and cover them with their own strained brine or a fresh batch. Refrigerate them or can them as described below.

## From the source books

- Edwin LeFevre's 1924 USDA bulletin ferments dills fastest at about 86°F, finishing in 10 days to 2 weeks. He notes that the weak brine of dill pickles cures quickly but keeps poorly unless air is shut out.
- LeFevre says soft or slippery pickles come from bacteria. It happens when pickles float above the brine or the brine is too weak, and "no treatment will restore them."
- He warns against "greening" pickles in copper kettles, because the copper compound formed is poisonous. He calls alum unnecessary when the salt and acid are right.
- For hard water, LeFevre adds a little vinegar to counter the alkalinity. The 1977 USDA yearbook boils hard water, lets it settle 24 hours and pours it off the sediment.
- The 1977 yearbook says to brine only in crocks that never held fat or milk. It also warns against waxed grocery-store cucumbers, which brine cannot penetrate, and against iodized salt, which darkens pickles.
- Older households sealed crocks under a layer of hot paraffin. LeFevre himself thought moving the pickles into sealed glass jars was the safer plan.

## Safety and modern practice

- Current tested recipes from the National Center for Home Food Preservation ferment dills at 70-75°F for about 3 to 4 weeks. Above about 80°F, pickles tend to turn soft, so LeFevre's 86°F is warmer than today's advice.
- Follow a current tested recipe's salt and vinegar amounts exactly. Do not dilute the brine. The acid it forms is what makes the pickles safe.
- Store finished pickles in the refrigerator, or process them in a boiling-water canner using a tested recipe and time adjusted for your altitude.
- Discard the batch if the pickles turn soft, slimy or off-smelling, or if mold goes below the surface layer.
- Use only food-grade containers and covers. Never use copper, brass, galvanized or iron pots with brine or vinegar.
