# Make apple jelly without added pectin

Apples and crab apples are rich in pectin and acid, the two things fruit juice needs to set into jelly, so you can make them into a clear, firm jelly with nothing but sugar. The method also works for currants, grapes and quinces. The skill is in drawing out the juice gently and stopping the boil at exactly the right moment.

## What you need

- About 8 quarts of tart apples or crab apples, ideally including some slightly underripe fruit
- Cold water, about 4 quarts for 8 quarts of apples
- Sugar: 3/4 cup per cup of juice for mild apples, 1 cup per cup for crab apples or very tart apples
- A large kettle for the fruit, and a broad, flat-bottomed kettle of 8 to 10 quarts for the jelly
- A jelly bag or several layers of damp cheesecloth, with a bowl to drip into
- A jelly or candy thermometer, a metal spoon, a skimmer, and measuring cups
- Clean jelly jars with new two-piece lids, kept hot in hot water

## Steps

1. Wash the apples, remove the stems, and clean the blossom ends well. Cut them into quarters, skins, cores and all.
2. Put the apples in the kettle and add cold water until they are barely covered. Simmer gently, without stirring, until the apples are soft.
3. Pour the fruit and liquid into the damp jelly bag and let it drip into a bowl. Do not squeeze the bag; pressing makes the jelly cloudy.
4. Measure the juice. Expect about 3 quarts from 8 quarts of apples. If you have much more, boil it down. Work in batches of 4 to 6 cups of juice.
5. Spread the sugar in a pan and warm it in a low oven, so it does not chill the juice when you add it.
6. Before you heat the juice, check the boiling point of water with your thermometer. Your target for the jelly is 8°F above that number.
7. Bring the juice to a rapid boil and boil it about 5 minutes, skimming off the scum as it rises.
8. Add the warm sugar gradually, stirring until it dissolves. Then boil hard without stirring.
9. Test often. The jelly is done when it reaches the target temperature, or when it sheets off a cool metal spoon: the last syrup forms two drops that run together and drop off as one, instead of a thin stream.
10. Take the kettle off the heat at once, skim, and pour the jelly quickly into the hot jars, leaving 1/4 inch of headspace. Wipe the rims and put on the lids.
11. Process the jars or refrigerate them, as described under safety below. Then let them stand undisturbed overnight so the gel is not broken.
12. Label the jars and store them in a cool, dry, dark place. Jelly tastes and looks best within a few months.

## From the source books

- Maria Parloa ranks the best jelly fruits in this order: currant, crab apple, apple, quince, grape, blackberry, raspberry, peach. She says jelly made with apples in spring needs the juice of one lemon per pint of apple juice. For cider apple jelly, she covers the apples with fresh-pressed cider instead of water.
- Parloa blames crystallized jelly on too much sugar, or on hard boiling that throws syrup onto the kettle sides. The USDA authors suggest wiping the sides with a damp cloth.
- The Woman's Institute course gets more juice from three extractions. It reboils the pulp with a little water each time the dripping slows, then mixes all the juice.
- The same course boils the juice 5 to 8 minutes before adding the heated sugar. Adding sugar at the start, it says, loses sugar to skimming and invites crystals.
- To test pectin, the course stirs together 1 tablespoon each of juice and alcohol. One firm lump means a cup of sugar per cup of juice, and several lumps mean 3/4 cup. The USDA authors warn never to taste this mixture.
- The period books sealed jelly with paraffin or with brandy-soaked paper. Parloa herself notes that mold spores can survive under paraffin.

## Safety and modern practice

- Paraffin seals are no longer recommended. Current USDA and National Center for Home Food Preservation directions call for canning jars with new two-piece lids, 1/4-inch headspace, and processing in a boiling-water canner for the tested time, adjusted for altitude. If you skip processing, refrigerate the jelly.
- Discard any jelly that shows mold, fermentation or moving bubbles.
- Boiling sugar syrup is far hotter than boiling water and clings to skin. Use a deep kettle and long-handled tools, and keep children away from the stove.
