# Dry apples and other fruit at home

Drying removes enough water from fruit that molds and bacteria cannot grow, leaving a light, compact food that keeps for months without a freezer or canner. It works for apples, pears, peaches, apricots, cherries and berries. The keys are thin pieces, steady gentle heat, moving air, and dry storage.

## What you need

- Fresh, fully ripe, sound fruit. One bruised or moldy piece can spoil a batch
- A dip of 1 teaspoon salt to each quart of water, or a pure ascorbic acid (vitamin C) solution
- Drying trays with slatted wood, plastic mesh or rustproof screen bottoms so air reaches both sides
- Cheesecloth to line trays or cover them outdoors
- An oven, a dehydrator, or a sunny spot, and a household fan if you have one
- Glass jars or heavy freezer bags, plus labels

## Steps

1. Prepare the fruit as soon as it is picked. Wash it and sort out any piece with decay, bruises or mold.
2. Cut the fruit. For apples, pears and quinces, peel, core and cut them into eighths or thinner slices. Halve and pit peaches and apricots, leaving the skins on but rubbing off the peach fuzz. Pit cherries.
3. Drop each piece into the dip as you cut it, and leave it there until the whole batch is ready, so it does not darken.
4. Blanch tough-skinned fruit such as grapes, small dark plums, cherries and figs briefly in boiling water to crack the skins.
5. Drain the fruit, blot it dry with towels, and spread it in one thin layer on the trays.
6. Dry it at 135-140°F in a dehydrator or oven. If your oven's lowest setting is about 150°F, prop the door partly open. For larger loads, aim a fan at the gap. Keep drying without long interruptions, or the fruit may spoil before it dries.
7. Turn the pieces and rotate the trays now and then. The bottom trays dry fastest.
8. Test a handful after letting it cool a few minutes, since warm fruit feels softer than it is. Done fruit is leathery and pliable. It bends without cracking and has no moist spots.
9. Condition the batch in a large non-aluminum, non-plastic container in a dry, airy place. Stir it, or pour it between two containers, once a day for several days to a week. This evens out the moisture between pieces.
10. Pack the fruit tightly in small, clean, dry, insect-proof containers. Label each with the fruit and date, and store in a cool, dry, dark place.

## From the source books

- The Woman's Institute course dries fruit on covered trays in the sun. It notes that glass covers add heat. The trays are turned as the sun moves and brought indoors every night until the fruit is leathery.
- The same course starts oven drying at about 110°F with the door open and lets the heat rise gradually toward 180°F, warning that hotter ovens scorch. The later USDA yearbook settles on 135-140°F, noting that too much heat makes a hard crust that traps moisture inside.
- The USDA authors say home-dried fruit must be drier than the store-bought kind if it is to keep.
- The Woman's Institute stored dried food in paraffin-dipped paper bags hung from attic rafters, out of reach of mice, in amounts for a meal or two. Jars or freezer bags do the job better today.
- To use dried fruit, the USDA authors add water just to cover and soak it 1 to 8 hours, then simmer it in the soaking water. One cup swells to about 1½ cups.

## Safety and modern practice

- Sun-dried fruit can carry insects or their eggs. Before storing, heat it at 150°F for 30 minutes or freeze it for 48 hours, as the USDA authors advise.
- Refrigerate fruit that soaks longer than 1 to 2 hours. Drying does not kill bacteria, yeasts or molds.
- Check stored fruit from time to time. Discard anything off-colored or moldy, and re-dry pieces that have only gone soft.
- Never use galvanized or zinc-coated mesh. The acid in fruit reacts with the coating.
- If you dry with the oven door ajar, keep children and pets away from it, and keep the fan cord clear of the heat.
