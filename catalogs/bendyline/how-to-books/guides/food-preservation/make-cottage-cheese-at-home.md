# Make cottage cheese at home

Cottage cheese is the simplest cheese there is. Milk is soured until it sets into a soft curd, then gently warmed to firm it, and drained of its whey. It needs no press and no aging, and a gallon of skim milk gives about 1½ pounds of fresh cheese. Success depends on clean, good-flavored milk and on gentle heat, because overheating makes the curd tough and dry.

## What you need

- 1 gallon of skim milk, or whole milk with the cream skimmed off to add back later
- A starter for pasteurized milk, such as a commercial culture or clean-flavored soured milk: 1 to 5 percent of the milk, or a pint for 3 to 4 gallons
- A rennet or junket tablet (optional), for a firmer, finer curd
- A pan for the milk and a larger pan of warm water to set it in
- A floating dairy thermometer, a long knife, and a spoon
- Cheesecloth or a clean cloth bag, with a colander or a hook to hang it from
- Salt, about 1 teaspoon per pound of curd
- Sweet or sour cream to taste

## Steps

1. Scald all utensils, and keep the milk at 50°F or below until you start.
2. Warm the milk to about 75°F and stir in the starter, plus the dissolved rennet if you are using it. Cover the pan.
3. Hold the milk near 75°F, for example in a pan of water at that temperature. Leave it until it sets into a firm curd: overnight, or about 12 to 15 hours, with a starter, and about 30 hours if you let raw milk sour on its own.
4. Test the curd by inserting a finger. It is ready when it breaks cleanly and whey collects in the break.
5. Cut the curd into 2-inch squares with the long knife, then stir it gently with a spoon.
6. Set the pan in hot water and bring the curd up to warm, 90 to 100°F. Hold it there for about 30 minutes, stirring gently for a minute every 5 minutes. Never let it get hotter.
7. Pour the curds and whey into the cloth-lined colander or bag. Leave it undisturbed for 5 to 10 minutes, then lift the corners of the cloth now and then to speed draining.
8. Tie the bag and hang it. Stop draining when the whey no longer runs in a steady stream; curd drained too long becomes dry.
9. If the curd tastes too sour, rinse it with warm water and let it drip again.
10. Turn the curd into a bowl and work it with a spoon until it is fine-grained and smooth, about like mashed potatoes.
11. Salt to taste, about 1 teaspoon per pound. Press in sweet or sour cream until creamy.
12. Pack the cheese in a covered glass or earthenware dish and refrigerate it. Eat it within several days.

## From the source books

- The 1920s USDA plan reprinted in *One Thousand Ways to Make a Living* calls for clean, sweet skim milk. It says natural souring risks slow setting, bitter flavors and uneven results, which is why it recommends a starter. It also says pasteurized milk must have "a vigorous starter," or the curd will not drain.
- The same plan adds rennet at about 3 drops per 10 pints of milk, diluted in cold water. It notes that junket tablets vary in strength.
- The Woman's Institute course warms clabbered milk in a crock on the back of the stove, or in a pan of warm water on a gas range. It warns that above 90°F "the curd will become tough and dry."
- For a sweet curd, the course warms fresh milk to no more than 98°F and sets it with one junket tablet per quart. For a finer "buttermilk cream cheese," it warms buttermilk to no more than 100°F until the curd sinks, then mixes the curd with thick cream.
- The course suggests flavoring the cheese with chopped pimiento, parsley, olives or nuts.
- The USDA plan warns that cottage cheese held 36 hours at room temperature goes off, and that cheese weeping whey spoils quickly.

## Safety and modern practice

- Start with pasteurized milk and a starter culture. Raw milk can carry Listeria, E. coli and other pathogens that souring does not reliably kill. Store-bought cultured buttermilk works as a starter, but ultra-pasteurized milk often sets poorly.
- Do not use milk that has spoiled with off or bitter odors. Clean souring smells pleasantly tart.
- Keep finished cheese refrigerated at 40°F or below and eat it within about a week. Discard it if it turns slimy, moldy or sharply off-smelling.
