# Make orange marmalade

Marmalade is a citrus preserve: thin shreds of peel suspended in a clear, softly set jelly. The natural pectin and acid in oranges and lemons set it without any commercial pectin. The two things that matter are soaking and simmering the peel until it is truly tender before the sugar goes in, and stopping the boil at the jelly point.

## What you need

- 2 oranges and 1 lemon for a small batch of a few jars. A larger batch from the Woman's Institute uses 12 oranges and 6 lemons
- Water: 2 cups of boiling water for each cup of sliced fruit
- Sugar: 1 cup for each cup of sliced fruit, measured before the water is added
- A sharp knife and cutting board, a measuring cup, and a covered bowl or pot for soaking
- A heavy, wide kettle, a long-handled wooden spoon, and a jelly thermometer or a few chilled saucers
- Clean jars with new two-piece lids

## Steps

1. Wash and dry the fruit. Spring is the best time: Seville, Valencia and other well-flavored oranges are in season then.
2. Cut each orange and the lemon lengthwise into quarters. Pick out the seeds and cut away the tough center core.
3. Slice each quarter crosswise as thinly as you can, peel and all.
4. Measure the sliced fruit and note the number of cups. Measure the same number of cups of sugar into a separate pan and set it aside.
5. Put the fruit in a pot, add 2 cups of boiling water for each cup of fruit, cover, and let stand 24 hours.
6. The next day, uncover the pot and bring it to a boil. Boil gently for about an hour, until the peel is very tender. Sugar firms the peel, so it must be soft before the sugar goes in.
7. While the fruit cooks, warm the sugar in a low oven or at the back of the stove, and get your jars hot.
8. Add the warm sugar gradually, stirring until it dissolves.
9. Boil the marmalade, stirring every few minutes so it does not scorch.
10. After about half an hour, start testing. Drop a little onto a cold saucer: it is done when it begins to thicken and set. With a thermometer, stir first, then cook to 9°F above the boiling point of water, about 221°F at sea level. Allow up to an hour, because fruit varies.
11. Take the pot off the heat and fill the hot jars at once. Wipe the rims clean and put on the lids.
12. Process the jars or refrigerate them, as described under safety below. Then label them and store them in a cool, dark, dry place.

## From the source books

- In *A Little Preserving Book for a Little Girl*, Amy Waterman has the young cook make marmalade from just 2 oranges and 1 lemon. Small batches, she says, keep the work from becoming tiresome.
- For orange-and-grapefruit marmalade, Waterman adds one grapefruit. She grates its rind instead of slicing it, and uses only its juice and pulp, cut free from the membranes.
- The Woman's Institute course takes another route. It peels the fruit like an apple and scrapes the white pith from the peel. It simmers the thin yellow strips for 1 hour in 1½ quarts of water. Then it adds 5 pounds of sugar and the orange and lemon pulp, and boils until thick.
- The USDA authors define marmalade as peel and pulp "suspended in a clear, jellied liquid," with citrus peel "sliced very thin." They cook marmalades to 9°F above the boiling point of water, one degree past jelly.
- The two older books sealed marmalade under melted paraffin, swirled to coat the glass. The 1977 USDA authors already recommended a short boiling-water process for marmalades instead.

## Safety and modern practice

- Do not seal with paraffin. Follow a current tested recipe from the USDA or the National Center for Home Food Preservation. That means canning jars with new two-piece lids, 1/4-inch headspace, and processing in a boiling-water canner for the stated time, adjusted for altitude. Or skip processing and keep the jars in the refrigerator.
- Discard any jar that shows mold, a bulging lid or signs of fermentation.
- Marmalade spits as it thickens. Use a deep pot and a long-handled spoon, and wear oven mitts while filling jars.
- Wash citrus well, since the peel is eaten. Organic or unwaxed fruit is a good choice.
