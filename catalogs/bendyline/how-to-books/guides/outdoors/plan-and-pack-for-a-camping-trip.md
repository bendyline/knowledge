# Plan and pack for a camping trip

A good trip is mostly decided at home: where you will go, what you will carry, and how much weight you can enjoy hauling. The old campers' rule is to go light: a waterproof ground layer, a warm bed, a small shelter, a simple cook kit, and little else.

## What you need

- A notebook for your gear list and route notes
- A waterproof ground sheet (Gould's rubber blanket weighed about 2½ pounds)
- One stout wool blanket or its modern equal. Wallace found a single blanket of no more than 3 pounds enough for summer.
- A light shelter, such as half of a two-person shelter tent (about 2 pounds), or Wallace's lean-to tent 7 feet long, 4 feet wide and 4 feet high, weighing under 3 pounds
- A small cook kit: a frying pan, a coffeepot and a pail (about 3 pounds for the party, per Gould). Wallace's kit was a 9-inch folding-handle frying pan, a small pan, a pint cup, a spoon and a cooking knife.
- A spare shirt, underwear and two pairs of socks
- Matches in an airtight match safe, a compass, maps and a guidebook
- A pack with broad shoulder straps, plus a small shoulder bag for lunch and things you use all day
- Food for the days between stores. Gould allows nearly 3 pounds a day per person.

## Steps

1. Decide how you will travel and where you will camp. Find out whether you can buy food along the way, and ask someone who has been there.
2. Study the map early, so that you already know the country before you go.
3. Keep a running list, and gather finished gear in one place so nothing gets left behind.
4. Get in shape by taking long walks for a week or more before you leave.
5. Set a weight limit. Gould thought few young walkers could enjoy more than 20 pounds at ten miles a day for a week. Wallace put the limit for the inexperienced at 25 pounds, including shelter, bed and a week's food.
6. Pack the essentials first, in this order: ground sheet, blanket, shelter, cook kit and spare clothing. Weigh the total.
7. Cut what is left. Skip new "patent" gadgets, crockery and glassware, and anything nice you hope to keep clean.
8. Roll your bed. Spread the tent flat and fold the blanket once, end to end, on top of it with its sides toward the tent ends. Fold the tent ends in, roll it tight, double the roll and tie it near the ends. Wear it over one shoulder with the ends meeting at the opposite hip.
9. Pack the rest so that hard objects sit in the middle and soft things are on the outside. Adjust the straps so the bottom of the pack hangs no lower than your hips.
10. Plan short days and some days in camp. Remember that wet tents, blankets and clothes weigh about a quarter more.

## From the source books

- Gould's 20-pound budget for one walker: rubber blanket 2½ pounds; wool blanket and lining 4½; knapsack, haversack and canteen 4; spare clothes 2; half a shelter tent with ropes 2; toilet articles and small wares 2; one day's food 3. Each person also carried a share of the cook kit, the hatchet and the party's gear.
- Gould points out that a blanket roll worn across the body saves the 2½ pounds of a knapsack and is easy to shift from one shoulder to the other. He also notes that "a pound of shirt is as warm as two pounds of overcoat."
- For heavy canoe portages, Wallace preferred a tumpline. It is a broad leather band of about 18 to 20 inches worn high on the forehead, with long thongs tied around the load. He says beginners should start with 60 to 70 pounds and put about 50 pounds as the most that is comfortable on shoulder straps alone. Those are loads for short portages, not for all-day hiking.

## Safety and modern practice

- Leave a written trip plan with someone at home: where you are going, your route, and when to call for help.
- Check the weather, campground rules, permits and fire restrictions before you leave.
- Carry the modern "ten essentials": map and compass, a headlamp, sun protection, a first-aid kit, a knife, a fire starter, an emergency shelter, plus extra food, water and clothing.
- Boil, filter or chemically treat all stream and lake water.
- A modern pack's hip belt moves most of the weight onto your hips. Tumpline loads strain the neck, so build up slowly.
- Store food where local rules require, such as a bear canister or a food locker.
