# Split firewood safely with an axe

An axe is the camper's most useful tool and the easiest one to get hurt with. Beard once rode a train full of injured lumbermen and found that none had been hurt by falling trees. All their wounds came from the axe itself or from chips and knots flying off it. Good splitting comes down to a sharp axe, a clear swing, a solid block, and letting wedges do the hard work.

## What you need

- An axe that suits your strength. For a camp axe you carry, Beard suggests a 30-inch hickory handle with a head of about 2¾ pounds. Kreps used a 2-pound head on a 30- to 34-inch handle.
- A chopping block: a sound log end, or a crotch flattened on top
- Two wooden wedges ("gluts"), preferably seasoned applewood or locust, or dogwood or ironwood used green, plus a wooden mallet
- An 8-inch flat mill file and a whetstone
- A leather sheath for the blade

## Steps

1. Check the axe before every use. The head must be tight and the edge sharp and straight. If a dry handle loosens in the head, Beard soaks it overnight so the wood swells. Do not drive a nail into it.
2. Clear the area. Remove every twig, vine and branch within reach of your swing, and make sure there is nothing overhead, such as a clothesline. Even a small twig can turn the blade toward your foot.
3. Stand the piece on the chopping block so the block supports it right where the axe will strike.
4. Hold the axe with one hand near the end of the handle and the other near the head. Let the upper hand slide down to meet the lower one as you swing.
5. Strike the end grain squarely. If the piece does not open, strike again in the same place with a slight slant to wedge it apart. If there is a knot, strike straight through the knot.
6. To cut across the grain, use a slanting blow, because a square blow just bounces off. Do not slant it so much that the blade skims off and swings around toward your legs.
7. For thick rounds, start a crack with the axe, set the wooden wedges in it and drive them with the mallet. Use the axe only to cut the stringy fibers that hold the halves together.
8. Never pry a split open with the axe. Kreps calls this the easiest way he knows to break a handle.
9. When you stop, put the sheath on the blade or strike the axe into a stump. Never leave it on the ground or leaning against a tree.
10. To sharpen, push the file from well back on the blade toward the edge, never from the edge toward the handle. Then whet the edge smooth with the stone.

## From the source books

- Beard lists the period axe sizes: a quarter axe has a 19-inch handle and weighs 2 pounds, a half axe 24 inches and 2½ pounds, a three-quarter axe 28 inches and 3 pounds, and a full axe 36 inches and 5 pounds.
- Beard warns against laying a stick with one end on a log and one on the ground and chopping it in the middle. He says flying pieces from this have cost many woodchoppers an eye.
- He also warns never to set a log in front of a crotch-style chopping block. If the wood splits on the first blow, the handle slams down on that log and shatters.
- Kreps saw an entire bit broken off by one careless blow at a hemlock knot, and he says frozen wood breaks axes too. He knew two men who cut their heads by splitting wood under a clothesline.
- Beard calls the double-bitted axe "a nuisance around camps," because the second blade is always exposed. He also considers it bad manners to borrow another man's axe.

## Safety and modern practice

- Wear safety glasses and sturdy leather boots, and keep other people well outside the reach of your swing.
- Never hold the stick you are splitting with your hand or foot. For small kindling, a hatchet with a stick or a splitting tool keeps your fingers clear.
- For large rounds, a splitting maul and steel wedges are safer and less tiring than a light camp axe. Stop when you are tired, because most slips happen then.
- Use dead and downed wood or bought firewood. Cutting standing trees is prohibited in most parks.
- Carry a first-aid kit that can handle a deep cut, and know how to reach help.
