# Build and put out a campfire

A campfire needs two things, small dry fuel and air, and it must be put out completely when you leave. The old woodsmen's advice is simple. Collect everything before you strike a match, start with slivers of wood rather than leaves or grass, and build up gradually.

## What you need

- Matches in a waterproof container. Kreps dipped spare matches in melted paraffin to waterproof them.
- A pocketknife
- Tinder: fine dry slivers of pine, curled shavings, or the loose outer bark of white birch
- Kindling from pencil to thumb thickness, sorted by size
- Fuel: dry split wood. Beard says logs of 12 to 14 inches split best for firewood.
- Two green logs or flat stones to rest the wood on (fire-dogs)
- A bucket of water and a shovel

## Steps

1. Find out whether fires are allowed and whether you need a permit.
2. Pick an open spot away from tents and overhanging branches, never against a living tree or on leaves or rotten wood. Clear the ground and ring the spot with stones or dug earth.
3. Do not build directly on a smooth, damp rock. Spread sand or earth on it first, because Beard warns that heated wet stone can burst.
4. Gather all your fuel first. Standing dead wood is drier than wood lying on the ground. In wet weather, split logs and take the dry wood from the center.
5. Make two or three "fuzz sticks." Split dry pine into pieces about a foot long and as thick as your thumb. Shave curls around each one, leaving the curls attached, and always whittle away from your body.
6. Lay the fire on your fire-dogs so air can flow underneath. Put the finest kindling first, then finely split wood, then coarser pieces, crossed loosely or set up like a little tepee. Do not let heavy wood crush the kindling.
7. Face into the wind, strike the match and cup it in your hands. Light the kindling from beneath on the windward side, where the breeze will push the flame into the fuel.
8. Add slightly larger sticks as each layer catches, then split fuel.
9. For cooking, use a small fire or a bed of coals. Kreps sets his pan on two small green logs, flattened on top, about half a foot apart with the fire between them.
10. To put the fire out, let it burn down. Pour water over it, stir the ashes and pour again. Soak the earth for 2 or 3 feet around until nothing is warm. Never leave a fire burning.

## From the source books

- The 1911 Boy Scout handbook quotes Chief Forester H. S. Graves: "A fire is never out until the last spark is extinguished." It adds that it is "criminal to leave a burning fire."
- Beard watched twenty or thirty men fail to light a heap of arm-thick wood with matches. His point is that fine slivers of dry wood are far more reliable than paper, leaves or grass.
- Beard notes that pitch pine is a fine fire starter but a poor cooking fuel, because its smoke blackens pots and flavors food.
- Both Beard and Kreps say that rubbing a damp match through your hair dries it. Kreps preferred large "parlor" matches to safety matches in wind.
- In the rain, Beard lays a sheet of bark as a hearth and holds a blanket over the fire while lighting it.
- For a warming fire in front of an open camp, Sears drove two stakes slanting slightly back and stacked green butternut back logs against them, largest at the bottom. He added fire-dogs, a 5-foot front log and a pile of bark and small wood. He wrote this for wilderness winter camps, not for today's campgrounds.

## Safety and modern practice

- Obey burn bans and fire-danger levels, and use an existing fire ring where there is one. Where fires are not allowed, use a camp stove.
- Keep fires small. Burn only dead and downed wood no thicker than your wrist, or buy firewood locally. Moving firewood spreads tree-killing insects.
- Never use gasoline or other accelerants, and never burn plastic or trash.
- Feel the ashes with the back of your hand before leaving. They must be cold, not just dark ("drown, stir, feel").
- Keep children and loose clothing well back from the flames, and keep water and a shovel within reach the whole time.
