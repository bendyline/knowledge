# Bake bannock bread over a campfire

Bannock is quick bread leavened with baking powder and baked in a frying pan in front of an open fire. It was the everyday bread of North Woods guides because it needs no oven, no yeast and no waiting. The trick is to set the loaf with gentle heat underneath, then finish it by heat reflected from the flames.

## What you need

- 1 cup flour
- 1 teaspoon salt
- 1 tablespoon sugar
- 1 heaping teaspoon baking powder
- Water (or half water and half milk) to make a medium-stiff batter or soft dough
- Grease or bacon fat for the pan
- A frying pan and a forked stick to prop it up
- A clean whittled sliver of wood or a fork for testing
- A fire burned down partly to coals, with some live flame at the back

## Steps

1. Light the fire well ahead of time so you have a bed of coals. Set up a cooking place: two small green logs or two flat stones about half a foot apart, with coals between them, will hold the pan steady.
2. Mix the flour, salt, sugar and baking powder thoroughly while they are still dry.
3. Heat the pan and grease it well.
4. Only when the pan is hot, stir in water a little at a time until you have a medium-stiff batter. Stir lightly, and do not knead or beat it.
5. Pour or press the dough into the pan about half an inch thick.
6. Set the pan flat over or beside slow heat for a few minutes until the loaf rises and firms enough to hold its shape.
7. Prop the pan almost upright with the stick so the top of the loaf faces the flames, and let the reflected heat brown it.
8. When the first side is brown, turn the loaf over and brown the other side.
9. Push a clean sliver into the center. If it comes out without dough sticking to it, the bread is done.

## From the source books

- White's ratio is the one above. He warns that stiff batter makes close, heavy bread and thin batter makes light, crisp bread. In an oven he uses a slightly thinner batter.
- Beard describes the bannock his friend Vreeland made. The dough was mixed like biscuits, dropped half an inch thick into a hot greased pan, set over a slow fire to rise, then propped up with a stick and browned slowly by reflected heat to "a nice brown crust."
- Beard's camp biscuits use 2 cups flour, one level and one heaping teaspoon of baking powder, and half a teaspoon of salt, mixed dry. For his camp corn bread, he uses half cornmeal and half flour and props the pan in front of a quick fire so the bread does not turn out heavy.
- Sears disliked flapjacks and made "club bread" instead. He peeled a sassafras or black birch club two feet long and three inches thick at one end, then heated it beside the coals. He wound a ribbon of dough two inches wide and half an inch thick around the hot end, then baked it in front of the fire, turning it, for about 30 minutes.
- Sears used three tablespoons of baking powder per quart of flour for wheat bread and the same proportions for pancake batter.
- Kreps keeps the cooking fire small and separate from the big campfire. He finds two green logs flattened top and bottom, or two straight-sided stones, the steadiest support for a frying pan.

## Safety and modern practice

- Pan handles get very hot near a fire. Use a glove or pot holder, and make sure the pan sits firmly so it cannot tip into the coals.
- For stick bread, use a clean stick of a known non-toxic hardwood. Avoid unknown woods, and never use yew, oleander or other poisonous plants.
- Wash your hands before mixing, and bake the loaf all the way through.
- Pack out leftovers and store food and greasy pans away from wildlife, as local rules require.
- Where fires are banned, cook bannock the same way in a pan on a camp stove over low heat, with a lid on to brown the top.
