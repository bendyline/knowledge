# Build a lean-to shelter

A lean-to is a sloping roof open on one side. Classic campers liked it because the roof reflects a fire's heat down onto the bed. It can be roofed with a cloth sheet or tarp, or in an emergency with evergreen boughs. The same frame serves for both.

## What you need

- Two forked uprights about 6 feet high, or two trees 8 to 10 feet apart with branches 6 to 8 feet up
- A straight crosspole long enough to span them
- Several roof poles to lean from the ground to the crosspole, plus a few lighter poles to lay across them
- Roofing: a tarp or cloth sheet (Sears used one 9 by 7 feet that weighed 2¼ pounds and sheltered three people), or a large pile of fan-shaped evergreen boughs
- Cord for lashing
- A hatchet or knife
- A ground sheet and blankets

## Steps

1. Arrive early enough to build before dark. Choose a dry, level spot near water and firewood, with just enough slope to drain rain away from the bed.
2. Decide which way the open front will face. Face it away from the wind and toward where the fire will go.
3. Set the two forked uprights firmly in the ground, or lash the crosspole between two trees, and lay the crosspole in the forks.
4. Lean the roof poles from the ground up to the crosspole at a fairly steep angle, about 6 inches apart. A log along the bottom ends helps hold them.
5. Lay lighter poles crosswise over the slanting poles to carry the roofing.
6. If you are using a tarp, stretch it over the frame and tie it down at the ridge, the corners and the bottom edge.
7. If you are using boughs, start at the ground. Lay the fans with the feathery tips pointing down and the bottom side up, and shingle each row over the butts of the row below until you reach the crosspole. Sears says a thatch a foot thick will shed a pretty heavy rain.
8. Lay a few poles over the thatch to hold it down.
9. If the night is breezy, close the sides by driving light stakes and weaving boughs between them.
10. Make the bed under the roof with its head toward the back and its foot toward the opening. Put the waterproof sheet on the ground, waterproof side down, and the blanket on top.
11. Build a small fire a safe distance in front of the opening.

## From the source books

- Beard calls this the Adirondack shelter and says the form is older than the Adirondack resorts, noting that Daniel Boone camped this way in Kentucky. Where birch bark is available, he shingles the roof with slabs of bark held down by poles.
- Two lean-tos built facing each other about 6 feet apart, with a small fire between them, make a warm camp for several days. Both Beard and the 1911 Boy Scout handbook describe this.
- Sears's quickest shelter, his "Indian camp," uses one 12-foot pole sharpened at both ends. One end is jammed into the ground and the other into the rough bark of a tree, and boughs or a cloth are laid over it.
- For a bough bed, Beard gathers balsam twigs 18 inches long for the bottom layer and 10 to 12 inches long for the top. He snaps them off with a twist of the thumb and shingles them so the tips point toward the head. Sears stuffs a small muslin bag with moss to make a pillow.
- Sears warns that sparks from an overfed fire burn holes in a cloth roof, and he covers the cloth with green boughs before lighting up.

## Safety and modern practice

- Cutting live trees, poles or boughs is illegal in most parks and forests, and bough beds damage young trees. Use a tarp and a sleeping pad. Use only dead, downed wood where that is allowed, and save cut-bough shelters for real emergencies or private land with permission.
- Take the shelter down and scatter the materials before you leave.
- Check overhead for dead limbs, and avoid lone trees and ridgetops in thunderstorms.
- Keep the fire small and several feet from the opening. Modern tarps, tents and sleeping bags are often nylon, which melts and burns quickly. Check local fire restrictions before lighting any fire.
