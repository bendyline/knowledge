# Choose a campsite and pitch a tent

Where you put a tent matters as much as how you put it up. A good site drains, is sheltered from the worst wind, and has nothing overhead that can fall. A good pitch is square, tight and staked against the wind. The old guides wrote about canvas wall tents, but the same habits keep any tent dry and standing.

## What you need

- Tent with poles, guy lines and a bag of stakes (pins)
- Stakes of several sizes. Gould used 15 to 18 inches for corners and stays and 8 to 10 inches for walls and door, with stakes 2 feet long or more for storms.
- A mallet or a flat stone for driving stakes
- A ground cloth
- Stones, sandbags or short sticks to anchor the lines in sand or soft ground

## Steps

1. Start looking for a site in the afternoon, and stop early enough to have everything snug before dark.
2. Choose a high spot with a gentle slope, near water. Avoid hollows, the foot of very tall trees, trees with dead branches, and standing dead trees. Avoid single trees or small groups of trees in open country, because they draw lightning.
3. Look around for hazards. Stay upwind of marshy, mosquito-prone ground and away from ant hills and rotten wood. In the West, cross a shallow stream before you camp, because a distant storm can flood it overnight.
4. Clear the tent spot of stones, sticks and rubbish. Do not pitch over stones that stick up.
5. Lay the tent out. If the ground slopes, plan to sleep with your head uphill so you do not roll all night.
6. If it is windy, spread the tent on the windward side and fasten the windward guy lines before you raise it.
7. To pitch alone, drive the stakes in a row and fasten the two end guy lines first. Raise one pole part way, then the other, and keep raising them in turn until the tent stands against its lines.
8. Fasten the lines on the other side loosely, then straighten the poles and tighten everything evenly.
9. Close and tie the door before the final tightening so that the walls set square.
10. Slacken the guy lines a little at night and when rain is coming, because cotton lines shrink when wet and pull the stakes or tear the tent.
11. Keep clothes and bedding off the bare ground. A line strung between the front and back poles makes a handy clothes rack.
12. When you strike camp, knock each stake on all four sides before pulling it. Never pack the tent damp if you can help it, and keep the stakes in their own bag.

## From the source books

- Gould's quick-pitch trick: once the tent is set up right, mark the distance from a pole to its corner stake on the pole itself. Next time, use the marked pole to place the corner stakes before raising the tent.
- Beard lays a rod across the guy lines between the stakes and the tent. Its weight keeps the lines taut when they stretch and gives way when they shrink. For sand he buries billets of wood, stones, bundles of sticks or bags of sand as anchors.
- The sources disagree about driving stakes. Beard uses the flat of the axe so the stake tops do not split. Gould warns that this usually breaks the handle and prefers a wooden mallet.
- Gould recommends a fly (an outer roof) that reaches at least a foot past the eaves for a long stay. He also says never to cut the tent for any purpose and to dry it fully before storing it.
- Both Gould and Beard dig a ditch around the tent to carry off rain. That was standard then but is discouraged now (see below).

## Safety and modern practice

- Do not trench around tents. Choose ground that drains naturally, use a ground cloth folded so it does not stick out past the floor, and use established sites or durable surfaces such as gravel or bare ground.
- Camp at least 200 feet (about 70 steps) from lakes and streams where rules allow. In canyons and dry washes, camp well above flash-flood level.
- Before you stake out, look up for dead limbs ("widowmakers").
- Never run a stove, heater, lantern or grill inside a tent or vestibule. Carbon monoxide and fire can kill.
- Keep campfires well away and downwind of tents. Modern nylon tents melt and burn quickly.
- Follow the maker's instructions for a modern freestanding tent, and still stake and guy it out in wind.
