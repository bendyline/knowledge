# Find north with a compass, the sun and the stars

People rarely get lost because they cannot find north. They get lost because they stop paying attention to direction. The old woodsmen combined three things: a compass, the sun's position, and known landmarks such as a stream or road. Used together, these let you hold a straight course and find your way back.

## What you need

- A simple compass. Kreps preferred an open-faced, watch-shaped one on a ring fastened to the coat.
- A map of the area, or at least a known landmark such as a stream, lake or road
- A watch with hands, for the sun method
- A pocketknife, for the cloudy-day shadow trick

## Steps

1. Before you leave camp, learn the nearest long landmark, such as a stream or road, and note which side of it you will be on.
2. To read the compass, hold it level and still until the needle stops swinging. Turn the compass case until the north end of the needle sits over N, and the other directions will read correctly.
3. Keep the compass away from iron and steel such as a belt buckle, a knife or a gun, all of which pull the needle.
4. Sight on a distant landmark in the direction you want to go, such as a hill or a tall dead tree, and walk to it. Then pick the next mark along the same bearing.
5. Between compass checks, keep the sun in the same place relative to your body. The sun moves, so check the compass again every so often.
6. To use a watch as a compass in the Northern Hemisphere, point the hour hand at the sun. South lies halfway between the hour hand and the 12.
7. On a cloudy day, rest the point of a knife blade upright on your thumbnail and turn it slowly. The faint shadow it casts shows which way the sun is.
8. At night, find the Big Dipper. The two stars on the side of the bowl farthest from the handle point toward the North Star.
9. Treat moss on trees and the size of branches as rough hints only, not as a compass.
10. If you lose your bearings, stop and calm down. Note the spot carefully, walk a straight line in the most likely direction, and come back if you do not reach familiar ground. Try other directions the same way. If none works, make camp at the starting point and wait for help.

## From the source books

- Kreps learned the watch-and-compass trick from a Canadian land guide. The guide called a halt for tea at noon "because the sun is directly south," as his compass showed.
- Kreps insists the compass is almost always right. If it says the sun is rising in the northwest, believe it. He found people more often trust a "very unreliable instinct."
- Sears warns that lost travelers drift, nearly always to the right, and he advises carrying the compass in your hand and checking it every few minutes. Kreps tells of snowshoeing south on a cloudy day and twice walking into his own fresh trail.
- Sears explains how to correct a drift. If you realize you have swung from south to northwest, steer southeast for a while to make up the difference before resuming south.
- Kreps names the Boothia Peninsula as the location of the magnetic north pole. The pole has wandered a long way since he wrote.

## Safety and modern practice

- A compass points to magnetic north, which differs from true (map) north by an amount called declination. It can be more than 15 degrees in parts of the United States. Read the declination on a current map and set or correct your compass for it.
- During daylight saving time, use 1 o'clock instead of 12 in the watch method. South of the equator the method is reversed: point the 12 at the sun, and north lies halfway between the 12 and the hour hand.
- Phones and GPS units are useful, but batteries fail. Carry a paper map and compass and practice with them close to home.
- Leave a trip plan with someone. Carry a whistle; three blasts is a widely recognized call for help. If you are truly lost, staying put makes you much easier to find.
