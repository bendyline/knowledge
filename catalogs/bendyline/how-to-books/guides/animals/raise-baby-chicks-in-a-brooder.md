# Raise baby chicks in a brooder

Chicks without a mother hen need artificial warmth for their first several weeks. This care is called brooding. The first weeks are the critical ones, and R. A. Power and the Corning Egg Farm both blame most early losses on chilling, poor air and overfeeding. The idea is a warm spot the chicks can move toward or away from, then a slow hardening off.

## What you need

- A brooder box with two compartments. One holds the heater under a canopy (the "hover") that keeps heat near the floor. The other is a run, with a low opening in the partition between them.
- A heat source you can turn down, or a canopy you can raise.
- Litter about 2 inches deep on the run floor. Corning uses short-cut wheat straw.
- Drinkers the chicks cannot climb into.
- Grit, finely ground oyster shell, and a covered hopper or pan for mash.
- Starting feeds. Power used pinhead oatmeal and cracked grains, then a dry mash with plenty of wheat bran.
- Green feed, such as fresh grass or the tops of sprouted oats.

## Steps

1. Run the brooder before the chicks arrive so the hover is warm. Power keeps it at about 96 °F the first week. Corning starts at 95 °F.
2. Keep the chicks close to the hover at first so they cannot wander off and chill. For the first two or three nights, Corning gently herds them back under the hover.
3. Give fresh water and a little grit at once. Clean and refill the drinkers morning, noon and night.
4. Feed small amounts, only what the chicks clean up quickly. Power's rule is "much safer to under feed than to over feed." Corning scatters about five handfuls per hundred chicks morning and afternoon, and two at noon.
5. Lift the hover each morning and check every chick.
6. Lower the heat at least 2 degrees a week by turning the heater down or raising the canopy.
7. Add green feed early. Corning gives the green tops of sprouted oats from the third day, never the roots.
8. Once the chicks are eating well, scatter a scratch mix in the litter so they work for it. Power's mix is cracked corn, cracked wheat and pinhead oatmeal, with a little grit and fine oyster shell.
9. After about two weeks, add dry mash in a covered hopper so they cannot walk in it.
10. Open a small door to an outside run on fine days, about the fifth or sixth day early in the season. Let the chicks find it themselves. Bring them back in if they huddle against the warm wall.
11. Harden them off so they need no heat by about two months old.
12. Grow them on with grass, insects, shade, grit and water always available. Power expects pullets to start laying at four to six months.

## From the source books

- Power fed nothing but water and grit for the first two days, then sour skim milk or buttermilk, which he gave for life. Corning offered its own chick food as soon as the chicks went under the hover.
- Corning blames wet cornmeal mash, a common farm chick feed of its day, for heavy losses, because "Corn in any form was never intended for a chick to eat."
- Corning compares a chick to a flower that is set back every time it is transplanted, so it moves chicks only once, from brooder to colony house.
- Corning keeps the whole brooder house at 85 °F three feet above the floor. Its chicks lie on the floor without hovers by four weeks.
- For summer shade, Power suggests letting growing chicks range in an orchard or a patch of tall corn.

## Safety and modern practice

- Heat lamps start many barn and coop fires. Clamp or chain them securely, keep them well away from litter and wood, or use a radiant heat plate. Watch the chicks: huddling under the heat means too cold, crowding to the edges means too hot.
- Current extension guidance is about 90–95 °F at chick level the first week, then about 5 °F less each week until the chicks are fully feathered.
- Give a commercial chick starter and water from the first day. Power's two-day fast is not recommended now.
- Live poultry can carry *Salmonella*. Wash your hands after handling chicks, supervise children, and keep chicks out of kitchens.
- Buy from a hatchery that tests for pullorum disease. Ask a veterinarian about any sick chicks.
