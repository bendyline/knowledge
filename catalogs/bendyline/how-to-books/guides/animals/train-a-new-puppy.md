# Train a new puppy

A puppy's first lessons are a place to sleep, clean habits indoors, its name, coming when called, and walking on a lead. A. F. Hochwalt, writing about dogs as home companions, and Williams Haynes, writing about Airedales, agree on the basics. Be consistent, be firm without being harsh, and make sure the dog always knows exactly what you want.

## What you need

- A box or basket big enough for the puppy to stretch out in, raised a few inches off the floor and out of drafts. Hochwalt suggests a corner of the kitchen.
- A collar and a light lead.
- Small, tasty food rewards.
- A ball for play-retrieving, and a whistle if you want to use one.

## Steps

1. Choose one person to feed the puppy and give its early lessons. Hochwalt finds a puppy fed by one person is fed regularly and not too much, while a whole household tends to overfeed.
2. Show the puppy its bed on the very first night. Feed it last thing, put it in the bed, turn out the light and leave. If it comes out and whines, take it back and tell it to lie down. Expect three or four noisy nights before it settles.
3. Take the puppy outside to relieve itself as soon as it arrives. Haynes keeps a new dog on a short lead in one convenient place for the first couple of days and takes it out at regular, fixed hours.
4. Watch closely indoors. If you catch the puppy starting to soil, take it outside at once. Puppies naturally avoid soiling their beds, so a regular routine builds the habit quickly.
5. Pick a name early and use it every time you want the puppy's attention.
6. Teach "come" during play. Call the puppy by name, and when it reaches you, pet it, repeat the name and give a treat. To add a whistle, blow it and then call the name at once until the puppy links the two.
7. Put a collar on and leave it for a day or two. Then clip on the lead and call the puppy. If it pulls back, keep the lead taut and wait. When it stops struggling, draw it in, pet it and reward it.
8. Teach heel once it leads well. Walk out together. Each time it pulls ahead, draw it back and say "Heel!" Go back to the lead whenever it forgets off-lead.
9. Make every command mean one thing. Haynes says that when you say "Come here," see that the dog comes, and "Lie down" means just that.
10. Add house manners, such as staying put in a chosen corner or chair and not jumping on visitors.
11. If you like, teach "Fetch!" through play by throwing a ball and rewarding the puppy for bringing it back.

## From the source books

- Hochwalt prefers to start with a puppy under three months old. He would rather house-train a 12-week-old puppy than trust a year-old dog sold as "house-broken." He found Airedales and cocker spaniels quick to learn, and pointers and setters slower.
- Haynes disagrees about the youngest puppies, calling it "cruelty pure and simple" to house-train one too young to understand.
- Both writers allowed physical correction: Hochwalt a light tap with a switch, Haynes a slap under the jaw. Haynes also insists that a dog never be punished when the owner has lost their temper: "temper and dog-training do not live together successfully."
- Haynes wants a city dog to keep close to heel when crossing busy streets. He dislikes teaching tricks to large terriers.
- Hochwalt keeps bedding clean by airing the box in the sun every week or so and scrubbing it when needed.

## Safety and modern practice

- Most trainers now use reward-based methods and advise against hitting a dog or rubbing its nose in a mess. Punishment, especially after the fact, tends to make puppies afraid or teaches them to hide when they soil. Calmly interrupt, take the puppy outside, and praise it the moment it goes there.
- Take young puppies out often: on waking, after meals and after play. A crate sized to the puppy is today's usual alternative to Haynes's short tether, but never leave a puppy tied up or wearing a lead when you are not watching.
- Ask your veterinarian when the puppy's vaccinations make it safe to walk in public places and meet other dogs.
- Supervise young children with puppies, and teach them to let a sleeping or eating puppy alone.
