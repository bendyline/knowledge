# Groom a horse

Grooming takes out the dirt, sweat and loose hair a brush can reach, and puts your hands over every part of the horse every day. The three sources here follow one order. Loosen the dirt, brush it away, wipe the coat smooth, then do the head, mane, tail and feet.

## What you need

- A curry comb.
- A body brush. Samuel and Sarah Adams call theirs a "whalebone brush."
- A wisp of twisted straw or hay, or a leather grooming pad.
- A woolen rubbing cloth or clean dusters.
- Sponges and a bucket of clean water.
- A mane comb and a water brush.
- A hoof pick.
- A rug or blanket for after grooming.

## Steps

1. If the horse has just been ridden, take off the bridle, slacken the girth and run up the stirrups. Nunn leaves the saddle on until the back cools. Put a light or warm rug over the quarters and walk the horse until he is cool.
2. Tie or hold the horse in the stable or another sheltered place, out of cold wind.
3. Rub off any dried, caked mud with a wisp of straw before you use the brush.
4. Curry the body all over to loosen dirt and dust in the coat. Nunn warns against scraping a thin-skinned horse with the comb. He uses it chiefly to clean the brush, or cleans the brush on his palm.
5. Brush the whole body with the body brush, working in the direction of the hair.
6. Go over the coat with the wisp. Nunn twists bedding straw into a rope about 3 feet long, doubles and twists it into a flat pad, and brings two of these down in turn with a light slap and stroke along the hair.
7. Finish the body by rubbing with your palms, then with a woolen rubber or clean cloth.
8. Do the head. Brush it and wipe it smooth. The Adamses draw the ears gently through the hands until warm, then wipe inside the ears with a damp sponge.
9. Rinse the sponge and wipe the eyes and nostrils, then rub the head over with a cloth.
10. Use a separate sponge to clean under the tail.
11. Comb out and lay the mane and tail with the mane comb and water brush. Mahon wants them washed occasionally with soap and water.
12. Pick out each foot, clearing stones and dirt from around the frog. The Adamses then wash the heels with the water brush.
13. Put on the rug, and bandages if they are needed. Nunn grooms twice a day, before the morning and evening feeds, and takes about half an hour for a clipped horse.

## From the source books

- In The Complete Servant, the groom's day starts at 5 a.m. in summer and 6 in winter. He waters each horse, beds and mucks out, and dresses the horses while they eat.
- Mahon forbade water above the hoof, except for the mane and tail. He thought grooms used it to save the work of drying by friction.
- Mahon's rules for a hired groom include telling the owner at once about any wound or sign of illness, never treating a horse himself, and never grooming out of doors.
- Nunn, writing about stables in India, grooms outdoors in hot weather and indoors in winter. He also hand-rubs the legs upward for five minutes if they tend to fill.
- Singeing the winter coat with a flame was common in Mahon's day. Clipping is the usual method now.

## Safety and modern practice

- Speak to the horse as you approach. Stay near his shoulder, and keep a hand on him as you move around the hindquarters. Wear sturdy boots, and crouch rather than kneel or sit, so you can step away quickly.
- Tie with a quick-release knot or a breakaway tie, to something solid at about the height of the horse's withers.
- Bathing horses with water is routine today in warm weather, if you scrape off the excess and walk or rug the horse until dry.
- Keep a separate brush set for each horse to limit the spread of skin infections such as ringworm.
- Skip the old hoof "stoppings" of dung and tar. Ask your farrier or veterinarian about dry or cracked hooves, and call the vet for any swelling, heat or wound you find.
