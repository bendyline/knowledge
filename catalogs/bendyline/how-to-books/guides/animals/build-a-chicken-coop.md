# Build a chicken coop

A good coop keeps hens dry, out of drafts and in sunlight. It does not need to be big or heated. Mary Roberts Conover and R. A. Power, two early twentieth-century poultry writers, agree on the core design: three tight walls and a south front open to light and air.

## What you need

- A dry, preferably sandy site that slopes away from the coop, with a windbreak on the cold side if possible.
- 2×4 studs about 2 feet apart, plus dry lumber. Use matched (tongue-and-groove) boards, or rough boards made tight with battens or roofing paper.
- Roll roofing over closely fitted boards, or shingles.
- Window sash, and hinged frames covered with burlap, sacking or coarse muslin.
- Close-meshed galvanized wire or tin strips against rats.
- Roost poles about 2 inches across.
- Nest boxes. Conover uses compartments of 16 × 12 × 14 inches or 13½ × 10½ × 12.
- Straw or shavings for litter, dry sand, a dust box, a hopper feeder and a drinking fountain.

## Steps

1. Choose a spot where surface water runs away from the building. On wet ground, set the coop on posts.
2. Allow at least 4 square feet of floor per bird. Power's example is 25 × 16 feet for 100 hens, with a shed roof about 8 feet high in front and 5 feet at the back.
3. Build a dry, warm floor. A board floor can be raised about 6 inches on posts and doubled with roofing paper between the layers. An earth or concrete floor must sit at least 6 inches above the outside ground, over a layer of stone.
4. Close the north, east and west walls tightly, with sheathing paper under the siding and no cracks behind the roosts.
5. Fill the upper south wall with windows and cloth-covered openings. Conover wants at least a third of the front in windows. Power gives half or more to glass, and the rest to muslin curtains that reach well down so damp air can escape.
6. Make the roof watertight. Lap roll roofing as marked and turn it over the edges. Keep the eaves from shading the windows.
7. Fit the roosts along the back wall. Power sets them level, about 14 inches apart, allows about 10 inches per bird, and hangs a dropping board about 6 inches below.
8. Raise the nests 4 to 5 inches off the floor, with slat or wire bottoms. Darken them with a hinged board or a sacking curtain.
9. Spread about 6 inches of dry litter. Put the dust box in the sun.
10. Let any plaster or cement dry fully before moving the birds in.
11. Remove droppings daily and dust the boards with dry sand. Change straw litter about every third day and sweep the walls weekly.

## From the source books

- Conover tells of 75 hens kept in a large building next to a barn that was ventilated only through a loft. They had colds all winter. The next winter, 40 hens in a 7 × 12 foot house with a canvas-covered window came through "in fine condition."
- Power says early glass-fronted and heated houses failed because they swung between warm days and cold nights. Hens can stand cold, he writes, "providing it is not a damp cold."
- Conover sets the windows level with the roosting hens' heads. Her roosts slope like a ladder at about 30 degrees. Power keeps his level so the hens do not fight over the top rung.
- Against rats, Power caps the posts with upturned tin pans and Conover nails tin around the floor edges.
- Conover whitewashes the walls about four times a year. Her perch wash used kerosene, and she also suggests dilute carbolic acid. Neither is advised today.

## Safety and modern practice

- Hardware cloth with 1/2-inch openings keeps out raccoons better than chicken wire. Shut the birds in at night.
- Wear a dust mask and gloves when cleaning. Wash your hands afterward, because poultry can carry *Salmonella*.
- Lime whitewash is caustic. Wear gloves and eye protection.
- Skip kerosene and homemade insecticides. Wash perches with soap and water, and ask a veterinarian about mites.
- Check local zoning rules on backyard flocks before you build.
