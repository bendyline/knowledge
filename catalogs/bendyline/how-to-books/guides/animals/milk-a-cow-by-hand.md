# Milk a cow by hand

Hand milking is a skill of grip and rhythm, and it is easy to learn with practice. The old dairy writers agree on three things. Be clean, be quiet and gentle, and milk out fully and quickly, because a nervous or half-milked cow gives less over time.

## What you need

- A clean pail large enough to hold the whole milking. Flint says you should not have to change pails partway.
- A milking stool.
- A brush, a sponge or cloth, tepid water and a dry towel for the udder.
- A strainer and a clean covered can.
- A little meal or hay to occupy the cow. Curtis calls this "a lick of meal."
- Clean hands and clean clothes.

## Steps

1. Milk at the same hours every day, as near 12 hours apart as you can, and milk several cows in the same order each time.
2. Bring the cow into a clean, quiet stall or stanchion. Curtis gives her a lick of meal or a little hay as she comes in.
3. Brush loose dirt and hair off her side and flank so nothing falls into the pail.
4. Wash the udder and teats. In Keeping One Cow, one keeper sponges with tepid water and wipes dry just before milking, except in the coldest weather.
5. Sit close at the cow's right side and hold the pail between your knees, where she cannot kick it over or step in it.
6. Grasp a teat so that it fills with milk. Close your thumb and forefinger to stop the milk running back up. Then close the other fingers in turn, firmly but gently, to press the milk down and out. Use the whole hand, not just thumb and finger.
7. Start gently and build to a steady, quick rhythm. Once you begin, do not stop, stand up or change pails, or the cow may hold back her milk.
8. Avoid pinching warts, opening cracks or digging in your fingernails.
9. Milk the udder out completely before you finish. Flint says milk left behind lowers the yield afterward.
10. Let the cow go, and take the milk away from the stable at once so it does not pick up odors.
11. Strain the milk into a clean can, and cool it before you cover it tightly.
12. Wash the pail and strainer straight away. Rinse in clean water, scald, rinse again and dry them on a rack.

## From the source books

- Flint explains that milk sits in pouches inside the udder. A slight upward "lift" of the udder before drawing helps move the milk down into the teat.
- Flint would milk fresh cows three times a day for the first two or three months after calving. He says the same person should milk the same cow every day, and that a cow treated harshly learns to kick.
- Joseph Earnest, in Keeping One Cow, says to "milk clean but do not 'strip,'" and to sing or whistle if you like. Flint insists on stripping to the last drop.
- One keeper in Keeping One Cow prefers that the cow not eat while being milked. Flint and Curtis think feed at milking time helps her let down her milk.
- Curtis advised cooling milk to 70 °F if it was kept overnight. That is far too warm by today's standards.
- Flint describes Dutch dairies that rinse utensils in cold running water, scald them, rinse them again and dry them on racks.

## Safety and modern practice

- Raw milk can carry harmful bacteria such as *E. coli* O157:H7, *Listeria*, *Salmonella* and *Campylobacter*. Health agencies recommend pasteurizing it. Chill milk to 40 °F or below within about two hours of milking.
- Many keepers now squirt the first streams from each teat into a dark cup to check the milk. Flakes, clots, blood or watery milk, or a hot, swollen or painful quarter, can mean mastitis. Keep that milk out of the supply and call a veterinarian.
- Stay at the cow's side, not behind her. Wear sturdy boots, and sit so you can move away quickly. Learn a calm cow's routine before milking a nervous one.
- Wash your hands before milking, and cover any cuts on them.
