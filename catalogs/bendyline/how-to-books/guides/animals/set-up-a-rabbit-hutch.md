# Set up a rabbit hutch

A traditional hutch gives a rabbit two rooms. One is an open, wire-fronted day room, and the other a dark, snug sleeping room. Every Boy's Book and Lewis F. Allen's Rural Architecture describe the same design. It should be dry, easy to clean, safe from rats, and kept up with daily care.

## What you need

- A strong box or boards for the hutch. Francis Rotch, quoted by Allen, gives about 3 feet long, 2 feet deep and 14 inches high as the best size. Alfred Rodman's hutches are 16 inches high.
- A board partition with a doorway near the bottom. Every Boy's Book adds a sliding door worked by a stiff wire through the roof.
- Stiff wire for the front bars, plus fine wire to bind them.
- Hinges and a hasp for the sleeping-room door.
- Tin or zinc strips to cover edges the rabbit may gnaw.
- A feed trough and a small water dish.
- Straw for bedding, and a scraper or small hoe for cleaning.

## Steps

1. Divide the hutch into a large day room and a small sleeping room. Rotch makes the sleeping room nearly a foot wide at one end. Cut a doorway near the bottom of the partition.
2. Fix the partition and its sliding door first, before the front, because the partition also stiffens the box.
3. Close the day room with upright wires about half an inch apart. Bind them with a cross wire and nail a strip of wood over the top ends.
4. Hang a solid wooden door on the sleeping room with hinges and a hasp.
5. Slope the floor about 2 inches toward the back, and leave notches or holes at the back so wet drains away.
6. Cover exposed edges with tin or zinc so they cannot be gnawed.
7. Put the hutch under a roof, where air moves freely. Stand it on a table whose top sticks out 5 or 6 inches beyond the legs, so rats cannot climb up.
8. Fit the feed trough with a wire along it to stop scratching out, and an upright wire in the middle so the rabbit cannot sit in it.
9. Bed the sleeping room with straw and change it daily.
10. Clean every day. Shut the rabbit in one room with the sliding door, scrape the other room clean and rub it with a wisp of straw, then switch rooms. Take out the trough, check it, and remove all stale food.
11. Feed on a regular schedule. Rotch gives a gill (about half a cup) of oats morning and evening with a cabbage leaf or another green of similar size, and a handful of cut hay or clover at noon. He feeds greens only when free of dew or rain.
12. Let the rabbit run for about half an hour a day, on a floor it cannot burrow through.
13. Keep adult bucks apart from does and from each other. Rotch separates young rabbits by about six months to stop fighting.

## From the source books

- Every Boy's Book makes a first hutch from an old tea chest or egg box, with only a few basic tools.
- Its safe greens include fine grasses, lettuce, parsley, carrots and their tops, turnip tops, clover and beet leaves. Parsley is a special favorite, and cabbage is to be avoided "as far as possible."
- The same book warns that cow parsley, a favorite rabbit food, looks very like poisonous hemlock.
- Rodman's rabbitry had hutches stacked four high, with a zinc plate and trough behind each floor to carry away urine.
- Both books say rabbits on green food need little or no water. Modern practice disagrees.

## Safety and modern practice

- Never lift a rabbit by its ears, although Every Boy's Book says to. Support the chest with one hand and the hindquarters with the other, and hold it close. A rabbit that kicks free can break its back.
- Give fresh water at all times, from a bottle or a heavy crock.
- Unlimited grass hay should make up most of a rabbit's diet, with fresh leafy greens and a measured amount of commercial pellets. Make changes slowly. Do not feed bread, potatoes or plants gathered from the wild.
- Modern welfare groups recommend much more space than a 3-foot hutch. The rabbit should be able to stretch out fully and stand upright, with an attached run or daily time in a secure exercise area.
- Give wire floors a solid resting board. Protect the hutch from predators, heat and direct summer sun, because rabbits suffer in heat above about 80 °F.
- Ask a veterinarian about spaying or neutering, and about vaccinations recommended where you live.
