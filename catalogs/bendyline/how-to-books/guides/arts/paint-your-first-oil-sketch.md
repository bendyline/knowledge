# Paint your first oil sketch

A quick oil sketch from life is the best way to learn the medium. Daniel Burleigh Parkhurst calls the sketch "the germ of the picture." It is one idea recorded in a single sitting, so you concentrate on color and value instead of finish. His rules for setting the palette and handling paint keep a first attempt fresh rather than muddy.

## What you need

- A canvas or panel. For outdoor sketches Parkhurst likes a size between 10 × 14 and 15 × 20 inches, and suggests sticking to one size.
- At least a dozen brushes of assorted shapes and sizes. He says you can't paint every kind of touch with one or two brushes.
- A palette bigger than you think you need, a palette knife, and rags.
- Oil paints: white and at least two of each primary color. Safe choices from Parkhurst's list of permanent colors are yellow ochre, raw sienna, burnt sienna, raw umber, rose madder, ultramarine, cobalt blue, viridian, terre verte and ivory black. For white, use titanium white (see the safety notes).
- A card with a rectangular hole cut in it, to frame your view.
- Mild soap and warm water for cleanup.

## Steps

1. Set the palette. Squeeze out generous amounts of white and at least two each of red, yellow and blue, choosing one of each that leans warm and one that leans cool. "Don't be niggardly with your paint," Parkhurst warns.
2. Look at your subject through the card window and decide what single effect drew you to it. Leave out everything that doesn't serve that idea.
3. Map the big shapes with a few exact lines and masses. Take a couple of measurements if you have time.
4. Rub in a *frottée*: thinly scrub in the light and shade with one color, such as sienna or umber, using very little medium. Every later color will then be judged against the right surroundings rather than bare white canvas.
5. Mix each tint fresh for the spot where it goes. Use no more than three pigments in a tint. Pull them together loosely with the brush and don't over-stir, because flecks of unmixed color keep the paint lively.
6. Lay the stroke on "frankly and fairly," and leave it. "Never tap, tap, tap, your paint," Parkhurst says. Paint in solid body color with large brushes, and work at arm's length, striking "from the shoulder."
7. If a stroke is wrong, scrape it off with the knife and mix a new tint. Don't pile wet paint on wet paint. Bigger corrections can wait until the layer is dry.
8. Finish the sketch in one sitting. Parkhurst says a second sitting makes it "neither one thing nor the other."
9. Clean up. Wipe the brushes on a rag, then work each one in soap and warm, not hot, water until the lather runs clean. Smooth the bristles straight before you set the brushes aside to dry.

## From the source books

- Parkhurst describes the French method *premier coup* ("first stroke"), in which every touch is laid once and never painted over. He calls it excellent training.
- He explains glazing (thin transparent color flowed over dry paint) and scumbling (opaque color rubbed thinly over dry paint). He warns that oil-heavy glazes turn brown over time and must be laid only over thoroughly dry paint.
- For the in-between daily cleaning, he rinsed brushes in a tin brush-washer filled with kerosene. He said turpentine made bristles brittle, and recommended a weekly wash with lye soap.
- His list of permanent colors also includes lead whites, vermilion, emerald green and strontian yellow. In his day these were standard. Today they are recognized as highly toxic.

## Safety and modern practice

- Don't use lead white (flake or Cremnitz) or vermilion (mercury). Historical emerald green was copper arsenite, and strontian yellow contains hexavalent chromium. Use titanium white and modern "hue" substitutes, keep paint off your skin, and don't eat while painting.
- Avoid kerosene and turpentine. If you need a thinner, use odorless mineral spirits sparingly with good ventilation and keep the lid closed. You can also clean brushes in a vegetable oil such as safflower before washing them with soap.
- Oily rags can catch fire on their own. Lay them flat outdoors to dry, or keep them in a closed metal can filled with water. Never pour solvent down a drain; take it to a hazardous-waste collection site.
