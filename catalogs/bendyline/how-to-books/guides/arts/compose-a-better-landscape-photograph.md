# Compose a better landscape photograph

A camera records everything in front of it. A good picture comes from choosing what to include and where to stand. The advice here comes from two contributors to The Barnet Book of Photography: A. Horsley Hinton on pictorial composition and W. Thomas on the hand camera. P. H. Emerson adds his views on light and exposure. Their advice applies just as well to a phone or a digital camera.

## What you need

- Any camera with a viewfinder or screen that shows exactly what will be in the frame. Thomas stresses a finder blocked off to show the true picture area.
- A tripod, or something solid to brace the camera against.
- If possible, a longer lens or zoom setting, to leave out distracting objects.
- Time to walk around the subject and to wait for the light.

## Steps

1. Decide what the picture is about. Hinton's rule is that "there must be one and only one chief object." A tree, gate or windmill near the edge that competes with it will make the eye wander.
2. Place the main subject near the center, with lesser objects close by to support it. Avoid perfect symmetry, which looks contrived. Hinton's advice is to follow the rules "only so far as to avoid the appearance of having infringed or ignored them."
3. Keep anything eye-catching away from the edges, where it makes the viewer aware of the frame.
4. Keep the horizon out of the middle of the frame, top to bottom.
5. Read the lines of the scene, such as shorelines, cloud edges, roads and hedges. Move until they balance and lead toward a point inside the picture. Avoid a road or fence that runs straight across and "cut[s] the picture in two."
6. Change your position. Hinton found that a few steps can transform a scene. Sometimes you need to wait for the light to shift or the tide to change.
7. Judge the light and dark masses. Close your eyes, then open them suddenly: your eye goes straight to the brightest light or the deepest dark. That spot should be on or near your main subject, not at the margin.
8. Use the light that suits the subject. Hinton prizes misty evenings, when objects stand out as broad masses against each other. Emerson insists that "beautiful pictures are to be obtained on the grey dull days of November as in sunny June."
9. Control sharpness. Hinton found that stopping the lens down very small, around f/32, made everything crisp but "cut out" the sense of atmosphere. Use only as much sharpness as the picture needs.
10. Hold the camera still. For an exposure of a second or two without a tripod, Thomas rests the camera on a stone, wall or gate, or presses it against a tree trunk.
11. Crop afterward if needed. Hinton says a picture half the size but well composed is better than a large one with a distracting arrangement.

## From the source books

- Thomas says the most common failure with hand cameras was under-exposure. He advises giving "the slowest exposure possible," so moving water looks natural rather than frozen. Emerson argues the opposite: quick exposures are "absolutely essential" to catch fleeting effects. Today you can try both and compare.
- Thomas found that with the lens at f/11 and the focus set at 15 feet, there was enough depth of field for most ordinary hand-camera work. Photographers still use this "zone focusing" approach.
- Hinton thought wide-angle lenses flatten the view and make near objects look out of proportion.
- Hinton calls "Clear glass shadows" (shadows with no detail at all) "an enormity." He wanted soft gradation from light to dark.

## Safety and modern practice

- The source books include darkroom developer formulas, which are left out here. If you develop film, use current commercial chemistry and follow its label. Wear gloves and eye protection, work with good ventilation, and keep chemicals away from food and children.
- Never look at the sun through a viewfinder or lens. Mind your footing near cliffs, water and roads while you concentrate on the frame.
- Respect private property and people's privacy when photographing.
