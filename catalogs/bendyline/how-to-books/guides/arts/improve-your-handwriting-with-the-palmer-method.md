# Improve your handwriting with the Palmer method

The Palmer Method is a self-teaching course in "muscular movement" writing. The arm rests on the desk and does the work, while the fingers simply hold the pen. The result is quick, even, unshaded handwriting that doesn't cramp the hand. A. N. Palmer insists that you master position and movement before you write a single letter.

## What you need

- A desk or table and a chair that let you sit upright with room for both forearms.
- Ruled paper. Palmer's drills use lines ⅜ inch apart on sheets 8 × 10½ inches.
- A pen that moves smoothly. Palmer used an ordinary dip pen; any smooth-writing pen will do.
- A watch or clock with a second hand, for timing the drills.

## Steps

1. Sit facing the desk squarely, or turned slightly to the right if the desk is small. Sit well back so your body doesn't touch the desk. Rest your left forearm on the desk to keep yourself upright, and use your left hand to move the paper.
2. Find your arm position. Raise your right arm a few inches, relax it, and let it drop onto the desk. Wherever it lands is right, with a square bend at the elbow.
3. Check your resting points. The main rest is the thick muscle of the forearm near the elbow. The third and fourth fingers glide lightly over the paper as a second, movable rest. The wrist and the side of the hand never touch the paper.
4. "Run the writing machine" without a pen. Make a loose fist and push the hand forward and pull it back. Your sleeve stays still on the desk while the arm muscle rolls inside it.
5. Pick up the pen. Let the holder rest a little below the big knuckle. The first finger lies on top about one inch from the point, and the thumb sits opposite the first joint of that finger. Hold it lightly, without pinching.
6. Position the paper so your forearm crosses its bottom edge a little right of center. As you write across a line, lift the pen and slide the paper left three or four times so your arm keeps its position.
7. Practice the straight-line drill. Make push-pull strokes two line-spaces high, slanting toward the center of your body. If you can't make them that tall with the arm alone, practice the motion with the pen just above the paper first.
8. Practice ovals. Go round and round the same oval, about six times at first and later ten, keeping the strokes light and close together.
9. Time yourself. Aim for 200 downward strokes a minute, counting only the downstrokes aloud. Too slow makes shaky lines, and too fast makes ragged ones.
10. Practice 15 to 30 minutes a day, and use the same arm movement in everything you write, not just during drills.

## From the source books

- Palmer's test is blunt: "It is not Palmer Method if the lines are tremulous."
- He believed slant takes care of itself when the position is right. Writers' slants differ with the length of their arms, and what matters is that each writer's slant is uniform. He warns never to make ovals on a "back slant."
- He claimed that a diligent pupil could master the style within six months to a school year. One boy of twelve made 3,000 ovals across an 8-inch line at 200 strokes a minute.
- If your arm is so thin that the elbow bone grates on the desk, Palmer lets the elbow hang off the edge, but by no more than an inch.
- His summary of practice: "A few minutes in the right way are worth more than hours of practice in the wrong way."

## Safety and modern practice

- Palmer's directions assume a right-handed writer. Left-handers should mirror them, tilting the paper the other way and keeping the hand below the line of writing to avoid smudging.
- Take short breaks to stretch your hands and shoulders. Pain, numbness or tingling that lasts after writing may be a repetitive-strain problem worth checking with a clinician.
- If you use a dip pen and bottled ink, cap the ink and keep it away from small children.
