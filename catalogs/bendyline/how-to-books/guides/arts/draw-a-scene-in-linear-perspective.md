# Draw a scene in linear perspective

Linear perspective is a set of rules for making flat lines suggest depth. It is how floors look level, roads recede and buildings stand square. G. A. Storey calls it "the sure and unerring basis" of freehand drawing, even though "Nature does all his perspective for him" when an artist sketches outdoors. This guide covers his main rules, plus Frank Fowler's way of checking proportions by eye.

## What you need

- Paper on a drawing board, a pencil and an eraser.
- A ruler or straightedge for construction lines.
- A long pencil to sight with at arm's length.
- Optional: a window or a pane of glass. Storey explains perspective by imagining tracing the scene onto a sheet of glass, the "picture plane," held between your eye and the view.

## Steps

1. Decide where your eye is, and draw the horizon line across the paper at that height. Storey points out that the horizon always rises and falls with your eye. Put it low to give a building or mountain "height and dignity," or high to show a wide landscape.
2. Mark the "point of sight" on the horizon. This is the spot directly opposite your eye, usually near the middle of the picture.
3. Choose your viewing distance and mark the "point of distance" on the horizon, as far from the point of sight as you are from the picture. If it is too close, squares look distorted and floors seem to tilt uphill. Storey prefers the French rule of thumb: a distance about twice the width of the picture.
4. Draw the faces that look straight at you at their true shape. Vertical lines stay vertical, and horizontal lines parallel to the bottom of the picture stay level.
5. Draw every horizontal edge that runs straight away from you so that it points to the point of sight. Storey calls this "the most important rule in perspective." It gives you the rails, curbs, ceiling beams and floorboards of one-point perspective.
6. Measure depth with the point of distance. Lines at 45 degrees to the picture run to it. Draw a diagonal from a corner on the base line to the point of distance, and where it crosses a receding line, you have marked off a true square. Repeat this to lay out floor tiles or fence posts that shrink correctly.
7. For objects turned at an angle, find a separate vanishing point on the horizon for each set of parallel edges. Edges that run at more than 45 degrees to the picture vanish between the point of sight and the point of distance. Edges at less than 45 degrees vanish beyond the point of distance. Roads going uphill and stairs vanish above the horizon, and slopes going downhill vanish below it.
8. Remember that the farther away something is, the closer it sits to the horizon. Use this to place distant figures and buildings.
9. Check proportions by sight, as Fowler teaches. Hold a pencil at arm's length, close one eye, and mark one distance on the pencil with your thumb. Then turn the pencil to see how many times that distance fits into another dimension.

## From the source books

- Storey says the strict rules assume one fixed eye. Outdoors the artist may move his head and allow for uneven ground.
- He insists that the vertical lines of a tall building stay parallel unless you tilt your head to look up at it. Converging verticals are what we now call three-point perspective.
- He blames wide-angle camera lenses for distortions like a church's east end appearing "higher than the steeple," and points to a Paul Potter cattle painting with the horizon only a quarter of the way up the canvas.
- To prove that receding lines converge, he suggests sketching yourself and your room in a mirror on the wall. The lines at right angles to the glass all lead to your own eye.
- Fowler forbids measuring with a ruler at the start of a freehand drawing, and uses a weighted string as a plumb line to check verticals.

## Safety and modern practice

- Drawing is low-risk, but when sighting outdoors toward the horizon, never look directly at the sun.
- Work at an easel or a sloped board in good light, and take breaks to rest your eyes and wrist.
- Many drawing apps and tablets offer perspective grids that apply the same rules automatically. They are useful for checking a hand-built construction.
