# Shade a drawing with charcoal

Shading makes a flat outline look solid. Frank Fowler's method works for any subject, whether a cast, a still life or a face. First divide the subject into "two great masses" of light and shadow. Lay the shadow in as one even tone, then refine the in-between tones and add the final accents. John Ruskin's pen-and-pencil exercises train the even, graded tone the method depends on.

## What you need

- Charcoal sticks, and a black Conté crayon (Fowler uses No. 2) for finishing.
- Rough charcoal paper, with two or three spare sheets underneath for padding, tacked to a drawing board about 20 × 24 inches. An easel is better than a flat table.
- Paper stumps for blending. Fowler keeps six: "two large, two medium, and two very small."
- Fowler's erasers are crumb from day-old bread made without butter or milk, rolled to a point, and a pointed rubber stump. A kneaded eraser does the same job today.
- A soft cotton rag, a sharp knife and sandpaper for pointing the charcoal, and fixative.
- For practice: smooth paper and a hard (H or HH) pencil, as Ruskin suggests.

## Steps

1. Practice even tone first. Draw a small square and fill it with crossed lines until it looks like "a square patch of gray silk." Then shade a band that grades evenly from black to white. Ruskin has you do this first in pen, then with the pencil point.
2. Light the subject from one direction only. Close off any cross light. Fowler prefers a north window, with the light coming from above and over your left shoulder.
3. Mark the top, bottom and sides of the subject with small dots so it sits well on the page.
4. Sketch the outline lightly with long, sweeping lines, "seeking for angles and avoiding curves." Curves come later.
5. Find the darkest dark and the brightest light in the subject, and compare every other tone against those two.
6. Lightly outline the shape of each shadow where it meets the light. Fill it with close, slightly slanted parallel strokes of charcoal.
7. Blend each shadow into one flat, even tone with your largest stump. Hold it so about an inch of its point lies on the paper.
8. Step back and check the proportions now, while corrections are still easy.
9. Make the half-tints. With a clean, medium-sized stump, drag charcoal out of the shadows into the lights. Don't add fresh charcoal, so these tones stay light.
10. Add the dark accents with sharp charcoal or the Conté crayon. Lift reflected lights and crisp highlights with the pointed bread or eraser. If a shadow gets too dark, brush it lightly with the rag.
11. Keep your hand off the drawing as you work. Rest it on the margin or on a clean sheet of paper.
12. Spray the finished drawing with fixative.

## From the source books

- Fowler describes two schools of charcoal drawing. Illustrators shaded with lines only, because that reproduced well in print. Art schools blended with the stump.
- Fowler warned that fixative homemade from shellac and alcohol yellows the paper over time. Instead he sprayed a bought fixative through a glass atomizer by blowing into it by mouth. French students sometimes just washed the back of a drawing with milk.
- Ruskin distrusted the stump. He said natural shadows always hold "gleams of light" that only the point can render, and that "Coarse art is always bad art." He promised that "an hour's practice a day for six months" would bring real progress.

## Safety and modern practice

- Don't blow charcoal dust off the paper; tap it off over a wastebasket instead. Wash your hands, and wipe your work area with a damp cloth.
- Use a commercial spray or pump fixative outdoors or with strong ventilation, away from flames. Wear a mask rated for organic vapors if you spray often. Never use a mouth-blown atomizer with solvent fixative.
- When you sharpen charcoal with a knife, cut away from yourself.
