# Paint a watercolor landscape with washes

Watercolor rests on one skill: laying liquid color evenly or in a smooth gradation. John Ruskin's chessboard exercises teach the wash. David Cox's treatise shows how to build a landscape from washes, working from the sky down to the foreground. Start with one color before trying many.

## What you need

- One dark, easily mixed color to practice with. Ruskin used a cake of Prussian blue. Cox studied light and shade in sepia or India ink before using color.
- A soft brush with a good point. Ruskin's camel's-hair brush was about ¾ inch long. Cox calls brushes "hair pencils."
- White saucers or a palette with wells, one for each tint you mix.
- Smooth, but not glossy, heavy paper or board stretched on a drawing board. Something to prop the board at a slope.
- Blotting paper or a rag, and clean water.

## Steps

1. Mix enough color. Rub the paint with a drop of water until it is dark and thick, then stir in about two teaspoonfuls of water.
2. Prop your board at the slope of an ordinary writing desk. Rule some squares about the size of large chessboard squares.
3. Lay a flat wash. Load the brush fully and run a "pond" of color along the top edge of a square. Lead it down the square evenly, like adding a row of bricks, but building downward. Recharge the brush often so the bead stays wet.
4. Don't go back to fix gaps or edges, because speed matters more than neatness. At the bottom, dry the brush on blotting paper and use it to soak up the pool of extra color.
5. Fill alternate squares, let them dry, then turn the board upside down and give each one a second coat. Turning the board evens out the darker band that forms at the bottom of each wash.
6. Lay a graded wash. Start with strong color at the top. Before each new brushful, dip the brush in water and stir it into the mix, so every stroke is a little paler, until the color fades to clear. Repeat over the dry wash until the band runs smoothly from dark to white.
7. For complicated shapes, flood the whole shape first with very pale, wet color. Then drop darker color into the middle and lead it out to the edges.
8. Now plan a landscape. Draw a clear, decided outline. In the distance give only the general shapes, such as a mountain's skyline, and save detail for the foreground.
9. Mix three or four tints, each in its own saucer, one for each distance in the scene.
10. Paint the sky first, keeping the board well sloped and carrying the tint cleanly up to the outline.
11. Lay in the masses of shadow from back to front: the far distance, then the middle distance, then the foreground. Keep the brush "tolerably full of colour" so the paint floats and dries clear.
12. Lay each tint with decision. Don't scrub the brush back and forth timidly. Finish by softening shadows into lights with a slightly lighter tint and adding the darkest touches last.

## From the source books

- Ruskin judged progress by the edges of the washes. The jagged dark lines where squares meet are "the exact measure of your unskillfulness."
- He later builds a value scale from graded strips of several colors, so a colored object can be translated into the right gray.
- Cox advises making the lights warm in color, except on glossy surfaces such as leaves or glazed pots, and putting a cool sky over a warm landscape. Put the strongest light on the main subject, and let tints grow lighter and grayer in the distance.
- Cox's "pencil" means a brush, and "strained" paper means paper stretched on the board.

## Safety and modern practice

- Ruskin told students to dry the brush on blotting paper or a rag "instead of your lips," to avoid "partial poisoning." Some of the pigments he goes on to list, such as vermilion (a mercury compound) and gamboge, are toxic. Never point a brush in your mouth. Choose modern paints labeled as conforming to ASTM D-4236, and don't eat or drink while you paint.
- Cadmium and cobalt pigments are best kept off your skin and out of the drain. Wipe brushes on paper towels before rinsing, and wash your hands afterward.
