# Prepare a beehive for winter

Most winter losses are decided in early fall. Benton's rules come down to a young queen, plenty of young bees, ample good food, a dry and warm hive, and no disturbance once cold weather arrives.

## What you need

- A colony headed by a queen no more than two years old, preferably raised that season
- At least 30 to 45 pounds of well-ripened, sealed honey per hive
- Granulated sugar and a feeder, if stores are short
- For a strong colony, two ten-frame Langstroth hive bodies (Phillips and Demuth)
- An entrance reducer, or a block that leaves a very small opening
- Dry packing such as sawdust, planer shavings, dry leaves gathered the previous year, chaff or cork, held in a rainproof outer case

## Steps

1. Check the queen and the bees in late summer. Benton wants young bees still emerging into October. In cool fall weather a good colony should cover at least six spaces between combs, preferably eight or nine, in a cluster at least 8 and preferably 10 to 12 inches across.
2. Weigh up the stores. Benton calls 40 pounds a good supply in the North and 30 in the middle states. Phillips and Demuth leave at least 45 pounds with their big two-story colonies, saying "the best time to do spring feeding is the fall before."
3. Remove poor winter food. Dark, gummy tree honeys, and especially honeydew with its bad taste and muddy look, can give wintering bees dysentery. Replace them with combs of good honey.
4. Feed any shortfall early, soon after the last honey flow. Use syrup made from granulated sugar, never molasses, brown sugar or glucose. Benton gives several pounds at a time, at nightfall, in the top story, so the bees store it within about a week without starting to raise extra brood.
5. Leave the pollen. Benton points out that colonies start rearing brood in late winter and need it then.
6. Arrange the hive so the honey sits compactly around the center of the brood nest, where the cluster will form.
7. Reduce the entrance. Phillips and Demuth bored ⅜-inch holes in the packing case, used four in fall and spring, and left only one open in the coldest weather. Benton recommends an indirect or screened entrance that admits air without drafts.
8. Pack the hive where winters are hard. For a climate like Washington, D.C., Phillips and Demuth used 4 inches of packing beneath the hive, 6 on the sides and 8 or more on top. For New York or Wisconsin they used about 8 inches on the sides and a foot on top. Keep all packing dry inside a rainproof case.
9. Leave the colony alone once the weather turns cold. Breaking up the cluster or making the bees gorge when they can't fly out brings on dysentery.
10. During winter, check that dead bees or ice aren't blocking the entrance.

## From the source books

- The sources disagree about ventilation. Benton wanted moist air to escape slowly through packing above the bees. Phillips and Demuth say a really well-packed hive has no condensation and needs no upward ventilation, and that wet top packing means too little packing.
- Phillips and Demuth found that most heat escapes through the bottom board. Until the bottom is packed, anything over about 2 inches on the top and sides adds little.
- They dismissed straw or corn fodder wrapped around a hive, and roofing paper, as nearly worthless insulation.
- Benton gives cellar wintering as an alternative in severe climates: a dry, dark cellar near 42°F early in winter, with 20 to 25 pounds of stores per hive.
- In the far South, Benton says little preparation is needed beyond keeping stores from running out.

## Safety and modern practice

- Heavy packing is rare today. Many beekeepers in cold regions use a wrap of roofing felt or foam board, an upper entrance, and a moisture-absorbing quilt or insulated cover. In mild climates hives are often left unwrapped.
- A strong mite load in late summer is now the leading cause of winter colony loss. Test for Varroa mites well before fall and follow current extension guidance.
- Don't feed honey from other apiaries or unknown sources. It can spread American foulbrood.
- Full hives are heavy. To judge stores, tip up the back of the hive slightly instead of lifting it.
