# Check a hive for a laying queen

A colony lives only as long as its queen keeps laying, so checking for her is the most important routine inspection. You rarely need to see the queen herself. Fresh eggs and young larvae prove she was there and laying within the last few days.

## What you need

- Veil, smoker and the usual inspection gear
- Good daylight, so you can see into the bottoms of cells
- A notebook to record the date and what you find in each hive
- If the hive turns out queenless, a comb with eggs and young brood from a strong colony

## Steps

1. Watch the entrance first on a warm day. Langstroth says a colony with a fertile queen brings in pollen eagerly to feed its brood. Queenless bees roam restlessly in and out, linger idly at the entrance, and stay unsettled after other hives have quieted.
2. Open the hive gently, using as little smoke as possible so the bees stay on the combs. See [Open and inspect a beehive calmly](open-and-inspect-a-beehive-calmly.md).
3. Lift one of the central combs. Benton describes a normal brood nest as brood in the middle of the hive, a band of pollen cells above and beside it, and honey stored outside that.
4. Tilt the comb toward the light and look into the bottoms of the worker cells for eggs. Eggs hatch on the third day, so finding them means the queen was laying very recently.
5. Look for tiny white larvae curled in the cells, and for sealed worker brood. Worker cappings are brown and nearly flat; drone cappings are more domed and paler, over larger cells.
6. Check the pattern. If you find many drone larvae in small worker cells, the queen's store of sperm may be used up and she can now lay only drone eggs. Langstroth describes this happening to old queens.
7. Look for queen cells, which are shaped like a peanut and hang downward from the face or lower edge of a comb.
8. If you find no eggs and no young larvae, suspect that the queen is lost. Give the colony a comb with eggs and young brood from another hive. If the bees start queen cells on it within a few days, they had no queen.
9. After a swarm, or when a young queen has just emerged, wait about a week before you check. Langstroth says that in good weather a young queen flies out to mate the day after she becomes sole queen and starts laying about two days later. Allow longer in bad weather or a weak colony.
10. Decide what to do. A strong queenless colony can raise a new queen from the brood you gave it, if drones are flying, or you can introduce a laying queen. A small queenless colony is best combined with a stock that has a good queen.

## From the source books

- Benton gives development times from egg to adult as about 15½ days for a queen, 21 for a worker and 24 for a drone.
- A good colony at the start of the season, Benton says, has 30,000 to 40,000 workers and four or more combs of brood. A queen can lay as many as 4,000 eggs a day. He advises rarely keeping a queen past two years, and none past three unless she is a valuable breeder.
- Benton puts mating at five to nine days old and believed a queen mated only once, storing enough sperm for years. We now know she mates with many drones, over one or a few flights.
- Langstroth checked every hive for brood early in September. A queenless colony he either broke up, adding its bees to another stock, or, if it was strong, gave a queen by uniting a weaker stock with it.
- Langstroth found that bees left queenless for a long time sometimes refuse both brood and a new queen, so act quickly.

## Safety and modern practice

- Most beekeepers today simply buy a mated queen from a breeder and introduce her in a cage, rather than wait for a colony to raise its own.
- Many workers laying eggs (several eggs per cell, scattered drone brood) is a different problem from a failing queen. Ask a local mentor before you try to requeen.
- Reading glasses or a hand lens and sunlight over your shoulder make eggs much easier to see.
- Keep inspections short in cool weather to avoid chilling the brood.
