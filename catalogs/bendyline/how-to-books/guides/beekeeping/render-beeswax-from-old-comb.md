# Render beeswax from old comb

Cappings sliced off during extracting, broken bits of comb, cut-out drone comb and worn-out brood combs all contain beeswax worth saving. Benton recommends the sun-heated solar wax extractor as the cleanest method for an ordinary apiary. It renders small batches easily and bleaches the wax light.

## What you need

- A solar wax extractor: a metal tank with a glass cover (double glass works better), a wire-cloth strainer inside, and a pan at the lower edge to catch the wax, mounted so it can be tilted toward the sun
- A mirror or sheet of bright metal as a reflector (optional)
- A close box for collecting scraps of comb
- A coarse bag or colander for draining cappings
- Rain water, and a tin or copper melting pot that sits inside a larger pot of water
- Pans or molds for the finished cakes

## Steps

1. Drain the honey from the cappings through a coarse bag or colander. Cook says gentle warmth that doesn't melt the wax, with frequent stirring, speeds this up.
2. Collect every scrap of comb in a closed box. Don't let it sit long in warm weather; Benton warns that stored comb breeds wax moths. Cook says comb containing larvae should be melted often so it doesn't start to smell.
3. Set the solar extractor in the sunniest spot near the apiary and tilt it to face the sun directly.
4. Load cappings and bits of comb onto the strainer, close the glass, and add more scraps through the day. The melted wax trickles through the strainer into the pan.
5. Remove the cake each morning. Overnight it cools and shrinks enough to come away from the pan easily.
6. For dark old brood combs, break them up and soak them for 24 hours in rain water before melting. Benton says this keeps the cocoons and pollen in the residue from soaking up as much wax.
7. To purify cakes, put them in the tin or copper pot and stand it in the larger pot of water. Melt them slowly, using rain water wherever water touches the wax.
8. Keep the wax liquid for some time so the dirt settles to the bottom, then let it cool into a cake.
9. Scrape the dark layer of sediment off the bottom of the cake. If it still holds a lot of wax, save it to melt again.
10. Store the clean cakes. Benton says cakes going to a foundation maker can go straight from the extractor, but wax for general sale should be remelted this way first.

## From the source books

- Benton says the old way, boiling comb in a weighted cloth sack in a kettle of water, was messy and often gave burned, water-soaked or dirty wax.
- Cook improves on the sack method by keeping the bag off the kettle bottom with an upturned basin and repeating the melt until the wax is clean. He jokes that a proper wax extractor avoids the wax boiling over onto the stove and upsetting "the best woman in the world."
- Benton notes that iron vessels and iron-bearing well or spring water discolor wax. Heating it too fast makes it pale and grainy, but slow remelting, especially in the sun, restores it.
- Benton says solar extractors work four or five months a year in the northern states and longer in the South. In other seasons beekeepers used steam extractors.
- Because bees use 12 to 15 pounds of honey to make a pound of comb, Benton advises taking wax only as a by-product and turning it back into foundation.

## Safety and modern practice

- Beeswax is flammable. Never melt it in a pot directly over a flame or burner. Use a double boiler or a solar melter, never leave melting wax unattended, and keep a lid and a fire extinguisher handy. Never throw water on a wax fire.
- Hot wax causes serious burns. Wear gloves and long sleeves, and keep children and pets away.
- Use dedicated pots and utensils for wax rather than your cooking pans.
- Benton mentions fumigating stored comb with burning sulfur or carbon bisulfide. Both are dangerous and are not used by hobbyists today. Many beekeepers freeze stored comb for a couple of days to kill wax moth eggs and larvae instead.
- Comb from colonies with American foulbrood should not be rendered for reuse in hives; follow your state apiarist's instructions.
