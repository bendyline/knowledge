# Extract honey from hive frames

A honey extractor spins uncapped combs so the honey flies out of the cells, leaving the combs nearly whole to go back into the hive. Cook ranked its inventor second only to Langstroth among beekeeping's benefactors. Reusing combs matters because, by Benton's estimate, bees use 12 to 15 pounds of honey to build a pound of comb.

## What you need

- A honey extractor: a metal can with a light revolving basket, best driven by gearing for an even speed
- An uncapping knife and a pan of hot water to keep it warm
- A sieve over a pan, or a special can, to catch and drain the cappings
- Bee escapes set in a board (Benton), or a large feather for brushing bees off
- A light box or empty hive body with a close cover to carry combs
- A bee-tight room for the work
- A cloth or wire sieve for straining, and clean containers

## Steps

1. Wait until the bees have sealed the cells. Cook urges never extracting before then, because unripe honey can sour. Benton says sealed honey is generally ripe.
2. The day before you extract, slip a board fitted with bee escapes between the honey stories and the brood chamber. Do it in the morning if the bees are still gathering, or the evening before if not. The supers will be nearly empty of bees when you return. This only works if the queen has been kept out of the upper stories.
3. If you have no escapes, smoke the bees, then shake or brush them off each comb with a large feather.
4. Put each comb into the covered box as soon as it is free of bees, and carry the combs to the extracting room. Keep everything covered so robber bees can't find it.
5. Hold a comb upright over the cappings pan. Slice the cappings off with the hot knife, using the point to reach low spots.
6. Load the extractor with combs of nearly equal weight on opposite sides to reduce strain. Benton suggests putting each top bar to your right and turning the basket from right to left, since cells slope slightly upward.
7. Turn the basket fast enough to empty one side, but not so hard that you break new combs or throw out brood. Then reverse the combs and empty the other side.
8. Draw the honey off and strain it through a cloth or wire sieve. Alternatively, Cook lets it stand in a tank with a gate near the bottom until wax bits rise, then draws off the clear honey below.
9. Bottle or store the honey in a dry place. Benton says it can improve further if kept a while in a dry, warm room.
10. Return the emptied combs to the hives. If the honey flow is over, do it just before nightfall so the bees clean them up without being stirred to rob. After hard frosts, store the combs in a dry, cold room, hung so they don't touch.

## From the source books

- Benton reckons colonies given plenty of ready-built combs can store more than twice as much honey as colonies that must build comb while storing.
- Cook describes a time-saving rotation. Empty one colony's combs, put them into the next hive in exchange for its full ones, and repay the first colony at the end.
- Granulated honey can be made liquid again by warming the container in a water bath. Cook says never above 200°F, with wood or a tin basin under the jar so it doesn't touch the hot bottom.
- Benton warns that the acids in honey attack galvanized iron, zinc and plain iron, so extractor parts that touch honey should be tinned. Cook still allowed galvanized iron.
- Cook coated storage barrels inside with hot beeswax or paraffin to stop leaks.

## Safety and modern practice

- Use stainless steel or food-grade plastic for anything that touches honey. Avoid galvanized metal and lead-soldered cans.
- Warm crystallized honey gently, ideally below about 110°F (43°C). Much hotter darkens it and harms its flavor, so Cook's 200°F ceiling is far too high by today's standards.
- Many beekeepers check moisture with a refractometer before bottling. Honey above roughly 18.6 percent water can ferment.
- Never give honey to infants under one year old, because of the risk of infant botulism. Label jars accordingly.
- A full deep super can weigh 60 pounds or more. Lift with your legs, keep floors free of sticky spills, and keep the room sealed against bees.
