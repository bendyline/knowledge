# Catch and hive a swarm of bees

A swarm is the old queen leaving with many of the workers to found a new colony. The bees fill up with honey before leaving, so a freshly settled swarm is usually gentle. The goal is to get the whole cluster, queen included, into a cool hive quickly.

## What you need

- An empty hive, made ready before swarming season and kept in the shade, with its entrance opened full width
- Frames with foundation starters, a small piece of worker comb, or a line of melted wax on each top bar as a guide
- A veil and a lit smoker, or a sprinkler of sweetened water
- A light, open basket or swarm catcher; a pole to raise it if the swarm is high
- A sheet to spread in front of the hive
- Pruning shears, a large feather, and a big spoon or small dipper

## Steps

1. Learn the warning signs during the honey flow. Workers stop foraging and loiter around the entrance of a strong, crowded colony while other hives keep working, and the combs hold queen cells with eggs or larvae in them. Benton says the first swarm usually leaves 24 to 48 hours after the first queen cell is sealed.
2. Watch on fair, still days. Langstroth found most swarms come out between 10 a.m. and 2 p.m., and most often between 11 and 1.
3. Let the swarm settle into a cluster. Langstroth notes a clustered swarm will almost always stay an hour or two, so work calmly, but hive it before another swarm can join it.
4. Put on your veil. Give the cluster a little smoke or sprinkle it with sweetened water.
5. Spread the sheet smoothly in front of the hive entrance, raising the hive slightly in front if needed.
6. Collect the bees. If the cluster hangs from a low limb, hold the basket under it and shake the limb so the bees drop in. If the limb is worthless and small, cut it with pruning shears and carry it, bees and all. If the hive can sit right under the cluster, shake the bees straight in.
7. For a high swarm, raise the basket on a pole under the cluster and jerk it. For bees on a trunk, brush or smoke them in, or scoop them with the dipper.
8. Carry the bees slowly to the hive and pour them onto the sheet close to the entrance. They will march in, fanning their wings. Spoon a few to the entrance, or part clumps with the feather, to start them.
9. Watch for the queen as she crosses the sheet. If the bees refuse to enter, or pour back out and cluster on the tree again, the queen was left behind. Repeat the process.
10. As soon as the bees are inside, move the hive to its permanent stand. Don't leave it until evening; scouts returning from the search for a new home can lure the swarm away.
11. Shade and ventilate the new hive. Add supers for honey after two or three days, once the colony has made a good start.

## From the source books

- Benton preferred to set the new swarm on the parent colony's old stand and move the parent hive to a new spot. The swarm then collects nearly all the field bees and is ready to store honey.
- Langstroth warns that fresh paint, or a thin hive left in hot sun, can make a swarm desert.
- To prevent swarming, Benton advises giving plenty of storage room early, ventilating and shading hives, and replacing old queens with young ones of the current season. He found that cutting out queen cells once or twice a week is laborious and unreliable.
- Benton describes clipping one wing of a laying queen so a swarm cannot fly off with her. The beekeeper catches the queen on the ground and lets her run into a new hive set on the old stand.
- Langstroth calls the old way of climbing trees and sawing off large limbs, which crushes bees and risks the queen, something to "be entirely abandoned."

## Safety and modern practice

- Don't climb ladders into trees to reach a swarm. Use a pole, or leave a high or hard-to-reach swarm for an experienced swarm collector.
- In parts of the southern and southwestern United States, swarms may be Africanized and highly defensive. Follow local guidance and call a professional there.
- Langstroth recommends paint with no white lead. Today, never use lead-based paint on hives.
- Wear a veil even with a gentle swarm, and keep onlookers well back.
