# Set up a first beehive

A first hive succeeds or fails on three choices made before any bees arrive: a strong, healthy colony, an accurately built frame hive, and a good site. Benton and Cook advise starting small, in spring, with the hives close enough to the house to watch.

## What you need

- One or two colonies of a gentle race (Benton names Italians or Carniolans), bought from a reputable beekeeper nearby
- Frame hives built accurately, holding ten frames per story; standard Langstroth frames measure 17⅝ by 9⅛ inches outside
- Frames spaced 1⅜ inches center to center, with comb foundation or foundation starters
- A tight, well-painted cover and a bottom board
- A stand that lifts the hive 6 to 8 inches off the ground, with a sloping board up to the entrance
- A bellows smoker and one or two bee veils

## Steps

1. Plan to start in spring. Benton notes that spring mistakes can be corrected before winter, while a late start can be fatal.
2. Choose a site near the house where the hives can be seen. It should be well drained, on a gentle slope, and not facing north. In cold regions, face it southeast.
3. Give the site a windbreak on the north and west, such as a hedge, a board fence or a row of evergreens.
4. Arrange shade for the heat of the day but sun early and late. Cook faced his hives east and shaded them with evergreens (he found board fences "gloomy and forbidding") or a grapevine trellis. Avoid dense, damp shade and tall trees, because swarms that settle high up are hard and dangerous to reach.
5. Level the stand and set the bottom board on it. Make sure the hive sides overlap the bottom board so rain runs off.
6. Assemble the hive body. Leave ¼ to ⅜ inch between the frame ends and the hive walls so bees can pass without gluing the frames fast.
7. Inspect the colony before you buy it. Look for brood in several combs, straight combs, and no more than about one-eighth drone comb (larger cells in thicker comb). In early spring a good colony carries several pounds of sealed honey; Benton prefers about 20.
8. Reject any colony with brown spotting on its combs or a putrid smell, or with sunken, perforated cappings over brown, ropy cell contents. Those are signs of foul brood. Buy nothing from that apiary or from any apiary near it.
9. Move the bees late in the day with the entrance closed, using an open cloth for ventilation. Pack the hive so it can't shift, and drive slowly.
10. Set the hive on its stand, open the entrance, and leave the colony alone to settle.

## From the source books

- Benton put the cost of essential tools (a smoker, a wax extractor, a few queen cages and a veil) at only $5 to $6 in his day. He warns that cheap common bees in box hives cost no less in the end once they have been transferred and requeened.
- Benton also suggests a beginner can agree to receive a neighbor's prime first swarms, hived the same day on foundation starters.
- Benton favors at least ten brood frames, or twelve for extracted honey, and notes that a fashion for eight-frame hives was already giving way to larger ones.
- Cook, following A. I. Root, set each hive in a 4-inch bed of sawdust over old bricks so that a queen dropped during handling would be easy to spot.

## Safety and modern practice

- Check local ordinances, setback rules and any state registration requirements before you buy bees. Place hives so the bees' flight path rises over a fence or hedge rather than crossing walkways.
- Anyone with a known bee-sting allergy should talk to a doctor before keeping bees. Keep a phone nearby when you work the hives.
- Modern beginners also buy a hive tool, gloves and a full jacket or suit, and provide a nearby water source so bees don't crowd neighbors' taps.
- American foulbrood is a legally reportable disease in many states. Buy bees from inspected sources, and contact your state apiarist if you suspect it.
- Varroa mites, unknown to these authors, now kill more colonies than anything else. Join a local beekeeping club or extension program to learn how to monitor and manage them.
