# Open and inspect a beehive calmly

Every other beekeeping job starts with opening the hive, and the nineteenth-century manuals all agree on how: slow movements, a little smoke, and no crushed bees. Benton quotes Langstroth: "Let all your motions about your hives be gentle and slow; never crush or injure the bees."

## What you need

- A veil: Cook's is a tube of black tarlatan half a yard long, gathered over a straw hat brim and drawn close at the neck with rubber cord or tucked into a coat
- Gloves for beginners (Cook suggests buckskin or sheepskin; Langstroth suggests rubber gloves)
- Long sleeves gathered at the wrist and trouser legs closed over the shoes
- A bellows smoker
- Smoker fuel: dry, rotten wood, tightly rolled dry cotton cloth, or seasoned hardwood such as hickory or hard maple split into bits half an inch or less across

## Steps

1. Pick a fair, warm time of day when most of the bees are out in the field. Benton explains that the young bees left at home are easy to manage and the returning foragers are laden.
2. Put on your veil, gloves and closed clothing. Cook advises nervous beginners to protect their faces and hands until experience shows them there is little to fear.
3. Light the smoker and work the bellows until it gives a steady volume of smoke.
4. Approach the hive quietly and puff a little smoke into the entrance.
5. Lift the cover slowly. As soon as the smallest gap opens, blow smoke in, then increase it just enough to drive the bees down as you take the cover off.
6. Set the smoker on the windward side of the hive so the smoke drifts across the top of the frames.
7. Pry a frame loose gently and lift it out carefully without crushing bees. Benton warns that crushed bees fill the air with the smell of venom, which sets others stinging.
8. Tilt the comb to bring its other face into view, as Benton's illustrations show, rather than jerking it around.
9. Give a puff of smoke whenever the bees start to boil up or sound angry, but use it lightly. Too much smoke drives bees off the combs into clumps, which makes it hard to find the queen or see the brood.
10. If you are stung, step back for a moment, since the odor of the venom provokes other bees. Remove the sting at once.
11. Return the frames, close the hive gently, and never jar or bump it.

## From the source books

- Langstroth built his system on the observation that bees filled with honey "lose all disposition to volunteer an assault." Cook gets the same effect by closing the hive and rapping on it for four or five minutes, or by sprinkling the bees with sweetened water.
- Benton and Cook both found the black or German bees far crosser than Italians, and Benton says pure Carniolans, given some smoke, never needed a veil at all.
- Bees are crankiest when no nectar is coming in. Cook says even experienced beekeepers want a veil then.
- Langstroth blamed strong body odors, bad breath and sweaty horses for provoking attacks. Cook thought sudden motions mattered more than sweat.
- If a colony does become furious, Langstroth says never to strike back. Retreat to a building or thick bushes and keep still with your head covered.
- Benton mentions that tobacco smoke subdues bees faster and puffball smoke stupefies them, but he recommends plain wood.

## Safety and modern practice

- Burn only plain fuel such as wood, pine needles, burlap or cotton. Never use tobacco or other substances in a smoker.
- A lit smoker is a fire hazard. Set it on bare ground or a metal surface, keep it away from dry grass, and put it out completely afterward by plugging the nozzle or emptying it into a metal can.
- Modern beekeepers often scrape a sting out with a fingernail or hive tool. Speed matters more than method.
- Get emergency help immediately for hives or swelling away from the sting site, swelling of the face or throat, or difficulty breathing. Anyone with a known allergy should carry prescribed epinephrine.
- Work with a partner when you can, and keep children and pets well away during inspections.
