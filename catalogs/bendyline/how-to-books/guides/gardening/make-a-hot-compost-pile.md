# Make a hot compost pile

A hot compost pile turns leaves, weeds, clippings, kitchen scraps and manure into crumbly humus in weeks rather than years. The heat comes from microbes multiplying in a heap that is big enough to hold its own warmth and has the right mix of air, water and nitrogen-rich material. Get those right and the pile does the rest.

## What you need

- A sheltered spot, out of wind, ideally shaded or under a roof
- Coarse "brown" material: dry leaves, straw, old hay, tall weeds, small prunings
- Moist "green" material: grass clippings, kitchen scraps, fresh weeds, garden trimmings, stable manure
- Garden soil, about one-tenth of the pile
- A manure fork, a hose with a spray nozzle, and a wheelbarrow
- Optional: a bin about 4 feet square and 3–4 feet tall, or a cylinder of 2 x 4 inch welded-wire fencing 36–48 inches high and 4–5 feet across (a 5-foot circle takes about 16 feet of wire)
- Optional: old plastic sheeting to keep off winter rain

## Steps

1. Choose a site out of the wind; Solomon notes that wind both cools and dries a heap.
2. Lay a base of fine sticks, small prunings and brushy stems so air can enter from below.
3. Gather enough material for a heap at least 4 feet on each side. Solomon says piles under 3 feet rarely heat, and that 6–7 feet wide and 5 feet high is about the home maximum.
4. Mix coarse dry material with soft wet material instead of laying them in neat layers. Solomon compares this to kneading bread dough rather than stacking a layer cake.
5. Blend in soil as you go, about 10 percent of the pile, and finish with a thin coat of damp soil over the outside.
6. Water while building until a hard-squeezed handful feels very damp but gives only a few drops.
7. If you collect waste gradually, keep a stack of dry coarse material beside the pile and bury each load of scraps, clippings or manure in it.
8. Watch the heat. A well-balanced pile gets very hot within days and stays hot for a few weeks; Solomon expects more than 140°F.
9. Turn the pile when it cools or slumps. Pull it apart with a fork and restack it so the dry outer skin goes to the center, spraying each layer with water as you go.
10. Repeat for two or three turnings. The compost is finished when it is dark brown to black and crumbly, smells sweet, and none of the original materials can be recognized.

## From the source books

- Solomon aims for a starting carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of about 25:1 but judges by results:
  - A pile that barely heats lacks nitrogen. Turn it and mix in manure or other rich material plus a little water.
  - An ammonia smell means too much nitrogen, which is escaping into the air.
  - Sudden cooling and a foul smell mean the pile has run out of air and needs turning.
- In large piles, Solomon sets posts every 3–4 feet while building, then wiggles them out to leave air shafts. His four-bin system moves each batch into the next, smaller bin as it settles.
- Rockwell starts a heap with a few wheelbarrow loads of fresh horse manure, treads it down firmly and keeps it moist, "never soaked." He says light dressings of lime aid decay, and he keeps wood ashes out of the heap for separate use.
- Bailey suggests a permanent pile in an out-of-the-way corner, screened by plantings and turned several times a year.
- Solomon covers his heaps with plastic through Oregon's rainy winter. He suggests using compost that still seems weak as a mulch rather than digging it in just before sowing.

## Safety and modern practice

- Wear gloves when handling manure and compost, and wash your hands afterward.
- Leave meat, bones, dairy, greasy food and dog or cat feces out of home piles; they attract pests and can carry pathogens.
- If you use fresh manure, compost it fully before it goes on vegetable beds. Current produce-safety guidance allows about 90–120 days between applying raw manure and harvesting food crops.
- Solomon recommends ear protection, goggles, heavy gloves and a dust mask when running a shredder, especially with moldy hay. For bins, untreated rot-resistant wood is a simpler choice than the wood preservative he names.
