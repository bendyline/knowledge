# Grow staked tomatoes

Staking and pruning tomatoes to a single stem takes more plants and a little weekly work, but it gives cleaner, larger, earlier fruit and saves space in a home garden. Tracy calls the single stake the best support where plants are pruned "as they should always be in gardens." Rockwell agrees that garden tomatoes should always be staked or trellised.

## What you need

- Sturdy, hardened-off tomato plants (see [Start vegetable seedlings in flats and transplant them](start-vegetable-seedlings-in-flats-and-transplant-them.md))
- One stake per plant, 1–2 inches thick and 4–5 feet long
- Raffia or soft string
- Old, well-rotted manure or compost for the planting holes
- Straw for mulch (optional)
- Paper caps, baskets or newspaper for frost emergencies

## Steps

1. Wait until frost danger is past and the weather has settled. In Work's example, where the average last frost is May 1, growers set plants out May 18–25. Tomatoes do not thrive in "raw, mean, chilly weather."
2. Prepare the planting spots with old, fine manure or compost. Rockwell warns against making the ground too rich, especially with strong, fresh manure.
3. Water the plants well a few hours before moving them. Dig them loose rather than pulling them.
4. Space staked plants about 1½–2 feet apart in rows 3–3½ feet apart.
5. Set each plant a little deeper than it grew in the flat and pack the soil firmly around the roots.
6. Drive a stake beside each plant right away, deep enough to hold through storms.
7. Tie the plant to the stake. Wrap the string once around the stake, then loop it loosely around the stem so it cannot slip down or choke the stem as it thickens.
8. Add new ties as the stem lengthens, which Tracy notes happens very quickly.
9. Every few days, pinch or rub out the side shoots that form where each leaf joins the main stem. Also remove any shoot that grows from a fruit cluster.
10. Take the side shoots off while they are small, and check that you are not removing the main stem by mistake.
11. Once the plants are well established, mulch with straw if you like.
12. In dry spells, water deeply and evenly rather than in frequent light sprinkles.
13. Hand-pick the big green tomato hornworms as soon as you see chewed leaves.

## From the source books

- Tracy sometimes lets two or three stems grow, choosing the strongest shoot below the first fruit cluster as the extra stem. Some growers stop the main stem above the third or fourth cluster, but Tracy "seldom practiced it."
- For plants left unpruned, Tracy supports them on one to three hoops held up by three stakes, or on strips run a foot off the ground along each side of the row.
- Work describes a New England trellis that needs no tying:
  - Set posts about every 25 feet, with one wire along the top and another a foot above the ground.
  - Run jute twine between the wires and twist each plant around its string as it grows.
- Work reports experiments showing that single-stem training lowers the yield per plant but gives larger, cleaner fruit. It also makes sunscald, blossom-end rot and cracking worse, which even, adequate watering and humus-rich soil help prevent.
- Work notes that cracking follows heavy watering of dry soil. He says hoeing beyond weed control did not raise yields in Thompson's trials.
- Rockwell hastens a few early fruits by tying paper bags over the clusters. He keeps most suckers off for "quality and quantity of fruit."
- Work says paper plant protectors let growers set plants out a couple of weeks earlier.

## Safety and modern practice

- Tomato foliage can irritate sensitive skin, so wear gloves or wash after pruning.
- Rotate tomatoes away from beds that grew tomatoes, potatoes, peppers or eggplant in the last two or three years to reduce soil-borne disease.
- Blossom-end rot is now understood as a calcium-uptake problem driven mostly by uneven watering. Steady moisture and mulch prevent it better than added fertilizer.
