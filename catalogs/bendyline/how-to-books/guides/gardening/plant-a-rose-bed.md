# Plant a rose bed

Saylor writes that the "secret" of good roses lies in a congenial location and a deeply prepared bed, not in any spray or fertilizer. Do that work weeks ahead, plant early, and prune hard at the start.

## What you need

- Dormant field-grown roses budded onto a vigorous rootstock (Saylor prefers two-year-old plants), or potted roses
- A spade, a pick and a garden rake
- Plenty of well-rotted manure
- Stones or similar coarse material for drainage, if the ground is wet
- Sand and leaf mold to lighten the soil for tea and hybrid tea roses
- A sharp knife and good pruning shears
- Lawn clippings for summer mulch, and rough manure or leaves for winter cover

## Steps

1. Choose a spot in full sun, on a gentle south slope if possible, sheltered from the north by a wall or building.
2. Keep the bed out of low frost pockets and well away from trees, whose roots spread about as far as the tree is tall. Grow nothing else in it.
3. Lay out narrow beds, about 20 inches wide for one row or 4 feet for two, so you can reach every bush from a path.
4. Several weeks before planting, dig the bed out at least 2 feet deep, keeping the topsoil and sod in one pile and the subsoil in another.
5. Break up the floor with a pick. If the ground is soggy, lay in a layer of stones for drainage.
6. Refill the bed in layers, keeping all manure in the lower two-thirds, since fresh manure near the surface harms new roots:
   - first the best of the subsoil, mixed with well-rotted manure
   - then the chopped sod and topsoil, also enriched with manure
   - finally unmanured topsoil, 2–3 inches above the surrounding ground
7. Plant at the first chance in spring in the North. Saylor says fall planting suits areas south of Philadelphia.
8. When the plants arrive, trim off broken roots and any buds on the rootstock with a sharp knife. If the roots look dry, dip them in thin mud.
9. Dig each hole wide enough for the roots. Spread them out almost flat, sloping slightly downward, with none crossing.
10. Set the bud union, the knob where the variety joins the rootstock, 2 inches below the surface.
11. Firm the soil well around the roots and rake the surface loose.
12. Space small mail-order plants a foot apart each way, and two-year plants 18–24 inches apart.
13. Cut each cane back by one-half to two-thirds, removing dead or bruised wood. Make each cut about 1/4 inch above an outward-facing bud.
14. Through summer, keep the surface loosened with a hoe and rake, or mulch it with lawn clippings.

## From the source books

- **Buying roses:** Saylor favors roses budded on Manetti or brier stock and calls cheap imported plants budded on multiflora "short-lived." A buried bud union keeps rootstock suckers from taking over; cut off any that appear.
- **Mail-order plants:** Bennett pots small plants into 3-inch pots and keeps them in cool shade for a few days before bedding them out.
- **Feeding:** Saylor hangs a burlap sack holding a bushel of cow manure in a barrel of water for two days. He dilutes the liquid with an equal amount of water and gives each plant a half-gallon a week in May and June, after a soaking rain. Bennett says to give no liquid manure until plants are "growing vigorously."
- **Pruning established roses:** Saylor prunes hybrid perpetuals in March and waits to prune teas and hybrid teas until their buds swell. Ramblers need little more than dead canes removed.
- **Pests and disease:** Saylor relies on a daily, forceful fine spray from the hose and on picking caterpillars and beetles by hand. He welcomes toads, ladybugs and birds. Both authors avoid mildew with open, airy sites, and Bennett waters early so leaves dry before night.
- **Winter protection:** In the North, Saylor hills soil around the bases, spreads 3 inches of rough manure, and covers tender roses with leaves held down by brush until March.

## Safety and modern practice

- Both books also recommend poison sprays and dusts, such as Paris green, which contains arsenic, hellebore, tobacco and kerosene. Do not use them. Hose sprays, hand-picking and good siting are enough for most home gardens.
- Wear thick gloves and long sleeves. Keep your tetanus protection current, since thorn punctures carry soil into the skin.
- Current advice on bud-union depth depends on climate: bury it where winters are cold, and set it at or just above soil level in mild regions.
