# Build a cold frame or hotbed

A cold frame is a low, glass-covered box that traps the sun's heat so seedlings can start weeks before the open garden is ready. A hotbed is the same frame set over fermenting manure, which supplies steady bottom heat for very early sowing. Both need daily attention to airing and watering more than they need skill.

## What you need

- Window sash, traditionally 3 x 6 feet and glazed; muslin or light duck cloth can cover cheaper frames
- Lumber for the frame: 2 x 4 posts with 1-inch boards, or 2 x 12 planks
- For a hotbed: fresh manure from grain-fed horses, with some straw bedding or leaves mixed in; Rockwell says a cord covers about 3 sash if the frame sits on top of the pile, or 7 sash if the manure goes in a pit
- Rich, crumbly garden soil, 4–6 inches deep across the bed
- A soil thermometer, a manure fork and a spade
- Mats, old carpet or board shutters for cold nights

## Steps

1. Choose a well-drained spot in full sun, sheltered from the north by a building, a board fence 5–6 feet high or an evergreen hedge.
2. Build the frame so that it slopes toward the south, with the back about 6 inches higher than the front. Tracy makes his 10 inches high in front and 16–18 inches at the back.
3. Join the boards so the frame can be taken apart and stored when not in use.
4. **For a cold frame,** dig well-rotted manure into the soil inside, lay on the sash and start sowing.
5. **For a hotbed,** get the manure several days early. Stack it in a compact, trodden heap, and after three or four days turn it so the outside goes to the middle. Bailey keeps turning until the whole heap steams evenly.
6. Dig out the bed 6–12 inches deep over an area 1–2 feet larger than the frame on every side.
7. Fork in the manure in layers 4–8 inches thick, treading each one down. Bailey says properly mixed manure feels springy underfoot but does not fluff back up.
8. Build the manure 1–2 feet deep. Bailey uses about 2 feet for a bed that must stay warm for two months.
9. Set the frame on the manure and spread 4–6 inches of soil inside it.
10. Close the sash and push the thermometer down to the manure. It will heat strongly for a few days. Sow warm-season seeds such as tomatoes once it falls below 90°F, and cool-season crops below 70–80°F.
11. Prop the sash open with a notched stick whenever the inside reaches 60–80°F, and strip it off on mild days.
12. Water thoroughly in the morning only, and cover the glass with mats or shutters on cold nights.

## From the source books

- Rockwell's other hotbed method sets the frame on a flat heap of manure 9–10 feet wide and 18 inches deep, which works on frozen ground. He notes that spent hotbed manure is "as good as ever" for the garden afterward.
- Bailey lays coarse litter under the manure and a thin layer of leaf mold above it to spread the heat. He wets a slow-starting pile with hot water.
- Bailey's standard cold frame is 12 by 6 feet under four sash. A field version of two staked planks 6 feet apart starts radishes, beets and lettuce two or three weeks early.
- Tracy found that seedlings started with heat, then moved to unheated frames, did better than plants raised to full size on heat. He covered large frames with cloth on rollers.
- All three warn that too little air makes "drawn," spindly plants.

## Safety and modern practice

- Wear gloves to handle fresh manure, wash afterward, and keep raw manure away from any crop that will be eaten from the frame.
- Old window sash often carries lead paint and brittle glass. Test before reuse, or glaze with polycarbonate or twin-wall panels.
- A closed frame overheats fast in sun and can cook seedlings, so vent it every sunny morning.
- If manure is hard to get, use a plug-in heat mat or soil-heating cable rated for outdoor use, plugged into a GFCI-protected outlet.
