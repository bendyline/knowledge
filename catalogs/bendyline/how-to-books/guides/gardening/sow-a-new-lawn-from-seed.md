# Sow a new lawn from seed

A lawn is a permanent planting of thousands of small plants, and Doogue stresses that no seed or fertilizer can make up for a badly prepared bed. The work comes in three parts: deep, rich soil made ready the fall before; careful, even sowing; and gentle handling of the young grass until it toughens.

## What you need

- A spade or plow, an iron rake and a wooden rake
- Plenty of well-rotted manure
- Good loam to replace gravel or very poor soil
- Clean lawn seed, bought by weight. For a sunny lawn, Doogue mixes 12 pounds of Kentucky bluegrass, 5 pounds of redtop and 3 pounds of perennial ryegrass.
- A pail for the seed
- A lawn roller, ideally one you can fill with water or sand to change its weight
- A hose with a fine spray

## Steps

1. In fall, dig the area 2 feet deep or plow it, working in plenty of well-rotted manure. Let it lie rough over winter so frost can mellow the soil and kill weeds.
2. If the ground is gravelly, dig out the poor soil to about 2 feet and replace it with good loam.
3. If moss and sorrel show the soil is sour, lime it in fall at about 1½ bushels of air-slaked lime per 1,000 square feet.
4. In spring, once the frost is out, harrow or fork the plot both ways.
5. Level it by shaving off bumps and filling hollows with the soil, as Rexford describes.
6. Rake the surface fine with an iron rake, removing every stone and bit of rubbish.
7. Spread any fertilizer evenly and rake it in thoroughly. Uneven spreading leaves patches of thick and thin grass.
8. Wait for a still day, ideally a damp one, since even a light wind piles the seed up in drifts.
9. Sow half the seed walking in one direction and the rest at right angles to it. Keep your hand close to the ground and let the seed run through your fingers as your arm swings.
10. Do not sow too thick. Doogue's rate is about 3–4 bushels an acre, and he warns that overcrowded seedlings "mat and damp out."
11. Rake the seed in lightly, then roll the whole area.
12. Water often with a fine spray that will not wash the seed out.
13. Let the new grass reach 3 inches before the first cut, and make that cut on a cloudy day. Doogue used a scythe because mowers of his day could not be set high enough.
14. After a few weeks the grass will have toughened and can be cut often. Leave short clippings where they fall, but rake up heavy ones with a wooden rake.

## From the source books

- Doogue says Kentucky bluegrass alone makes the best permanent lawn but is "not a nervous man's grass": it is slow. The ryegrass and redtop in his mix green the plot within a month while the bluegrass fills in.
- For light shade, Doogue sows:
  - 35 percent Kentucky bluegrass
  - 35 percent wood meadow grass
  - 15 percent various-leaved fescue
  - 15 percent crested dog's-tail
- For banks, he adds bentgrasses, sheep fescue and a little white clover to hold the slope.
- Rexford advises doubling the amount a dealer suggests, since thick seeding looks established by midsummer. Doogue's caution against over-thick sowing is the safer middle ground.
- Doogue tells buyers to ask for samples and to reject seed full of chaff and dust. He says re-cleaned seed is worth its price.
- To renew a thin old lawn without digging, Doogue cuts out weeds with an asparagus knife and scratches the soil half an inch deep. He then sows thickly, rakes, waters and rolls.

## Safety and modern practice

- For cool-season grasses, most current guides prefer seeding in late summer or early fall, when weeds are fewer and the heat is ending. Spring sowing as early as possible, as Doogue advises, is the second choice.
- Lime only when a soil test calls for it. Ground limestone is the usual modern choice; hydrated lime is caustic and needs gloves, eye protection and a dust mask.
- Use a light roller just to firm the seed in. Repeated heavy rolling compacts soil, especially clay.
- Wear sturdy shoes and eye protection when mowing, and keep children and pets away from power mowers.
