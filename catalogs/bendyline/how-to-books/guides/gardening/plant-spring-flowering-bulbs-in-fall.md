# Plant spring-flowering bulbs in fall

Crocuses, scillas, hyacinths, tulips, daffodils and other narcissi must go into the ground in autumn so they can grow roots before winter and flower in spring. The bulbs arrive dormant and ready to grow, so the work is mostly choosing a well-drained spot and planting early enough.

## What you need

- Firm, healthy bulbs: hyacinths, tulips and narcissi are the easiest for beginners
- A sunny bed, ideally sloping slightly south, with soil that never stays soggy
- Old, well-rotted manure or compost
- Coarse sand for heavy soil
- A spade, a trowel, and a garden line or straight board for marking rows
- Leaves, straw or coarse litter for a 6-inch winter mulch

## Steps

1. Plant as soon as bulbs are available, usually in September, and in any case before the ground freezes. Rexford favors late September to early October so roots are made before winter.
2. Check drainage. Rexford says "no bulb can be grown successfully" in waterlogged soil. If water stands, dig the bed out about 1½ feet deep, put 5–6 inches of broken brick or crushed stone in the bottom, and return the soil.
3. Work the soil fine and mellow, and dig in plenty of old, well-rotted manure. Keep the manure deep below the bulbs or use it as a top dressing; Bennett insists it must never touch them.
4. In a formal bed, remove a few inches of topsoil and level the surface. Mark lines both ways across it and set one bulb at each crossing. Bennett spaces tulips 5 inches apart and hyacinths 7 inches.
5. Plant daffodils and other narcissi about 4 inches deep and a foot apart, in double or triple rows. They spread into clumps, so leave them undisturbed until crowding cuts down the bloom.
6. Set tulips 3–4 inches deep and about 6 inches apart for ordinary display, an inch deeper on light soil. In heavy soil, put a little coarse sand around each bulb.
7. Replace the topsoil over the bulbs and firm it.
8. Before winter sets in, cover the bed with about 6 inches of leaves, straw or litter. Rexford explains that the cover keeps the ground from freezing and thawing repeatedly, which tears bulbs loose and breaks their new roots.
9. When shoots appear in spring, pull back the coarse part of the mulch and leave the finer part in place.
10. After bloom, let the foliage ripen and turn yellow before cutting it off or mowing over it.

## From the source books

- **Bulbs in grass:** For crocuses in a lawn, Bennett lifts a flap of sod with a trowel, slips a bulb under it and presses the sod back. Arnott suggests scattering bulbs by hand and planting them where they fall, for natural-looking drifts. Both warn not to mow until the leaves have ripened.
- **Bulbs in borders:** Arnott thinks tulips look best in bold, informal clumps among other plants rather than in "regimental" lines. He plants them from October to late November, holding off until November where late frosts are common.
- **Freeing the bed for summer:** Bennett lifts tulips from beds after flowering and heels them in elsewhere until the tops die. She then stores them in paper bags until fall.
- **Leaving bulbs in place:** Bennett instead sows light-rooted annuals over the bulbs, such as forget-me-nots, pansies, verbena or annual phlox.
- **Pests:** Bennett once planted 500 tulips and saw three come up, the rest lost to moles. She also blames most bulb failures on wireworms carried in manure or on poor drainage.
- **Propagation and cutting:** Arnott increases most bulbs from offsets removed after the foliage dies down. He cuts flowers before they are fully open so they last longer in water.

## Safety and modern practice

- Daffodil and narcissus bulbs are poisonous if eaten, and tulip and hyacinth bulbs can irritate skin. Wear gloves, and store bulbs away from children, pets and the onion bin.
- Many current guides plant large bulbs deeper, at about two to three times the bulb's height (6–8 inches for big tulips). Deeper planting helps them survive cold winters and digging animals.
- To stop voles and squirrels, line the planting area with hardware cloth, or favor daffodils, which rodents generally leave alone.
