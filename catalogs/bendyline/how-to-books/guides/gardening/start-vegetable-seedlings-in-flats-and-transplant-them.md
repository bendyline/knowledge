# Start vegetable seedlings in flats and transplant them

Tomatoes, peppers, cabbage, lettuce, onions and many other crops do best when they go into the garden as sturdy young plants. Raising them yourself means you get the varieties you want, ready on the day the soil is. The aim is a short-jointed, deep-green plant, neither soft and drawn up nor stunted.

## What you need

- Flats: shallow wooden boxes with a few drainage holes. Rockwell uses 2-inch-deep boxes for sowing and 3-inch-deep ones for transplants, about 13 x 19 inches.
- Seed-flat soil: equal parts rotted sod, leaf mold and sand, sifted, with no manure
- Transplant soil: the same kind of soil sifted through a coarse screen and mixed with one-third well-rotted manure
- A flat board for leveling and firming, labels, a fine-rose watering can or a piece of burlap
- A pointed stick or dibber
- A sunny frame or window where you can hold the temperatures below (see [Build a cold frame or hotbed](build-a-cold-frame-or-hotbed.md))

## Steps

1. Put a layer of coarse material in the bottom third of each seed flat, then fill it with sifted soil.
2. Press the soil firmly into the corners and level it smooth with a board.
3. Sow in rows 2–3 inches apart, or broadcast if you need hundreds of plants, and label every row.
4. Press the seed into the surface with the board. Cover it 1/8–1/4 inch deep with sifted soil, which is about twice the seed's own diameter.
5. Water through a fine rose or through burlap so the soil does not wash.
6. Keep the flats warm while they germinate. Rockwell's targets are:
   - about 55°F for cabbage, cauliflower, lettuce and beets
   - 50°F for onions and celery
   - 75°F for tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, cucumbers, melons and squash
   - no more than 10 degrees below or 15 degrees above these figures
7. Seedlings appear in 2–12 days. If they lift a crust instead of breaking through, crumble it with your fingers and water gently.
8. Water only when the soil is fairly dry but before plants wilt. Then soak it, preferably before 11 a.m. on a sunny day.
9. Give air whenever the frame reaches 60–80°F, and keep stems and leaves as dry as you can.
10. When the second true leaf is forming, water the seed flat. The next day, lift out a clump of seedlings with its soil.
11. Gently pull the seedlings apart, discarding crooked or weak ones. Never pull them straight out of the flat.
12. Set each seedling in a dibber hole deep enough to bury about half its stem, and firm the soil against it with your fingertips. Put about 100 plants in a 13 x 19 inch flat, or 50–75 if you want bigger plants.
13. Water the new flats well and shade them from midday sun for two or three days.
14. For five to seven days before planting out, harden the plants off by exposing them more each day, until they stay out a few nights unprotected.
15. On planting day, water the flats a few hours ahead. Cut the plants apart with a knife, keeping a ball of soil on each.
16. Set each plant deep enough to cover its roots and most of its stem. Firm it in with your knuckles, then press the soil down on both sides with your feet.

## From the source books

- For a garden planted out in April, Rockwell sows cabbage and cauliflower from February 1, tomatoes, peppers and eggplant from March 1, and melons from April 1.
- Rockwell's "sub-irrigation" flats hold soaked moss, then soaked soil, topped with dry soil for sowing. He said this gave a near-perfect stand with little watering.
- In dry weather, Rockwell pours a pint of water into each hole before planting, or dips the roots in thick mud, and cuts the large leaves back by half.
- Bailey also removes much of the foliage at transplanting. He shades new plants with shingles, paper or inverted pots, and waters at nightfall only if he must.
- Both prefer to transplant in cloudy weather or just before rain.

## Safety and modern practice

- Damping-off is caused by soil-borne fungi. Clean, sterile seed-starting mix and washed containers prevent it better than garden soil does.
- Fluorescent or LED grow lights hung a few inches above the seedlings are a practical modern substitute for a glass frame.
